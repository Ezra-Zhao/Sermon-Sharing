## Summary (about 100 words)

Judah went down from his brothers to Adullam, married a Canaanite woman, lost two sons in succession, and sent his widowed daughter-in-law Tamar away with an empty promise. Years later, by the pledge Judah himself had left in her hands, Tamar brought him to confess publicly: She is more righteous than I. At the very depth of human unrighteousness, delay, and shame, God still preserved the Messianic lineage in the detour, and Perez was born from it.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter we read that Joseph was sold by his brothers into Egypt, and Potiphar, the captain of Pharaoh's guard, bought him. By the ordinary logic of a story, this chapter should go on to tell how Joseph gained a foothold in Egypt. But the pen of Genesis suddenly turns and moves the camera away from Joseph onto another man — Judah. The very one who spoke up and proposed, "Let us sell him to the Ishmaelites," the one among the brothers with the most weight, whose word counted the most. The Bible does not let him hide behind the crowd; it reserves a whole chapter for him alone, recording the most unseemly page of his life.

Let us say this at the outset: this chapter is not easy to preach. There is death in it, betrayal within marriage, the grievance of a widow, and episodes that are hard to speak of. But precisely because it is not easy to preach, we must preach it honestly. The Bible never puts powder and paint on God's people; Abraham's weakness and David's sin are all recorded as they were, and this chapter of Judah's is the same. It is placed here not so that we can watch a spectacle, but so that we can see two things: to what depth a human being can fall, and how God, in a person's most unseemly detour, still preserves His own promise.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And Judah said unto his brethren, What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood? Come, and let us sell him to the Ishmeelites..." (Genesis 37:26-27, excerpt, KJV)

"And Judah went down from his brethren, and turned in to a certain Adullamite, whose name was Hirah. And Judah saw there a daughter of a certain Canaanite, whose name was Shuah; and he took her, and went in unto her." (Genesis 38:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And Judah said unto Onan's widow in effect: Remain a widow at thy father's house, till Shelah my son be grown: for he said, Lest peradventure he die also, as his brethren did." (paraphrased excerpt, see Genesis 38:11)

"And in process of time the daughter of Shuah, Judah's wife, died; and Judah was comforted, and went up unto his sheepshearers to Timnath, he and his friend Hirah the Adullamite." (Genesis 38:12, excerpt, KJV)

"And it came to pass about three months after, that it was told Judah, saying, Tamar thy daughter in law hath played the harlot; and also, behold, she is with child by whoredom. And Judah said, Bring her forth, and let her be burnt." (Genesis 38:24, excerpt, KJV)

"When she was brought forth, she sent to her father in law, saying, By the man, whose these are, am I with child: and she said, Discern, I pray thee, whose are these, the signet, and bracelets, and staff. And Judah acknowledged them, and said, She hath been more righteous than I; because that I gave her not to Shelah my son. And he knew her again no more." (Genesis 38:25-26, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, this chapter today is like a mirror, reflecting human unrighteousness, weakness, and shame, and also reflecting Your faithfulness that does not let go in the detour. Give us the courage to face ourselves honestly, and give us hearts of confession and repentance; and let us see all the more that Your genealogy is advanced not by human respectability, but by Your grace and mercy. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Going Down from His Brothers — The Beginning of a Man Drifting Away (38:1-11)

The very first sentence of the text sets the tone of the whole chapter: "At that time Judah went down from his brethren." Please note this "went down." In the narrative of Genesis, going down is often more than a geographical movement. What Judah left was not only a dwelling place; he left the company of his brothers, his father's house, that family which, though broken, was chosen by God. He went down to Adullam and stayed in the house of a man named Hirah; there he married a wife, the daughter of a Canaanite named Shuah. The Bible passes no comment on the details of this marriage; it simply records, in a level tone, that he "saw" a woman and "took her" as his wife. From seeing to taking her in, there is no prayer in between, no seeking, no father's blessing — only a string of hasty actions. A man whose word carried the most weight at home, once he had left the community, made his decisions by his own eyes and desires, and step by step his feet drifted far away.

Brothers and sisters, a person rarely falls to the bottom overnight. Usually he first leaves the community, then lowers his standards, and then comforts himself with "everyone does it this way." Judah was not a man who did not believe in God; he was Jacob's son, a descendant of the covenant of promise. But living alone in the midst of Canaan, the customs around him and the way of life around him wore him down day by day until he was no different from those around him. The seeds of all the later disgrace in this chapter are buried in its first sentence: he first left his brothers, and only then arrived at the point where he could not keep himself. It is the same for us today: do not make light of "leaving" as a small matter. Leaving the gatherings, leaving Bible reading and prayer, leaving the people who will tell you the truth and remind you — this is how a person drifts: first the heart goes far, then the feet follow, and only when the matter is exposed does he discover that he is long since no longer where he was.

Judah and his Canaanite wife had three sons: the firstborn Er, the second Onan, and the third Shelah. When Er grew up, Judah took a wife for him named Tamar. Then the text says: "And Er, Judah's firstborn, was wicked in the sight of the LORD; and the LORD slew him." The Bible does not explain what wickedness Er actually committed; it leaves only one weighty sentence: he was wicked in the sight of the LORD. People look at people and see the facade, the family standing, the usefulness; God looks at a person and sees the true condition of the heart. Er's death reminds us: having a name in the genealogy is not the same as standing firm before God; being born into the family of promise cannot substitute for a person's own reverence. Every generation must live honestly before God for itself; no one can coast through life on the blessing of his ancestors.

After Er died, according to the custom of the time, the younger brother was to raise up offspring for his elder brother, so that the elder brother's name would not be cut off. This is the levirate duty later written explicitly in the Law of Moses: the brother was to take his widowed sister-in-law, and the firstborn she bore would be counted under the name of the brother who had died. Judah said to Onan: "Go in unto thy brother's wife, and marry her, and raise up seed to thy brother." This was a command, and it was a duty. But Onan secretly did an unrighteous thing: he knew that the offspring would not be counted as his own, so in carrying out this duty he deliberately frustrated it, refusing to raise up seed for his brother. The Bible's record of this matter is restrained and solemn; it points only to his motive — he refused. He took the advantage of the relationship but refused to bear its responsibility; he occupied the position but would not pay the cost. The text says: "And the thing which he did displeased the LORD: wherefore he slew him also."

Please do not misread this passage. The point of this chapter is not to talk in a prurient way about one man's private act, but to let us see a more universal sin: knowing one's duty clearly, yet deliberately not doing it; taking the benefit, yet pushing away the responsibility. Onan's wickedness lay in how shrewdly his heart calculated: he wanted the advantages of the standing, but would not pay the price of continuing his brother's name. There is no shortage of such people today — taking without bearing responsibility in marriage, enjoying without doing one's part at home, wanting a position without serving in the church. What God counts as evil is often not some earth-shaking crime, but this kind of heart that calculates shrewdly in secret and profits itself at others' expense. The Bible speaks so weightily in order to make us examine ourselves: is there some duty I clearly know, which I have kept deliberately delaying, evading, and pretending not to see?

When two sons had died in succession, Judah's inward reaction is captured by the text with piercing accuracy. He said to Tamar: "Remain a widow at thy father's house, till Shelah my son be grown." On the surface this was an arrangement; at its core, the text writes out his inner words directly: "for he said, Lest peradventure he die also, as his brethren did." He was afraid. He did not dare to say it plainly, so he gave a promise that sounded entirely reasonable: wait. Wait until Shelah grows up. But this "wait" carried a reservation from the very beginning: he silently laid the deaths of his two sons at Tamar's door, as if the misfortune had been brought by this woman; he would neither admit the evil in his own house nor give her an honest explanation, but used a delaying tactic to send her back to her father's house. From then on, Tamar put off her identity as a bride, put on a widow's garments, and waited day after day in her father's house. The end of Part One is a man's fear and calculation, left for a helpless woman to carry: whether the promise she was waiting for would ever count at all, Part Two will give us the answer.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Pledge and the Confession — "She Is More Righteous Than I" (38:12-26)

"In process of time" — with these few words the text presses out the weight of the years. In all those years, Judah's wife died, he was comforted, and life went on as usual; Shelah also grew up year by year. But the word Judah had spoken was never fulfilled. The text states it plainly: Shelah had grown up, yet Judah did not bring Tamar back to give her to Shelah as his wife. The promise had expired, no one mentioned it, and it seemed as if everyone had forgotten — everyone except the widowed Tamar, who had not forgotten. What she was waiting for was not a wedding; she was waiting for the standing this family owed her, a way forward, the security that by custom ought to have been hers. In that era, a widow with no son, suspended by her husband's family, was left hanging in midair: a sojourner in her father's house, without a place in her husband's house, with no one to support her in old age. Judah's delay delayed no small matter; it delayed a person's whole life.

Brothers and sisters, delay sometimes wounds more than refusal. A refusal is at least spoken plainly; delay makes a person live on hope while being slowly worn away. If we have no intention of keeping a promise we make to someone, we should not make it in the first place; and if we have made it, we should act on it early. Judah's lesson today is one that husbands, fathers, and leaders must all hear: words spoken at home must count, things promised to children must count, and an explanation owed to someone cannot be put off with a "let's wait." God is faithful, and God's people should also be faithful in small things. Judah broke faith first — this is the cause of the whole chapter; all the disgrace that follows grew from this seed of unrighteousness. If in reading this chapter we see only Tamar's stratagem and miss Judah's prior loss of righteousness, we have read the Bible's order backwards.

After Judah's wife died and the days of mourning had passed, he went up with his friend Hirah to Timnah for the sheep-shearing. Sheep-shearing was a major occasion for a farming household, a season of feasting and indulgent celebration. Someone told Tamar the news. She made a bold and heavy decision: she put off her widow's garments, veiled her face, and sat by the road on the way to Timnah. She knew Judah would pass this way, and she also knew that the ordinary road was already closed — she had nowhere to appeal, no one honored the promise, and only by such a decisive act could she lay before Judah the question of the offspring this family owed her. We need not beautify what she did, and the Bible does not commend her means; but neither can we fix our eyes only on her extraordinary act while letting go the man who broke faith and drove her into a corner. What she sought, from beginning to end, was only one thing: to raise up offspring for her dead husband and to regain in this family the standing that was due her. This line of intent runs along the same line as the levirate duty later written explicitly in the Law: what she insisted on accomplishing was exactly what Judah's house ought to have accomplished and kept delaying.

Judah passed by on the road. He did not recognize the veiled Tamar, mistook her for a harlot by the roadside, and went up to bargain with her. Here we must point out Judah's sin solemnly and clearly, without embellishing details: a man who had just come through the period of mourning, on a road of feasting and indulgence, once again acted on his own desire; this may not have been his habit ordinarily, but precisely when no one recognized him and no one restrained him, his true condition was revealed. The choices a person makes in secret show most clearly who he really is. In the family Judah was an elder whose word counted, a man who could decide life and death for others; yet before his own desire he was no stronger than any other weak man.

In the course of the bargaining, Tamar asked him for one thing as a pledge, to be redeemed when the young goat was sent. He asked her what pledge he should give her. Judah asked what she wanted, and she said: "Thy signet, and thy bracelets, and thy staff that is in thine hand." The signet was the token by which the ancients stamped their mark on documents and objects, equal to a person's signature and identity; the cord carried the signet and was worn on the person; the staff was an object a head of household carried with him, often engraved with his mark. All three things pointed directly to identity; they could not be lost and could not be lent. Yet Judah handed them over. That a man, for a moment of private desire, can casually pawn in sin the tokens that represent his own identity and dignity — this picture is itself a warning: sin never asks us to pay only a moment; what it asks us to pawn is our own name, our own character, the evidence against ourselves that one day cannot be denied. Afterward, Judah sent his friend with the goat to redeem the pledge, but the woman was nowhere to be found, and the people of the place said there had never been a harlot there. Judah said a sentence that well represents the attitude of the world: "Let her take it to her, lest we be shamed." What he cared about was respectability, about not being shamed; he thought the matter had passed just like that. But what has been pawned away must sooner or later return before its owner for a reckoning, and that scene came faster than he imagined.

About three months later, someone came and told Judah: your daughter-in-law Tamar has acted unrighteously and is with child. Judah's reaction was frighteningly fast: "Bring her forth, and let her be burnt!" Please note the weight of this sentence. In name Tamar was still the one betrothed to Shelah, and her offense, under the custom of the time and the later Law, was a grave one; but the man who opened his mouth to condemn her did not yet know, three days earlier as it were, that he himself was the other party in the same case. That Judah judged so fast and so harshly precisely exposes the absurdity of the human heart: toward the sins of others our eyes are sharp as snow and punishments are on the tip of our tongue; toward our own sins we layer cover upon cover and ask only not to be shamed. Such scenes are not rare in the church and in the family: the person sitting on the judgment seat is not necessarily the holier person; he may simply be the one who has not yet been exposed. It was at this very moment, as Tamar was being brought out to be punished, that she sent those three things, with one sentence: "By the man, whose these are, am I with child... Discern, I pray thee, whose are these, the signet, and bracelets, and staff."

This sentence is the summit of the whole chapter. She did not weep or make a scene, she did not expose him to his face, she did not cry out Judah's name before the crowd; she sent the evidence before him and left the final verdict to himself: Discern, I pray thee. She left him room to open his mouth and confess, and she also backed him into a corner from which there was no evasion. And Judah's answer is the most important sentence of his life: "She hath been more righteous than I; because that I gave her not to Shelah my son." Please hear this sentence carefully. He made no excuse, he did not push the responsibility onto Tamar's stratagem, he did not say he had been momentarily confused, and still less did he fly into a rage of shame and order her silenced. Before the very people who had brought the things, he admitted two things: first, she is more righteous than I — the one in the wrong is me, and the standing and offspring she had insisted on contending for were what I ought to have given her; second, he named the root of the sickness with perfect clarity — because I did not give her to my son Shelah. His confession was not a vague "we are both at fault," but a specific owning of this one article: that he had broken faith first. The text adds one more sentence: "And he knew her again no more." Knowing his wrong, he also stopped; confessing his sin, he also turned back. In these few words, Judah the man began, from this chapter on, to be different.

We must say this solemnly: this sentence, "She hath been more righteous than I," is the turning point of Judah as a character. Before this, when he sold his brother he calculated profit, when he married he followed his eyes, when he sent Tamar away he used delay, and when he condemned Tamar he relied on his authority — he had always lived inside calculation and respectability. From this moment, for the first time he laid his respectability down before others and owned his sin. The reality of a person's repentance does not lie in how sorrowfully he weeps, but in whether he dares to admit specifically: in this matter, the wrong is mine. Judah did it. That is why the Bible's account of the second half of his life is written in an entirely different tone: in the years of famine, it was he who stood surety before his father and took Benjamin down to Egypt, saying, "I will be surety for him; of my hand shalt thou require him: if I bring him not unto thee... then let me bear the blame for ever"; and before the high official in Egypt, it was he who stood out and pleaded with earnest words, willing to remain himself as a slave in exchange for his brother's return to their father. The Judah who stood surety for Benjamin and pleaded for his father grew out of the Judah who confessed in this chapter. The light of Genesis chapter 44 has its source in this publicly owned sentence in chapter 38. A person who will not confess has no turning point; once he has confessed, a detour has the possibility of becoming a straight road.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Breaking Forth — God Still Works in a Broken Genealogy (38:27-30)

The end of the story returns to Tamar. She was carrying twins. When she was in labor, one child put out his hand first, and the midwife took a scarlet thread and bound it on his hand, saying, this one came out first; but the child drew back his hand, and his brother broke forth instead. The midwife exclaimed: "How hast thou broken forth? this breach be upon thee." Therefore his name was called Pharez — Perez — and Perez means precisely breaking forth, a breach. Afterward, the brother with the scarlet thread on his hand came out, and his name was called Zarah — Zerah. A scarlet thread was meant to mark who the firstborn was, yet it could not hold down the order God had determined; the precedence people assumed was, once again in God's hand, gently turned over — just as Isaac came before Ishmael, Jacob before Esau, and Ephraim, Joseph's younger son, was blessed before the firstborn: the thread of God's choosing never runs according to human queueing.

If the name Perez stopped in the birthing room, it would be no more than an interesting anecdote. But the Bible itself draws this line out long, to a length that makes one solemn. The end of the Book of Ruth records the genealogy of Perez: Perez begat Hezron, Hezron begat Ram, Ram begat Amminadab, and on it goes, begetting Boaz, begetting Obed, begetting Jesse, begetting David. The words with which the elders blessed Boaz and Ruth were exactly these: "The LORD make the woman that is come into thine house like Rachel and like Leah, which two did build the house of Israel... and let thy house be like the house of Pharez, whom Tamar bare unto Judah." Come to the New Testament, and in the genealogy at the opening of Matthew's Gospel, counting down generation by generation, the third name is: "And Judas begat Phares and Zara of Thamar; and Phares begat Esrom." Brothers and sisters, please stop here and think: the genealogy of the Lord Jesus Christ is the holiest list in the whole Bible, yet at its head it writes, openly and without embarrassment, the names of Judah and Tamar and this chapter's affair. Matthew did not cover it up; Tamar's name stands in the same list as Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. God did not wait until Judah's house produced a respectable story before joining the line of the Messiah onto it; right in this most unseemly record, He carried the lineage of kings forward. David came from Perez, and David's Son, the Lord Jesus Christ, also came from Perez.

This is the deepest gospel of this chapter. God preserves the genealogy not by human respectability, but by His own faithfulness and grace. Judah's unrighteousness was real, Tamar's grievance was real, and the shame of this history was real; but God's sovereignty and mercy are greater than all of these. He can cause a family line that was delayed by people, covered up by people, and nearly cut off, to break forth and become a channel of blessing to all nations. Please note: that the Bible writes it this way by no means says that we may sin at ease because God will clean up the mess anyway. Exactly the opposite: this chapter first uses a whole chapter to let us see clearly the price of sin — two sons dead, a widow wronged for years, a head of household shamed in public. Grace is not indulgence toward sin; grace is that, after sin has been brought into the open as it truly is and specifically owned by the person, God still does not cast away that person and that house. Without the confessing Judah, there is no connection to the pleading Judah of chapter 44; without steps of turning back, the blessing of the genealogy would not come round to us to receive today.

There is one more layer we must not miss: Tamar's name is solemnly recorded in the New Testament genealogy. In Matthew's genealogy very few women are named, and the several who are named — Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and the wife of Uriah — each had an unusual history. By writing Tamar in, Matthew is telling every reader of the genealogy: the grace of God has never come upon a clean list without any stain, but upon a company of people who received mercy. Tamar was widowed for years and no one spoke for her; had it not been for those three pledges, her grievance would almost have been buried together with her punishment; but God Himself preserved a witness for her — not only causing Judah to restore her justice publicly, but also bringing her name into the genealogy of the Messiah, so that readers of the Bible in every age would know: God hears the voice of the wronged, and God remembers those who have been wronged. As the church reads this chapter today, it should also learn this: do not judge whose words are credible only by position and by volume of voice; the more a person has no standing and the less he can speak, the more we need to stop, establish the facts, and restore him his justice. This is not worldly sympathy; this is righteousness in the Bible — because our God is precisely such a God, who preserves a name for Tamar and redresses the wronged.

Finally, let the words of this chapter land on us. First, learn Judah's confession — be specific. In our prayers, the easiest thing to say is "Lord, forgive my weakness," and the hardest thing to say is "in this matter the wrong is mine: I broke faith, I delayed a debt I owed, I said what I should not have said." What Judah confessed had a name and an address: I gave her not to Shelah my son. May we also, before God and before people, speak out our own one article. The more specific the confession, the more real the repentance, and the deeper the healing that comes. Second, learn to keep promises without delay. Is there a "Shelah" in your home — a matter that should long ago have been dealt with and has been put off ever since? An explanation long owed, a debt long unpaid, a duty long unperformed? Do not wait until the evidence is delivered before you and you are forced to admit it; while it is still today, go and do it yourself first. Third, hand the genealogy back to God. If you are anxious over your children and over your family's past, feeling that this house has too many detours and too much shame to be worthy of blessing, please remember Perez: what God does best is to carry on at the very place where people thought the line was cut off. The parents' responsibility is to confess, to turn back, and to bring the children before God; the outcome of the genealogy is not in the hands of our respectability, but in the hands of the God who breaks through every obstruction. Fourth, make room in your heart for those who, like Tamar, have been wronged. The church should be a place that protects the wronged, not a place that hears only those with authority. Tamar's voice was heard only by means of evidence; may we learn sooner to bow down and listen to the end to those who have no place and no voice, because God hears them, and God also redresses their wrongs.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through the heaviest chapter in Genesis. Judah left his brothers and drifted step by step; he broke faith in his duty, fell in his private desire, and treated another harshly from the judgment seat, until those three pledges were delivered before him and he bowed his head and said: She is more righteous than I. This one sentence of confession became the turning point of his life; and right in this broken record, God preserved the birth of Perez, preserved the house of David, and preserved the coming of the Messiah. Human detours are real, but God's faithfulness is greater; human shame is real, but the grace after confession is deeper.

Let us pray together: Faithful Lord, we confess before You that we too are like Judah: there are times when we break faith, times when we delay, times when we care only for respectability and refuse to admit our wrong. Enlighten us, point out to us that concrete matter, and give us the courage to confess before others and admit our wrong before people; comfort also all among us who have been wronged, delayed, and left suspended, and prepare a way out for them, as You redressed Tamar's wrong. We thank You all the more that You did not cast our house away because of our unseemliness; You caused Perez to break forth, and caused David's Son, the Lord Jesus Christ, to be born from this lineage. May our house also, beginning from confession and turning back, be joined together again by You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "When she was brought forth, she sent to her father in law, saying, By the man, whose these are, am I with child: and she said, Discern, I pray thee, whose are these, the signet, and bracelets, and staff. And Judah acknowledged them, and said, She hath been more righteous than I; because that I gave her not to Shelah my son." (Genesis 38:25-26, KJV) "And Judas begat Phares and Zara of Thamar; and Phares begat Esrom; and Esrom begat Aram." (Matthew 1:3, KJV)

The most important words of Judah's life were not the calculation he made when he sold his brother, but this sentence he spoke with bowed head when the pledge was brought before him: She is more righteous than I. He made no excuse and shifted no blame; he specifically owned that he had broken faith first. The hardest part of confession is not admitting that we are not good, but naming the concrete matter aloud: I broke my promise, I delayed what I should have done, I wronged that person. Judah owned that one matter, and his life turned from that point; later he could even stand as surety for his brother and plead for his father. More wonderfully still, God did not disdain this unseemly record: the genealogy at the opening of Matthew's Gospel openly writes the names of Judah, Tamar, and Perez. Today, bring that matter you have long delayed and long covered before God, and name it specifically. Confession is not the end; it is the starting point where God joins your household back together again.

# Commentary on the Gospel of John, Volume One, Chapter 8: Has No One Condemned You?

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «没有人定你的罪吗».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

- Volume One, Chapter 8
- Author: Guangyi Zhao (Guangyi Zhao)
- Scripture: John 8:1-36

---

In the previous chapter, on the last day of the Feast of Tabernacles, Jesus stood and cried out that if anyone thirsts, he may come to Him and drink. The feast dispersed and the crowd dispersed; early the next morning, He returned to the temple. This time, what awaited Him was not thirsty people, but a trap set in public. Right in the middle of the trap stood a woman whose name has not even been left behind.

In this chapter there are two sentences, facing each other across a judgment. One is the condemnation cried out by the crowd; the other is the words Jesus spoke slowly only after He had stooped down. In reading this chapter, one might pay attention to the order in which Jesus spoke: He first dealt with the people holding stones, and then turned back to speak to the one standing in the middle, trembling.

## I. The Scripture

The events took place in the temple early in the morning, as Jesus sat down to teach the people who had gathered around Him.

> Then the scribes and Pharisees brought to Him a woman caught in adultery. And when they had set her in the midst, they said to Him, "Teacher, this woman was caught in adultery, in the very act. Now Moses, in the law, commanded us that such should be stoned. But what do You say?" This they said, testing Him, that they might have something of which to accuse Him. But Jesus stooped down and wrote on the ground with His finger. (John 8:3-6, NKJV)

Everyone present understood this trap. If Jesus said, Let her go, He would be openly violating the law of Moses, and His reputation as a Teacher would be smashed on the spot; if He said, Stone her, all the mercy He ordinarily spoke of would become empty words — besides which, the Jews at that time were under Roman rule and did not themselves have the authority to carry out the death penalty. Either way was wrong. Those who asked the question were waiting for Him to open His mouth.

> So when they continued asking Him, He raised Himself up and said to them, "He who is without sin among you, let him throw a stone at her first." And again He stooped down and wrote on the ground. Then those who heard it went out one by one, beginning with the oldest even to the last. And Jesus was left alone, and the woman standing in the midst. (John 8:7-9, NKJV)

Only after the crowd had dispersed did the quietest dialogue in the whole chapter take place:

> When Jesus had raised Himself up, He said to her, "Woman, where are those accusers of yours? Has no one condemned you?" She said, "No one, Lord." And Jesus said to her, "Neither do I condemn you; go and sin no more." (John 8:10-11, NKJV)

Not long afterward, Jesus spoke again to the crowd, saying a sentence connected with this judgment:

> Then Jesus spoke to them again, saying, "I am the light of the world. He who follows Me shall not walk in darkness, but have the light of life." (John 8:12, NKJV)

In the latter half of this chapter, He had an argument about freedom with those who heard His teaching. Some felt that they had been born free and had never been slaves to anyone, yet Jesus put the matter very weightily:

> Then Jesus said to those Jews who believed Him, "If you abide in My word, you are My disciples indeed. And you shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." (John 8:31-32, NKJV)

> Jesus answered them, "Most assuredly, I say to you, whoever commits sin is a slave of sin. And a slave does not abide in the house forever, but a son abides forever. Therefore if the Son makes you free, you shall be free indeed." (John 8:34-36, NKJV)

A few words need to be made clear first.

One is condemn. This word appears very early in the Gospel of John. In chapter 3 there is a sentence that illuminates this chapter exactly: "For God did not send His Son into the world to condemn the world, but that the world through Him might be saved" (John 3:17, NKJV). To condemn is a verdict in a court of law, the judge striking the gavel and saying this person is guilty. When Jesus said to that woman, Neither do I condemn you, He did not mean that this thing never happened, nor that right and wrong need not be distinguished; He meant that that hammer-blow — He would not strike it.

Another is light. In the original language, this word is both the light of ordinary illumination and that light which John consistently uses to refer to truth and life. The Gospel of John says at its opening: "And the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it" (John 1:5, NKJV). When a person does something he is ashamed of, his instinct is to hide in a dark place, because as soon as the light comes, the matter can no longer be hidden. But the light in this chapter has an unexpected temperament: it shone upon that woman, yet it did not push her out to be displayed before the public; that business of public display was exactly what the people holding stones were doing.

Another is truth. In the original language, besides truth in the sense of doctrine, this word also carries the meaning of true, real, the kind of truth without adulteration. When the Lü Zhenzhong Translation handles this stretch of Scripture, it also deliberately lets the words land on the level of reality: the truth makes people free not because it is a pleasant-sounding slogan, but because it leads people out of illusion. A person living in illusion, propping himself up by fabricating stories, looks free, but in fact must everywhere guard against being exposed; the truth wounds a person for a moment, yet it is the only thing that can enable a person to stand upright.

## II. Exposition

First look at how unjustly this trap was set.

How was the original text of the law written? "The man who commits adultery with another man's wife, he who commits adultery with his neighbor's wife, the adulterer and the adulteress, shall surely be put to death" (Leviticus 20:10, NKJV). In Deuteronomy the same meaning is recorded once again (see Deuteronomy 22:22): two people were caught, and two people should have been standing in the midst. But on this day only one woman was dragged in; the man was nowhere to be seen. Adultery cannot be committed by one person alone. A judgment flying the banner of the law had itself violated the law from the very moment the person was dragged in. Where the man had gone, the Scripture does not explain; perhaps he was let go, perhaps he was arranged from the start by those who set the trap: in this scene the woman was never the defendant; she was a prop, a stone used to trip Jesus.

Look again at Jesus stooping down and writing. What He wrote on the ground, John did not record, and later generations have guessed for two thousand years without reaching a conclusion. The guesses may be left unsettled; what matters is the action itself. Over there they pressed forward step by step, waiting for one of His sentences to fall into the snare; He, however, stooped down and slowed down. In the whole temple courtyard, the most anxious were the people holding stones, and the quietest was He. He did not snatch to defend Himself, nor ask a question back; He first let the anger filling the courtyard cool in silence. When He raised Himself up, He said only one sentence: He who is without sin among you, let him throw a stone at her first.

The power of this sentence lies in that it did not abolish the law in the least. The law required the witnesses to strike first (see Deuteronomy 17:7); its intent was to make the one giving testimony weigh it himself first: Can you bear the weight of the sin you are testifying against? Jesus tightened the condition further: the one who strikes first must himself be without sin. At this, the judgment seat and the defendant's seat quietly exchanged positions. Everyone present held a stone in his hand, and suddenly discovered that he too had been placed in the row of those being judged. The order John records is thought-provoking: beginning with the oldest even to the last, one by one they went out. The older ones left first, probably because the longer the years, the more shameful things one remembers, and the shorter one could stand before that sentence. Their way of putting down the stones was to turn around and leave. In this chapter, those who truly understood the word sin were, on the contrary, this group who had come to condemn another person's sin.

There is also one matter that must be honestly explained. This passage of Scripture — that is, from the end of John chapter 7 to chapter 8 verse 11 — does not appear in the earliest extant Greek manuscripts, and not a few modern translations make a note of this in the margin. For two thousand years the church has nevertheless treasured and read aloud this passage as a precious record, and its content agrees completely with Jesus' conduct in the other Gospels. To lay this matter out in the open is not to make the reader suspicious; on the contrary: everything spoken of in this book can withstand being laid out in the open and examined in this way.

The people dispersed, and the woman remained in the midst. Note that she did not slip away in the confusion; perhaps she simply could not move. The dialogue is only three sentences: Jesus asked, Has no one condemned you? She answered, No one, Lord. Only at this moment did He speak that sentence that has been preached for two thousand years: Neither do I condemn you; go and sin no more. Neither half of the sentence can be missing. Hearing only the first half would turn mercy into indulgence, as though Jesus were saying, It does not matter, let the past be past; He did not say that. Go and sin no more points out a new road for her to see: You do not have to go back to that road. He did not want her life; what He wanted was for her days from then on to be lived in a different way. In the whole courtyard full of people, only He was qualified to condemn her, and only He said to her that He would not condemn her. This was not a cheap letting-off; this was a letting-off in which someone bore the cost in her place — and what that cost was will only be revealed at the cross in chapter 19 of this Gospel.

The I am the light of the world that follows immediately is not a new start either. On the nights of the Feast of Tabernacles, tall lampstands were lit in the Court of the Women in the temple, their light filling the temple courts, and the people danced joyfully in the light, in remembrance of the guidance of the pillar of cloud and the pillar of fire in the wilderness. The feast had just ended, and the lamps seemed still to be before their eyes, when Jesus said, I am the light of the world. Once again He gathered the visible ceremony of the feast back onto Himself: first living water, now light. And the scene that had just taken place happens to be the footnote to this sentence. When the light came, the first thing that happened was not exposure and humiliation; it was that a person hiding in shame was helped to stand upright.

The root of the argument about freedom in the latter half lies still here. Those hearers said, We have never been slaves to anyone — said with full confidence, yet historically it does not stand: their ancestors had been slaves in Egypt, and later were taken captive to Babylon, and they themselves at this moment were living under Roman rule. But what Jesus spoke of was not political slavery; it was another kind of bondage: whoever commits sin is a slave of sin. A person clearly knows something is wrong, yet goes back and does it again and again; after doing it he hates himself, and after hating himself he goes again next time — this is not called freedom; this is called being unable to help oneself. The way out He gave carries the picture of a household: a slave has no permanent place in the house, but a son is in the house forever. Therefore if the Son makes you free, you shall be free indeed. That woman's experience is the living sample of this teaching: she was gripped by sin, used by others, sentenced to death by the law, and in the end what enabled her to walk out of the temple was not that she had struggled free herself, but that the Son personally spoke.

## III. Application

This chapter wants to speak to those who carry a shameful matter in their hearts.

Almost everyone has one. The only difference is how deeply it is hidden: a past one dares not let one's spouse know, a failed marriage, a sum of money that cannot bear the light, an addiction one cannot quit and cannot speak of. In the daytime one goes to work neatly dressed, talking and laughing with people, yet in one's heart there is always a place with its door shut. What one fears most is not being scolded, but being known. If it were known, one fears that in that person's eyes one would from then on look like a different person. So many people live very tired lives: deleting records, fabricating explanations, skirting certain topics, spending their strength guarding that door. That woman being dragged into the midst of the temple is the most extreme form of this fear: her secret was laid open before everyone, standing before the whole city, with nowhere even to hide.

Jesus' attitude toward her is the most important point of this chapter. He did not judge her in public, did not ask for details, did not make her guarantee anything first. He first cleared the scene, sent away the people holding stones, and only then spoke with her one to one. In this order is hidden His heart: He protects a person's dignity, even when that person has truly done wrong. Note also that He did not condemn her, yet He also did not say that the thing was right. People today often stir these two things together: either condemn, nailing a person to his most unbearable page; or say it is nothing, calling what is wrong right, so that everyone can feel comfortable. Jesus did neither. He admitted that it was sin, which is why there is go and sin no more; He also truly let her go, so that the woman walked out — she was not dragged out. With Him, honesty and mercy for the first time do not have to fight.

A word must also be said about stones. Most of us have no opportunity to stone anyone with actual stones, but stones have plenty of modern versions: a forwarded post, a crowd watching in a comment section, a vivid retelling at the dinner table. When someone else has a scandal, in the hearts of onlookers there is often a secret delight, because in those few minutes they themselves seem cleaner. That sentence of Jesus — He who is without sin — was spoken toward this delight. He did not deny that the woman was guilty; He merely reminded those lifting stones to turn back and look at their own account books. If a person has truly and seriously looked at his own account book, then looks again at another's fault, his tone will change. Not becoming unable to distinguish right from wrong, but becoming less quick to strike.

There is also that phrase, being unable to help oneself. The word freedom is used very cheaply today — to do whatever one wants is called freedom. But that woman had presumably once also done whatever she wanted, and the result was standing in the midst of the temple. True bondage often begins with one voluntary act: the first time, one walks in oneself; later the door closes behind, and when one wants to come out, one discovers it cannot be pushed open. Addiction is like this, an extramarital relationship is like this, lies rolling into lies are like this. Jesus did not stand at the door shouting for you to climb out yourself; what He said was that the Son makes you free. The son of the house opening his mouth and leading a person into the door — that is called coming home, not fleeing. The identity of the person led back is also changed: no longer the one standing in the midst awaiting sentencing, but a member of the household.

Finally, a word for those who to this day dare not draw near to faith. Perhaps you have always felt that before believing in Jesus you must first tidy yourself up, at least deal with the things behind that door, before you could have the face to enter. The order in this chapter is exactly reversed: that woman was dragged in covered in shame; she had no time to tidy anything, and the first thing Jesus said was, Neither do I condemn you. The cleaning up is a later matter; entering the door comes first. You do not have to first become a good person before you are qualified to come and see Him.

## IV. Response

Having read this far, you might pause and think about a few questions.

- Suppose that day you too were in the crowd, with a stone in your hand as well. Jesus said, He who is without sin, let him throw first — at what moment would your stone be put down?
- Behind that shut door in your heart, what is placed there? If there were One who already knows it all completely, yet still says, Neither do I condemn you, would you dare to open the door?
- Of Neither do I condemn you and Go and sin no more, which half is harder for you to accept? Is it hard to believe you can be let go, or hard to believe you truly can live a different way?
- When that woman in Scripture walked out of the temple, behind her was a whole courtyard of empty ground. What do you think the first thing in her heart would have been, on her way home?

If you are willing, you may follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, I have a past I dare not show others. I loathe it myself, yet cannot get out of it. You said to that woman, Neither do I condemn you; go and sin no more. Please treat me in the same way: do not nail me to that page; lead me home, to walk a new road. Amen."

There are also entrances for continuing onward. In the next chapter we will read John chapter 9, where a man born blind opened his eyes in the light. In this chapter, Jesus said He is the light of the world; in the next chapter, you will see what it looks like when this light falls upon a specific person. You may also find a trusted Christian friend and show him, about that matter you most fear being known, only an outline, and see his reaction. Many people's experience is that the mercy in the other person's eyes is far more than they themselves expected.

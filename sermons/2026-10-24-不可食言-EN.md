## Summary

Chapters 28 and 29 arrange the public sacrifices — when the day comes, the whole congregation offers as appointed. This chapter turns to the words that proceed out of an individual's mouth. Moses speaks to the heads of the tribes, conveying what the LORD has commanded (30:1). There is only one general rule, standing at the head of the whole chapter: "If a man vow a vow unto the LORD, or swear an oath to bind his soul with a bond; he shall not break his word, he shall do according to all that proceedeth out of his mouth." (30:2) A vow is voluntary — no one forces you to vow; but once the vow has left your mouth it is voluntary no longer: the word has bound the speaker himself. The fourteen verses that follow are all verdicts of this one rule in four situations. The vow of a woman still young and in her father's house: if her father hears it and holds his peace, the vow stands; if on the day he hears it he disallows it, the vow shall not stand, and the LORD shall forgive her (30:3-5). A woman who marries with a former vow upon her, or who has uttered a rash binding word: if her husband hears and is silent, the vow stands; if on the day he hears it he disallows it, the vow is counted void and she is forgiven (30:6-8). A widow, or a woman who is divorced, with neither father nor husband beside her to render a verdict: every vow of hers shall stand, and no one can void it for her (30:9). A woman who vows in her husband's house: if her husband is silent, the vow stands; if on the day he hears it he makes it utterly void, the vow shall not stand and she is forgiven (30:10-12). The last two provisions are the general rule and the time limit of the husband's authority: every vow and oath of the wife her husband may establish, or may make void (30:13); silence day after day is itself establishment (30:14); and if he makes it void only after the day he heard it, he shall bear her iniquity (30:15). Verse 16 closes the chapter: these are the statutes between a man and his wife, and between a father and his daughter. The skeleton of the whole chapter is therefore plain: once a word has left the mouth it has weight, and God Himself sets the verdict for it — who can make it stand, who can make it void, within what time a position must be declared, what silence counts as, and who bears the consequence when it is late — nailed down item by item. In the end, this chapter guards one word: faithfulness. A vow made to God is not an outlet for emotion; it is a covenant of credit.

## Main Text

### Part One: The General Rule — Once a Word Has Left the Mouth, It Binds the Speaker (Numbers 30:1-2) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) Verse 1, the scene of delivery of this chapter, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And Moses spake unto the heads of the tribes concerning the children of Israel, saying, This is the thing which the LORD hath commanded." (30:1) Registered item by item: first, the speaker is Moses and the addressees are "the heads of the tribes" — this chapter's provisions do not reach the individual directly but are given first to the heads, who are the nodes of verdict and execution in each tribe; second, this verse is numbered 30:2 in the Masoretic text (the Chinese-and-English 29:40 being numbered 30:1 there; see Endnote 1), the difference of numbering is registered as it stands, and the verse numbers throughout follow the Chinese and English numbering. Verse 2, the general rule, quoted verbatim in full: "If a man vow a vow unto the LORD, or swear an oath to bind his soul with a bond; he shall not break his word, he shall do according to all that proceedeth out of his mouth." (30:2) Registered item by item: first, the two kinds of utterance — "vow a vow" and "swear an oath": a vow is actively undertaking something before the LORD, an oath is binding oneself with a sworn bond, and the two often appear as a pair in the rest of this chapter (the vow and the bond wherewith she bound her soul); second, the object of the vow is expressly "unto the LORD" — a vow is not made for men's faces; it is made before God; third, the direction of the binding is "to bind his soul" — it does not bind another; the speaker puts the rope on himself; fourth, the prohibition is "he shall not break his word" — to break one's word is to eat back what one has said; fifth, the positive sentence is "he shall do according to all that proceedeth out of his mouth" — the standard is not to do it in the main, but "all that proceedeth": as much as has come out of the mouth, so much is to be done. This verse has no exception clause: there is no "if the difficulty is too great it need not count," and no window of hearing-and-verdict is left for the man — a man's vow has no father or husband to render a verdict for him; standing is the default, and the only outcome.

(Argument) Take first the voluntary and the involuntary in vowing. Scripture never commands a man to vow: a vow is a step a man voluntarily takes beyond his duty — a movement beyond duty, an undertaking beyond duty. God honours this step, even giving it a chapter of its own provisions; but God also says it plainly beforehand: before you vow, it is your freedom; after you vow, it is not. Once the word has left your mouth, it has left your feelings and become a debt you owe. This is exactly where we most often confuse things today: we treat vows as lyric — in the moment of being moved we dare to undertake anything, and when the moving has passed we act as if we had never said it; we treat promises as atmosphere — when the occasion arrives we speak more weightily than anyone, and when the occasion disperses we forget faster than anyone. The first provision of this chapter blocks this road dead: God's memory is better than ours; our casual words He registers sentence by sentence; the words we thought were scattered once spoken are, with Him, "he shall do according to all that proceedeth out of his mouth." So the lesson at the head of this chapter is not to teach men to vow more, but to teach them to vow carefully and to prize their pledged word — whether, afterwards, you still acknowledge the few sentences you undertook in a moment of being moved is the truest measure of a man's faithfulness to God.

(Argument) Take next the direction of the words "bind his soul." What we naturally want to bind is always others: bind the children to keep the rules, bind the spouse to change a fault, bind fellow workers to meet the schedule. In this chapter the rope goes first round one's own neck: the one who vows binds himself. If faith is used only to make demands of others, that is not faith; it is management technique. What God asks first is whether you yourself acknowledge the words that came out of your own mouth: you said you would rise early to pray, you said you would repay that money, you said you would break that habit, you said you would go and visit that person — some of these words no one else heard; only God heard them; and precisely because only God heard them, they most reveal the quality of your credit. Keeping faith with men, there is still face to hold it up; keeping faith with God, no one supervises — it rests entirely on whether you fear the One who heard. This chapter writes the verdicts out in such detail precisely to dismantle our last evasion: do not suppose that a word that went out lightly is lightly gone; every word spoken to God stands before Him, waiting for you to do it.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Vow" is neder (H5088); the "oath" of "swear an oath" is šebûʿâ (H7621); "bond" (the binding oath) is ʾissār (H632); and the "soul" that "himself" denotes is nepeš (H5315) — these four numbers have been checked and registered in this sermon; see Endnote 5. "Break his word" in the base text is a rendering; its original construction is "not make his own word void / profane"; this sermon registers only the base text's rendering and the general sense of the original, and makes no assertion of a number for it; see the pending items in Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Whom "a man" in verse 2 denotes is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings are more than one: one takes "man" as generic, laying down first a general rule for everyone, the fourteen verses that follow being the special verdicts for a woman's situations; another takes "man" here as standing in contrast with "woman" below, denoting in fact the adult male — a man's vow has no verdict window and stands directly, forming a contrast with the woman's cases, and the chapter's closing statement of scope (30:16) likewise lists only the two relations, a man and his wife and a father and his daughter, and does not return to a general rule for men. This sermon registers only the two readings and the contrast of wording within the chapter, and asserts nothing at the fact layer; the main text's argument uses only the five items this verse states of itself (vow, oath, bind his soul, not break his word, do according to all that proceedeth out of his mouth), and these five hold under both readings.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the verdict structure of the general rule: addressees — the heads of the tribes (30:1); one verse of general rule (30:2), containing two kinds of utterance (vow, oath), one object (the LORD), one direction (bind his soul), one prohibition (not break his word), one standard (do according to all that proceedeth out of his mouth); exception clauses — none. And the chapter's position is registered: this chapter comes from the "vows" of the closing verse of the previous chapter (29:39, preceding-text layer) — the sacrifices of the public feasts are to be offered apart from, and in addition to, what is vowed in freewill, while the individual's vow has a chapter and verdicts of its own; read together, the two chapters make both the public and the personal account clear.

### Part Two: Still in Her Father's House — the Father's Silence and His Disallowing (Numbers 30:3-5) (10:00-22:00)

(Fact) Verse 3, the situation of the first case, in the base text: "If a woman also vow a vow unto the LORD, and bind herself by a bond, being in her father's house in her youth;" (30:3) Registered item by item: first, the subject is "a woman," and doubly qualified — "in her youth" and "in her father's house": in both age and dwelling she is not yet independent; second, her act is in the same form as the man's in verse 2 — she vows a vow unto the LORD and binds herself; the vow itself is a real utterance before God, and is not treated as child's play because she is young. Verse 4, the verdict of silence, in the base text: "and her father hear her vow, and her bond wherewith she hath bound her soul, and her father shall hold his peace at her: then all her vows shall stand, and every bond wherewith she hath bound her soul shall stand." (30:4) Registered: the father's conditions are two — he "hear" and he "hold his peace": having heard, and not declaring a position, the verdict is established — her vows and every bond wherewith she hath bound her soul "shall stand," not one lacking. Verse 5, the verdict of disallowing, quoted verbatim in full: "But if her father disallow her in the day that he heareth; not any of her vows, or of her bonds wherewith she hath bound her soul, shall stand: and the LORD shall forgive her, because her father disallowed her." (30:5) Three items registered: first, the time limit of the refusal is "in the day that he heareth" — valid that day; the text in this case gives only that day (compare the husband's late voiding in 30:15; see Part Four); second, the effect of the refusal is that not any "shall stand" — vow and bonds, none of them is established; third, the consequence sentence is "and the LORD shall forgive her" — she does not bear the sin of breaking her word, and the reason is stated expressly: "because her father disallowed her": the vow was not repudiated by herself; it was lawfully voided under the authority of the house, and God Himself strikes this account through.

(Argument) The most piercing sentence in this case is in verse 4: "shall hold his peace" also counts. The father did not nod, did not sign, and may not even have taken it seriously — he simply heard it and said nothing. The provision says: this counts as settled. A father's silence, in God's account, is not blank; it is default assent. How many tragedies in families come out of just this "held his peace": the child is walking a road that is plainly not right; the father has heard it, has seen it, inwardly disapproves, yet is too lazy to open his mouth — afraid of conflict, afraid of trouble, afraid of seeming authoritarian; and when the thing has become a settled outcome, he says, "I never agreed to it." This chapter says: on the day you heard it and did not speak, the account counts as settled by you. Authority is something that is used even when it is not used — you hold the power of verdict in your hand and do not speak, and the force of speaking falls to silence. To those among us today who are fathers and leaders, this case speaks directly: declare your position when a position is due; disallow on the day when disallowing is due; what silence drags past is not peace — it is an account you will acknowledge later.

(Argument) A word of justice must also be spoken for the daughter of verse 5: to the woman whose vow her father voided, God gives not shame but forgiveness. Her heart in vowing was real; her will to bind herself was real; that the vow does not stand is not because her vow was frivolous, but because she is still under her father's authority. Within this provision lies God's consideration for those not yet independent: He does not require a person who does not yet have full autonomy to bear sin for a word that was lawfully stopped. This sentence, "and the LORD shall forgive her," occurs three times in the whole chapter (30:5, 8, 12) — at every voiding of a vow, God first takes the woman's share of sin clean off with His own hand.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The "stand" of "shall stand" is the verdict use of the verb family of qûm (H6965, to arise, to be established); "forgive" is sālaḥ (H5545) — in the Old Testament the subject of this word is invariably God, and its three occurrences in this chapter all read "and the LORD shall forgive her"; the original verb form behind "disallow" is marked pending verification in this sermon (see Endnote 5), and only the base text's rendering is recorded. In this case the father's two declarations — holding his peace and disallowing — form the basic pair of this chapter's verdict formulas: silence makes it stand; disallowing on the day makes it not stand; every later case adds to or subtracts from this pair. This sermon registers the counts of the chapter's verdict formulas in Endnote 4.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The nature of the father's power of verdict is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings are more than one: one looks to the economic and clan structure of the ancient household — the vows a young woman made often concerned the household's property and her own future (undertakings connected with dedication or marriage, for example), and the father, as head of the house, had to hold the power of verdict, lest the property and order of a house be carried along by a minor's undertaking; another looks to protection — the power of verdict is at the same time a power of protection: the young are easily moved, pressured, or urged by others into vows they cannot bear, and the father's disallowing on the day gave her an exit that bore no sin (the forgiveness sentence of 30:5 connects with this reading). Both roads have grounds within and beyond the text; this sermon registers them side by side only: what this chapter states of itself is only the conditions (young, in her father's house, hears, holds his peace or disallows) and the consequences (stands / does not stand / forgiven), and the purpose of the power of verdict is not among the things this chapter states expressly. The main text's argument uses only the conditions and consequences the chapter states of itself.

(New Testament Reading) When the Lord Jesus teaches on oaths, the edge of the teaching is aimed straight at the heart that vows casually: "But let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay: for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil." (Matt 5:37, cross-reference, reading layer; KJV wording of that place, taken directly — see the Production Record) One contrast is registered at the reading layer: this chapter does not abolish vowing; it fences vowing inside strict verdicts — an utterance has an account, voiding and establishing have rules, breaking one's word has sin; the New Testament teaching draws the centre one layer further inward: rather than relying on oaths to add weight to a word, let everyday words themselves be trustworthy, sentence by sentence. The reading layer registers only how the New Testament handles this topic, "the trustworthiness of words"; it does not read the teaching of Matthew 5 back into this chapter as its legislative fact. What this chapter establishes remains the verdict procedure for the vows of the congregation in the wilderness, and its conditions all rest on the original text.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the verdict table of the first case: subject — a woman young and in her father's house (30:3); the one who renders the verdict — her father; condition — he hears; window for declaring a position — the day he hears; result of silence — her vows and every bond wherewith she hath bound her soul shall stand (30:4); result of disallowing on the day — not any shall stand, and she is forgiven by the LORD, the reason being that her father disallowed her (30:5). And the case's position in the whole chapter is registered: this is the only one of the four cases in which the one who renders the verdict is the father; in the other three cases (30:6-12) it is the husband in each. In this chapter the power of verdict over a woman's vows throughout her life is handed over according to her dwelling and status — in her father's house it belongs to her father, on marriage to her husband, and with neither father nor husband no one can void it (30:9); the track of that handing over is the chapter's very structure.

### Part Three: Marrying with a Vow upon Her, and the One No One Can Void a Vow For (Numbers 30:6-9) (22:00-36:00)

(Fact) Verse 6, the situation of the second case, in the base text: "And if she had at all an husband, when she vowed, or uttered ought out of her lips, wherewith she bound her soul;" (30:6) Registered item by item: first, the woman of this case enters marriage carrying a former vow — she had a husband "when she vowed": the vow was made first, the marriage completed after; in the Chinese base text this state is worded as the vow being upon her person; second, the other situation is that she "uttered ought out of her lips, wherewith she bound her soul" — the Chinese base text's wording for this, a rash word, occurs only here in the whole chapter and denotes a binding word uttered unthinkingly, in a rush out of the mouth; the text lists this situation separately, does not confuse it with a proper vow, and yet brings it equally under the verdict. Verse 7, the verdict of the husband's silence, in the base text: "and her husband heard it, and held his peace at her in the day that he heard it: then her vows shall stand, and her bonds wherewith she bound her soul shall stand." (30:7) Registered: the conditions are of the same construction as in the father's-house case — the day he heard it, held his peace, shall stand; the one who renders the verdict has changed to the husband. Verse 8, the verdict of the husband's refusal, quoted verbatim in full: "But if her husband disallowed her on the day that he heard it; then he shall make her vow which she vowed, and that which she uttered with her lips, wherewith she bound her soul, of none effect: and the LORD shall forgive her." (30:8) Registered: first, the time limit is still "on the day that he heard it"; second, the verb of this case is "disallowed her," and its effect is that he makes the vow and the uttered word "of none effect" — a slight difference of wording from the father's-house case's "shall not stand," and each is recorded by its own base text; third, the forgiveness sentence occurs a second time — she does not bear sin because her vow was voided by her husband. Verse 9, the third case, quoted verbatim in full: "But every vow of a widow, and of her that is divorced, wherewith they have bound their souls, shall stand against her." (30:9) Three items registered: first, the subjects are the widow and the woman who is divorced — both verdict-givers, father and husband, are absent; second, this verse has no verdict procedure, no forgiveness sentence, and no exception — only the one sentence, shall stand; third, the "shall stand" of this verse is of a different nature from the standing under silence: that one stands having passed a verdict; this one stands because no one can void it — her vow returns directly under the general rule of verse 2, on the same track as the general case of the man.

(Argument) Take first the thoughtfulness of this case of marrying with a vow upon her. A person who walks into marriage carrying an undertaking to God — that undertaking is not automatically voided by the wedding, nor does it change masters because the roof has changed: the vow was made unto the LORD, it precedes the marriage, it continues in force within the marriage, unless the husband on the day he hears it expressly disallows it. This puts a cross through every thought of "once married, all former undertakings to God are struck through at a stroke": a vow of service, a vow of offering, a vow to keep some discipline, does not clear itself to zero at a change of status. Every time our life passes a gate — graduation, marriage, a change of work, a move to another city — we easily issue ourselves an amnesty that "former words no longer count." This chapter says: there is only one situation in which it does not count — where the one with the power of verdict has expressly disallowed it on the day he heard it; apart from that, a vow upon the person binds for as long as it is upon the person. Conversely, this case also reminds the one who is a husband: the undertakings to God that your wife has brought through the door with her — once you have heard them, you must declare a position: approve, and establish them by your silence, and keep them together with her thereafter; disapprove, and say so expressly that day — do not wait until she can no longer keep them and then say, "I never thought much of it." Declaring a position on the day is the line of honesty this chapter sets for the one who renders the verdict.

(Argument) Take next that "rash word" (so the Chinese base text; in the KJV, what she "uttered... out of her lips"). Scripture is endearingly honest: it knows that a person will blurt things out — on an occasion of being moved, in a fit of temper, amid the urging of a crowd, a word binding oneself slips out. The provision does not say "a rash word does not count," nor does it say "a rash word is the same sin as a proper vow"; it brings the rash word under the verdict: on the day he hears it the husband may disallow it and make it void; silence makes it stand. That is, God has left impulse a formal channel of regret, but this channel has a door, a time limit, and one who renders the verdict; it is not a door one can open oneself with one sentence of "I was only joking at the time."

(Argument) Verse 9 needs a pause of its own. The widow and the divorced woman were, in ancient society, the two persons with the least to rely on — one has lost her husband, the other has been put away by her husband. For the vows they make, there is no father to hear and no husband to judge; there is only the one sentence, that they shall stand. This is not God's severity toward them; it is precisely God's lifting up of their standing: in this provision they stand on the same line as the adult man — they speak for themselves, answer for themselves, and render their account to God themselves, and no one can step over them and void it for them. The presence or absence of a power of verdict is, in this chapter, always two-sided: to have someone render a verdict is protection, and also restraint; to have no one render a verdict is a burden, and also dignity. God has not written women without support as persons outside the law, nor has He written them as perpetual children; their words count in full before God. In the church, too, our treatment of those whose situation is lonely should learn from this verse: do not, in the name of "pitying her," remit all her undertakings for her — that is to slight her; take seriously the words she has spoken to God, and walk with her as she carries out, one by one, the vows that stand — that is what it is to treat her as a whole person.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The original word-form and number behind the Chinese base text's "rash word" (30:6) are marked pending verification in this sermon (see Endnote 5); only the Chinese base text's rendering and this chapter's use of it (a binding word rushed out of the mouth) are recorded; on the KJV letter the place reads "uttered ought out of her lips, wherewith she bound her soul," and "uttered" occurs twice in the chapter (30:6, 8). "Shall stand" is still of the qûm family (H6965), and "forgive" is still sālaḥ (H5545), in the register already made. The verdict formulas of these three verses are recounted: the shall-stand verdict occurs at 30:7 and 30:9 (with 30:4 and 30:11, four verses in the whole chapter; on the KJV letter the phrase "shall stand" itself occurs eight times, the verses 30:4, 7, and 11 each containing it twice, 30:9 containing it once, and 30:5 once in the negated construction "not any of her vows ... shall stand" — for the details see Endnote 4); "and the LORD shall forgive her" occurs at 30:8 (with 30:5 and 30:12, three times in the whole chapter); the day-of-hearing formula occurs at 30:7 and 30:8 (with 30:5, 30:12, and 30:14, five times in the whole chapter; on the KJV letter its wording varies with the verse — see Endnote 4).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) To whom the power of verdict belongs in the case of marrying with a vow is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings are more than one: one holds that, the vow having been made in her father's house, the verdict originally belonged to the father, and whether the father's power of verdict ends when the woman marries — this case's making the husband, without more, the one who hears the vow — is further evidence of the verdict being handed over with the dwelling; another holds that the vows of this case need not all have been made in her father's house — the verse says only that the vow already existed when she married (in the Chinese base text's wording, the vow was upon her), and the time and place of the vowing this verse does not state expressly, and it need not be settled as an old vow of the father's house. This sermon registers only this verse's wording (married, a vow already upon her, the husband hears) and the divergence of the two readings, and does not supply the time and place of the vowing at the fact layer. The main text's argument uses only the track of handing over that this verse states of itself: the one who renders the verdict is fixed by her dwelling — on this point both roads agree.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the verdict table of the second and third cases: the second case — subject, a woman who marries with a vow upon her or who has uttered a rash word (30:6); the one who renders the verdict, her husband; if he is silent, it stands (30:7); if on the day he hears it he disallows it, the vow is counted void and she is forgiven (30:8). The third case — subject, a widow or a woman who is divorced (30:9); the one who renders the verdict, none; the result, it stands in every case, with no forgiveness sentence and no exception. And one contrast for the whole chapter is registered: the forgiveness sentence appears only where there is one who renders a verdict and voids the vow (30:5, 8, 12) — where no one voids the vow (30:2, 9) there is no forgiveness sentence, because there is no sin to forgive: the word stands, and it is simply to be carried out.

### Part Four: Vowing in Her Husband's House — Establish, Make Void, and the Bearing That Comes Too Late (Numbers 30:10-15) (36:00-48:00)

(Fact) Verse 10, the situation of the fourth case, in the base text: "And if she vowed in her husband's house, or bound her soul by a bond with an oath;" (30:10) Registered: the division of labour between this case and the second lies in the place and sequence of the vowing — the vow of the second case precedes the marriage (30:6); the vow of this case is made within the marriage, in her husband's house, and "vowed... or bound her soul by a bond with an oath" sets the two forms side by side, answering to the two kinds of utterance in the general rule of verse 2. Verse 11, the verdict of silence, in the base text: "and her husband heard it, and held his peace at her, and disallowed her not: then all her vows shall stand, and every bond wherewith she bound her soul shall stand." (30:11) Registered: this verse writes silence out most fully — "held his peace at her" and "disallowed her not" are set side by side as a double sentence, which is to say that the principle "silence is establishment" is stated once in advance, laying the ground for the general rule of verse 14. Verse 12, the verdict of utter voiding, quoted verbatim in full: "But if her husband hath utterly made them void on the day he heard them; then whatsoever proceeded out of her lips concerning her vows, or concerning the bond of her soul, shall not stand: her husband hath made them void; and the LORD shall forgive her." (30:12) Registered: first, the verb of voiding in this case is "hath utterly made them void" — different in wording from the "disallowed her" of 30:8, and each is recorded by its own base text; second, "them" denotes the two items, her vows and the bond of her soul, and only when both are utterly made void do both fail to stand; third, the forgiveness sentence occurs a third time, its reason still being that the one who renders the verdict voided the vow, not that she broke her word. Verse 13, the general rule of the husband's authority, quoted verbatim in full: "Every vow, and every binding oath to afflict the soul, her husband may establish it, or her husband may make it void." (30:13) Registered: first, "every" gathers the scope in full — not limited to this case; every vow of the wife and every oath to afflict the soul is within it; second, the pair "establish / make void" here formally appears as a pair: the husband's verdict has only two roads, and no third road (such as delay); third, this verse singles out in the oath the "afflict the soul" — its wording connects with the "afflict your souls" of the Day of Atonement (29:7, preceding-text layer), and only its existence is registered. Verse 14, the general rule of silence, quoted verbatim in full: "But if her husband altogether hold his peace at her from day to day; then he establisheth all her vows, or all her bonds, which are upon her: he confirmeth them, because he held his peace at her in the day that he heard them." (30:14) Registered: first, "from day to day" extends the one day's silence of verse 11 to continuing silence — the longer the days go on, the firmer the establishment; in the Chinese base text this phrase reads as "day by day," and its original construction is "from day to day"; second, the reason sentence points back to "the day that he heard them" — the force of the establishment originates in the silence of that first day, and every later day's silence is a restatement of it. Verse 15, the consequence of a late voiding, quoted verbatim in full: "But if he shall any ways make them void after that he hath heard them; then he shall bear her iniquity." (30:15) Registered: first, this verse is the chapter's only penalty clause — the husband who voids late must "bear her iniquity": the vow having already stood because of his silence, to void it now is to make her break her word, and its sin is transferred to the one who voided late; second, this verse has no forgiveness sentence — the one who bears is the husband, and the sin cannot be forgiven away; it can only be borne.

(Argument) The centre of gravity of this section has already moved from the one who vows to the one who renders the verdict. Verse 13 states the husband's authority crisply: he may establish it, or he may make it void — either is permitted; the one thing that is not permitted is the third thing: to make no declaration either way. God gives the one who renders the verdict great freedom; to the delay of the one who renders the verdict He leaves no ground at all: not to speak on the day is establishment; to void it only after the days have passed, the sin is reckoned yours. This set of rules holds for everyone who leads people, manages people, and makes decisions. The fault we leaders most easily commit is not making decisions wildly; it is not making decisions — a subordinate's proposal is submitted, and we say neither that it is good nor that it is not, and shelve it; a household matter is brought up for discussion, and we neither nod nor shake our heads, and drag it out. The one who drags it out thinks himself clever: he makes no mistakes and bears no responsibility. This chapter says: the silence of the one who renders the verdict is never zero; silence is a declaration, and the most expensive kind of declaration — it makes the matter stand, and makes others proceed in the direction of its standing; if afterwards you change your mind, the bearing is yours. You who hold authority: use it on the day. To say no on the day is stronger than to bear it on a later day.

(Argument) The "bear" of verse 15 must be spoken of one layer deeper. What the husband who voids late bears is "her iniquity" — not his own sin, but hers. Why does her sin fall on his head? Because the breaking of the vow was caused by him: she was already proceeding according to the vow that stood, and it was he who, after the days had passed, voided the vow, so that what she was doing suddenly became a breaking of her word. It turns out that God keeps the account of breaking one's word very finely: whoever makes a person break their word, the account is entered under that person's name. This entry gives us an extremely weighty reminder: do not lightly shake another's undertaking to God. Someone is earnestly keeping a vow — keeping rest, keeping an offering, keeping some discipline — and a person beside them says one sentence: "Why be so earnest about it? Let it go." The speaker is light and airy; if the keeper truly listens, God may well not reckon the account of the broken word to the hearer alone. Before we advise people to be flexible, let us first think of this verse: do you have the authority to void this vow? Can you bear this "bearing"? If you cannot bear it, take that sentence of "let it go" back. To honour the words another has spoken to God is to honour God Himself.

(Argument) It must also be seen that the authority of verse 13 comes with the responsibility of verses 14 and 15 nailed to it; authority and responsibility are nailed together. The husband may make his wife's vow void — that is authority; but if he voids it one day late, the sin is transferred to his own name — that is the time limit of responsibility. The pattern of authority in Scripture is always like this: the greater the authority, the tighter the time limit for declaring a position, and the heavier the weight of the bearing. What many people today envy is only the first half of authority: to carry the day with a word, to void with a word; but the second half in Scripture says: if this word of yours comes out late, the weight of it presses on your own shoulders. The leading place in church and household, if it wants only to enjoy the satisfaction of establishing and making void and will not bear the pressure of declaring a position on the day and the consequence of delay, is not leadership; it is occupying a seat. Let this pair, "establish / make void," and this one clause, "bear," be the motto of everyone who is a husband, a father, or a leader, pasted on the heart.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Establish" is the causative use of qûm (H6965, to cause to stand), and "make void" is of the verb family of pārar (H6565, to break, to make void); both numbers have been checked and registered in this sermon; see Endnote 5. "Iniquity" is ʿāwōn (H5771). "Them" (in the Chinese base text's wording, "these two") occurs four times in this part (twice at 30:12, and once each at 30:14 and 30:15) and denotes her vows and the bond of her soul, of the same construction as the double item set side by side in verses 4 and 5. On the KJV letter the establishment of verse 14 is carried by two verbs in sequence, "establisheth" and "confirmeth," where the Chinese base text uses its "establish" verb twice; each text is registered by its own letter (see Endnote 4).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The point in time of the late voiding in verse 15, and the nature of the "bearing," are set out side by side without adjudication. As to the point in time: in the father's-house case (30:5) and the case of the vow carried into marriage (30:8) this chapter says only that the refusal is "in the day that he heareth" / "on the day that he heard it," and does not state expressly the consequence of a late voiding by the father or by the husband of the carried-in case; the general rule of 30:14-15 stands after the husband's-house case, and whether its range also covers the earlier cases is read in more than one way — some hold that the general rule covers all who render verdicts in the whole chapter (silence establishes, late voiding bears, all alike), others that the general rule gathers only the husband's-house case, the earlier cases being governed only by the one sentence of "the day that he heard." This sermon registers only the position of the wording (the general rule stands after the fourth case and before the closing sentence) and the two readings, and does not expand or contract its range at the fact layer. As to the nature of the "bearing": some read it as a transfer of guilt (the late voiding lands the wife in breaking her word, and the blame returns to the husband); others as a bearing of consequences (all the responsibility arising from the late voiding is the husband's to bear); this chapter does not state expressly the manner or rite of the bearing, and this sermon registers only the base text's own sentence, without extrapolation. The main text's argument uses only the one point on which both roads agree: a late voiding has a consequence, and the consequence rests on the one who renders the verdict.

(New Testament Reading) When James writes on the tongue and on oaths, he says: "But above all things, my brethren, swear not, neither by heaven, neither by the earth, neither by any other oath: but let your yea be yea; and your nay, nay; lest ye fall into condemnation." (Jas 5:12, cross-reference, reading layer; KJV wording of that place, taken directly — see the Production Record) And on the tongue he writes, in key points, that it is a fire that defileth the whole body, and that "But the tongue can no man tame; it is an unruly evil, full of deadly poison." (Jas 3:6, 8, cross-reference, reading layer; the quoted clause is the KJV wording of 3:8, taken directly; the clause on the fire is given in key points and is not a quotation). One contrast is registered at the reading layer: this chapter's management of words that have left the mouth is institutional — verdict, time limit, bearing, laid down item by item; James's management is of the heart — the tongue's problem is in the end the heart's problem; rules can govern voiding and establishing, but they cannot govern the source. The two layers read together do not abolish each other: with a system guarding the word and a heart governing the tongue, the word truly stands. The reading layer registers only how the New Testament takes up this topic; it does not read James's teaching back into this chapter as its provisions. This chapter's provisions still rest on the original text.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the verdict table of the fourth case and the general rule: the fourth case — subject, a wife who vows in her husband's house (30:10); if he is silent (held his peace, and disallowed her not), it stands (30:11); if on the day he hears he makes them utterly void, it does not stand and she is forgiven (30:12). The general rule — over every vow of the wife and every oath to afflict the soul the husband may establish, or may make void (30:13); silence from day to day is establishment, originating in the silence of the day he heard (30:14); if after he has heard he makes them void late, the husband bears her iniquity (30:15). And the chapter's account of penalties is registered: in the whole chapter of sixteen verses there is only this one penalty clause, and it falls only on the one who renders the verdict — the one who vows is in no verse of this chapter condemned: either the vow stands and is carried out, or it is voided and she is forgiven; the only one recorded as "bearing iniquity" is the husband who voided late. This distribution is registered as it stands, without extrapolation.

### Part Five: The Scope of the Statutes and the Close of the Whole Chapter — How a Word Stands (Numbers 30:16 and the Whole Chapter) (48:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 16, the close of the whole chapter, quoted verbatim in full: "These are the statutes, which the LORD commanded Moses, between a man and his wife, between the father and his daughter, being yet in her youth in her father's house." (30:16) Registered item by item: first, this verse is a sentence of scope — it states of itself the two relations governed by this chapter's statutes: between a man and his wife, and between the father and his daughter (young, and still in her father's house); the general rule for the man (30:2) is not separately named in this sentence, because a man's vow has no verdict procedure and no statutes to state — there is only the one sentence of the general rule — and this is registered as it stands; second, this verse is a sentence of source — "the statutes, which the LORD commanded Moses": the power of verdict is not taken by fathers and husbands themselves; it is commanded down by the LORD, and it has a source, and with it the time limit and the bearing registered above; third, this verse is numbered 30:17 in the Masoretic text, and with it the chapter is complete at 16 verses in the Chinese and English numbering (30:2-17 in the Masoretic). The chapter's structure is recounted: one verse of delivery (30:1), one verse of general rule (30:2), three verses for the father's-house case (30:3-5), three verses for the case of marrying with a vow (30:6-8), one verse for the widow and the divorced woman (30:9), three verses for the husband's-house case (30:10-12), three verses for the general rule of the husband's authority (30:13-15), one closing verse (30:16) — sixteen verses in all.

(Argument) Having read the whole chapter, its skeleton must be set up for the hearers: the life and death of one sentence must pass four gates. The first is the gate of utterance — a vow is made unto the LORD; once spoken it binds oneself; one's word must not be broken (30:2). The second is the gate of verdict — if the one who vows is still under a father's or a husband's authority, her word must pass the gate of the one who renders the verdict: disallowed on the day, it is void; silence, it stands (30:4-5, 7-8, 11-12). The third is the gate of the time limit — the verdict is available only on the day of hearing; once silence passes the day it hardens into establishment, and a late voiding must bear (30:14-15). The fourth is the gate of forgiveness — every vow lawfully voided leaves the person bearing no sin: the LORD shall forgive her (30:5, 8, 12). When the four gates have been passed, the fate of a sentence is settled: either it is carried out, or it is forgiven away; the one thing there is not is a sentence left rotting halfway — said and not counted, yet never formally settled, mildewing in the conscience year after year. Those of us who have believed in the Lord for many years — who does not have a few such half-rotting sentences in his heart? The good news of this chapter is: God has prepared for every sentence a proper way of settling it — what can be carried out, carry out; what was vowed rashly at the time and what now, for proper reasons, truly cannot be borne, come frankly before God and ask Him to settle it by the road of forgiveness, and do not leave it rotting. Only a settled word does not block the road; the words that block the road are mostly those we pretend to have forgotten.

(Argument) The whole chapter leaves one sentence for each of three kinds of people. First, for those who undertake things casually: your mouth is a mouth that makes covenants, not a mouth for lyric — in the moment of being moved, to say one sentence less is stronger than repudiating ten afterwards; before you vow, ponder it three times: this word is spoken unto the LORD — can I bear it? If you cannot bear it, rather do not vow; God never blames you for not vowing an extra vow; He asks only whether what you vowed you do. Second, for those who hold the power of verdict — fathers, husbands, leaders: declaring a position on the day is your duty; silence is itself a declaration; delay is itself bearing; the words your people have spoken to God are waiting for your attitude on the day you hear them: establish what should be established, void what should be voided, and do not leave anyone in suspense within your silence either way. Third, for those whose situation is lonely, whose words no one judges: your words God counts in full — to have no one to check them for you is a burden, and also His perfecting of you: sentence by sentence they stand, sentence by sentence carry them out, and let your credit grow, in the place where no one supervises, into the hardest kind of credit in the whole chapter.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Statutes" is ḥuqqîm (the plural form of ḥōq, H2706). This chapter has not one place-name; of personal names there is only Moses (30:1, 16); the structure of the speaking is: the LORD commands, Moses speaks to the heads (30:1), and the whole chapter has no speech by the people and no narrative of a case — it is a chapter of pure verdict procedure, recording not one instance of a vow; this is registered as it stands: instances of the Hannah's-vow and Jephthah's-vow kind are all in other books (cross-reference layer), not in this chapter, and this sermon does not read instances back into this chapter.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The historical readings of this chapter's power of verdict are set out side by side without adjudication. This chapter places the adult woman's vowing under the verdict of a father's and a husband's authority, and the historical readings of this arrangement have always been more than one: one holds that it reflects the reality of the patriarchal household of ancient Israel — woman was not independent economically or legally, and an undertaking that bound the household's property and her own person had to pass the verdict of the head of the house; the provision writes reality into procedure, and with the forgiveness sentence and the time limit sets a protection for woman above that reality (a voided vow bears no sin, the verdict is limited to the day, and the blame for a late voiding rests on the man); another holds that the provision's centre of gravity lies not in suppression but in perfecting — it presupposes that a woman's vow is truly valid before God (it is not voided except by a verdict; if the one who renders the verdict is silent it stands; if no one judges it, it counts in full), and the verdict procedure is a railing added to "validity," not a certificate issued to "invalidity." The two readings each stress different elements of the same set of provisions (the shall-stand verdict in four verses, forgiveness three times, time limit and bearing once each); this sermon registers only the provisions themselves and the coexistence of the two roads, and takes no side at the fact layer; the main text's argument uses only the conditions and consequences the provisions state of themselves. As for the relation of this chapter to the New Testament household teaching (Ephesians 5, for example, cross-reference, reading layer), that belongs to the topics of the reading layer and is not adjudicated in this section.

(New Testament Reading) When Paul writes on authority within marriage, his words have points of comparison with this chapter: "The wife hath not power of her own body, but the husband: and likewise also the husband hath not power of his own body, but the wife." (1 Cor 7:4, cross-reference, reading layer; KJV wording of that place, taken directly — see the Production Record) One contrast is registered at the reading layer: this chapter's power of verdict is registered one-way — the father judges the daughter, the husband judges the wife; the provisions are so, and are recorded without evasion; the sentence on power in 1 Corinthians 7 is, by contrast, set out in both directions — the husband's body is also the wife's. The reading layer registers only the two texts' wording and direction; it does not harmonize them and does not read one back into the other: how this chapter's procedure is to be applied today is a matter for the pulpit and the fellowship discussion layer (this sermon leaves the question in its discussion prompt and draws no conclusion here); the verdict conditions and time limits this chapter establishes still rest on the original text.

(Fact) The chapter's data are gathered in closing, every item already counted (for the details see Endnote 4): the whole chapter has 16 verses (Chinese and English numbering; in the Masoretic text numbered 30:2-17, continuing from 29:40, which is 30:1 there); the five parts have, in order, 2 verses (30:1-2), 3 (30:3-5), 4 (30:6-9), 6 (30:10-15), and 1 (30:16) — 16 in all. The four cases: the daughter in her father's house; marrying with a vow; the widow and the divorced woman; vowing in her husband's house. The shall-stand verdict stands in four verses (30:4, 7, 9, 11; on the KJV letter the phrase itself occurs eight times); the shall-not-stand verdict in two verses (30:5, 12); "and the LORD shall forgive her" three times (30:5, 8, 12); the holding-of-peace formula five times (once each at 30:4, 7, 11, and twice at 30:14); the day-of-hearing formula five times (30:5, 7, 8, 12, 14); two pairs of verdict verbs — disallow (four occurrences: two at 30:5, and one each at 30:8 and 30:11) and utter voiding (30:12, 15; with "make it void" at 30:13 besides); one pair of general-rule verbs — establish, make void (30:13; establish-family verbs further at 30:14, twice, the second under the form "confirmeth" on the KJV letter); one penalty clause (30:15, bearing iniquity); one sentence of scope (30:16). The chapter's connections with the chapters before and after: it continues from the "vows" of 29:39 (preceding-text layer) — the sacrifices of the public feasts stand apart from the individual's vow, and this chapter governs precisely that "apart from" individual vow; it opens toward the war against Midian in chapter 31 (following-text layer) — only its existence is registered: the chapter on vows is set between the register of feasts and the war — the word is first settled, and then the people set out — and it is not read back into this chapter.

## Devotional Excerpt

The most moving thing about a vow is that it is entirely voluntary; the most solemn thing about a vow is that, once spoken, it is voluntary no longer. "If a man vow a vow unto the LORD, or swear an oath to bind his soul with a bond; he shall not break his word, he shall do according to all that proceedeth out of his mouth." (30:2) No one forces us to say anything extra to God, yet every sentence we have said, He takes seriously. Today, bring out those half-rotting words in the heart: one was undertaken in a moment of being moved and never mentioned again afterwards; one was promised to someone and has been dragging; and one more, which we know should be settled, and we pretend to have forgotten. God has left every word a proper ending — what can be carried out, take the first step today; what was truly vowed too rashly at the time and cannot be borne, come frankly before Him and ask Him to forgive it and settle it, and do not let it go on blocking the road. And if you are one who holds the power of verdict in the home or in the church, please remember: on the day you hear, silence is establishment, and delay is bearing; to say on the day the words that should be said is your gentlest responsibility toward the whole household. May the Lord also remind us: vows are not prized for being many, but for being pondered before they are spoken — this word is spoken unto the LORD; can I bear it? What cannot be borne, rather do not vow; what can be borne, once spoken, make no more excuses. May our words, before God, stand sentence by sentence.

English (KJV-based):

"If a man vow a vow unto the LORD, or swear an oath to bind his soul with a bond; he shall not break his word, he shall do according to all that proceedeth out of his mouth." (Num 30:2, KJV) No one forces us to vow; that is what makes a vow beautiful. But once the words leave our mouth, they bind us — that is what makes a vow serious. Bring out today the promises left half-rotting in your heart: keep the ones you can keep, beginning with one step today; and for the ones rashly made and truly beyond you, come honestly before God and let Him forgive and settle them. A word settled before God no longer blocks the road.

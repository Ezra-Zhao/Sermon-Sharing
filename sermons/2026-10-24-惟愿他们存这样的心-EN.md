## Summary

Deuteronomy 5 opens Moses' second address (5:1–26:19). The first address (1:1–4:43) looked back over history and set down the confession of the one God; this chapter turns to the restatement of the body of the law: Moses calls all Israel together, first reaffirms the covenant at Horeb, then recites the Ten Words item by item, then records the people's request for a mediator and God's answer, and finally closes with an exhortation. The chapter's road runs from the ear into the heart: God says again what He said forty years before, makes the covenant again with the people of today, finishes the ten commandments — and then says aloud the wish that gives this sermon its title: "O that there were such an heart in them."

## Main Text

Introduction (00:00–05:00). (Fact) Deuteronomy 5 is the opening of Moses' second address (5:1–26:19). The first address (1:1–4:43) reviewed history and set down the assertion of the one God; this chapter turns to the restatement of the body of the law: Moses calls all Israel together, first reaffirms the covenant at Horeb, then recites the Ten Words again item by item, then records the people's request for a mediator and God's response, and finally closes with an exhortation. The chapter has thirty-three verses, and its structure is registered first: introduction, verses 1–5 (5 verses); the Ten Words, verses 6–21 (16 verses); the record of the tables of stone, verse 22 (1 verse); the request for a mediator and God's response, verses 23–31 (9 verses); the closing exhortation, verses 32–33 (2 verses) — thirty-three verses in all (Endnote 1). This chapter and Exodus 20 are two records of the same set of Ten Words: there, the record of the promulgation on the spot; here, the restatement forty years later, east of the Jordan, to the second generation — the same Ten Words, yet with a number of visible textual differences, registered item by item in this chapter (Endnote 12). (Argument) A restatement is not a mere copying over. Most of Moses' first-generation hearers had already fallen in the wilderness; the generation standing on the plains of Moab had mostly been born in the wilderness, and for them Horeb was not a memory but their parents' telling. Before restating, Moses makes this plain: this covenant was made with "us, who are all of us here alive this day" (v. 3) — the hearers of the law are not readers of an archive but parties to the covenant. In Deuteronomy the Ten Words are not quoted; they are promulgated again. (Summary) The governing sentence of this chapter can be put like this: God says again what He said forty years before; He makes the covenant again with the people of today; the ten commandments are finished — and then He Himself says aloud that wish: O that there were such an heart in them. The chapter's road runs from the ear into the heart.

### Part One: This Covenant Was Made with Us (5:1–5) (05:00–14:00)

(Fact) Verse 1 is the opening of the second address, quoted in full from the base text: "And Moses called all Israel, and said unto them, Hear, O Israel, the statutes and judgments which I speak in your ears this day, that ye may learn them, and keep, and do them." (5:1) Registered item by item: first, four verbs form a chain — hear (šāmaʿ, H8085), learn (lāmad, H3925), keep (šāmar, H8104), do (ʿāśâ, H6213; the KJV closes the chain with "keep, and do them"): hearing is not the end; doing is. Second, "the statutes and judgments" (ḥuqqîm ûmišpāṭîm, H2706, H4941) is the general name for the body of the law in the rest of the book; the same pair returns at verse 31 (there with "commandments" added; Endnote 5). Third, "this day" (hayyôm) in this verse is the first of its three occurrences in the chapter (Endnote 7). (Fact) Verse 2 fixes the place and the manner of the covenant, quoted in full: "The LORD our God made a covenant with us in Horeb." (5:2) — "made a covenant" is kārat bərît (H3772 + H1285, literally "cut a covenant"), occurring once each in verses 2 and 3; "Horeb" (ḥōrēḇ, H2722) occurs only here in this chapter, and is to be read together with its two occurrences in Deuteronomy 4 (registered in that manuscript, Endnote 8 there): Deuteronomy regularly calls the place of the covenant Horeb. Verse 3 is the chapter's first weighty sentence, quoted in full: "The LORD made not this covenant with our fathers, but with us, even us, who are all of us here alive this day." (5:3) Registered item by item: first, "made not … but …" is a construction of negation plus replacement: what is set in contrast is "our fathers," what replaces it is "us" — and the stress falls again on "here alive this day": the covenant's tense is the present, and its place is the scene of the address itself. Second, "alive" (ḥayyîm, H2416) answers at a distance to the "liveth" of verses 24 and 26 (Endnote 7): they heard God speak then and still lived, and those standing here today are likewise people who are alive. Third, what "our fathers" denotes is set side by side in the discussion layer below. (Fact) Verse 4 records the manner of the covenant-making, quoted in full: "The LORD talked with you face to face in the mount out of the midst of the fire," (5:4) — in the KJV, as in the Chinese base text, the parenthesis opened at the end of this verse closes at the end of verse 5; it is the chapter's only parenthetical structure, registered as it stands. "Face to face" is pānîm bəpānîm (H6440); "talked" is the piel of dāḇar (H1696), which occurs five times in this chapter (vv. 4, 22, 24, 26, 31) — God's characteristic action in this chapter is not appearing in a form but speaking, in intertextual relation with Deuteronomy 4:12, where only a voice was heard (registered as a reported summary). Verse 5 is Moses' placing of himself, quoted in full: "(I stood between the LORD and you at that time, to shew you the word of the LORD: for ye were afraid by reason of the fire, and went not up into the mount;) saying," (5:5) Registered item by item: first, "stood between" (ʿāmad, H5975) — the mediator's place is written down already in the first part; it was not set up only after the people asked (the request for a mediator is recorded at verse 27; there the people put this place into words). Second, the people's one reason for not going up the mount is that they "were afraid" (yārēʾ, H3372) — in this part fear is a registered fact, not yet an accusation. Third, the closing word "saying" introduces the text of the commandments in the next part: from verse 6 onward, the words are, within the narrative frame, God's words as cited by Moses in his restatement. (Argument) The theological centre of this part is the contemporaneity of the covenant. Moses does not allow the second generation to treat the Ten Words as the previous generation's inheritance: an inheritance can be passed down, but a covenant must be stood in personally. The one sentence "made not … with our fathers, but with us" flattens forty years of distance — before the covenant there are no spectators and no latecomers, only people alive this day. There is a further layer: verse 4 says God talked "face to face," and verse 5 says the people "were afraid by reason of the fire, and went not up into the mount": intimacy and fear stand together in one part. The Horeb experience was never a single colour — He was near enough to speak face to face, and fierce enough that no one dared go up the mount; the mediator stands between those two poles. (New Testament Reading) This part's motifs as echoed in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: Hebrews 8, citing Jeremiah, establishes the new covenant and states plainly that it is "not according to" the covenant made when God took the fathers by the hand to lead them out of Egypt (registered as a reported summary) — the wording "the covenant with the fathers" can be read against verse 3 of this chapter; Galatians 3 says the law was "ordained by angels in the hand of a mediator" (registered as a reported summary), set against Moses "between" in verse 5 of this chapter. Neither is read back as this chapter's original meaning. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters set side by side. First, what "our fathers" in verse 3 denotes: the readings are set side by side — one reading takes it as the generation that stood at Horeb forty years before (they are precisely the "fathers" generation, set by Moses in contrast with today's generation, the nearness being rhetorically heightened); another takes it as the earlier patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob — the covenant was sworn to them, but the covenant in the form of the Ten Words was made only at Horeb). The two readings do not differ over what verse 3 establishes, that the covenant is made with the people of today; they differ only over the identity of the term of contrast. This manuscript registers the construction only and does not adjudicate. Second, the expression "face to face" alongside the same expression at Exodus 33:11 and Numbers 12:8 (registered as a reported summary): there it describes the manner of Moses' speaking with God; here it is used of God's promulgation to the whole assembly; one reading takes it as an idiom (unreserved, intimate converse), another reads it together with the special designation of Moses at Deuteronomy 34:10. Only the distribution is registered. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: this part does one thing first — it writes the hearers' names into the covenant. Not with our fathers, but with us; not another day, but this day; not heard from afar, but spoken face to face. Only when the covenant stands made does Moses open his mouth to recite the commandments again.

### Part Two: The Ten Words (I) — The Four Words Toward God (5:6–15) (14:00–26:00)

(Fact) The Ten Words open not with a command but with a self-introduction. Verse 6, quoted in full: "I am the LORD thy God, which brought thee out of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage." (5:6) Registered item by item: first, "I am" is ʾānōkî (H595) — the first speaker of the commandments first gives His name: the One who promulgates the law is the One who first delivered. Second, "brought thee out" (the hiphil of yāṣāʾ, H3318) and "the house of bondage" (bêt ʿăḇāḏîm; ʿeḇeḏ, H5650; for the difference between the KJV's "house of bondage" and the Chinese base text's "house of slaves," see the Base-text notes) — one sentence of salvation history serves as the preface to all the commandments: the deliverance comes first, the commands after (in mutual attestation with Deuteronomy 4:45, "after they came forth out of Egypt," registered as a reported summary). Verse 7, the first commandment, quoted in full: "Thou shalt have none other gods before me." (5:7) — "other" is the plural of ʾaḥēr (H312); in the Hebrew there follows "before me" (ʿal-pānāy, "upon my face"), answering at a distance to the pānîm of "face to face" in verse 4, registered as it stands: before that face which spoke face to face, no other god is tolerated. (Fact) Verse 8, the first half of the second commandment, quoted in full: "Thou shalt not make thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath, or that is in the waters beneath the earth:" (5:8) — the two words "graven image" (pesel, H6459) and "likeness" (təmûnâ, H8544) are the echo of the prohibition in Deuteronomy 4 (registered in that manuscript, Endnote 4 there); the scope covers three realms — in heaven above, in the earth beneath, in the waters beneath the earth — the same bounds as the list of created things at 4:16–18 (registered cross-reference). Verse 9 continues, quoted in full: "Thou shalt not bow down thyself unto them, nor serve them: for I the LORD thy God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me," (5:9) Registered item by item: first, "bow down thyself" (šāḥâ, H7812) and "serve" (the hophal of ʿāḇaḏ, H5647); the KJV reads the plural "them" throughout, where the Chinese base text reads the singular (see the Base-text notes). Second, "jealous" (qannāʾ, H7067) is God's self-description here, the same word as at Deuteronomy 4:24. Third, the scope of "visiting" (pāqaḏ, H6485) the "iniquity" (ʿāwōn, H5771) carries a qualifier — those "that hate me" (śānāʾ, H8130) — from father to children "unto the third and fourth generation" (šillēšîm, ribbēʿîm, H8029, H7256). Verse 10 is the other side of the second commandment, quoted in full: "And shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me and keep my commandments." (5:10) — the counter-account stands within the verse itself: for those who hate, the third and fourth generation; for those who love Him and keep His commandments (ʾāhaḇ, H157; šāmar), "thousands" (ʾălāp̄îm; ʾelep, H505); "mercy" (ḥeseḏ, H2617) and "commandments" (miṣwâ, H4687) are both pivot words of this chapter (Endnote 5); the Hebrew of this verse has a ketiv/qere variant (Endnote 11). (Fact) Verse 11, the third commandment, quoted in full: "Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain: for the LORD will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain." (5:11) — "take" in the Hebrew is nāśāʾ (to lift up, H5375) with šāwʾ (vanity, H7723): to lift up God's name for a vain use; "will not hold him guiltless" is the negated form of nāqâ (H5352) — one of the few penalty clauses stated within the commandments themselves in the Ten Words. (Fact) Verses 12–15 are the fourth commandment, the longest of the Ten Words (four verses). Verse 12, quoted in full: "Keep the sabbath day to sanctify it, as the LORD thy God hath commanded thee." (5:12) Registered item by item: first, the Hebrew for "keep" is šāmôr, the infinitive absolute of šāmar — set directly against the zākôr ("remember," from zākhar, H2142) of Exodus 20:8: the same commandment, two records, one reading "keep" and one "remember," set side by side in the discussion layer (Endnote 12); the KJV likewise reads "Keep" here and "Remember" there, and the two are not to be confused. Second, "as the LORD thy God hath commanded thee" (ṣāwâ, H6680) is this chapter's formula of backward reference: at the very moment the commandment is restated, it is already declared to be according to the old command — the self-awareness of the restatement is written inside the commandment itself. Third, "to sanctify it" is the piel of qādaš (H6942). Verse 13, quoted in full: "Six days thou shalt labour, and do all thy work:" (5:13) — "labour" (ʿāḇaḏ) is the same word as "serve" in verse 9, in a different setting: for six days of the week the "service" is work; on one day it stops. Verse 14, quoted in full: "But the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work, thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, nor thy manservant, nor thy maidservant, nor thine ox, nor thine ass, nor any of thy cattle, nor thy stranger that is within thy gates; that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest as well as thou." (5:14) Registered item by item: first, the list covered by the rest has nine items — thou, son, daughter, manservant, maidservant, ox, ass, cattle, and the stranger sojourning with thee (gēr, H1616; "within thy gates" in the Hebrew is "within thy gates" literally, šaʿar, H8179); the list at Exodus 20:10 has seven items, the ox and the ass being folded into the single word "cattle" without separate listing (Endnote 9). Second, the purpose clause, "that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest as well as thou" (nûaḥ, H5117) — Exodus 20:10 has no such clause (Endnote 12): in this record the Sabbath is not only the master's order to stop work; it is the servants' charter of rights. Verse 15 gives the reason, quoted in full: "And remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt, and that the LORD thy God brought thee out thence through a mighty hand and by a stretched out arm: therefore the LORD thy God commanded thee to keep the sabbath day." (5:15) Registered item by item: first, "remember" (zākhar) occurs in this chapter only here — the word that Exodus 20:8 places at the head of the commandment, Deuteronomy places inside the commandment's reason (Endnote 12). Second, the reason is not creation but redemption: thou wast a servant (ʿeḇeḏ, answering to the "house of bondage" of verse 6), brought out by "a mighty hand" (yāḏ ḥăzāqâ, H3027, H2389) and "a stretched out arm" (zərôaʿ nəṭûyâ, H2220, H5186) — the same words as the list of the means of the Exodus at Deuteronomy 4:34 (registered cross-reference). Third, the Hebrew at the end of the verse, "commanded thee to keep the sabbath day," is laʿăśôt et-haššabbāṯ, literally "to do / make the sabbath": the Sabbath here is something "made" — made, by the whole household's stopping work, into one day of rest. (Argument) The inferences of these four commandments: first, the root of the commandments is gratitude, not a transaction — the deliverance of verse 6 stands before every "thou shalt not": He first brought thee out, and only then asks thy loyalty; obedience is the delivered person's response, not the price of deliverance. Second, the ethics of the Sabbath are especially clear in this chapter: a person who remembers that he himself was a servant has no right to keep his own servants from rest; a person brought out by God's mighty hand must learn to still his own hand. In this chapter rest is both worship (the sabbath "of the LORD thy God") and social mercy (the servants rest "as well as thou"). Third, the arithmetic of the second commandment — three and four generations against thousands — has "measured" the jealousy: His visiting has a bound, His mercy has none; the generations of those who hate Him are limited, and for those who love Him and keep His commandments the mercy runs to thousands. (New Testament Reading) This part's motifs as echoed in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: Mark 2 records the Lord's saying that "the sabbath was made for man" (registered as a reported summary); Hebrews 4 rereads "rest" across creation and the promised land (registered as a reported summary); Matthew 11, "I am meek and lowly in heart … ye shall find rest unto your souls" (registered as a reported summary). All are registered at the reading layer only. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The differences between Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5 on the Sabbath are set side by side, four items in all (the full account is at Endnote 12): first, the opening verb — Exodus reads zākhar (remember), Deuteronomy šāmar (keep): the readings are set side by side — one reading holds that the two were originally two sides of one coin (to remember is to keep, to keep is to remember; verse 15 of this chapter itself uses zākhar as the reason, and the two words are in fact one coin); another holds that Deuteronomy deliberately rewrote the opening with šāmar, its own pivot word throughout the book. Second, the commandment's reason — Exodus 20:11 grounds it in the six days of creation and the rest of the seventh day (registered as a reported summary), Deuteronomy 5:15 in the Exodus and in having been a servant: the readings are set side by side — one holds that the two theological grounds of one commandment run in parallel without contradiction; another holds that the change of ground is connected with the hearers (the children of servants about to become landowners) and belongs to the restatement's setting. Third, the list and the purpose clause — Deuteronomy lists ox and ass separately and adds "that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest as well as thou": registered only; no assertion of addition or deletion is made. Fourth, the heading "as … hath commanded thee" is peculiar to Deuteronomy and is the restatement form's self-reference: registered only. All the above are set side by side without adjudication. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: the four words toward God open with deliverance and close with rest: I am the One who brought thee out — therefore thou shalt have no other gods, make no image, and not take the name in vain; therefore on the seventh day the whole household, with ox, ass and stranger, stops its hands together, and as the servants of that day were brought out, so the servants of today also obtain rest.

### Part Three: The Ten Words (II) — The Six Words Toward Man (5:16–21) (26:00–36:00)

(Fact) Verse 16 is the fifth commandment, quoted in full: "Honour thy father and thy mother, as the LORD thy God hath commanded thee; that thy days may be prolonged, and that it may go well with thee, in the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee." (5:16) Registered item by item: first, "honour" is the piel of kāḇaḏ (H3513), in the Hebrew "to give weight / honour to." Second, the heading "as the LORD thy God hath commanded thee" is the formula of verse 12 occurring again (Endnote 5) — the Sabbath and the honouring of parents are the two commandments that, in the restatement, wear the same heading. Third, the promise has two layers: "that it may go well with thee" (yāṭaḇ, H3190; Endnote 10) is absent from Exodus 20:12 (Endnote 12), while "that thy days may be prolonged" (ʾārak yāmîm, H748) stands in both places. Fourth, the place of the promise is "the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee" (ʾăḏāmâ, H127; nātan, H5414) — honouring parents and long continuance in the land are tied on one line in this chapter. (Fact) Verses 17–20 are the four shortest commandments, quoted in full verse by verse: verse 17, "Thou shalt not kill." (5:17) — in the Hebrew, loʾ tirṣāḥ (rāṣaḥ, H7523), and this verse begins directly without the conjunction waw; verse 18, "Neither shalt thou commit adultery." (5:18) — waw plus loʾ with nāʾap (H5003; the KJV's "Neither" carries the waw); verse 19, "Neither shalt thou steal." (5:19) — waw plus gānaḇ (H1589); verse 20, "Neither shalt thou bear false witness against thy neighbour." (5:20) — waw plus ʿānâ (H6030) plus ʿēḏ šāwʾ (false witness, H5707, H7723). Three registrations: first, verse 17 has no waw, and the four commandments of verses 18–21 each open with waw, a consecutive arrangement visible in the Hebrew line (Endnote 8); in Exodus 20 the corresponding commandments each open directly with loʾ, without this consecutive arrangement. Second, the object "harmed" in verse 20 in the Hebrew is rēaʿ (thy neighbour); the Chinese base text does not render "neighbour," as registered in its itemized record (see the Base-text notes), while the KJV renders it in full, "against thy neighbour." Third, the šāwʾ of verse 20 is the same word as the šāwʾ of "in vain" in verse 11 — the vain use of God's name and the bearing of false witness belong, in the Hebrew, to one class of vanity; Exodus 20:16 here reads šeqer (H8267), a difference of wording between the two places, set side by side at Endnote 12. (Fact) Verse 21 is the tenth commandment, the only commandment among the Ten Words that reaches to the "heart," quoted in full: "Neither shalt thou desire thy neighbour's wife, neither shalt thou covet thy neighbour's house, his field, or his manservant, or his maidservant, his ox, or his ass, or any thing that is thy neighbour's." (5:21) Registered item by item: first, two sections, two verbs — for the wife, ḥāmad (H2530; the Chinese base text, "desire / lust after"; the KJV, "desire"), and for the house and what follows, the hitpael of ʾāwâ (H183; the Chinese base text, "covet / long for"; the KJV, "covet"): at Exodus 20:17 both sections use ḥāmad (Endnote 12). Second, the order: the wife stands first, the house second — at Exodus 20:17 the house stands first and the wife second, the two places reversed (Endnote 12). Third, the list: the one item of the wife, plus the seven items of house, field, manservant, maidservant, ox, ass, and any thing that is his — eight items in all; "his field" (śāḏeh, H7704) is absent from the list at Exodus 20:17 (Endnote 9) — the people about to enter the land are warned first, precisely, about the fields of that land. Fourth, the KJV and the ASV form an exact contrast in their English renderings of the two verbs: the KJV renders ḥāmad by "desire" and ʾāwâ by "covet," the ASV exactly the reverse (registered as it stands, Endnote 12). (Argument) The inferences of these six words: first, the order gathers inward: parents (the source of the household), life, marriage, property, speech (witness), and last, desire itself — the first nine words govern the hand, the mouth and the body; the tenth governs the heart that has not yet lifted its hand: a desire unfulfilled makes no court case, yet is already the commandment's object. At its end, the law in this chapter pushes the judgment seat into the place no one can see. Second, the promise structure of verse 16 deserves attention: among the Ten Words only this commandment expressly carries a promise, and the promise is bound to "the land" — in an address spoken on the verge of entering the land, Moses ties the ethics of the family and the length of the nation's continuance together. Third, the answering echo between the šāwʾ of verse 20 and that of verse 11 (this is an inference): God's name may not be lifted up in vanity, and a man's good name may not be harmed by a vain witness either — the third word and the ninth word share one root-image in the Hebrew, and honesty is one line toward God and toward man. (New Testament Reading) This part's motifs as echoed in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: Ephesians 6 cites this commandment and calls it "the first commandment with promise" (registered as a reported summary); Matthew 5 carries the words against killing and adultery forward to anger and desire (registered as a reported summary), in the same inward-gathering pattern as this part; Romans 7, where Paul by the one word "Thou shalt not covet" describes his own learning of sin through the law (registered as a reported summary); Romans 13, where "love worketh no ill to his neighbour" gathers up the words toward man (registered as a reported summary). All are registered at the reading layer only. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Three matters set side by side. First, the numbering of the Ten Words: the traditional countings are more than one — three are set side by side: one begins counting from verses 6–7 (preface plus first word) and makes the Sabbath the fourth word (the Reformed usage); one treats the preface as independent, merges the first two words, and divides the tenth word into two (the Catholic and Lutheran usage); one makes "I am the LORD" the first utterance and the making of images the second word (the Jewish traditional usage). The fact layer of this manuscript registers only the verse spans (the commandments run across verses 6–21, sixteen verses in all) and the common four-and-six division as one reading (four words toward God, through the Sabbath; six words toward man, from honouring parents); the dispute over numbering is not adjudicated. Second, the semantic range of rāṣaḥ in "Thou shalt not kill": the word is used in the Old Testament mostly for killing / murder (it is also used for manslaughter; compare the registration at Numbers 35), and is distinct from the vocabulary of general warfare and judicial execution; the dispute between the English renderings "murder" and "kill" is a dispute of translation, registered only. Third, verse 17 without waw and the waw-consecutive arrangement of verses 18–21: the readings are set side by side — one takes it as an incidental unevenness of the writing; another takes it as a deliberate gathering in the arrangement (four commandments in a string, closing at verse 21). The phenomenon only is registered. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: the six words toward man run from parents to the neighbour's field: first a person's place in his home, then his life, his marriage, his property, his good name, and last what his eye sees and his heart wants. The Ten Words are finished: the hand is governed, the heart not yet — and that is precisely what the fear of the next part and the wish of the last part have to deal with.

### Part Four: Fear in the Fire — The People Ask for a Mediator (5:22–27) (36:00–45:00)

(Fact) Verse 22 is the closing record of the Ten Words, quoted in full: "These words the LORD spake unto all your assembly in the mount out of the midst of the fire, of the cloud, and of the thick darkness, with a great voice: and he added no more. And he wrote them in two tables of stone, and delivered them unto me." (5:22) Registered item by item: first, "these words" (haddəḇārîm; dāḇār, H1697) — the Ten Words' self-designation in the Hebrew is "words," in mutual attestation with Deuteronomy 4:13, "the ten commandments" (the ten words). Second, "all your assembly" (qāhāl, H6951) — the object of the promulgation was the whole body, not Moses hearing first alone and then relaying. Third, "and he added no more" (loʾ yāsap, H3254) — the completeness of the Ten Words is stated here by the text itself: not one sentence more. Fourth, He "wrote" (kātaḇ, H3789) them "in two tables of stone" (lûaḥ ʾeḇen, H3871, H68) and delivered them to Moses — a double track, oral and engraved: what was heard was carved down. (Fact) Verse 23 records the reaction after the promulgation, quoted in full: "And it came to pass, when ye heard the voice out of the midst of the darkness, (for the mountain did burn with fire,) that ye came near unto me, even all the heads of your tribes, and your elders;" (5:23) — the "voice" (qôl) coming out of the "darkness" (ḥōšekh, H2822) is once again the centre of the event; those who came near to Moses were the heads (rōʾš, H7218) of the tribes and the elders (zāqēn, H2205) — the people's request was brought by representatives. Verse 24 is the representatives' report, quoted in full: "And ye said, Behold, the LORD our God hath shewed us his glory and his greatness, and we have heard his voice out of the midst of the fire: we have seen this day that God doth talk with man, and he liveth." (5:24) Registered item by item: first, confession first, petition after — the first half of the report is a confession: His "glory" (kāḇôḏ, H3519) and His "greatness" (gōḏel, H1433) have both been seen. Second, "God doth talk with man, and he liveth" (dāḇar; ʾāḏām, H120; ḥāyâ, H2425) is astonishment, not complaint: what they have seen "this day" (hayyôm, the second of its three occurrences in the chapter, Endnote 7) is a thing they had thought impossible. Verse 25 continues with the reason for the fear, quoted in full: "Now therefore why should we die? for this great fire will consume us: if we hear the voice of the LORD our God any more, then we shall die." (5:25) — "now" (ʿattâ, H6258) joins directly onto the astonishment of verse 24; "will consume" (ʾākhal, H398, to devour) is the echo of the same word at Deuteronomy 4:24, the "consuming fire" (registered cross-reference); "if we hear … any more, then we shall die" (mûṯ, H4191) — the fear's account is reckoned out to death. Verse 26 puts the matter as unprecedented, quoted in full: "For who is there of all flesh, that hath heard the voice of the living God speaking out of the midst of the fire, as we have, and lived?" (5:26) — "all flesh" is bāśār (H1320); "the living God" in the Hebrew is ʾĕlōhîm ḥayyîm (God who lives, ḥayyîm in plural form): that He is alive, and that those who heard Him are still alive, is in the representatives' mouth the single instance of its kind (this is the representatives' rhetorical statement; this manuscript registers its wording and makes no separate statistical assertion). Verse 27 is the formal request, quoted in full: "Go thou near, and hear all that the LORD our God shall say: and speak thou unto us all that the LORD our God shall speak unto thee; and we will hear it, and do it." (5:27) Registered item by item: first, "go thou near" is the imperative of qāraḇ (H7126) — the people ask Moses to go where they themselves dare not go. Second, the promise is "we will hear it, and do it" (šāmaʿ + ʿāśâ) — precisely the last two links of the chain of verse 1: the people hand Moses' teaching back, word for word, as their own promise. Third, the quotation closes here: the words of the heads and elders in verses 23–27 form one whole block of quotation. (Argument) The inferences of this part: first, the origin of the mediator's office in this chapter is the people's request, approved by God (v. 28); it was not taken by Moses for himself — the mediator's place ("between," v. 5) existed before the request; the request only put that place into words. Second, the fear here is not unbelief: the representatives confess first (v. 24) and petition after (v. 27), and the direction of their petition is not "speak to us no more" but "make another way by which we may still hear" — fear here is the first form of reverence; what it shrinks from is not God's word but the unmediated encounter. Third, the promise "we will hear it, and do it" stands in intertextual relation with the chain of verse 1: Moses' opening words were "hear, learn, keep, do," and the people's answer is "hear … and do" — the address is not yet finished, and its first lesson has already had its response. (New Testament Reading) This part's motifs as echoed in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: 1 Timothy 2, "there is one God, and one mediator between God and men" (registered as a reported summary); Hebrews 12, which with "see that ye refuse not him that speaketh" takes up Sinai's fear again and points to the mediator of the new covenant (registered as a reported summary); Galatians 3, on the mediator and the ordaining of the law (registered as a reported summary). All are registered at the reading layer only. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Set against Exodus 20:18–21 (registered as a reported summary), three differences are set side by side: first, Exodus records that the people stood afar off and Moses drew near to the thick darkness (where God was); Deuteronomy records that the heads and elders came near to Moses and stated their case first in the form of a report. Second, in Exodus Moses' answer is "Fear not," with a further explanation (God has come to prove them, so that His fear may be before their faces and they sin not); in Deuteronomy Moses has no answer — the answer is given by God Himself in verses 28–31, and it is an approval. Third, Exodus has no separate writing of the people's return to their tents and Moses' remaining alone before God; Deuteronomy 5:30–31 has it as its own feature. The readings are set side by side: one takes the two places as the fuller and the briefer accounts of one event complementing each other (Exodus stressing the scene, Deuteronomy the frame of the law's restatement); another takes Deuteronomy as a retelling of the Horeb tradition, in which the addition and omission of details serve the address's purpose. Registered only, without adjudication. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: the first thing after the promulgation of the Ten Words is not cheering but fear — and what the fear gives birth to is not flight but a request: go thou near, and we will hear it, and do it. That is how the mediator's road was opened.

### Part Five: O That There Were Such an Heart in Them (5:28–33) (45:00–57:00)

(Fact) Verse 28 is God's answer to the request, quoted in full: "And the LORD heard the voice of your words, when ye spake unto me; and the LORD said unto me, I have heard the voice of the words of this people, which they have spoken unto thee: they have well said all that they have spoken." (5:28) Registered item by item: first, "the voice of your words" (qôl) here is the people's words being heard by God — hearing in this chapter runs in both directions: man hears God's voice (vv. 22–26), and God also hears man's words. Second, the Hebrew of "they have well said all that they have spoken" is hêṭîḇû (the hiphil of yāṭaḇ, H3190), literally "they have spoken well / rightly" — the verdict God gives on the people's request for a mediator is approval (Endnote 10). Verse 29 is the heart of the whole chapter and the source of the title, quoted in full: "O that there were such an heart in them, that they would fear me, and keep all my commandments always, that it might be well with them, and with their children for ever!" (5:29) Registered item by item: first, the Hebrew of "O that there were" is mî-yittēn (nātan, H5414), a wish-construction, literally "who will give …" — and the speaker is God Himself: the chapter's tone of command turns, at this verse, into a tone of wish. Second, "such an heart" (lēḇāḇ zzeh, H3824) — what God wants is a configuration of the heart: fear (yārēʾ) and keeping (šāmar) of the commandments (miṣwâ) bound together, and carrying the time-word "always" (kol-hayyāmîm, all the days). Third, the purpose lands on "that it might be well" (yāṭaḇ, the same word as at verse 16, Endnote 10), and the range of the well-being is "with them, and with their children for ever" (ʿôlām, H5769). (Fact) Verse 30 is the request carried into effect, quoted in full: "Go say to them, Get you into your tents again." (5:30) — "go" is the imperative of hālak (H1980), "get you … again" is šûḇ (H7725), "tents" is ʾōhel (H168): the people are permitted to withdraw to daily life, and the mediator remains alone — the answer fear received is not "go up the mount again" but "go home." Verse 31 is God's separate charge to Moses, quoted in full: "But as for thee, stand thou here by me, and I will speak unto thee all the commandments, and the statutes, and the judgments, which thou shalt teach them, that they may do them in the land which I give them to possess it." (5:31) Registered item by item: first, "stand thou here" uses ʿāmad, the same word as "stood between" in verse 5 — the same Moses stands between God and man in verse 5 and stands beside God in verse 31: the mediator's place moves as the work is accomplished. Second, the content is "all the commandments, and the statutes, and the judgments" (hammiṣwâ, ḥuqqîm, mišpāṭîm) — the general name of verse 1 here has "commandments" added to it, making a threefold formula, from which the body of the law in the latter half of the book (chapters 6–26) is led out. Third, "thou shalt teach them" is the piel of lāmad, the two ends of the same word as the "learn" of verse 1: God delivers to Moses, Moses teaches the people, the people learn — the chain of transmission closes at the chapter's head and tail. Fourth, God's speech closes here, and the stage for "do them in the land" is the land "which I give them to possess it" (yāraš, H3423). (Fact) Verse 32, Moses resumes the address, quoted in full: "Ye shall observe to do therefore as the LORD your God hath commanded you: ye shall not turn aside to the right hand or to the left." (5:32) — "observe to do" (šāmar + ʿāśâ) is in exactly the same form as the last two links of the chain in verse 1: the chapter begins with this pair of verbs and ends with this pair of verbs (Endnote 4); "turn aside" (sûr, H5493) to the right hand (yāmîn, H3225) and to the left (śəmōʾl, H8040) are both under the prohibition — deviation is forbidden in either direction. Verse 33 is the chapter's last sentence, quoted in full: "Ye shall walk in all the ways which the LORD your God hath commanded you, that ye may live, and that it may be well with you, and that ye may prolong your days in the land which ye shall possess." (5:33) Registered item by item: first, the centre-word of the Hebrew is "way" (derekh, H1870): "in all the ways which the LORD your God hath commanded you, in them ye shall walk" — the Chinese base text renders it "what He has commanded you to do, you shall all do," without rendering derekh directly, as registered in its itemized record (see the Base-text notes), while the KJV renders "walk in all the ways" in full. Second, the purpose has three layers — live (ḥāyâ, H2421), be well (ṭôḇ, H2896), prolong days (ʾārak yāmîm): the promise of verse 16 for honouring parents (well-being + prolonging) is here expanded into the promise of the whole chapter, and the hearers are expanded from children to the whole people. Third, "the land which ye shall possess" (yāraš) closes the whole chapter: the covenant was made at Horeb (v. 2); the road is walked in the land about to be possessed (vv. 31, 33). (Argument) The inferences of this part: first, verses 28–29 are God's self-disclosure after the Ten Words: He approved the fear, and He also spoke plainly of fear's insufficiency — the people's heart at this moment is right ("they have well said"), and the difficulty is in the "keeping": keeping such a heart, keeping always. The commandments can bind conduct; they cannot bind the heart's continuance; God Himself speaks this gap aloud — not as a denial of the law, but marking out the law's limit, and placing the wish beyond the limit: for it to be well for ever, the heart must first be kept. Second, the division of labour in verses 30–31 is the shape of grace: the people return to their tents (to live their daily days), Moses remains (to receive the words still to be delivered) — most of the life of faith is not on the mount but in the tents; the words of the mount must be carried, by teaching, verse by verse into the tents. Third, the "way" imagery of verses 32–33 (this is an inference): doing here is not keeping a string of rules but walking a way — turning neither to the left nor to the right, walking in it, and so living, being well, and prolonging days. (New Testament Reading) This part's motifs as echoed in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: the "new heart" promises of Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 36 (Old Testament prophetic books, registered as reported summaries) answer, before and after, to the wish for "such an heart" in this chapter; Hebrews 8, citing Jeremiah, records that God puts His laws into the mind and writes them in the hearts (registered as a reported summary); 2 Corinthians 3 sets "tables of stone" against "tables of the heart" in contrast (registered as a reported summary), set against the record of the tables of stone in verse 22. All are registered at the reading layer only, without being read back as this chapter's original meaning. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters set side by side. First, the distribution of "ye shall not turn aside to the right hand or to the left": besides this verse, the same formula is found at Deuteronomy 17:20 (of the king), 28:14, Joshua 1:7, 23:6, and Proverbs 4:27 (registered from the cross-references on the verse pages): within the Deuteronomic tradition this formula is a fixed closing sentence for covenant-keeping; the readings are set side by side — one takes it as a formula of the style, another as a deliberate cross-chapter intertextuality (Joshua, on succeeding, receives this charge again). The distribution only is registered. Second, the nature of the wish-construction mî-yittēn in verse 29: the readings are set side by side — one takes it as an anthropopathic sigh of God (the rhetoric of the address form, taking its surface sense, "God earnestly desires that the people's heart be kept long"); another takes it as a serious theological statement (God's wish for the human heart is real, while the heart's continuance still lies on the side of human responsibility, joining directly to the exhortation of verse 32). Both readings preserve the exhortation of verses 32–33 in its place; registered only, without adjudication. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: the chapter's last act is God's approving the people's fear, dismissing the assembly, keeping Moses — and speaking the chapter's softest sentence: O that there were such an heart in them. The commandments were written on tables of stone; the wish is spoken toward the heart; and Moses' close is one sentence only: observe to do as commanded, turn not aside to the right hand or to the left, walk in this way — and live, and be well, and prolong your days.

Conclusion (57:00–60:00). (Argument) Looking back over this chapter: it begins with a summons (v. 1, "And Moses called all Israel") and ends with a way (v. 33). Between them stand ten commandments, two tables of stone, one fear, and one wish. Moses spoke the words of forty years before over again, because the hearers had changed to a new generation; God recited the Ten Words over again, because the covenant was to be made with the people alive this day. It comes to today's hearers: we too are not at the foot of Horeb; we stand on this side, on the plains of Moab — for us too the law is not a historical artefact but words to be heard today, learned today, and observed and done today. The ten commandments most of us can recite; the difficulty is that one sentence of verse 29 — keeping such a heart. May the God who spoke in the fire, and who also heard the people's words, place His wish in our hearts: to fear Him, to keep all His commandments always, to turn neither to the left nor to the right, to walk in the way He has commanded — that it may be well with us and with our children for ever.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotional use)

"And remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt, and that the LORD thy God brought thee out thence through a mighty hand and by a stretched out arm: therefore the LORD thy God commanded thee to keep the sabbath day." (Deut 5:15, KJV) When the Ten Words reach the fourth commandment, the reason Deuteronomy gives for the Sabbath is not creation but remembrance: you were servants. Those who have been servants know best what it is to be unable to stop — the work in Egypt had no seventh day. So God commands this generation, about to become masters: you stop first, and let your manservants, your maidservants, your ox and ass, and the stranger within your gates, rest as well as you. Rest is not laziness; it is memory: remembering that you yourself were brought out by a mighty hand, and that today's freedom was not earned by yourself. Most of us have not been servants, yet each of us has his own Egypt — work that will not stop, a phone that will not be put down, gains and losses that will not stop being counted. The Lord's word comes today in the same way: remember how you were brought out, and stop in the same way; as you have freely received rest, so let those around you receive rest. God's wish is not only that we keep one commandment — O that we would keep such a heart, and keep all His commandments always, that it might be well with us and with our children for ever. Prayer: Lord, thank You for bringing me out with a mighty hand. Teach me to remember, teach me to stop, and teach me to share rest with those around me; may I keep a heart that fears You, and keep all Your commandments always. Amen.

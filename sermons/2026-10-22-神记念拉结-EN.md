## Summary (about 100 words)

Rachel, barren, strove with Leah: through her maid Bilhah she had Dan and Naphtali; Leah answered with Zilpah, and by mandrakes traded for a night with Jacob bore still more sons. God remembered Rachel, listened to her, and enabled her to bear Joseph. Jacob agreed with Laban on speckled and spotted sheep as his wages; though the wages were repeatedly pressed down, God made him exceedingly prosperous: striving exhausts people, remembrance upholds them; human wages may be unfair, but God's blessing is not partial.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter we stopped at Leah holding Judah and saying: This time I will praise the LORD. That was a turn within tears, but the story did not grow calm after that. In the previous chapter Leah bore four sons in succession, and the favoritism and striving in Jacob's household continue to ferment in this chapter. Leah had sons, yet still did not have her husband's heart; Rachel had her husband's heart, yet for many years had no child. In one household, two people, each holding fast to the piece she lacked, and neither willing to let go first. This chapter reads a little chaotically: striving, maidservants, mandrakes, bargaining, and at last a great flock of speckled and striped sheep. But behind the chaos, the Bible gives us only two quiet verbs: one is "remember," and the other is "bless." Today let us lay hold of these two words, and see how God works amid human striving, and how, under unfair human wages, He makes His servant able to stand.

Let us read together several key passages of today's Scripture:

"And when Rachel saw that she bare Jacob no children, Rachel envied her sister; and said unto Jacob, Give me children, or else I die." (Genesis 30:1, excerpt, KJV)

"And Rachel said, With great wrestlings have I wrestled with my sister, and I have prevailed: and she called his name Naphtali." (Genesis 30:8, excerpt, KJV)

"And God remembered Rachel, and God hearkened to her, and opened her womb. And she conceived, and bare a son; and said, God hath taken away my reproach: and she called his name Joseph." (Genesis 30:22-24, excerpt, KJV)

"I will pass through all thy flock to day, removing from thence all the speckled and spotted cattle, and all the brown cattle among the sheep, and the spotted and speckled among the goats: and of such shall be my hire." (Genesis 30:32, KJV)

"And the man increased exceedingly, and had much cattle, and maidservants, and menservants, and camels, and asses." (Genesis 30:43, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, in this chapter there is a flavor very familiar to us all: the anxiety of waiting too long, the sourness of seeing others receive, and the grievance of earning a living under unfairness. Through Rachel and Jacob, let us see that human striving cannot save anyone, and only Your remembrance can uphold a person; human wages can be pressed down, but Your blessing cannot be pressed down. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: A Household of Striving — Maidservants, Names, and Mandrakes (30:1-24a)

First look at Rachel's suffering. Rachel was the one who was loved; the previous chapter already made this thoroughly clear: Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah. But being loved does not mean not suffering. For many years she bore no children, and in that era, for a woman to be barren was not only a physical regret; it would be discussed by people as a disgrace and as evidence that God was not pleased with her. Leah bore one child after another, while Rachel's room remained quiet all along. The beloved one also had her own desolation: she had everything, lacking only the one thing she wanted most; and the one thing she lacked was precisely what her sister received again and again. The hardest comparisons for a person are often not with outsiders but with family: the same husband, the same table, the same roof — her sister's children running all over the floor, and her own knees empty.

So she spoke, and her first sentence was full of gunpowder: "Give me children, or else I die." In this sentence there was despair, and there was also misdirection. She pressed all the pain of barrenness onto her husband, as if Jacob could decide childbearing. Jacob's reply also grew angry: "Am I in God's stead, who hath withheld from thee the fruit of the womb?" Theologically this was not wrong — sovereignty over childbearing belongs to God — but in tone it was a basin of cold water. Neither of them said anything incorrect, yet both wounded the other. Brothers and sisters, people who have waited a long time easily speak in extremes; and people who accompany those who wait easily use correct doctrine to block real tears. The deepest quarrels in a family are often not about who is unreasonable; they are two wounded people colliding with each other's pain.

Next, Rachel took an old road very familiar to us: obtaining a child through a maidservant. She gave her maid Bilhah to Jacob, saying: go in unto her, and she shall bear upon my knees, that I may also have children by her. Sarah had walked this road in her day, giving Hagar to Abraham, and what came out of it was Ishmael and the tragedy of a household driven out. Now Rachel walked it again. Bilhah bore Dan, and Rachel said: "God hath judged me, and hath also heard my voice, and hath given me a son." The name Dan carries the meaning of vindication and judgment. She regarded this child as the justice God had recovered for her before her sister. Bilhah bore Naphtali as well, and Rachel's words were even more direct: "With great wrestlings have I wrestled with my sister, and I have prevailed." Please hear this clearly: the name of this child commemorated not God's grace, but a victory and defeat between her and her sister. The birth of a child ought to be the joy of the whole family; in a striving household, it became a mark on a scoreboard. Before the child had grown up, his name first fought his mother's battle for her.

Leah on her side also refused to stop. Seeing that she had left bearing, she gave her maid Zilpah to Jacob. Zilpah bore Gad, and Leah said: "A troop cometh!" And she bore Asher, and Leah said: "Happy am I, for the daughters will call me blessed." Two maidservants, four children, and two mistresses calling to each other across the children. The Bible does not whitewash this family: it lets us see that once striving enters a household door, even something as sacred as childbearing can be swept into comparison. Children are God's reward, but when parents' hearts are filled with victory and defeat, the reward can also be used as a weapon. Here a fair word must also be said for Bilhah and Zilpah: they were maidservants, with no room to choose, and the children they bore were counted under their mistresses' names. In this striving they were the people with the least voice. The Bible records their names and records the tribes born of them — Gad and Asher later both became tribes of Israel — God did not delete them from the genealogy because their status was low. People seat others by status; God records names by His grace.

The next act of the striving is the mandrakes. At wheat harvest, the eldest son Reuben found mandrakes in the field and brought them back to his mother Leah. It was a plant that people of that time believed to be related to childbearing, carrying fragrance, and carrying hope. Rachel asked Leah for some: "Give me, I pray thee, of thy son's mandrakes." Leah's grievance, stored up for many years, burst out at once: "Is it a small matter that thou hast taken my husband? and wouldest thou take away my son's mandrakes also?" When sisters speak to this point, family affection has already been worn very thin. Rachel's answer is the sentence in this chapter that most tightens the heart, a transaction: "Therefore he shall lie with thee tonight for thy son's mandrakes." One night of her husband, exchanged for a few mandrakes. The most intimate matter in marriage was placed on a negotiating table. In the evening, when Jacob came in from the field, Leah went out to meet him and spoke very plainly: "Thou must come in unto me; for surely I have hired thee with my son's mandrakes." "Hired" — using this word of a husband, what sorrow that is: for Leah to receive one night of her husband's companionship, she actually had to pay a price to hire him.

But please notice what the Scripture says next: "And God hearkened unto Leah, and she conceived." The mandrakes showed no efficacy; what took effect was God's hearkening. Leah bore Issachar and said: "God hath given me my hire." She bore Zebulun as well and said: "God hath endued me with a good dowry; now will my husband dwell with me." Later she also bore a daughter and named her Dinah. Here the Bible quietly corrects the superstition of the whole chapter: people thought mandrakes could exchange for childbearing, that a maidservant could exchange for a victory, that a transaction could exchange for a husband's heart, yet the text again and again changes the subject to God — God hearkened to Leah, God heard, God remembered. The door of childbearing was never in the hands of a plant, nor in the hands of a maidservant, nor in the hands of a human abacus; it was only in God's hand. Brothers and sisters, today we do not worship mandrakes, but we have our own mandrakes: all kinds of folk remedies, back doors, connections, and shortcuts. Whatever we think can bypass waiting and directly buy the result is a mandrake. A mandrake can at most exchange for one night; it cannot exchange for a lifetime of blessing. What truly opens a situation has always been that unremarkable phrase: "And God hearkened."

Here we must also pause one more step, and look at the names the two mothers gave their children. In the Bible, naming a child often engraves the parents' state of heart at the time onto the child. Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher — these four names, read together, are like two diaries scolding each other across the air: one side says God has judged my case; the other side says, good fortune; one side says, in my wrestlings I have prevailed; the other side says, the daughters will call me blessed. The names were the mothers' battle reports, but a child is not a battle report; a child is a living person, one by one. Many years later, these children grew up and became tribes of Israel; they would march together in the wilderness and divide the land of Canaan together as an inheritance; their mothers' victories and defeats nearly got written into their destinies. We who are parents must be reminded: accounts between husband and wife, accounts between sisters-in-law, accounts of the previous generation — do not engrave them on a child's name, and do not tell them again and again at the dinner table and in the family group, making children take sides for the adults from childhood. Children are not tools of their parents' striving, nor bargaining chips for their parents to turn their fortunes around; children are a heritage entrusted to us by God, and our task is to give them back to God, not to push them onto the battlefield we have not finished fighting. That later, in Jacob's family, brothers would turn cruel on each other and sell Joseph into Egypt — the root was already buried in this chapter: when a child is treated as a scoreboard from birth, it is very hard, once grown, for them to treat one another simply as brothers. May God stop us, and not let striving be passed down as an inheritance.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: God Remembered Rachel — The Birth of Joseph (30:22-24)

At the end of Part One, the picture stops with Leah bearing still more sons. Striving carried to this point — who won? By the number of children, Leah led by far; by her husband's heart, she was still the loser. Rachel lost thoroughly by number, yet in her hand she held Jacob's love. The two women each won half and each lost half; striving never has a true winner, only exhausted people. Just at this moment, the Bible's pen turns gently and writes the warmest sentence of the whole chapter: "And God remembered Rachel, and God hearkened to her, and opened her womb."

Let us pause first on this word: remembered. In the original, zakar (זָכַר, H2142), to remember, call to mind, commemorate [1]. When the Bible says God remembered, it never means God had forgotten for a while and only now thought of it again. God remembered Noah, and caused the flood to recede; God remembered Abraham, and rescued Lot in the destruction of Sodom; God remembered His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and in Egypt heard the groaning of the Israelites. God's remembrance is His rising to act according to His own covenant and His own time. In other words, remembrance is not memory; it is the moment grace begins to be cashed in. Rachel had waited so many years, striven so many rounds, borrowed a maidservant, exhausted her schemes, and none of it opened the door of childbearing; when the door opened, the text does not write what Rachel did — it writes only that God remembered her. The hardest part of waiting is that we do not know God's timing; the most hopeful part of waiting is that God has never truly forgotten. Human memory leaks; God's covenant does not leak.

Look next at the second verb: hearkened. "And God remembered Rachel, and God hearkened to her." Had Rachel prayed? The text does not record the original words of her prayer, yet God said He had hearkened to her. Some prayers are voiced; some prayers have only tears and sighs. Rachel's "Give me children, or else I die" was shouted at Jacob — the tone was wrong, and the addressee was wrong too — yet God took even those shapeless cries of hers up to Himself. If among us there is anyone who feels their prayers are shapeless and that they cannot say beautiful words, please listen well: what God hearkens to is not rhetoric, but groaning. He heard Hagar's weeping in the wilderness, He heard the sighing of the Israelites in Egypt, and He also heard the grievance Rachel swallowed down for many years in this striving household.

"And opened her womb." Once the door of childbearing opened, Rachel conceived and bore a son, and named him Joseph. The name Joseph has two layers of meaning, and Rachel herself spoke two sentences. The first: "God hath taken away my reproach." The stone that had pressed on her for many years — the disgrace of barrenness, the shame of being talked about, the thorn that pierced her heart every time she saw her sister's children — God removed it with His own hand. Note this: no one won it back for her, and it was not exchanged for with mandrakes; it was taken away by God. The second sentence: "The LORD shall add to me another son." The name Joseph itself carries the meaning of "adding." Her heart turned from prevailing to thanksgiving and hope: she no longer spoke of winning over her sister, but wished that God would add again. The mark of a person walking out of striving is not in how much she obtained, but in how her prayer changed: from "Give me children, or else I die," to "God hath taken away my reproach," and then to one calm sentence, "May the LORD add to me another son." When the tone changes, the person has changed.

The birth of Joseph was a turning point for Jacob's whole household. This child was later sold by his brothers into Egypt, and there became a governor; in the famine he preserved the life of the whole family, and also preserved the line of God's promise. Who could have imagined that Joseph, who saved a whole family, was born only after such a messy striving, and after so many years of his mother's tears? God's timetable for working often is slow enough to make people anxious; yet once He moves His hand, all the waiting before it is gathered into a larger story. That we cannot see the whole picture today does not mean there is no whole picture. On the day Joseph was born, Rachel also knew only that her disgrace had been taken away; she did not know that the one she held in her arms was the future governor of Egypt and the savior of the whole family. The answers we hold in our arms are often far larger than what we imagined when we asked.

This part also has a few honest words for people in long waiting. First, waiting is not God's absence. In the years when Rachel bore no children, God was not absent; at the end the text uses the word "remembered" to tell us that those years all counted with Him. Second, striving cannot ripen God's timing ahead of schedule. Maidservant, mandrakes, transactions — after going around in a great circle, the door was still opened by God; knowing this in advance, why wound others and oneself? Sadly, people are always willing to admit the door is in God's hand only after they have finished walking the detours. Third, do not measure your own life by someone else's progress. Leah's progress was fast, Rachel's progress was slow, yet the stories God gave the two of them were completely different: from Leah's lineage came kings and priests, and Rachel's son saved the life of the whole family. Comparison only steals gratitude; only the person who sees her own portion of remembrance can rest.

There is one more layer worth speaking through for those who are waiting: God's remembering Rachel does not mean God favored Rachel and neglected Leah. We have already settled this account: God saw Leah's tears, and God also hearkened to Rachel's tears. God is not choosing sides between the two sisters; He remembers one by one, hearkens one by one, and shows grace to each in its season. Leah first received children, and later received a heart of praise; Rachel first received her husband's love, and later received Joseph. The order of what the two received differed, and their lessons differed, yet not one person was left out of God's remembrance. When we pray, we often unconsciously picture God as a parent who distributes unfairly, as if, having given to others, He would not have enough left for me. It is not so. God's grace is not a loaf that grows smaller as it is sliced; His remembering your neighbor does not delay His remembering you; His hearkening to your sister's prayer does not delay His receiving your groaning into His book. What you are to do is not to stare anxiously at someone else's portion, but to keep your own portion of waiting: cry to God what is to be cried, walk as usual the road that is to be walked, serve as usual the people who are to be served; if the days of waiting are not lived in vain, the day of remembrance will not fall through.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Speckled Wages — Under Unfairness, God Has His Own Blessing (30:25-43)

As soon as Joseph was born, Jacob proposed to Laban that he leave: send me away, that I may go to my own place and to my country. Fourteen years had passed; he had completed his service for his two wives, and now with children in a crowd, he wanted to go home. But Laban would not let him go. Laban said something very worth savoring: "I have learned by experience that the LORD hath blessed me for thy sake." Even Laban, who had schemed all his life, could see it: this son-in-law carried blessing on him; where he was, the household enterprise prospered. Laban's way of keeping him was still his most practiced set: talk wages. "Appoint me thy wages, and I will give it."

Jacob set the wages, and set them in a way that made everyone drop their glasses: he did not ask for gold or silver, nor for flocks of fine sheep; he asked only for those speckled and spotted ones in the flocks — among the sheep, all the speckled and spotted, among the goats, all the striped and speckled, and the brown among the sheep — to count as his wages. Such sheep were a minority in a flock of white sheep; they were the ones with unpleasing markings, ordinarily picked over and left behind. Jacob also voluntarily set a verifiable rule: in time to come, when you come to look at my wages, whatever is not like this shall count as stolen by me; my righteousness shall answer for me. Brothers and sisters, had Jacob changed? In his youth he grasped a heel, seized the birthright, and deceived his father for the blessing — everything was taken by grabbing. Now, setting wages, he left the most unfavorable conditions to himself, and handed the right of inspection to the other side. This was not that he had suddenly become unscheming; it was that over these years God, through Laban's hand, had ground him little by little: a man whose wedding had been deceived and whose wages would be changed — in the next chapter we shall see that Laban changed his wages ten times — slowly learned to replace grasping with honesty. Staying long under unfairness, a person either turns bitter or turns clean; Jacob was walking toward the clean side.

Laban agreed with his mouth, and immediately worked tricks with his hands: that very day he picked out all the speckled and striped sheep and handed them to his own sons, and put three days' journey between himself and Jacob. The wages had just been set, and the sheep matching the wages were first emptied out. What Jacob faced was the picked-over flock: by natural reasoning, his portion of speckled wages had almost no possibility of growing out. This is the cruelest part of unfairness: the rules were set by you, the contract was signed by you, but the opening hand — the other side has already drawn the cards away first. In the workplace, in business, and in the ways of the world, quite a few brothers and sisters have tasted this flavor: the conditions were agreed, but at execution everything changed; the credit belonged to everyone, the drudgery to you; the portion that was promised always shrank by the time it reached your hand.

The passage that follows reads somewhat strange: Jacob took fresh rods of poplar, almond, and plane tree, peeled white streaks in them, set them in the watering troughs before the flocks, and let the flocks conceive facing the rods, bringing forth striped, speckled, and spotted young; when the strong flocks conceived he set the rods, and when they were feeble he did not, so the feebler were Laban's and the stronger Jacob's. We must read this passage honestly: peeling rods was itself no reliable method, and the Bible does not teach us to copy it. The true explanation is in the next chapter: Jacob himself testified that in a dream, when the flocks conceived, God let him see that the rams which leaped upon the cattle were striped, speckled, and grizzled, and God said to him that He had seen all that Laban had done to him; God had taken the cattle of Laban and given them to Jacob. In other words, the author of the transfer of wealth was God, not the rods. The rods were the small bit of duty Jacob understood and fulfilled at the time, while the source of blessing was the God who sees unfairness. For us to fulfill our duty is no error; but to credit the result to the method is an error. The result is forever credited to God.

The end of this chapter has only one summarizing sentence, yet its weight is full: "And the man increased exceedingly, and had much cattle, and maidservants, and menservants, and camels, and asses." Increased exceedingly. When he had set out in that year, beside him there was only a staff; now, with his wages emptied and the rules changed by others, under a father-in-law as shrewd as an enemy, he increased exceedingly. Who gave it? Did Laban give it? Laban wanted to give the least. Did the flocks grow by themselves? Behind the flocks there is the God who remembers people. This is the truth Part Three must establish: human wages can be unfair, but God's blessing is not bound by wages. An employer can press down your price, a partner can draw away your cards, the environment can first pick away the good conditions, yet nothing can block the portion God means to give you — when the time comes, He can make even the speckled sheep multiply.

Here are three applications for brothers and sisters earning a living under unfairness. First, make covenants honestly, and hand out the right of inspection. Jacob said: in time to come, come and look; whatever does not match the rule shall count as stolen by me. In gray zones, the testimony of a Christian is not stretching out a hand to take the portion that does not belong to oneself. Second, fulfill your own duty, and do not take shortcuts of revenge. Laban drew away the sheep; Jacob did not go and steal sheep; he pastured as usual and was diligent as usual. When you are mistreated, the easiest thought is tit for tat, but the road the Bible gives us is: fulfill the duty, and hand justice over to God. Third, believe that God sees. In the next chapter Jacob said God had seen all that Laban had done to him. Every hidden loss you have suffered, God has an account of it in His book; He may not settle it on our timetable, but He has never missed recording a single entry. A person who works for human wages has eyes only for that paycheck; a person who knows God is watching, however aggrieved the work in his hands, has an anchor-stone in his heart.

One more sentence must be added, lest we read this passage as a prosperity manual: Jacob's great increase was not overnight riches; it was six years of pasturing, kept day after day; his wealth was the continuation of God's promise, a household that was to be taken back to build a nation — not a number for showing off. The purpose of God's blessing was never only to make us comfortable, but to make us a link in His plan. If today He gives something in your unfair situation, please remember to ask one sentence: Lord, this portion — what do You want me to do with it?

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today, we have seen three pictures: a striving bedroom, a negotiating table, and a flock of speckled sheep. In the bedroom, two women strove with every scheme they had, until in the end they were exhausted, yet the door was opened by God — God remembered Rachel. Beside the table, however clearly the wages were agreed, it could not stop a man from working tricks, yet God's blessing does not play its cards according to the human abacus — Jacob increased exceedingly. Brothers and sisters, in striving and waiting, God's remembrance is not late; under unfair wages, God has His own blessing. The portion you lack, that people cannot give, God remembers; the portion of your price that was pressed down, that people can calculate, God can overturn.

Let us pray together: Lord who remembers people, thank You; You remembered Noah, You remembered Rachel, and You remember every one of us who is close to being unable to hold on in waiting. Stop the striving in our hearts, take away the disgrace upon us, and teach us, under unfairness, to remain honest, to remain diligent, and to remain believing that You see. And may we, in places where wages are unfair, learn neither Laban's hands nor the grasping of the young Jacob, but only this Jacob who handed out the right of inspection and handed the result back to God: honest in hand and steady in heart, because we know that the One who remembers us neither slumbers nor makes mistakes. May our prayers become like Rachel's later prayer: from striving into thanksgiving, from pressing into rest. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[3] naqod: Hebrew נָקֹד (H5348), speckled, with point-like markings; Genesis 30:32, when Jacob set his wages, referring to the "speckled" among the sheep and goats; Genesis 30:35 and 30:39 also use this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[4] 'aqod: Hebrew עָקֹד (H6124), striped, with banded (ring-like) markings; first occurring at Genesis 30:35 and also at 30:39-40 (not at 30:32), referring to the "striped" on the sheep; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note. [5] barod: Hebrew בָּרֹד (H1265), spotted, with mixed-color grizzled markings; not found in Genesis 30; within Genesis it occurs only at 31:10 and 31:12 (see also Zechariah), used to describe the markings of the sheep Jacob obtained; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note. The three words used together (speckled, striped, and grizzled) make clear that Jacob's wages referred precisely to the minority, differently colored sheep in the flock.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Then God remembered Rachel; he listened to her and enabled her to conceive. She became pregnant and gave birth to a son and said: God has taken away my disgrace. She named him Joseph, and said: May the LORD add to me another son." (Genesis 30:22-24, NIV)

Rachel waited many years, and she strove for many years. She tried a maidservant, she asked for mandrakes, she exhausted her schemes, and the door stayed shut. On the day the door opened, the text uses only a few words: God remembered Rachel. Remembering does not mean God had only just thought of her; it means that in His own time, God rose to act for her. Brothers and sisters, the thing you are waiting for — waiting for a child, for an opportunity, for a belated justice, for an unfair wage to be turned around — God has forgotten none of it. People's progress differs: Leah's was fast, Rachel's was slow, but God never writes His stories by comparison. Today, stop tormenting yourself with someone else's progress, and stop using striving to hurry God's hand. Quietly place your name back into His remembrance: He remembered Rachel, and He will remember you. The One who takes away disgrace is He; the One who adds is also He.

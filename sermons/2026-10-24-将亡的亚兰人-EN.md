## Introduction (00:00–06:00)

[Summary Layer] The main body of Deuteronomy's legal corpus runs from ch. 12 to ch. 25: dense articles, case upon case. At ch. 26 the style suddenly changes: no longer articles of "thou shalt not" and "thou shalt," but two texts that a man must speak aloud with his own mouth. The first (vv. 3–10) is spoken when the firstfruits are offered — a concentrated confession of redemption history. The second (vv. 13–15) is spoken every third year when the tithe has been fully paid — a self-declaration joined with a prayer for blessing. After the two liturgical texts, vv. 16–19 close the whole book's law with the word "this day": thou hast avouched the LORD this day to be thy God, and the LORD hath avouched thee this day to be his people. [Fact Layer] The chapter's position may be registered from the chapter sequence: ch. 25 closes with the command to blot out Amalek; ch. 27 moves at once into the covenant ceremony (the altar built, the stones set up, the blessings and curses read from the two mountains — its cross-reference registered only); ch. 26 stands between the body of the law and the covenant ceremony, the conclusion of the law's presentation and the preparation for the covenant — the words are first taught to the people, and then the people answer between the two mountains. [Theological Development Layer] The one thing in this chapter most worth stating first: the text does not only command what a man is to offer and how much he is to pay; it gives, word for word, what he is to say. Offering here is not only an action of the hand but also a discipline of the mouth; faith here is not only compliance in conduct but also the claiming of a narrative — who you are, where you come from, what your God has done for you: you must be able to say it, and say it before the priest, at the altar. This sermon's four sections unfold along these two liturgical texts and one covenant conclusion: Section 1, the basket of firstfruits (vv. 1–4); Section 2, a history recited by heart (vv. 5–11); Section 3, the account settled every third year and the prayer to look down (vv. 12–15); Section 4, this day, the two avowals (vv. 16–19). [Summary Layer] The common skeleton of the two liturgical texts is registered here in advance: each has prescribed words to be spoken (not an impromptu testimony), each has a prescribed audience (one before the priest, one a self-declaration before the LORD), and each has a prescribed occasion (one at the place where the name is set, one in a man's own city) — words, audience, and occasion are all fixed by the article itself; this is the form of this chapter's liturgical texts, and the observational frame shared by the four sections that follow.

## 1. The Basket of Firstfruits (Deut 26:1–4; 06:00–15:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 1 is the chapter's temporal premise, quoted in full from the KJV: "And it shall be, when thou art come in unto the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee for an inheritance, and possessest it, and dwellest therein;" Register the items one by one: first, in the Chinese base text the discourse quotation opens at this verse and does not close within it (for the quotation-mark ledger see Note 1) — the whole chapter's words stand within the quotation of Moses' discourse; in the KJV there are no quotation marks, as registered in the opening note. Second, the premise is threefold: come in, possess, dwell — every action of the liturgical texts takes place after these three things; the offering of firstfruits presupposes that the three stretches of history, exodus, entry into Canaan, and settled dwelling, have already come true. Third, the land is "the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee for an inheritance" — "inheritance" (the semantic field of the original naḥălâ is registered here only as to its general sense) is one of this book's key words: the land is not spoils seized, but an inheritance apportioned. [Fact Layer] Verse 2 is the checklist of actions, quoted in full: "That thou shalt take of the first of all the fruit of the earth, which thou shalt bring of thy land that the LORD thy God giveth thee, and shalt put it in a basket, and shalt go unto the place which the LORD thy God shall choose to place his name there." Register the items one by one: first, the "first" (rēʾšîṯ, H7225) is a noun meaning "first, beginning" — what is offered is not what is left over, but what comes first. Second, the measure is "take of the first" — the base text fixes no quantity; the article's centre of gravity is not amount but order: the first belongs to God first, and only then does the rest belong to oneself. Third, the vessel is a "basket" (ṭeneʾ, H2935) — an everyday farm implement; in the Chinese base text the word occurs three times in the chapter (vv. 2, 4, 10), while in the KJV the word "basket" occurs only at vv. 2 and 4, v. 10 reading "thou shalt set it" (see Note 2) — the vessel of the offering is not gold or silver, but the farmer's own basket. Fourth, the destination is "the place which the LORD thy God shall choose to place his name there" — the formula of ch. 12, "the place to place his name," reappears here (cross-reference registered only); the place is chosen by God, not fixed by the offerer. [Fact Layer] Verse 3 is the first spoken sentence, quoted in full: "And thou shalt go unto the priest that shall be in those days, and say unto him, I profess this day unto the LORD thy God, that I am come unto the country which the LORD sware unto our fathers for to give us." Register the items one by one: first, the words are spoken to "the priest that shall be in those days" — the offerer's words have an audience and a witness; they are not spoken to oneself. Second, "I profess" (the original nāgad in its Hiphil form higgadtî, H5046) carries the sense of a solemn declaration, a formal report; the 2010 version renders it with its own word for "avow" (separate entry, Note 3). Third, the declaration's content is a single sentence: I am come — to have come to that land is itself the fulfilment of the promise; the first thing the offerer says is not a report of the harvest but a confirmation of the promise. Fourth, "this day" appears here for the first time, four times in the chapter in all (vv. 3, 16, 17, 18; Note 2) — the last three all stand in the closing section, answering head to tail: the "this day" of offering the basket is the "this day" of the covenant. Fifth, the words "in those days" in "the priest that shall be in those days" may not be slipped past: at the time the liturgical text was promulgated, the people were still east of the Jordan and no priest was yet serving at the place of the name — with the words "in those days" the article hands the duty of hearing the recitation to the priest on duty in every generation: these words are not spoken once for Moses' generation to hear, but are to be spoken again by every offerer before the priest of his own day. The temporality of the liturgical text lies here: it presupposes worship as a standing practice, and it presupposes history retold generation by generation. [Fact Layer] Verse 4 is the priest's action, quoted in full: "And the priest shall take the basket out of thine hand, and set it down before the altar of the LORD thy God." — The basket passes through the priest's hand, from the offerer's hand to before the altar: the offering's handover is complete at this point; the action "set … before" occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 4, 10), once at the head of the section and once at its tail, framing the confession between them. [Theological Development Layer] Notice the order of this section: first the land, then the harvest; first the promise, then the coming; first the coming, and only then the basket. A man does not exchange a basket for land; because he has received the land, he has a basket to offer. The logic of thanksgiving always runs backwards: not first offer and then receive grace, but first receive grace and then offer; what the basket holds is less the produce than a piece of evidence — proof that the land really did yield a harvest, proof that the oath to the fathers really did come to ground. [Summary Layer] This section may be taken in four points: first, the offering's premise is the history of grace (v. 1); second, the offering's order is the firstfruits first (v. 2); third, the offering's language is the confirmation of the promise (v. 3); fourth, the offering's completion is the handover through another's hand (v. 4). [New Testament Reading Layer] This section's reading against the New Testament registers one vocabulary echo only, with no reading backwards: the concept of firstfruits is used in the New Testament for Christ's resurrection and for the sanctification of believers (the verse indexes of 1 Cor 15 and Rom 11 are registered only; this sermon has not verified them directly word for word, and quotes nothing) — the order of the Old Testament's offering of firstfruits (what is first belongs to God) and the New Testament's calling Christ "the firstfruits" answer each other structurally at a distance; so the reading layer registers it, and makes no ruling on the relation. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two items may be registered for this section: first, the offering of firstfruits was not unique to Israel among the agricultural societies of the ancient Near East; many cultures had the custom of offering the first of the harvest to a god, and this chapter's distinctiveness lies not in the custom itself but in the confession that must be recited at the offering (Section 2) — so the discussion layer registers it, without developing a comparative-religion argument. Second, the identification, in the era of Deuteronomy's composition, of "the place which the LORD thy God shall choose to place his name there" (Jerusalem, or Shechem and Mount Gerizim, among the readings) is debated in scholarship; this sermon registers it only, with no ruling.

## 2. A History Recited by Heart (Deut 26:5–11; 15:00–30:00)

[Fact Layer] The first half of v. 5 is the introduction to the recitation, quoted in full: "And thou shalt speak and say before the LORD thy God," — the original word for "speak" here is ʿānâ (H6030), whose basic sense is to answer, to respond: in form, this confession is an "answer" — man's answer to God's prior acts. From the second half of v. 5 the confession proper begins; in the Chinese base text it is set out under a single quotation mark; its opening sentence, quoted in full: "A Syrian ready to perish was my father, and he went down into Egypt, and sojourned there with a few, and became there a nation, great, mighty, and populous:" Register the items one by one: first, "my father" is singular — the whole confession is cast in the first person singular (alternating "my father" and "we," see below), and every offerer claims the whole people's history in his own person. Second, "a Syrian ready to perish": the original ʾărāmmî ʾōḇēḏ ʾāḇî, where ʾōḇēḏ is the participle of ʾābad (H6), its semantic field lying between perishing, being lost, and wandering; the base text renders it "ready to perish," the KJV reads "ready to perish," and the 2010 version renders it "a wandering Syrian" (separate entry, Note 3) — the difference between the renderings arises from the word form's semantic field; this sermon registers it only, with no ruling (the readings set side by side stand in this section's discussion layer). Third, in "he went down into Egypt, and sojourned there," the sojourning is gûr (H1481) — the fathers' status in Egypt was not master but sojourner. Fourth, the first sentence's arc: from one man "ready to perish" to "a nation, great, mighty, and populous" — the confession's first sentence already states the whole text's trajectory: from nothing to something, from few to many, from perishing to preserved. [Fact Layer] Verses 6–7 are the Egypt passage, quoted in full: "And the Egyptians evil entreated us, and afflicted us, and laid upon us hard bondage: And when we cried unto the LORD God of our fathers, the LORD heard our voice, and looked on our affliction, and our labour, and our oppression:" Register first the three verbs of suffering in v. 6: "evil entreated," "afflicted," "laid upon us hard bondage" — from attitude (evil entreatment) to act (affliction) to system (hard bondage laid on), the oppression is stated in the confession as three ascending grades; the confession neither whitewashes Egypt nor exaggerates: it arrays the facts by grade, like testimony presented in court. Register the items one by one: first, the person shifts here from "my father" to "us" — the offerer's confession joins the fathers to himself: Egypt's suffering is what "we" suffered. Second, the three nouns of suffering stand in a row: "affliction" (ʿŏnî, H6040), "labour" (ʿāmāl, H5999), "oppression" (the original laḥaṣ, in noun form with suffix, of the same root as the verb lāḥaṣ, H3905) — the base text's word at v. 7 is rendered one way, the 2010 version's another (separate entry, Note 3). Third, God's answering verbs are three: he "heard our voice" (the "cried" is ṣāʿaq, H6817) and "looked on" the affliction — the objects of the hearing and the seeing are both the people's condition; the confession here states the motive of the exodus very plainly: not that Israel was strong, but that Israel suffered; not that God was persuaded, but that God saw and heard. [Fact Layer] Verses 8–9 are the exodus and the entry into the land, quoted in full: "And the LORD brought us forth out of Egypt with a mighty hand, and with an outstretched arm, and with great terribleness, and with signs, and with wonders: And he hath brought us into this place, and hath given us this land, even a land that floweth with milk and honey." Register the items one by one: first, the nouns of God's action: "a mighty hand," "an outstretched arm," "great terribleness" (môrāʾ, H4172), "signs" (ʾôt, H226), "wonders" (môpēṯ, H4159) — this set of wording is Deuteronomy's fixed formula for recounting the exodus (its correspondence with such passages as 4:34 is registered by index only, not developed). Second, the subject of v. 8 in the base text is "he," while the 2010 version reads "the LORD" (separate entry, Note 3); the KJV reads "the LORD" here — the one place where the KJV's count of the divine name exceeds the Chinese ledger (Note 2). Third, the two "broughts": brought forth, brought in — redemption history is compressed in the confession into the two actions of out and in, and the forty wilderness years are leapt over in a single step within the confession (the confession's genre is an outline, not a chronicle; the summary layer may register it so). Fourth, "a land that floweth with milk and honey" occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 9, 15), once at the end of the confession's historical recital and once at the end of the prayer for blessing, framing the chapter's two liturgical texts. [Fact Layer] Verse 10 is the confession's landing point and, in the Chinese base text, the closing of its quotation, quoted in full: "And now, behold, I have brought the firstfruits of the land, which thou, O LORD, hast given me. And thou shalt set it before the LORD thy God, and worship before the LORD thy God:" Register the items one by one: first, the confession's last sentence comes back to the basket — the history is told first, and only then is the basket set down: recount the history first, offer the thing second; the order may not be reversed. Second, the single quotation mark closes here in the base text (opened at v. 5, closed at v. 10); the whole confession is one continuous quotation; the KJV, as registered, has no quotation marks, and the confession is carried by the "speak and say" of v. 5. Third, within this single verse the Chinese base text has both forms of the divine-name formula side by side (separate entry, Note 3) — the two base-text forms occur ten times and twice respectively in the chapter, their distribution at Note 2; in the KJV this verse reads "the LORD thy God" twice alike, with "O LORD" once besides, and the two-form distinction belongs to the CUNPSS side only. Fourth, "worship" is the confession's bodily language: the mouth has spoken, the heart has claimed, the body bows; the liturgical text is complete at this point. [Fact Layer] Verse 11 is the whole section's close, and the end of the Masoretic text's first large section (samekh at the end of this verse, Note 1), quoted in full: "And thou shalt rejoice in every good thing which the LORD thy God hath given unto thee, and unto thine house, thou, and the Levite, and the stranger that is among you." Register the items one by one: first, the company of joy is threefold: the offerer, the Levite, and the stranger — the sacrificial feast was never the offerer's to enjoy alone. Second, "rejoice" (śāmaḥ, H8055) occurs only here in the chapter, yet it is the whole section's emotional fixed point: the confession's end point is not solemnity but joy. Third, the ground of the joy is "every good thing which the LORD thy God hath given unto thee, and unto thine house" — the good things include land, harvest, and household; the offerer's basket is only a sample of the good things, not the whole. [Theological Development Layer] This section repays chewing over, layer by layer. First, this confession is prescribed — the offerer may not improvise, may not speak only of his own feelings; he must speak this text: beginning from the Syrian, through Egypt, the exodus, and the entry into the land, down to the basket. Faith here shows its historical character: Israel's faith is not faith in an idea but the claiming of a history; whenever an offerer offers his produce, he must first retell this history in his own mouth — history not retold evaporates; identity not declared dilutes. Second, this confession's narrative shape is "descent–ascent": its starting point is a man ready to perish, its end point is joy; and nowhere in between is there a boast of Israel's righteousness or strength — the suffering is real, the crying out is real, and the deliverance is all in God's verbs. The confession is at once a confession of sin and a confession of grace: confessing one's own readiness to perish, confessing that God does not perish. Third, the alternation of "I" and "we" is the confession's grammar: my father, we cried, I have brought the firstfruits — the people's history and the individual's offering are sewn together in one text: what you offer is not your story, but your people's story; and your people's story must be spoken by your own mouth before it becomes yours. Fourth, notice also the confession's omission: between the exodus and the entry into the land, the forty wilderness years occupy, within the confession, only the comma between "brought us forth" and "brought us into" — rebellion, failure, a whole generation falling, of these the confession says not a word. This is not amnesia: the confession's genre is an outline of thanksgiving, not a complete chronicle; its principle of selection is to record only God's acts and man's crying out, leaving man's sin for the law's warnings to state (this book's ch. 9 has its own passage for that confrontation). Read the confession according to its genre: the history it tells is true; only, the angle of its telling is the offerer's angle — a man standing before the altar, looking back along the road he came, can count first only grace. [Summary Layer] This section may be taken in five points: first, the confession has the form of an "answer" (v. 5); second, the confession starts from a man ready to perish (v. 5); third, the confession turns on God's hearing and seeing (vv. 6–7); fourth, the confession's outline is out and in (vv. 8–9); fifth, the confession lands on the offering and on joy (vv. 10–11). [New Testament Reading Layer] This layer registers two echoes verified directly word for word. First, the echo of "confession": Rom 10:9 (verified directly against the retrieved KJV): "That if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved." The Old Testament confession recounts history; the New Testament confession confesses the Lord; both are structured by the mouth's declaration and the heart's claiming — so the echo is registered, and no ruling is made on the relation. Second, the echo of "joy": 2 Cor 9:7 (verified directly against the retrieved KJV): "Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver." The offerer's rejoicing in 26:11 and the giver's cheerfulness in 2 Corinthians answer each other at a distance in the logic of giving: the Old Testament prescribes joy by liturgical text, the New Testament completes willingness by exhortation — registered only, with no reading backwards. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] The identifications of "the Syrian" are set side by side here under the discipline, with no ruling: first, the Jacob reading — "my father" refers to Jacob: in the patriarchal narratives Jacob sojourned for years in Paddan-aram, married Syrian wives, gained many sons, and was several times in peril (the passage index of Gen 25–31, registered only, not verified word for word), and his course corresponds at several points with both "Syrian" and "ready to perish"; it is one of the readings. Second, the Abraham reading — "my father" refers to Abraham, first of the patriarchs, whose narrative of coming out of Haran and dwelling in the land of Aram can serve as this reading's ground (index registered). Third, the collective-patriarchs reading — "the Syrian" is locked to no one man; it is a collective name for the patriarchs' sojourning identity, and "my father" is the patriarchal company in a personified singular. Fourth, the word-form reading — the reading of ʾōḇēḏ itself has two readings: read "ready to perish / on the verge of perishing" (the base text, the KJV) and read "wandering / exiled" (the 2010 version); the two readings arise from the same word form's breadth of sense, and the versions' difference lies here. Fifth, the traditional re-reading — the liturgical tradition of the Jewish Passover Haggadah has another reading, reading this sentence as a Syrian (Laban) plotting harm against my father; it belongs to the later liturgical tradition's re-reading and is registered as one of the traditional readings. The five readings stand side by side; this sermon makes no ruling — the pulpit's measure is this: the confession's point does not lie in who exactly the Syrian was, but in the fact that a man ready to perish became the ancestor of a great nation, and on this point the five readings do not differ.

## 3. The Account Settled Every Third Year, and the Prayer to Look Down (Deut 26:12–15; 30:00–42:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 12 is the second liturgical text's statement of occasion; in the Chinese base text a new set of double quotation marks opens at this verse; quoted in full: "When thou hast made an end of tithing all the tithes of thine increase the third year, which is the year of tithing, and hast given it unto the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow, that they may eat within thy gates, and be filled;" Register the items one by one: first, "the third year" — the tithe has here an account settled every three years: not a vague annual matter, but a clearing once every three years. Second, the original of "the year of tithing" is šənat hammaʿăśēr (maʿăśēr, H4643, "the year of the tithe") — a year is even named after the tithe, which shows this payment's weight in Israel's calendar. Third, the recipients are four: the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow — against the company of joy in Section 1's v. 11 (the Levite, the stranger), the fatherless and the widow are added here: the feast of joy and the distribution of relief meet in their audiences at this point. Fourth, the place is "within thy gates" — this tithe is not sent up to the central sanctuary; it is distributed on the spot to the weak of the city (the base text reads "in thy city"; the KJV reads "within thy gates"; entered separately). Fifth, the punctuation of the recipient list in the base text is a separate entry (Note 3). Sixth, "made an end of tithing" is settlement language: it is not divided out casually at the harvest, but gathered in full, totalled, and only then distributed — the account must first be closed before it can be handed over; and the self-declaration of v. 13 below ("I have brought away the hallowed things out of mine house") answers precisely to this "made an end": at one end the full taking-in, at the other the bringing-out — both ends of the account have their place. [Fact Layer] Verse 13 is the first half of the self-declaration; in the Chinese base text a single quotation mark opens at this verse; this verse carries the first half of the tithe prayer (vv. 13–15, quoted in full in this section verse by verse), quoted in full: "Then thou shalt say before the LORD thy God, I have brought away the hallowed things out of mine house, and also have given them unto the Levite, and unto the stranger, to the fatherless, and to the widow, according to all thy commandments which thou hast commanded me: I have not transgressed thy commandments, neither have I forgotten them." Register the items one by one: first, "the hallowed things" (haqqōdeš; qōdeš, H6944) — the tithe is here called a hallowed thing: its holiness lies not in its being a sacrifice, but in its belonging to God already and only passing through the people's hands in transfer. Second, "I have brought away … out of mine house": the original is the Piel form of bāʿar (H1197), whose sense is to remove, to clear away — hallowed things may not be kept stored in the house: what ought to be handed over becomes a debt if it stays in the house. Third, the self-declaration's form is a negative declaration of innocence: I have not transgressed, neither have I forgotten — the self-declarations of vv. 13–14 are both led by "not" (for the count, Note 2: the negatives stand in a row in this section, one of the chapter's rhetorical sights). [Fact Layer] Verse 14 is the second half of the self-declaration, quoted in full: "I have not eaten thereof in my mourning, neither have I taken away ought thereof for any unclean use, nor given ought thereof for the dead: but I have hearkened to the voice of the LORD my God, and have done according to all that thou hast commanded me." Register the items one by one: first, the threefold "not" has its own referent in each case: not eaten in mourning (the mourner stands in the special state of grief and of ritual), not taken away for any unclean use (ṭāmēʾ, H2931), nor given for the dead (the hallowed things are not used for funerary offerings). Second, the original of "in my mourning" is bəʾōnî, the suffixed form of ʾāwen (H205) — the word's dominant senses are trouble and vanity, while BDB, under the sense "sorrow, trouble," expressly lists this verse as one of that sense's isolated instances (verified directly from the BDB section of the Strong's entry page, Note 4). Third, the self-declaration closes with a positive sentence: "I have hearkened (šāmaʿ, H8085) to the voice of the LORD my God" — the ground of innocence is not boasting of no fault, but a hearkening that has its evidence. [Fact Layer] Verse 15 is the prayer of the whole liturgical text, and, in the Chinese base text, the place where both quotations close; quoted in full: "Look down from thy holy habitation, from heaven, and bless thy people Israel, and the land which thou hast given us, as thou swarest unto our fathers, a land that floweth with milk and honey." Register the items one by one: first, in the base text the single and the discourse double quotation marks close in succession at the end of this verse (single first, then double, Note 1) — the second liturgical text is fully spoken at this point; the KJV has no quotation marks, as registered. Second, "look down": the original is the Hiphil imperative of šāqap, hašqîpâ (H8259, verified directly from the BDB section of the Strong's entry page, BDB citing this verse as an instance) — to look down from a height, to look upon with favour. Third, "thy holy habitation": the original is məʿôn qodšəkā (māʿôn, H4583, verified directly from the entry page) — God's dwelling is called "thy holy habitation," answering with the same word "holy" to the "hallowed things" the people offered on earth: on earth hallowed things are offered; in heaven dwells the Holy One. Fourth, the prayer asks for only one thing: blessing — and the objects of the blessing are the people and the land, not the declarer himself: the man who declares his innocence opens his mouth to ask for the blessing of the many and of the land. Fifth, the Masoretic text's second large section closes at the end of this verse (samekh, Note 1). [Theological Development Layer] This section's theological centre of gravity is that it makes "giving" into an "account." The tithe here is not the alms of a kind heart but a settlement on schedule: once every three years, the recipients have names (the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow of the city), and after the delivery the man must still declare himself before God and invite God to audit the account — "I have not transgressed thy commandments, neither have I forgotten them" is not boasting, but presentation in court: a man first states his own account clearly, and only then asks heaven for blessing. The order of self-declaration and prayer for blessing may not be reversed: not first ask for blessing and then make up the account, but first clear the account and then ask for blessing. The sentence "neither have I forgotten them" carries special weight in this book: forgetting is the great sin Deuteronomy warns against again and again — to be full and then forget is the pathology written out in ch. 8 (within-book cross-reference, registered only); and here the offerer must declare in God's presence: thy commandments, I have not forgotten them. Memory here becomes virtue: if the account is remembered, the commandments can be kept; if the history can be recited by heart, the faith can be handed down — and this is the same matter, on its two faces, as the first liturgical text's recounting of history. And the content of the prayer for blessing shows this account's breadth: what is asked is not one's own full barns, but that the people and the land be blessed together — the man who clears his account has a wide heart. [Summary Layer] This section may be taken in four points: first, the tithe has its term, cleared once every three years (v. 12); second, the hallowed things are not stored, none are left in the house (v. 13); third, innocence has its bounds, in a threefold "not" (v. 14); fourth, the prayer for blessing has its direction, look down and bless (v. 15). [New Testament Reading Layer] This section's reading against the New Testament registers only: the self-declaration wording of giving and relief in the New Testament (such as the giving narrative of 2 Cor 8–9, whose verse index is registered only; where this sermon has not verified directly word for word, nothing is quoted); the directly verified 2 Cor 9:7 stands registered in Section 2, and this section does not develop it again. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] The relation among the tithe texts is set side by side under the discipline, with no ruling: first, Lev 27:30 (verified directly against the retrieved KJV): "And all the tithe of the land, whether of the seed of the land, or of the fruit of the tree, is the LORD's: it is holy unto the LORD." — this text states ownership and holiness: the tithe is the LORD's, it is holy. Second, Num 18:21 (verified directly against the retrieved KJV): "And, behold, I have given the children of Levi all the tenth in Israel for an inheritance, for their service which they serve, even the service of the tabernacle of the congregation." — this text states use and recipient: the tithe is given over to the Levites, as reward for their service. Third, this chapter's vv. 12–15 state the local distribution and self-declaration of the third year's payment: the recipients extend to the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow, with a declaration of innocence and a prayer for blessing attached. Fourth, Deut 14:22–29 contains another passage of this book on the tithe (passage index registered only, not verified word for word). The four texts' angles — ownership, recipient, term, use — fall across one another: whether they are the many faces of one payment, several payments under one heading, or different payments for different occasions is variously read in scholarship; this sermon sets the four texts side by side only, makes no harmonization, and gives no ruling.

## 4. This Day: The Two Avowals (Deut 26:16–19; 42:00–54:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 16 is the closing section's opening sentence; in the Chinese base text a new set of discourse quotation marks opens at this verse; quoted in full: "This day the LORD thy God hath commanded thee to do these statutes and judgments: thou shalt therefore keep and do them with all thine heart, and with all thy soul." Register the items one by one: first, "this day" returns here (after its first appearance at v. 3, vv. 16, 17, 18 have it in succession, Note 2) — every sentence of the closing section is nailed to "this day." Second, "statutes and judgments" is the collective name of all the articles of chs. 12–25 (ḥuqqîm, H2706; mišpāṭîm, H4941) — the regulations spoken over fourteen chapters are gathered up in one grasp here: what is commanded is "these statutes and judgments," the whole that has just been spoken. Third, the specification for keeping is "with all thine heart, and with all thy soul" (lēḇāḇ, H3824; nepeš, H5315), together with "keep and do" (the fixed pairing of šāmar, H8104, with ʿāśâ) — the heart's full measure is the law's first specification. [Fact Layer] Verse 17 is man's avowal of God, quoted in full: "Thou hast avouched the LORD this day to be thy God, and to walk in his ways, and to keep his statutes, and his commandments, and his judgments, and to hearken unto his voice:" Register the items one by one: first, the original of "avouched" is the Hiphil form of ʾāmar (H559), heʾĕmartā — this verb's ordinary sense is "to say," and its Hiphil here carries the sense of declare, avow, claim; the base text renders it "avow," the 2010 version with its own word for "avow" (separate entry, Note 3); the KJV renders it "avouched." Second, the content of man's avowal is fourfold: to avouch the LORD to be his own God; to walk in his ways (derekh, H1870); to keep his statutes, commandments, and judgments (the three names in a row; v. 17's order is statutes, commandments, judgments, differing from v. 16's two-name form "statutes and judgments" — separate entry at Note 3); and to hearken unto his voice (qôl, H6963). Third, the "promise" in this verse is man's promise to God in the Chinese base text — in the whole chapter the base-text word "promise" occurs four times: once for God's oath-promise to the fathers at v. 3, once for man's at v. 17, and twice for God's to man at vv. 18 and 19 (Note 2): in this chapter promising runs in both directions, and God's side toward man precedes man's side toward God (v. 3 first, v. 17 after); the order is registered as it stands. On the KJV side the word "promised" occurs only once, at v. 18; v. 3 reads "sware," v. 17 "avouched," and v. 19 "as he hath spoken" — the two ledgers are set side by side in Note 2, not merged. [Fact Layer] Verses 18–19 are God's avowal of man, quoted in full: "And the LORD hath avouched thee this day to be his peculiar people, as he hath promised thee, and that thou shouldest keep all his commandments; And to make thee high above all nations which he hath made, in praise, and in name, and in honour; and that thou mayest be an holy people unto the LORD thy God, as he hath spoken." Register the items one by one: first, the original of "avouched" is the same verb's Hiphil form, heʾĕmîrəkā — at v. 17 man avouches God with this word, at v. 18 God avouches man with this word: one word, two subjects; the chapter's structural hinge is here (whether this verb's Hiphil use occurs only in these two places in the whole Bible has not been searched out; registered as pending, Note 8). Second, the content of God's avowal: he avouches man to be "his peculiar people" — the original is ʿam səgullâ (səgullâ, H5459), whose sense is a specially owned possession, a treasured people; the Chinese base text renders only "his people," without bringing out səgullâ's "specially owned" sense, while the 2010 version reads "his treasured people" (separate entry, Note 3); the KJV reads "his peculiar people," in which "peculiar" retains the "specially owned" sense (see Note 5). The cross-reference of this wording with Deut 7:6 is, per the format, registered only and not developed into a comparative reading (Note 6). Third, God's avowal has two layers of purpose: that man keep all his commandments (v. 18) — God's avowal of man comes first, man's keeping after; the order forms an exact contrast with v. 17's human avowal (avowal first, keeping after). Fourth, the three names of exaltation: "praise" (təhillâ, H8416), "name" (šēm, H8034), "honour" (tipʾārâ, H8597, verified directly from the BDB section of the Strong's entry page, BDB citing this verse's three names in a row as an instance) — and the comparison is "high above all nations which he hath made": the exaltation is not self-elevation, but being set up, among the peoples he made, as one specially established. Fifth, the close is "an holy people" (ʿam-qāḏôš; qāḏôš, H6918) — in the base text the discourse quotation closes at the end of v. 19 (opened at v. 16, closed at v. 19); the chapter's words are fully spoken, and the book's law is fully spoken; the Masoretic text's third large section closes here, and the chapter closes (samekh at the end of v. 19, Note 1). Sixth, "as he hath promised thee" and "as he hath spoken" stand one in each of vv. 18 and 19, the two framing the content of God's avowal: to avouch man as his people is according to promise; to make man an holy people is also according to promise — every act of God in the closing section annotates its own source: not a sudden impulse, but the keeping of a covenant. Seventh, in "high above all nations which he hath made," the word "made" bounds the exaltation's range and character: all nations are made by him; to exalt Israel is not to discard the nations, but to set up one people specially among those he made, as a witness — and the three names (praise, name, honour) are all reputation in others' eyes: the fruit of the exaltation lies not in Israel's feeling itself honourable, but in what all nations can see. [Theological Development Layer] Verses 16–19 are only four verses, yet they are the hinge of the whole book of Deuteronomy. In the preceding twenty-five chapters God spoke many words through Moses; these four verses say: the words are finished, and now it is the two parties' turn to settle the relation in words. Notice the two-way structure of the "avouching": neither one-way allegiance nor one-way choosing, but a mutual claiming on the same day, in the same word — man avouches God as God, God avouches man as his people. And the two avowals' contents are not symmetrical: what man avouches is obligation (to walk in his ways, to keep his statutes, to hearken to his voice); what God avouches is identity and promise (his people, the ability to keep, exaltation, holiness). What man offers up is obedience; what God gives out is standing. Notice also the grace in the order: v. 18's "that thou shouldest keep all his commandments" — even the "being able to keep" is something God makes after he has avouched man; man's keeping (v. 17) and God's making (v. 18) overlap here, and covenant theology stands exactly at this overlap: obedience is man's promise, and also God's workmanship. [Summary Layer] This section may be taken in four points: first, the law is gathered up into "this day" (v. 16); second, man avouches God, with obedience as its content (v. 17); third, God avouches man, with standing as its content (v. 18); fourth, the avowal's end point is holiness and exaltation (v. 19). [New Testament Reading Layer] This layer registers one structural echo only, with no reading backwards: the correspondence of the avowal's two-way character within New Testament confession (such as Rom 10:9, whose quotation stands directly verified at Section 2's reading layer) belongs to the believer's one-way confessing of the Lord; the indexing of statements of God's avowal of man in the New Testament is registered only, with no quotation and no comparative ruling. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two items may be registered for this section: first, the two-way declaration form of vv. 17–18 has comparable features with the placement of the two parties' clauses against each other in ancient Near Eastern covenant documents, much discussed in scholarship; this sermon registers the formal observation only and builds no argument on it. Second, the distribution of the word səgullâ within the Pentateuch and the genealogy of the "specially his people" formula (including the cross-reference index of Deut 7:6 and Exod 19:5) is registered by index only, not developed — the comparative reading of this word is another sermon's subject; this sermon stops here.

## Conclusion (54:00–60:00)

[Theological Development Layer] Deuteronomy 26 gathers fourteen chapters of statutes and judgments into two texts to be spoken and one "this day." Looking back over the chapter's shape: the basket is small, the history is large; the tithe is an account, and after the account, blessing; the articles are many, and their gathering leaves only two avowals. What this chapter teaches us is a grammar of faith: first recount the history, then offer the thing — if your basket has no history, it is only a basket of produce; with this confession, it is evidence of the promise. First clear the account, then ask for blessing — if your self-declaration has no recipients' names, it is only a boast; with the names of the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow, it is an account presented in court. First be avouched, then keep — if your obedience has no God's avouching you first as his people, it is only drudgery; with the two-way avouching of "this day," it is a son's portion. May every basket of produce we offer carry a history that can be told; may every opening of the mouth to ask for blessing be preceded by an account that can be cleared; and may every "this day" hear those two avowals — thou avouchest him to be thy God, and he avouches thee to be his people.

## Devotional Extract

The one who offers the firstfruits does not begin by reporting the harvest; he begins by confessing his origin: "A Syrian ready to perish was my father." The starting point of faith is a man who was ready to perish, kept alive, and made the ancestor of a great nation. If there is a basket of produce in our hands to offer, do not count the harvest first; count the history first: who cried out, and who heard; whose hand was stretched out, and who was brought forth. Only when the confession has been spoken is the basket set down, and after it is set down there is still joy — "thou shalt rejoice in every good thing which the LORD thy God hath given unto thee, and unto thine house, thou, and the Levite, and the stranger that is among you." The feast of offering was never eaten by one man alone. On the account of the tithe cleared every third year, the recipients' names are written out plainly: the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow. Only the man whose account is cleared dares open his mouth to ask for blessing: "Look down from thy holy habitation, from heaven, and bless thy people Israel." And the chapter's final scene is the two avowals meeting on the same day: thou hast avouched the LORD this day to be thy God, and he hath avouched thee this day to be his peculiar people. What he asks is not his own full barns, but that the people and the land be blessed together — the man who clears his account has a wide heart. Today, speak your history aloud, clear your account clean, and then, with joy, set the basket down.

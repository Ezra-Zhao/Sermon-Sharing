## Summary (about 100 words)

Once the furniture is ready, God calls Aaron and his sons near to serve as priests, and commands holy garments to be made for them, for glory and for beauty. On the two shoulders, onyx stones engraved with the names of the twelve tribes; on the breast, the breastplate lying against the priest's heart; on the front of the mitre, a golden plate engraved HOLINESS TO THE LORD. This chapter is not about clothing; it is about bearing: the priest carries the people on his shoulders and holds them in his heart, and even on his forehead what is written is bearing.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

For several chapters we have remained within the blueprint on the mountain. First came the offering and the furniture: the ark, the mercy seat, the table, and the candlestick. Then the dwelling itself: the curtains, the tent covering, the boards, and the veil. The last chapter gave the altar of brass, the court and its gate, and a lamp burning from evening to morning. Piece by piece the tabernacle's furnishings were in place and its court enclosed; and now, in chapter 28, the text's camera turns for the first time from things to men: who is to stand among these furnishings? Who lights this lamp, who ministers at the altar, who passes through the hanging for the door and lifts the veil, standing between God and the people? All forty-three verses of this chapter concern the clothing of these men. A first-time reader may easily feel that this is nothing but a tailor's work-list — how much golden thread, how many rows of stones, how many bells — and ask what it has to do with faith. But read the chapter's first sentence together with its last: it opens with Aaron and his sons being brought near to serve in the priest's office, and it closes with their wearing these garments when they enter the tabernacle to minister, lest they bear iniquity and die. At one end, being brought near; at the other, lest they die — and between the two, more than forty verses about garments are in fact about what kind of men can draw near to a holy God, and how they are to stand before God for the people. The garments are outward; the office is inward. Through visible holy garments, the text puts the invisible bearing, piece by piece, before our eyes.

Let us first see the structure of the whole chapter clearly. Its verses fall roughly into five parts. The first is the general charge, verses 1 to 5: Aaron and his sons are brought near to serve in the priest's office; holy garments are to be made for Aaron, for glory and for beauty; the makers are named as wise-hearted men filled with the spirit of wisdom; and the list of the garments is given. The second is the ephod and the shoulder stones, verses 6 to 14: golden thread with thread of three colours and fine linen, of cunning work, and on the shoulder pieces two onyx stones, each engraved with six names of the tribes. The third is the breastplate and the Urim and the Thummim, verses 15 to 30: the breastplate of judgment, four rows of stones, twelve in all, engraved with the names of the twelve tribes, and within the breastplate the Urim and the Thummim. The fourth is the blue robe and the golden plate, verses 31 to 38: the robe all of blue, with golden bells and pomegranates alternating at its hem; and a plate of pure gold engraved HOLINESS TO THE LORD, fastened on the front of the mitre. The fifth is the remaining garments and the general rule, verses 39 to 43: the coat of fine linen, the mitre, the embroidered girdle, the garments for Aaron's sons and the linen breeches to cover their nakedness, and the perpetual statute. When the five parts have been read, a priest stands covered from shoulder to heart, from hem to forehead, by the holy garments — and measured, throughout, by his office.

In preaching this chapter, this manuscript still keeps the rules of this series, stated honestly at the outset. First, workmanship and materials are taken up only where they relate to the main line, never spread out item by item: the arrangement of the stones and the making of the chains and rings have their own detail in the text; this manuscript treats only the few strokes that carry the chapter's main line — the names on the shoulders, the names before the heart, the sound of the bells, and the engraving on the forehead; the rest is left for verse-by-verse study. Second, every symbolic use is marked by its level: what the text states directly — borne upon the shoulders for a memorial, the sound heard so that he die not — this manuscript preaches as direct statement; homiletical exposition — for example, that one side of the priest is bearing and the other remembering — is an induction this manuscript draws from the text's structure and marks on the spot; and any New Testament reading cited is marked as the New Testament's retrospect, never poured back into what this chapter meant at the time. Third, where the text does not make a matter clear, this manuscript says plainly that we do not know: of the Urim and the Thummim within the breastplate — what form they took and how they were used — the text says not a word in detail, and later literature mentions them only in scattered places; this manuscript will never fill that gap with conjecture. To say we do not know where we do not know is itself reverence for the text.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And take thou unto thee Aaron thy brother, and his sons with him, from among the children of Israel, that he may minister unto me in the priest's office, even Aaron, Nadab and Abihu, Eleazar and Ithamar, Aaron's sons. And thou shalt make holy garments for Aaron thy brother for glory and for beauty." (Exodus 28:1-2, KJV, excerpt)

"And Aaron shall bear the names of the children of Israel in the breastplate of judgment upon his heart, when he goeth in unto the holy place, for a memorial before the LORD continually." (Exodus 28:29, KJV)

"And it shall be upon Aaron to minister: and his sound shall be heard when he goeth in unto the holy place before the LORD, and when he cometh out, that he die not." (Exodus 28:35, KJV)

"And thou shalt make a plate of pure gold, and grave upon it, like the engravings of a signet, HOLINESS TO THE LORD." (Exodus 28:36, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You call Your servants to draw near, and You Yourself appoint even what they are to wear. Open our eyes today to see, in the shoulder stones, the breastplate, the golden bells, and the golden plate, what it is that You would have Your servants bear; open our hearts to understand what glory and beauty truly look like before You — not a dignity worn for men to see, but the people borne upon the shoulders and held in the heart. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The General Charge and the Ephod — For Glory and for Beauty, Borne upon the Shoulders for a Memorial (28:1-14)

The first part looks first at the general charge, then at the first of the holy garments. Verse 1 opens with men being brought near: "And take thou unto thee Aaron thy brother, and his sons with him, from among the children of Israel, that he may minister unto me in the priest's office." Notice this "take thou unto thee." In the tabernacle chapters the order has been repeated again and again: God first promises to dwell among the people, and only then do men pitch the dwelling according to the promise. It is the same here: the priest is not a man who squeezes his own way into the holy place; the priest is a man who is brought near. The office's first lesson is not ability but calling; it is not that a man chose for himself the place nearest to God, but that God set Aaron's house apart from among Israel and brought them near. This manuscript here marks one direct statement and one exposition: the text states directly that God commanded Moses to bring Aaron's house near, to serve in the priest's office; the exposition is the preacher's induction from it — all ministry begins in being called; a man is first brought before God, and only then can he stand before God for the people. Reverse this order and ministry becomes achievement; set it right and ministry forever carries gratitude, because being brought near is itself grace.

Verse 2 gives the purpose sentence for the holy garments, and the title of this whole sermon comes from here: "And thou shalt make holy garments for Aaron thy brother for glory and for beauty." For glory and for beauty, in the original lekavod uletif'eret (לְכָבוֹד וּלְתִפְאָרֶת, H3519 + H8597) [1]: kavod is glory, honour, weight; tif'eret is beauty, splendour, glory. The two words stand side by side, and the text offers no further explanation; this manuscript states them by their plain word-sense: the purpose of these garments is glory and beauty. But ask at once: glory for whose eyes? Beauty for whose delight? When Aaron wore the holy garments he was mostly inside the holy place, while the people stood outside in the court and could not see them in full; this glory and beauty are not worn first for the applause of the crowd — they are offered before God. The priest stands between God and men, and the garment on his body must first answer to the One before whom he stands. As a homiletical exposition, marked as such: in Scripture, glory always carries the sense of weight; it is never a flimsy prettiness. And beauty is never mere piling up; it is every material in its appointed place, workmanship so fine that the text is willing to spend a whole chapter appointing it. Men use the finest golden thread, the finest workmanship, and the finest stones on these garments, not because God lacks display, but because the ministering man reminds himself by this garment: the place where I stand is holy, and the work I do is weighty.

Verse 3 names the makers of the garments — a sentence very easily skipped, and very important: "And thou shalt speak unto all that are wise hearted, whom I have filled with the spirit of wisdom, that they may make Aaron's garments to consecrate him, that he may minister unto me in the priest's office." Those who make the holy garments are no tailors picked up at random; they are wise-hearted men, filled with the spirit of wisdom. Handwork, in this chapter, is not an unspiritual chore outside spiritual things: hands that weave thread, engrave stones, and set gold are likewise filled with the Spirit. This manuscript here offers a forward look and marks it as such: this answers exactly to Bezalel in chapter 31 — there God says, "See, I have called by name Bezaleel… and I have filled him with the spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship." This manuscript cites chapter 31 as an intra-book intertext looking forward, and draws the line clearly: here in chapter 28 the text says only that the makers of the garments are filled with the spirit of wisdom; it is chapter 31 that gives the name and the fuller list. Read together, the two passages show us that from blueprint to finished garment, God not only showed the pattern — the craftsmen who would do the work with their hands were also prepared by Him personally, and filled by Him personally with His Spirit. Brothers and sisters, this is a lesson for us today as well: in the church's work, it is not only what is spoken from the front and prayed aloud that counts as spiritual; the work done behind the scenes, with the hands — weaving, mending, sewing — if it comes from God-given wisdom and the Spirit's filling, is holy work alike. The garment the priest wears is glorious; the hands that make the garment are glorious too.

Verse 4 gives the list of the garments — this chapter's table of contents: "And these are the garments which they shall make; a breastplate, and an ephod, and a robe, and a broidered coat, a mitre, and a girdle." And the text closes by saying that they shall make these holy garments for Aaron and his sons, that he may minister in the priest's office. The list has an order: first the breastplate and the ephod, then the robe and the coat, then the mitre and the girdle — the text names the weightiest pieces first, and the rest unfold one by one in what follows. This manuscript takes only the structure of this list for one use, and marks it: the holy garments are one whole; lacking any one piece, the dressing is incomplete. Whatever single piece we meet later, we must not read it as an isolated ornament — where it appears, and to what it is fastened, binds it to the whole body. Verse 5 closes the general charge: "And they shall take gold, and blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine linen." The list of materials answers, across the chapters, to the list of the offering in chapter 25: the gold and thread the people willingly offered have here been woven into garments. The offering did not vanish into a storehouse; the offering was worn upon the body of the ministering man.

After the general charge, the first garment treated in detail is the ephod, verses 6 to 14. Ephod, in the original 'ephod (אֵפוֹד, H646) [2], is the outermost and most splendid of the priestly garments, a kind of vest; this chapter and chapter 39 are the main sources for how it was made. The first sentence of its making is once more that set of materials: "And they shall make the ephod of gold, of blue, and of purple, and of scarlet, and fine twined linen, with cunning work." Note how the golden thread was made: according to the later text of this book and the usual exegetical understanding, the gold was beaten into thin plates and cut into wires, to work it in among the coloured threads — this manuscript marks this as a supplement from the intra-book intertext of Exodus 39:3; chapter 28, verse 6 says only that gold is to be used, and the process of beating gold into thread is given in chapter 39. The gold is not pasted on; it is woven in. Glory is not a layer of paint added outside; it is woven into the warp and woof of the whole garment. The ephod has two shoulder pieces joined at its two edges, so that it becomes one; and it has a curious girdle of the same work and the same materials, binding the garment upon the body — bound up, the posture of a man ready to minister, not loosely draped. As a homiletical use, marked as such: splendid as the holy garments are, they are work clothes; their splendour always faces toward ministry, never toward sitting idle.

The ephod's focal point is on the shoulders. Verses 9 to 12: "And thou shalt take two onyx stones, and grave on them the names of the children of Israel: six of their names on one stone, and the other six names of the rest on the other stone, according to their birth." The engraving is "with the work of an engraver in stone, like the engravings of a signet," and the two stones are set in ouches of gold and "put… on the shoulders of the ephod for stones of memorial unto the children of Israel: and Aaron shall bear their names before the LORD upon his two shoulders for a memorial." Onyx, in the original shoham, is a gemstone name recurring in this chapter; its exact identification is also discussed among scholars, and this manuscript follows the received translation without issuing a mineralogical ruling. What matters is the three things the text states directly. First, the names are engraved, not written: writing fades; engraving cuts the names into stone, not easily worn away. Second, the order is according to their birth: the twelve tribes are remembered before God not by strength and not by merit, but in the order of their birth — in this matter of being remembered, no tribe stands ahead of another by its own ability. Third, the place is upon the two shoulders: Aaron is to bear their names upon his two shoulders. Bear is the text's own verb, and this manuscript states it directly: the priest enters the holy place carrying the whole of Israel on his shoulders; he does not go in alone to enjoy a blessing — he goes in laden with the names of the twelve tribes, to stand before God. The shoulder is the part of a man that carries loads; it is not a place for decoration. Two stones press upon the two shoulders — the weight is real, and so is the reminder: the first shape of the office is a burden upon the shoulders.

Verses 13 and 14 close the account of the ephod: ouches of gold are made, and two chains of pure gold, like cords of wreathen work, fastening shoulder piece and breastplate together, and holding firmly in place the breastplate of the next section. This manuscript takes only the structure of this stroke: the pieces of the holy garments are joined to one another; the burden on the shoulders and the memorial before the heart will presently be linked into one whole by golden chains — bearing is never each part minding its own business; shoulder and heart are two sides of one and the same thing. That sentence is exposition, and it will be brought to ground in the next part, on the breastplate.


## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Breastplate of Judgment — Held before the Heart, and the Urim and the Thummim of Which We Do Not Know (28:15-30)

The second part treats the breastplate, verses 15 to 30 — the longest section of the whole chapter. Verse 15 opens by naming it: "And thou shalt make the breastplate of judgment with cunning work; after the work of the ephod thou shalt make it." Breastplate of judgment, in the original choshen mishpat (חֹשֶׁן מִשְׁפָּט, H2833 + H4941) [3]: choshen is the breastplate; mishpat is judgment, decision, righteous ruling. The name itself states the breastplate's office: it is not merely a plaque of memorial; it has to do with the priest's seeking judgment before God on the people's behalf. Its making is of the same materials and the same workmanship as the ephod — gold, thread of three colours, and fine linen, the work of the cunning craftsman. Its shape is foursquare, doubled over, a span its length and a span its breadth — made, that is, into a kind of pouch that can hold something, a point that becomes clear only at the end of this section, when the Urim and the Thummim are put into it. The breastplate and the ephod share one and the same splendour, because bearing and judgment are one office: the man who carries the people on his shoulders is the man fit to seek counsel for them before his heart; first the burden, then the decision. This sentence is exposition drawn from the order of the two sections, and is marked here as such.

The most striking thing on the breastplate is its stones. Verses 17 to 21: four rows of stones are to be set in it. The first row: a sardius, a topaz, and a carbuncle; the second row: an emerald, a sapphire, and a diamond; the third row: a ligure, an agate, and an amethyst; the fourth row: a beryl, and an onyx, and a jasper — so the KJV names them; between the ancient names of precious stones and modern mineral names an exact one-to-one correspondence can hardly be fixed, and this manuscript issues no dogmatic ruling, holding only to what the text can state directly: four rows, three stones in each row, twelve stones in all, every one set in an ouche of gold; and these stones are engraved with the names of the twelve sons of Israel, "like the engravings of a signet," each stone bearing one name, representing the twelve tribes. Read this section alongside the shoulder stones and the contrast is perfectly clear: on the shoulders, two stones engraved with twelve names; before the heart, twelve stones, one name upon each. What the shoulders bear, they bear together — the whole; what the heart holds, it holds one by one. As structural exposition, marked as such: the priest's bearing of the people is written in two scripts. The shoulders carry the whole — all Israel, not one tribe missing; the heart remembers the individual — every tribe has its own name and its own stone, not swallowed up by the whole. Remembrance before God is never a vague crowd of people; it is name by name, engraved one by one. And the church's lesson in shepherding lies here too: we are both to bear the whole company and to remember every name; bear only the whole and individuals are easily lost; remember only individuals and no bearing is formed.

The fastening of the breastplate is given in great detail in verses 22 to 28: chains of pure gold, rings of gold at the breastplate's four corners, rings on the shoulder pieces of the ephod, the rings at the front, and a lace of blue binding the breastplate to the curious girdle of the ephod. This manuscript does not spread out the detail ring by ring; it takes only the sentence the text stresses again and again: that the breastplate rest close upon the ephod and not be loosed from it. Why is it bound so tightly? Verse 29 gives the answer, and it is the most moving sentence in the whole chapter: "And Aaron shall bear the names of the children of Israel in the breastplate of judgment upon his heart, when he goeth in unto the holy place, for a memorial before the LORD continually." Upon his heart — lying, that is, against his very heart. The place was not chosen at random: the shoulder stones are upon the shoulders, and they speak of bearing; the breastplate is before the heart, and it speaks of remembrance. Every time Aaron enters the holy place, the names of the twelve tribes press against his breast; when he stands before God, the people's names are nearest to his heart. Here this manuscript brings to ground what the first part promised, and marks it as an exposition and induction: one side of the priest is the shoulder, the other side is the heart. The shoulder is the burden — the weight of responsibility; the heart is remembrance — the forgetting of not one single person. Use the shoulder without the heart, and bearing becomes mere function; use the heart without the shoulder, and remembrance becomes empty words. The holy garments engrave both upon stone and bind both upon the body, so that the ministering man remembers daily, with his own body: those you bear, you keep upon your heart; those you remember, you carry upon your shoulders.

Verse 30 is the last verse of this section, and the verse in the whole chapter that most requires honesty: "And thou shalt put in the breastplate of judgment the Urim and the Thummim; and they shall be upon Aaron's heart, when he goeth in before the LORD: and Aaron shall bear the judgment of the children of Israel upon his heart before the LORD continually." Urim and Thummim, in the original 'urim vetummim (אוּרִים וְתֻמִּים, H224 + H8550) [4]: 'urim is, on the word's face, connected with light; tummim, on the word's face, with perfection and completeness — and this manuscript marks the matter only at that level, the level of the words themselves. What form they actually took — two stones, two lots, or some other objects — and how a judgment was obtained in their use — by lot, by shining, or in some other way — of all this the text here says not one word. This manuscript must state it as it stands: the form and the use of the Urim and the Thummim are not set out in the text, and we do not know. The places where Scripture later mentions them are also extremely scattered: Leviticus chapter 8 records that when Moses arrayed Aaron he put the Urim and the Thummim in the breastplate; Numbers chapter 27 records that Eleazar was to ask counsel for Joshua before the LORD by the judgment of the Urim; Deuteronomy chapter 33, speaking of the tribe of Levi, mentions "thy Thummim and thy Urim"; in 1 Samuel chapter 28 Saul says that the LORD answered him no more, "neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets"; and Ezra and Nehemiah both record that certain things were to wait "till there stood up a priest with Urim and with Thummim" (Ezra 2:63; Nehemiah 7:65, cited here as intertexts within and beyond this book). Set all these scattered records side by side, and what can still be stated directly is only this: the Urim and the Thummim were objects placed within the breastplate, by which the high priest sought judgment before God on the people's behalf. What they looked like and how they were used, the Bible has left us no manual.

We do not know — so we say we do not know. And this sentence must be spoken without embarrassment. This manuscript here offers a direct exposition and marks it as such: the ages have produced many conjectures — some say two flat stones, positive and negative; some say lots that were drawn; some guess from the word-face at stones that shone. Conjecture may be kept on file as a possibility for reference; it must never be preached as a settled conclusion, still less turned into an invented mechanism palmed off as the text. Scripture's silence at this point is not an oversight left for us to exploit; it is a boundary set for us to keep: the authority of judgment belongs to God; the priest's office is to bear the people before his heart and to bring the question before God; and as for the way in which God gives His judgment, and the time at which He gives it — that is not in the priest's hand, nor in ours. The fault men most easily fall into today is to make the seeking of God into a technique, as though mastering the method guaranteed the answer; this very blank space within the breastplate dismantles that fantasy: what the priest can do is bear and remember; judgment remains, always, God's affair. Keep this boundary, and this section on the breastplate has been preached rightly — its greatest splendour is not its twelve stones, but the honest heart among them: bring the people before God, and leave the answer to God.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Blue Robe, the Golden Bells, and the Plate — A Sound That Can Be Heard, and Engraved Words of Bearing upon the Forehead (28:31-43)

The third part looks at the last three groups: the blue robe, the golden plate, and the general rule for the remaining garments. Verse 31: "And thou shalt make the robe of the ephod all of blue." The robe is worn beneath the ephod, blue throughout, of one colour, with no thread of three colours and no gold-thread embroidery — in the midst of all this splendour, this one garment is strikingly plain. This manuscript marks this as a structural observation: the splendour of the holy garments does not heap every piece to the fullest; gold thread and three colours are used on the ephod and the breastplate, pure blue is left for the robe; each has its own place, and only so does splendour become a whole. If every piece fought to catch the eye, the result would not be a garment at all. Of the robe's making, only two points are taken. The first is the hole for the head: the robe is to have a hole in its top, with a woven binding round about the hole, "as the hole of an habergeon," strong, "that it be not rent." The collar is the place on a whole robe that suffers the most pulling — in and out, on and off, it is always the first to give way; and the text commands precisely this place to be bound and strengthened, so that it be not rent. As a homiletical use, marked as such: the garments of ministry must be hard-wearing, able to endure the pulling of day after day; and when the years of service have grown long, what most easily tears is often the place of opening — the mouth that speaks, the door that goes in and out — and it is exactly these places that must be strengthened first. This use does not alter the text's plain sense: the text commands a way of weaving; the preacher, from the weaving, reminds his hearers to guard their own mouths.

The second point is the hem — this section's focal point. Verses 33 to 35: "And beneath upon the hem of it thou shalt make pomegranates of blue, and of purple, and of scarlet, round about the hem thereof; and bells of gold between them round about: a golden bell and a pomegranate, a golden bell and a pomegranate, upon the hem of the robe round about." The pomegranates are woven ornaments; the golden bells are instruments that sound; the two alternate, all the way round. Aaron is to wear this robe when he ministers, and verse 35 gives the purpose of this circle of bells, with the text's causation written out perfectly clearly: "And it shall be upon Aaron to minister: and his sound shall be heard when he goeth in unto the holy place before the LORD, and when he cometh out, that he die not." This manuscript preaches it as direct statement, no more and no less: the sound is heard, and he does not die — the text joins hearing and not dying directly together, and this manuscript stops there; it does not draw the sound of the bells out into other meanings. Why does being heard mean not dying? The text gives no further mechanism, and this manuscript will not invent a reason for it. What can be described directly is this picture: the high priest passes through the hanging for the door into the holy place; the people standing outside cannot see him; they can only listen. While the bells sound, the man within is ministering according to the rule, alive and serving; the sound of the bells is the only movement those outside have to go by. In a place that cannot be seen, sound becomes the signal of life. This manuscript marks this sentence as a direct description drawn from the scene, not a new doctrine: the ministry in the holy place cannot be seen by men, yet it can be heard; God would not have His servant's ministry vanish soundlessly behind the veil, but proceed with a sound, step by step, before Him and also in the people's ears. Pomegranate and golden bell alternate — one beautiful to see, one beautiful to hear — and beauty and bearing meet once more here at the hem: the pomegranate is beauty, the bell is the sign; and the man who wears this robe has even the sound of his walking made part of his office.

Verses 36 to 38 give the golden plate, the last of the chapter's great pieces: "And thou shalt make a plate of pure gold, and grave upon it, like the engravings of a signet, HOLINESS TO THE LORD. And thou shalt put it on a blue lace, that it may be upon the mitre; upon the forefront of the mitre it shall be. And it shall be upon Aaron's forehead." Plate, in the original tsits (צִיץ, H6731) [5], whose base sense includes shining and a flower; in this chapter it denotes specifically this plate of pure gold at the front of the mitre. The words engraved on the plate are qodesh laYHWH (קֹדֶשׁ לַיהוָה, H6944 + H3068) [6], meaning HOLINESS TO THE LORD — qodesh is holiness, that which is set apart as holy; laYHWH is "unto the LORD." Fewer than ten words in English, engraved on pure gold, hung at the highest and most conspicuous place on a man: his forehead. A man lifts his head, and the first thing seen is this sentence. See the place clearly: the priest's body is full of memorial — upon his shoulders, before his heart — and when we reach his forehead, what is written is not his name and not his title, but HOLINESS TO THE LORD. At the most splendid place on these garments, what is declared is not the wearer's glory, but this God's holiness. This manuscript states the contrast directly: the garments are for glory and for beauty, yet when the glory reaches the forehead, what is written there is being set apart as holy unto God — the summit of beauty is to return the glory to God, not to keep it upon one's own head.

Verse 38 has a second half, and its weight is heavier still: "that Aaron may bear the iniquity of the holy things, which the children of Israel shall hallow in all their holy gifts; and it shall always be upon his forehead, that they may be accepted before the LORD." The text states it directly: in the holy things the people offer — the gifts they hallow and present to the LORD — if there is trespass, uncleanness, or shortcoming, this iniquity Aaron is to bear. Bear — the same word again. From shoulder stones to breastplate, bearing meant bearing the names of the people; here at the golden plate, bearing goes one layer deeper: bearing the people's iniquity in the holy things. This manuscript offers an induction and marks it as such: at its deepest, the priestly office is not glory but bearing in another's place — he stands between God and men, at once bringing the people before God and taking the people's shortcomings upon himself. The golden plate is always upon his forehead — not a permanent ornament, but a permanent bearing: there is no day on which he may take this plate off and enjoy only the splendour of the garments without undertaking their weight. When we read this verse today, we should read it quietly: already in the old covenant's order of worship, God had prepared such a man to stand in the middle, with holiness written on his forehead and iniquity borne upon his shoulders. How this picture is fulfilled in Christ, this manuscript marks only with one sentence of the New Testament's retrospect here, leaving its unfolding to preaching elsewhere: Hebrews calls the Lord Jesus our great high priest — that is the New Testament author's exposition, not this chapter's plain sense; this chapter stays honestly with Aaron first, and preaches the bearing in full.

The last section, verses 39 to 43, gives the remaining garments and the general rule: the coat of fine linen is to be embroidered, the mitre made of fine linen, and the girdle of needlework; and for Aaron's sons, coats, girdles, and bonnets are to be made, "for glory and for beauty" — note that the purpose sentence of the general charge, for glory and for beauty, appears once more on the garments of the sons: this purpose belongs not to the high priest only, but to all who minister. Then come the linen breeches, to cover their nakedness, reaching from the loins to the thighs. Verses 42 and 43 close the whole chapter, in a tone answering to its opening: Aaron and his sons shall wear them when they come into the tabernacle of the congregation, or when they come near unto the altar to minister in the holy place, "that they bear not iniquity, and die: it shall be a statute for ever unto him and his seed after him." The chapter on garments ends, at the last, upon covering and upon a statute: the least conspicuous piece of all still concerns life and death. As a closing induction for the chapter, marked as such: from the most splendid, ephod and breastplate, to the plainest, the linen breeches, not one garment of this chapter is an optional decoration; every one is bound to the ministry, and every one is tied to the same reminder — drawing near to a holy God must be according to the pattern He has appointed. Splendour has its reason, plainness has its place, and what becomes a statute is this whole order together.


## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 28. Remember first the sentence of the general charge: for glory and for beauty — the purpose of the holy garments faces God first; they are not worn for men to see. Remember next the two onyx stones on the shoulders: the names of the twelve tribes, engraved in the order of their birth upon stone, borne upon the priest's two shoulders, a memorial as he enters the holy place — the shape of bearing is a burden upon the shoulders. Remember also the breastplate before the heart: twelve stones, one name upon each, lying against the heart for a continual memorial; and within the breastplate, the Urim and the Thummim, whose form and use the text does not tell us — so we honestly say we do not know, and leave judgment to God. Remember again the golden bells at the hem: the sound shall be heard, that he die not — in a place that cannot be seen, sound is the signal of life. Remember last the golden plate upon the forehead: HOLINESS TO THE LORD, written at the most conspicuous place, speaks in fact of bearing the iniquity of the holy things — the summit of glory is to take the people up upon oneself and to return the glory to God. May we learn today to put on our own office in the same way: to bear those who should be borne, to hold in remembrance the names that should be remembered, to keep the boundary of what we do not know, and to walk with a sound worthy of our calling and with the mark of holiness upon us.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You for calling us to draw near, and for showing us, piece by piece, what bearing looks like. Engrave the names of Your people upon our shoulders, and upon our hearts as well, so that we choose neither by weight nor by distance, but remember them one by one; forgive us for loving the outward look of beauty while fearing the weight of bearing, and teach us Aaron's shoulders and Aaron's heart before we envy Aaron's garments. May the words HOLINESS TO THE LORD be engraved upon our foreheads too, and become the mark of all we do. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And thou shalt make holy garments for Aaron thy brother for glory and for beauty." (Exodus 28:2, KJV) "And Aaron shall bear their names before the LORD upon his two shoulders for a memorial." (Exodus 28:12, KJV)

Meditate today on three places on the priest's body. The first is the shoulders: two stones engraved with twelve names rest upon them — not ornaments, but a burden. When we intercede for our family and for our brothers and sisters, we too bear their names upon our shoulders, day after day and year after year, not choosing by their strength but bearing them one by one as they have been entrusted to us. The second is the heart: the breastplate lies against the heart, and the names are nearest to it. Those we remember should not remain only on a list; they should be upon the heart — ask the Lord to keep our remembrance of people from becoming a mere form, so that when a name is mentioned, the heart is warm. The third is the forehead: the golden plate, engraved "HOLINESS TO THE LORD," hangs in the most conspicuous place, and yet it speaks of bearing. Let this sentence stand before us today, at home and at work: what I do belongs to the Lord and is set apart as holy. When glory comes, do not keep it on your own head; when the burden comes, do not pass it to another. And since the Lord has entrusted people to us, ask Him for Aaron's shoulders and Aaron's heart, and for hands willing to bear, so that in ordinary days we bring those we love, one by one, before Him — borne upon the shoulders, held in the heart, kept before His throne of grace and not let go, until He Himself gives the judgment.

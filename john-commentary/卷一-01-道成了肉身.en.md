# Volume One · Chapter 1 — God Is Not a Concept; He Has Come

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «卷一 · 第 1 篇　神不是一个概念，祂来过».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

Guangyi Zhao

(John 1:1-18)

---

The other three Gospels tell the story of Jesus, mostly beginning with His birth, His baptism, or His genealogy. John does not do that. He pushes the camera back, in a single move, to before time began: first he makes clear who this One really is, and only then does he let Him step into the human world. This passage has only eighteen verses, yet it is the door to the whole book. If you do not understand the door, the stories that follow are easy to read shallowly; if you do understand the door, you will discover that from his very first sentence John is answering a question many people have asked: How far is God, really, from me?

This is the first chapter of the whole volume, so let us not hurry on our way just yet. Let us read these eighteen verses slowly, and see how John tells of an invisible God as a person who can be met.

## I. The Scripture Text

John's opening is almost identical to the first page of the Bible:

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through Him, and without Him nothing was made that was made. In Him was life, and the life was the light of men. And the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it." (John 1:1-5, NKJV)

The words "in the beginning" are the very "in the beginning" at the opening of Genesis. Genesis says: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, NKJV) John deliberately uses the same starting point, meaning: the story of Jesus does not begin in Bethlehem; before heaven and earth existed, He already was.

In the middle John inserts a man named John — John the Baptist. He came to bear witness of the Light; he himself was not that Light (1:6-8). Then the focus returns to that One, and speaks a plain truth that makes the heart a little uncomfortable:

> "He was in the world, and the world was made through Him, and the world did not know Him. He came to His own, and His own did not receive Him. But as many as received Him, to them He gave the right to become children of God, to those who believe in His name" (John 1:10-12, NKJV)

The weightiest sentence in the whole passage is in verse 14, and it is also where the title of this chapter comes from:

> "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth." (John 1:14, NKJV)

Finally, John states the matter in full, yet leaves an opening:

> "No one has seen God at any time. The only begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, He has declared Him." (John 1:18, NKJV)

There are three words to clarify here first; once they are clear, the rest will flow.

One is "Word." In the Greek original this word is pronounced Logos, and its meaning is not merely "a sentence." In Greek usage, it refers to the intelligible principle, order, and rationality behind the universe; in Jewish tradition, when God's word goes forth, the thing is accomplished — in Genesis, God said there should be light, and there was light — and Proverbs also writes of "Wisdom" as though she were a companion who was with God. The Lü Zhenzhong Translation also renders it here as "Word" [Dao]. John uses this word that everyone regards as lofty, yet its final sentence lands on "became flesh": that highest principle is not a set of doctrines; it is a person of flesh and blood.

Another is "dwelt." The "dwelt" in verse 14 has in the original the meaning of "pitching a tent, dwelling in the midst." Those who have read the Old Testament will recall that God once commanded Israel to make a tabernacle, and His glory dwelt in the midst of the people. By using this word, John is saying that when Jesus came into the human world, it was as though God had pitched His tent on our street and moved into our everyday life — not looking in from afar for a moment and then leaving.

There is also "declared." The "declared Him" in verse 18 has in the original the meaning of "explaining, recounting clearly." God cannot be seen or touched; human beings can only guess. John says there is no need to guess anymore: there is One who has come from the Father, who has already told us of Him, and His own life is that explanation.

## II. Exposition

First look at John's way of writing. He does not begin by announcing Jesus' name; instead he first calls Him "the Word," pushing forward layer by layer: the Word was there in the beginning, the Word was with God, the Word was God, all things were made through Him. Only when the reader's heart holds enough reverence for this One does he reveal the answer in verse 14: this Word became flesh. Not until verse 17 does the name formally appear: grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. This order is deliberate. John was afraid we would treat Jesus only as a good teacher, a moral example, so he first tells of His origin to the very top, and then says that He entered the human world.

"The Word was with God, and the Word was God" — these two sentences must be read together. Read only the first half, and it is easy to treat the Word as an assistant beside God; read only the second half, and it is easy to treat Him as merely another name for God, with no distinction of person. John places the two sentences side by side: He has a real relationship with God, intimately present with Him, and He Himself is fully God. Later believers used a word like "Trinity" to sum up this relationship, and its source lies in passages like this. For readers who are seeking, it is enough here to remember one plain fact: in John's writing, Jesus is not an ordinary person sent by God to deliver a message; He is God Himself who has come.

"All things were made through Him" also attributes creation to Him. This does not conflict with Genesis. In Genesis God creates the world by His word; John says that word is this Word. Paul says something similar elsewhere, that all things were created through Him (see Colossians 1:16). In this way, the relationship between Jesus and this world is not that of a tourist and a scenic spot: the world exists through Him, and He came back to a place He Himself made. Only then does that sentence in verse 10, "the world did not know Him," read as so piercing to the heart — the Creator standing in the midst of created beings, yet treated as a stranger.

Light and darkness are the second thread in this passage. "And the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it." This "comprehend" also carries within it the meaning of "overcome, understand"; the rendering of the Lü Zhenzhong Translation may be taken as a reference. Darkness did two things to the light: it did not understand it, and it could not overcome it. John writes along this thread through the whole book: some people walk toward the light, and some hide from the light. John the Baptist's position must also be seen clearly: he repeatedly stresses that he himself is not the Light, but only bears witness of the Light (1:7-8). The duty of a witness is to guide people to the Light, not to make people stop at himself. This is a lesson every character in the rest of the book is learning, and this book itself is the same: it bears witness; it does not replace that One.

Verses 11 and 12 form a pair: on one side, "His own did not receive Him"; on the other, "as many as received Him, to them He gave the right to become children of God." Here John states the threshold as very low, and the outcome as very great. The threshold is low because the words he uses are "receive" and "believe," not passing an examination, not first perfecting one's conduct; the outcome is great because the return for receiving is not a membership card, but a new identity — to become children of God. Verse 13 immediately adds a sentence: such people were "born of God," not earned by bloodline, origin, or human will. In other words, entering God's household does not depend on origin; it depends on whether one is willing to receive this One.

Return again to verse 14. "Full of grace and truth" — these words must be read in the Old Testament. When Moses on Mount Sinai asked God to show him His glory, God, in proclaiming His own name, said that He abounded in lovingkindness and truth (see Exodus 34:6). "Grace" corresponds to lovingkindness, and "truth" to faithfulness or truthfulness. John says that the proclamation Moses heard from afar can now be seen on a face. And the word he uses is "we beheld" — this is the tone of an eyewitness. Writing this book in his old age, decades later, John still dared to say "we beheld." At the end of the whole book he will state plainly the purpose of his writing: "that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in His name." (John 20:31, NKJV) The "we beheld" in chapter one is precisely the preparation for that sentence.

There is also a contrast hidden in verse 17: the law was given through Moses, but grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. John is not belittling Moses; the law Moses transmitted lets people know right from wrong, and that was an exceedingly weighty step. But the law is like a mirror: it can show that there is dirt on your face, but it cannot wash your face for you. John says what can actually come and wash is grace. The mirror and the washing water are both given by God; only the latter has come nearer — so near as to become flesh and dwell among us.

## III. Application

Is God far from me? This chapter's point of entry begins right here.

Many people's feeling about God is summed up in one word: "far." Whether burning incense at New Year or silently calling out in the heart when trouble comes, the default picture is of One high above, who cannot hear human words clearly — to be sought at most in great matters, and otherwise not disturbing one another. Chapter one of John overturns precisely this picture. The God in his writing is not One waiting for people to climb up and reach Him. He is the One who came down Himself — and coming down was not enough; He also wanted to "dwell" among us. Placed in today, this word about pitching a tent is like moving into your residential compound and becoming your neighbor. The distance was not drawn near by human devotion; it was traversed by Him Himself, the whole way.

Let us also speak of what this word "Word" [Logos] means for our generation. In fact, every one of us is looking for our own Logos: some believe in striving, feeling that life makes sense by fighting for it; some believe in money, feeling that once there is a sense of security the logic falls into place; some believe a little of everything, and in the end nothing can hold them up. Sooner or later a person will come to a moment when the old set of principles no longer makes sense — illness, unemployment, the departure of loved ones — none of them plays by principle. Here John sets down a different account: that ultimate "principle" behind the universe is not a cold, icy law, but a God who is willing to become a human being, who can be seen and can be rejected. He is not a problem waiting for you to work Him out; He is a Person who has come looking for you. A problem you cannot work out will crush a person; a Person who comes looking for you, however, can be received.

The word "receive" is worth pausing over. For Chinese people, hospitality — receiving a guest — is something full of human warmth: open the door, pour tea, invite the guest in to sit. John says faith is like this: not first signing a document of intellectual agreement, but opening the door. Many people's misgiving about believing in the Lord is: I am not ready yet, I understand too little, I have too many faults. According to the order of this chapter, receiving comes first, the identity of a child comes afterward, and change happens slowly after He has moved in. No host waits until a guest has made his own home as clean as the host's before inviting him in. Think of it the other way: if you keep Him forever outside the door negotiating terms, what you negotiate will forever be only doorstep topics.

Then there is that sentence, "the world did not know Him." Not knowing is sometimes not because there is not enough evidence, but because the One who came was not in the form we expected. The god we expect is either so majestic that no one dares draw near, or so efficacious that He is like a wishing machine. When He actually came, He was a baby, raised in a small town, who worked, wept, and was finally crucified. Such a One looks so unlike the image of god in our hearts that He was treated as a stranger. If you feel you have always had "no feeling" toward Jesus, it may not be that you are duller than others; it may simply be that the portrait in your heart does not match Him Himself. An honest way to read the Gospels is to set the portrait aside for a while, and let Him, by His own life, paint His portrait for you anew.

Finally, a word for those who feel they are "outsiders." The promise in verse 12 carries no attached condition of origin. Verse 13 even deliberately closes off the three human channels — blood, the will of the flesh, and the will of man — in order to show that being born of God does not compete over fathers, backgrounds, or who is more devout. You may have no religious background, and generations of your family may not have believed; in John's account that is no obstacle. This identity of being a child of God is, in the original text, a right that is given, not a qualification that is earned. All you need to do is receive.

## IV. Response

After reading these eighteen verses, take a quiet moment and think about a few questions.

- Is the God in your heart a concept, a principle, or a person? If He is a person, when was the last time you spoke seriously with Him?
- "He came to His own, and His own did not receive Him." If Jesus came into your life in a form you did not expect, would you recognize Him, and would you receive Him?
- That matter you have always wanted to work out fully before believing — to what year have you set yourself as the deadline for working it out? Could it be that the order is reversed: receive first, and slowly understand after He has moved in?
- Verse 14 says He dwelt among us. If He moved into your residential compound today and became your neighbor, what is the first thing you would most want to ask Him about?

If you are willing, you may follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, I used to feel that You were very far from me, only a principle, a concept. John says You became flesh and came to dwell among people. If You really are such a God, I am willing to open the door a crack and receive You in first; as for what comes afterward, please let me slowly come to understand. Amen."

There is also an entrance for continuing onward. This chapter says John the Baptist bore witness of the Light; in the next chapter we will follow the direction of his finger and look ahead, reading John chapter 2, where at a wedding feast in Cana Jesus turned water into wine — after the joy of life has been drunk dry, He still has a way. You may also read this chapter aloud to a friend, each say one sentence that pierced the heart most, and start your conversation from there.

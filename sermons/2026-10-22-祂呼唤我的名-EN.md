## Abstract (About 100 Words)

On the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene came to the tomb, saw the stone moved away, and ran to report the news. Peter and John ran to the tomb, saw the linen clothes and the napkin, yet did not yet understand. After everyone had left, Mary stood outside the tomb weeping, heard someone call her name — one word, "Mary" — recognized the risen Lord, received a commission, and went to tell the brethren. This sermon looks at three things: seeking through tears, recognition brought by the calling of a name, and the mission received immediately after recognition.

---

## (00:00 - 06:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, happy Easter. He is risen!

Today's story begins very early, so early that it was still dark. A woman went out in the darkness and walked toward a burial place. When she went, she carried no hope in her hands; she thought she was going to a tomb that held a body. When she returned, she was running and shouting, the first person to report the news of the resurrection. What happened in between? In between there was only one call: Mary. Today let us stay in this garden and watch how tears become recognition, and how recognition becomes a commission.

Let us read aloud together the key passages of the text:

"The first day of the week cometh Mary Magdalene early, when it was yet dark, unto the sepulchre, and seeth the stone taken away from the sepulchre. Then she runneth, and cometh to Simon Peter, and to the other disciple, whom Jesus loved, and saith unto them, They have taken away the Lord out of the sepulchre, and we know not where they have laid him." (John 20:1-2, KJV)

"Peter therefore went forth, and that other disciple, and came to the sepulchre. So they ran both together: and the other disciple did outrun Peter, and came first to the sepulchre. And he stooping down, and looking in, saw the linen clothes lying; yet went he not in. Then cometh Simon Peter following him, and went into the sepulchre, and seeth the linen clothes lie, And the napkin, that was about his head, not lying with the linen clothes, but wrapped together in a place by itself. Then went in also that other disciple, which came first to the sepulchre, and he saw, and believed. For as yet they knew not the scripture, that he must rise again from the dead." (John 20:3-9, KJV, excerpted)

"But Mary stood without at the sepulchre weeping: and as she wept, she stooped down, and looked into the sepulchre, And seeth two angels in white sitting, the one at the head, and the other at the feet, where the body of Jesus had lain. And they say unto her, Woman, why weepest thou? She saith unto them, Because they have taken away my Lord, and I know not where they have laid him. And when she had thus said, she turned herself back, and saw Jesus standing, and knew not that it was Jesus. Jesus saith unto her, Woman, why weepest thou? whom seekest thou? She, supposing him to be the gardener, saith unto him, Sir, if thou have borne him hence, tell me where thou hast laid him, and I will take him away. Jesus saith unto her, Mary. She turned herself, and saith unto him, Rabboni; which is to say, Master. Jesus saith unto her, Touch me not; for I am not yet ascended to my Father: but go to my brethren, and say unto them, I ascend unto my Father, and your Father; and to my God, and your God. Mary Magdalene came and told the disciples that she had seen the Lord, and that he had spoken these things unto her." (John 20:11-18, KJV)

Let us pray: Risen Lord, this morning we too come before You; some come with tears, some with questions. Call our names, as You did in the garden, so that we may recognize You, and send us to proclaim the news of Your resurrection. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (06:00 - 17:00) Part One: While It Was Still Dark — The Seeker and the Emptied Tomb (20:1-10)

John deliberately records the time: early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark. The news of the resurrection first came, on the first day of the week, before dawn, to a woman. Why did Mary go? The text does not say she went to witness the resurrection. She went because of love, and because she could not let go. There was so little she could still do: the body had been buried in haste, and she kept wanting to go and look once more, to do one more small thing from her heart. When love reaches its end, it keeps watch beside the tomb. Many people's knowledge of God begins here: not by first understanding great doctrines, but by first being unable to leave.

She saw the stone taken away from the tomb. Tomb, in the original, is mnemeion [4]. Her first reaction was not resurrection, but loss: they have taken away the Lord, and we know not where they have laid him. She ran to report the news, and the news she reported was bad news. Brothers and sisters, this is honest: the first witnesses of the resurrection did not, at first, come with resurrection faith. They came with panic, tears and question marks. God did not wait until their faith was complete before He acted; He acted first, and then led them, step by step, to believe. If today you too come to church carrying only questions, do not be ashamed; the people in the garden were all like that at first.

When Peter and John heard, they ran. The two ran together; one ran faster, the other went in first. What was in the tomb? The linen clothes were lying there, and the napkin was wrapped together in a place by itself. John remembers these two details with great clarity. If someone had stolen the body, they would not have left the cloths behind, still less folded the napkin and placed it apart. The tomb was empty, yet not in disorder: this emptiness carried order. That disciple went in, saw, and believed. What did he believe? At least he believed this was not a grave robbery, and believed the Lord's words were being fulfilled, although the text immediately adds that they did not yet understand the Scripture, that He must rise again from the dead. Faith is often like this: first it sees a little evidence, the heart is moved first, and understanding catches up later. God allows that order. You do not have to wait until you have thought everything through before you believe; you may first, on the basis of what you have seen, take one step forward in faith.

After the two disciples had seen, they went back to their own homes. They left, but Mary did not. The turn in the next verse is beautiful: But Mary stood without at the sepulchre weeping. The appearance of the risen Lord was not given first to the one who ran fastest, nor to the one who entered the tomb first; it was given first to the one who would not leave. Sometimes spiritual seeing does not reward speed; it rewards staying. She stayed not because she was stronger than others, but because her love would not let the gathering end. This morning I want to say to those who stay: the tears you shed for those you love, the moments when you keep watch in an empty place and will not leave — the risen Lord has seen them all.

Notice also the restraint with which John writes about himself. He writes that that disciple arrived at the tomb first, saw and believed, yet he does not praise himself as a hero of faith; immediately he honestly adds: they did not yet understand the Scripture. The church on resurrection morning was such a group of people: some ran, some looked, some believed a little, some wept without stopping; not one of them understood everything. Yet it was to this incomplete group that the Lord entrusted the resurrection. Brothers and sisters, do not wait until you understand everything, until you are completely well and completely whole, before you are willing to call yourself a follower of the Lord. Those whom the Lord uses have always been people who still carry questions yet are willing to run toward the tomb, people who believe a little when they see a little, people who weep and still will not leave. That incomplete little faith of yours is enough in His hands, because the power of the resurrection does not rest on the measure of our faith, but on His own life.

Think again about that stone. The stone was rolled away not so that the Lord could come out — the risen Lord does not need anyone to move a stone for Him to come out — the stone was rolled away so that people could go in and look, and know that the tomb was empty. Much of what God does is like this: He rolls away the stone not because He is blocked by the stone, but because we are blocked by it. What blocks us is prejudice, despair, and that sentence of a conclusion we have already settled. This morning, may the Lord also roll away the stone in our hearts, and let us stoop down and look inside: perhaps the place we thought held death is already empty; perhaps the story we thought had ended has, in heaven, already begun anew.

## (17:00 - 30:00) Part Two: Woman, Why Weepest Thou — One Call of Her Name, and She Recognized the Risen Lord (20:11-16)

As Mary wept, she stooped down and looked into the tomb, and saw two angels in white sitting where the body of Jesus had lain, one at the head and the other at the feet. The angels asked her: Woman, why weepest thou? She answered: Because they have taken away my Lord, and I know not where they have laid him. Notice the form of address: my Lord. The body was gone, yet she still called Him Lord. This unwillingness to let go — even the angels did not move her. Having said this, she turned herself back, and saw Jesus standing there, and knew not that it was Jesus. The risen Lord stood behind her, and she did not recognize Him. Tears blur the eyes, and grief settles the answer in advance: she was convinced the Lord was dead, convinced that what she was looking for was a body, so when the living Lord stood before her, she first took Him for the gardener. Are we not the same? You stand behind us speaking, and we think it is a passerby; You walk in the garden, and we are still searching in the tomb.

Jesus asked her two questions: Woman, why weepest thou? Whom seekest thou? These two questions are the first lesson of resurrection morning. Why do you weep asks about the wound; whom do you seek asks about the direction. She answered earnestly: Sir, if thou have borne him hence, tell me where thou hast laid him, and I will take him away. A frail woman, going to take away a body — she never calculated whether she could lift it. When love reaches this point, it does not count the cost. The Lord did not reveal Himself at once; He first let her finish speaking. How many such words are there in our prayers: Lord, tell me where, and I will go and take him. He hears them all. He does not laugh at us for facing the wrong direction; He simply waits to answer in a better way.

Then came the lightest and yet the heaviest sentence in the whole chapter: Jesus saith unto her, Mary. Only a name, one word. He did not preach a sermon, He did not rebuke her, He did not explain the doctrine of the resurrection; He called her name. Mary turned herself — she had already turned once before, saw, and yet did not recognize; this time the turning was a turning of the heart. She cried out in Hebrew: Rabboni, rabbouni [1], which is to say, Master. Recognition happened in a single call of her name. Why? Because she knew this voice so well. The tone in which this name came from this mouth — how many times in these three years had she heard it: calling her when He taught, calling her at meals, and on the day she was healed of her sickness, perhaps He called her just like this. Sheep know the shepherd's voice not by analysis, but by familiarity. The Lord said, My sheep hear my voice, and I know them. For Mary, the evidence of the resurrection in the end was not the empty tomb, not the linen clothes, but this: He still remembered my name, and He called me with the voice of former days.

Brothers and sisters, this morning put your own name into this sentence. He called Mary, and He calls you too. Your name — He has not remembered it wrongly, He has not omitted it, He has not confused it with anyone else's. Your tears this year, your failures, your hidden shame — He knows them all, yet the way He calls you is not first to count up your faults; it is first to call your name. For many people, the road to recognizing the risen Lord is not a road of figuring things out, but of hearing: hearing Him, amid a confusion of voices, singly and clearly, call you once.

Look more deeply at this calling of a name. After the Lord rose, Mary was the first person He addressed by name. He did not first go to Peter to discuss the overall situation, He did not first appear before the council to prove Himself; He first, behind a weeping woman, called her name. Why her? Because she was there, because she loved, because she would not leave. The order of the resurrection often overturns our ordering: the important appearance is given first to the unimportant person; the headline of the kingdom of heaven is entrusted first to tears. This is not accidental; this is His consistent heart: when He was on earth He first called fishermen, first touched lepers, first spoke about the way with a Samaritan woman; risen, He still first calls a woman who had once been possessed by demons and looked down upon by others. If you feel your name cannot appear on any respectable list, remember the first list of resurrection morning: the first name on it is Mary Magdalene.

Notice also the way she recognized the Lord: not by her eyes, but by her ears. Her eyes saw twice, and did not recognize Him either time; her ears heard one call, and recognized Him. The saying that faith comes by hearing has, in the garden, found its picture. Sometimes God closes the door of our seeing in order to train the ear of our hearing: in our circumstances we can see no turn for the better, in the numbers we can see no hope, yet His words are there, sentence by sentence. Read the Scriptures every day, hear the teaching every day, grow familiar with His voice, so that one day, amid a clamour of voices, you too will recognize at once which sentence is His. After this morning, may the Lord give us a pair of garden ears.

## (30:00 - 39:00) Part Three: Do Not Cling to Me, Go and Tell My Brethren — From Holding On to Being Sent (20:17-18)

Having recognized Him, Mary's first reaction was to hold on. The text does not state her action explicitly, but the Lord's words reveal it: Touch me not, in the original mē mou haptou [2], whose more fitting meaning is, do not cling to me, do not hold on to me and not let go. Haptomai is to grasp, to hold fast. The Lord was not refusing her nearness; the Lord was stopping one way of keeping Him: she wanted to turn the resurrection back into the former days, to pull Him back into the three years of walking together. But resurrection is not a return to the former days; resurrection is an entering into a deeper presence: for I am not yet ascended to my Father. He was to ascend, He was to be with the disciples in a new way, unlimited by the garden and unlimited by distance. We too often want to cling to the Lord: to cling to one moment of being moved, to one good season, to the way He worked before. This morning the Lord gently says: Do not cling to me. It is not that He wants to leave you; it is that He wants to lead you forward, to the Father.

Immediately follows the sending: Go to my brethren, and say unto them. Go — depart; say — deliver the message. The original verbs of this command are πορεύου ("go") and εἰπέ ("say") [3] — no grand words, only a commission to go and to tell. And in 20:21 the Lord Himself names the nature of this mission: "As my Father hath sent me, even so send I you." Mary's "go and say" is the first step of that great sending. Mary had only just recognized the Lord, and she received a mission. Listen, sentence by sentence, to what she was to proclaim: I ascend unto my Father, and your Father; and to my God, and your God. My Father, and your Father — before the cross, the Lord had never joined the two in this way. The resurrection lifted the disciples' identity: they had scattered when He was arrested, and Peter had even denied the Lord three times, yet the risen Lord called them brethren, and called His Father their Father. This word was to be carried back by Mary; she was the first messenger of the resurrection. Church tradition calls her the apostle to the apostles (a title traceable to Hippolytus of Rome's third-century Commentary on the Song of Songs, and retained by the church ever since), not to exalt her, but to remember a fact: the news of the resurrection was first entrusted to a woman weeping beside the tomb, and through her passed to all the apostles.

How did Mary receive the commission? Mary Magdalene came and told the disciples that she had seen the Lord, and that he had spoken these things unto her. She went, she spoke — she did both. She did not hide the experience away as private property, she did not wait until her feelings had calmed, she did not wait for someone else to speak first. I have seen the Lord — this is the shortest version of the resurrection testimony: I saw Him; He is alive. Add the second sentence: she told them what He had spoken to her. Testimony is not only experience; it also contains the Lord's words. What she proclaimed was not merely how moved she felt; what she proclaimed was the relationship of Father and Son, the new identity of the brethren, and the new journey of the ascension. Experience cools; the Lord's words endure.

From beginning to end, this passage is one complete line: going to the tomb while it was dark, weeping before the empty tomb, hearing her name called behind her, turning and recognizing, receiving the commission and going to proclaim. The tears were not shed in vain, the staying was not waited in vain; the calling of her name became the starting point of the sending. Everyone sitting here today is at some station along this line. May no one this morning stop weeping outside the tomb and not turn around; and may no one recognize the Lord and yet refuse to go and proclaim.

Finally, look at the direction of Mary's turning. The first time she turned, she saw a man, and thought He was the gardener; the second time she turned, she recognized the Lord, and cried, Master; then she had to turn yet once more: from facing the Lord, to facing the brethren, running to proclaim the news. Three turnings are the road by which a believer is formed: first turn away from the tomb, then turn and recognize the Lord, finally turn to serve others. Many people stop at the second turning: they have recognized the Lord, they have been moved, and they want to hold on and not let go. But the Lord's words make the third turning very clear: go to my brethren. The end point of worship is sending, and the next step after recognition is proclamation. This morning, when we have sung the last hymn and walked out of this door, that is the time of the third turning. May each of us carry in our hands one sentence for one specific person: I have seen the Lord, and He still remembers your name.

One more word, to the sisters present this morning: this passage places the first baton of the resurrection in the hands of a woman. Church history afterwards has repeatedly proved it too: many of those who heard first, who stayed most willingly, and who proclaimed most quickly, have been sisters. Your tears, your watchfulness, your early-morning prayers have never been a supporting role in the story of the resurrection. And this morning, brothers, please hear this well too: do not despise anyone weeping beside a tomb, do not urge tears to dry quickly; resurrection often first comes to those willing to stand for a while alongside tears.

Return again to that sentence, my Father and your Father, and stay with it a little longer. What Mary carried back was not only a piece of breaking news about the resurrection; it was the founding of a new family. The address "brethren", from this day on, carried a new weight: they were no longer merely companions of the same school; they were children of the same Father. All the unity, love and mutual bearing of the church afterwards grew out of this one sentence. We who worship today in the same church are of different bloodlines, different backgrounds, different stories, yet we hear the same call and recognize the same Father. If this morning a wall still stands between you and a brother or sister, remember that the first message of resurrection morning was prepared for tearing down walls: His Father is also your Father; your shame He bore on the cross; your tomorrow He opened before the empty tomb.

Think too about the steps with which Mary ran to report the news. When she came, she walked; when she left, she ran. Before she met the risen Lord, her steps were heavy, because what she carried in her arms was despair; after she met Him, her steps were light, because what she carried in her arms was a mission. Whether a person has truly met the Lord can be known, half of it, by their steps: those who have truly met Him cannot sit still; they must go and tell people. If in a church one person can run to report the news, the hearts of a whole group will be lit. This morning, may the Lord raise up more such steps: running from the tomb to the congregation, from tears to testimony, from private emotion to public proclamation. The resurrection is not for keeping in a collection; the resurrection is for running with.

There is one more layer, about this place, the garden. The tomb was set in a garden; death and life met on the same piece of ground. Mary thought the one standing before her was the gardener, and inside this misunderstanding a portion of truth is hidden: the risen Lord is indeed the Gardener of the new garden. In the first garden, humanity was driven out for disobedience; in this garden, humanity is welcomed back by the calling of a name. In our lives there are also such gardens: there are graves, and there are flowers; there are things buried, and things now sprouting. This morning, do not stare only at the tomb side; may the Lord open our eyes to see the new green on the other side of the garden. Where He has planted tears, He will Himself water them, and make them grow into testimony. That plot of ground you have kept watch over for so long — you have not kept it in vain.

One final reminder: Easter comes only one day a year, but resurrection mornings can come again every day. Every moment when it is still dark, every moment of standing in an empty place in tears, may be the prelude to His calling your name. Leave your ears for Him, leave your steps for the sending, and this year of yours will not stop outside the tomb.

## (39:00 - 43:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: those who go out while it is still dark. You have come to today carrying one unsettled matter: a test result that has not arrived, a relationship that cannot go back, a dream that has been buried. Like Mary, you have only the strength left to go and look beside the tomb. The news of the resurrection speaks first to you: the stone has already been rolled away; the story has not stopped at the ending you imagined; do not leave yet — stay and listen.

The second kind: those who see the evidence and still dare not fully believe. You saw the empty tomb, you saw the linen clothes, your heart moved for a moment, and then you feared you had believed wrongly. John saw and believed, and yet had not yet fully understood. Do not laugh at your own incomplete faith; first believe one step on the basis of what you have seen, and the understanding that remains, the Lord will supply on the road.

The third kind: those weeping outside the tomb, unwilling for it to end. Everyone else has gone back, but you remain. You weep for your children, for loved ones who have passed away, for the church, for this generation. Your staying is not weakness; the appearance of the resurrection was first given to those who stayed. This morning, please turn around: He is not in the tomb; He is standing behind you.

The fourth kind: those who cannot recognize the voice behind them. You take Him for the gardener, you take His presence for coincidence, you take His words for the advice of a passerby. May the Lord give you a pair of familiar ears: hear His words often, until one day, at one call of your name in the crowd, your heart immediately knows it is He.

The fifth kind: those who want to cling to the Lord and not let go. You grip tightly the former emotion, the former way, the former season, and will not move forward. The Lord says to you: Do not cling to me. Letting go of your hand is not losing Him; it is letting Him, by the new way of the ascension and the indwelling of the Holy Spirit, dwell more deeply. Please loosen your hands, and let your heart follow.

The sixth kind: those who have already recognized the Lord, and yet have not yet opened their mouths. You have already seen the Lord; what your story lacks is not experience, but that sentence: I have seen the Lord. This morning, receive the commission: go and tell the brethren, go and tell one specific person. News of the resurrection left long in your mouth will grow cold; proclaim it while it is warm.

## (43:00 - 45:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked a stretch of road in the garden: seeking while it was dark, tears before the empty tomb, recognition brought by one call of a name, and mission brought by one word of sending. He calls my name — resurrection is not a concept; resurrection is a living Lord, personally calling out the name of His sheep.

Let us pray together: Risen Lord, thank You; You do not live in the tomb; You stand behind us. Thank You for remembering our names; when we could not yet recognize You, You called us first. Lord, wipe away the tears outside the tomb, loosen our hands that cling to the past, and send us to tell the brethren: Your Father is also our Father, Your God is also our God. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] rabbouni: Greek ῥαββουνί (G4462), Master, my teacher, an intensified honorific form of rabbi; in John 20:16, when Mary recognized the risen Lord, she cried out this word in Hebrew; meaning according to BDAG: rabbouni, my master/teacher. This manuscript's discourse that "recognition happened in a single call of her name" develops from this cry and the narrative of 20:16.

[2] haptomai and mē mou haptou: Greek ἅπτομαι (G680), to grasp, hold fast, touch; in John 20:17 the Lord's words μή μου ἅπτου are rendered in the King James Version as "Touch me not", while scholars mostly interpret them as "Do not cling to me"; meaning according to BDAG: touch, take hold of, cling to. This manuscript preaches according to this dynamic sense and does not enter into dispute over word-for-word translation, as noted here.

[3] On the original verbs of the sending: in John 20:17 the Lord's words use πορεύου (the imperative of poreuomai, "go") and εἰπέ ("say"); ἀποστέλλω does not occur in that verse. The verb "to send", ἀποστέλλω (G649), appears in John 20:21: "As my Father hath sent me, even so send I you" (cf. also 17:18); meaning according to BDAG: send out, dispatch on a mission. Part Three of this manuscript expounds Mary's commission from the "go and say" of 20:17, with the sending of 20:21 as its stated reference, as distinguished here.

[4] mnemeion: Greek μνημεῖον (G3419), tomb, place of remembrance; John 20:1-11 repeatedly uses it for the place where Jesus was laid; meaning according to BDAG: grave, tomb, monument. Part One of this manuscript, "the tomb was empty yet not in disorder", develops from the details of the linen clothes and the napkin in 20:5-7.

[5] On "he saw and believed" and "they did not yet understand": John 20:8-9 places side by side that disciple's seeing and believing and the company not yet understanding the meaning of Scripture; this manuscript preaches, according to this textual juxtaposition, that "faith moves first, understanding is supplied afterwards", a textual observation at the level of preaching, as noted here.

[6] On the Lord calling the disciples brethren: in John 20:17 the Lord, for the first time after the resurrection, calls the disciples "my brethren", and says "my Father, and your Father"; this manuscript's discourse on the lifting of identity rests on a preaching exposition of the original text of this verse.

## Devotional Excerpt

Mary stood outside the tomb, crying. The disciples had gone home, but she would not leave. She wept not because she was weak, but because her love had nowhere left to go. While tears blurred her eyes, the risen Lord stood behind her. He asked why she was crying, and whom she was seeking, and then He did one simple thing: He called her name. Mary. With that one word, she knew Him. Resurrection is not first a set of arguments handed to us. It is the living Lord calling His own sheep by name. Perhaps you are also standing outside a tomb today, keeping watch over a buried hope or a person who will not return, your eyes too full of tears to recognize anyone. Do not leave yet. Stay a little longer and listen for the voice behind you. He knows why you cry, and He knows whom you seek. When He calls your name, you will discover that you are not looking for a body to carry away. You are looking at a Lord who is alive. Hear Him, turn around, and then go and tell others: I have seen the Lord.

Do not think this call was meant for Mary alone. Every morning He still calls His own by name, lifting the heads of the sorrowful, drawing the hiding ones out into the light, and bringing wanderers home. In your life, many voices have misnamed you, labeled you, and ranked you, but His voice has never mistaken you, and it has never arrived too late. Hold on to that one call today. Let it teach you again who He is, and who you are in His hands. Then do what Mary did after she turned around: run to the brothers and sisters who are still weeping, and tell them the tomb is empty, the Lord is risen, and He is still calling names in the garden.

## Summary (about 100 words)

After Jacob died, Joseph had his father embalmed, Egypt mourned seventy days, and a vast funeral procession went up to Canaan and buried him in the cave of Machpelah. The brothers, fearing Joseph would repay them, relayed a purported dying charge from their father begging pardon; Joseph wept and said, Am I in the place of God? You meant evil, but God meant it for good. Dying, he charged them: God will surely visit you; carry my bones up from here. A whole book closes on hope.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Our study of Genesis has traveled a long road. From "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth," to Abraham called out of Ur; from Isaac's obedience, to Jacob's ladder and his wrestling; from the pit into which Joseph was sold, to the throne of the ruler of Egypt. Today we come to the last chapter of Genesis. This chapter has no new miracle and no new covenant; the whole chapter handles only two funerals: first Jacob the father is buried, then Joseph himself is sent off. Yet between these two funerals is hidden the weightiest sentence of the whole book of Genesis, and the story of how a man who endured every grievance finally handed all the right and wrong of his life back into God's hand.

At the end of the previous chapter Jacob gathered his feet into the bed, breathed his last, and was gathered to his people. As this chapter opens, the first thing we hear is not words but weeping. Let us read together several key passages from today:

"And Joseph fell upon his father's face, and wept upon him, and kissed him." (Genesis 50:1, excerpt, KJV)

"And Joseph said unto them, Fear not: for am I in the place of God? But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." (Genesis 50:19-20, excerpt, KJV)

"And Joseph said unto his brethren, I die: and God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land unto the land which he sware to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob. And Joseph took an oath of the children of Israel, saying, God will surely visit you, and ye shall carry up my bones from hence." (Genesis 50:24-25, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, thank You for leading us through the whole book of Genesis. Along this road You have let us see that human intent is full of scheming, favoritism, envy, and harm, while Your intent all along turned harm into salvation and tears into hope. Teach us two things today: how to forgive, and how to walk out this life in peace carrying a hope that is not yet fulfilled. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

In today's sermon we will hold fast to three pictures: the first is a funeral procession in a foreign land, vast and grand, yet walking back to the promised land; the second is a weeping Joseph, standing in the very position from which he could have taken revenge, yet saying, I am not in God's place; the third is a set of bones resting in Egypt — they did not settle with this generation, they must wait, wait until the day of God's visitation comes.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: A Vast Funeral in a Foreign Land and Its Landing in the Promised Land (50:1-14)

First look at Joseph's tears. "And Joseph fell upon his father's face, and wept upon him, and kissed him." Falling upon his face is a heavy movement. Joseph was the ruler of Egypt, second only to Pharaoh, a man before whom people bowed; but the moment his father breathed his last, he forgot every rank and was only a son, throwing himself on his father's already cold face, weeping, and kissing him. Genesis wrote Jacob's whole life — how he deceived his father, wrestled with his brother, schemed against his uncle, and was wounded to the heart by his own children; at the end the Bible gives him no epitaph, only this weeping of his son. When a person's accounts are closed at the coffin, the tears of his children are truer than any verdict from outside. Joseph's tears are the cleanest writing in this passage: power had not hardened his heart, and seventeen years of separation had not worn away this bond between father and son.

After weeping, Joseph did a very Egyptian thing: he commanded the physicians in his service to embalm his father. Embalming was a mature craft in Egypt and took forty days to complete. The text carefully records two numbers: forty days for the embalming, and seventy days during which the Egyptians mourned. What weight do seventy days carry? That was mourning of a rank given only to a figure of Pharaoh's own level. The father of the Hebrews, an old man who had come down from Canaan fleeing famine, received in a foreign land mourning almost equal to royalty. Brothers and sisters, notice this contrast: all his life the heaviest place in Jacob's heart was Canaan, and dying he charged them again and again, do not bury me in Egypt, bury me in the burial place of my fathers. Yet God let him first receive the full honor of strangers in Egypt, and only then go back to Canaan in great procession. The honor was given by Egypt, but his resting place was not in Egypt. A person in a foreign land may be lifted up, may be mourned, may have a season of dignity; but the one who trusts God knows clearly that dignity is not home, and however lively the foreign land is, it is not the promised land.

When the seventy days of mourning were fulfilled, Joseph asked Pharaoh. He spoke with great propriety; he did not use the ruler's power to set out directly, but conveyed his request through Pharaoh's servants: my father made me swear, when he was dying, to bury him in the grave he had prepared in Canaan; let me go up and bury my father, and I will come back. Pharaoh granted it, and granted it plainly: "Go up, and bury thy father, according as he made thee swear." A foreign king fulfilled a Hebrew man's oath to his father. All along this road we keep seeing that God does not work only within the tabernacle; He also opens the way for His people in Pharaoh's mouth and in the customs of a foreign land.

So a rare funeral procession set out. The text counts them for us: all Pharaoh's servants, the elders of Egypt, Joseph's whole household, his brothers, his father's household, and also chariots and horsemen — a very great company. Only the little ones and the flocks and herds stayed in the land of Goshen. In this company were Egyptian nobles and a Hebrew family; there were chariots and there was weeping. They set out from Egypt, passed beyond the Jordan, and came to the threshing floor of Atad, where they lamented with a very great and grievous lamentation, and Joseph made a mourning for his father seven days. How great was the weeping? The Canaanites who lived there saw it and said, this is a grievous mourning to the Egyptians, and they named the place Abel-mizraim, the meadow of Egypt's mourning. A place's name was changed by a funeral. Brothers and sisters, this is sometimes how faith affects a place: not by conquest, but by a funeral and a company's tears, letting strangers know that this family's death is not like other deaths — there is hope in their death.

Then they entered Canaan and buried Jacob in the cave of the field of Machpelah before Mamre. This field Abraham had bought long ago for four hundred shekels of silver from Ephron the Hittite, with a contract and witnesses — the only piece of the promised land secured by purchase. Abraham was buried there, Sarah was buried there, Isaac, Rebekah, and Leah were buried there, and now Jacob came back too. Fix this picture firmly in mind: a vast company setting out from Egypt, escorting not a triumphant general but a corpse; they traveled so far not to seize anything, only to lay an old man back in a cave bought long ago. Why such solemnity? Because this land was the token of the promise. Jacob's body lying down in the cave of Machpelah was, with his corpse, saying the oath once more: this land God promised to us, our descendants will surely return; for now let my bones hold the place here on behalf of my children. When the burial was done, Joseph and the company returned to Egypt. Note — they went back. Into the promised land there entered only a coffin; the people still had to return to the foreign land to wait. The landing point of faith was already driven in; the fulfillment of faith had not yet come. The geography of Genesis is this honest: the grave enters first, and the people come slowly.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Am I in the Place of God? — From Your Intent to God's Intent (50:15-21)

Only after the funeral did the family's real storm arrive. When the brothers saw that their father was dead, their hearts immediately panicked. They said to one another, "Joseph will peradventure hate us, and will certainly requite us all the evil which we did unto him." This sentence tore open at a stroke the surface peace of this family over seventeen years. All these years the brothers had eaten Joseph's grain and lived in the Goshen Joseph arranged, yet in their hearts they had never truly been at rest. What they feared was not that Joseph had changed; they feared that Joseph had never changed — that he had endured for seventeen years only because their father was still alive, and now that their father was gone, the account would be settled. Brothers and sisters, the most tormenting thing about a guilty deed is not the moment it was done, but every anxious year afterward. On the day the brothers sold their brother, they thought the matter would be finished once it was done; in fact, from that day they spent a lifetime waiting for revenge. This is how the interest on sin rolls on year after year.

They worked out a plan: they sent messengers to Joseph with a message saying, thy father did command before he died, saying, So shall ye say unto Joseph, Forgive, I pray thee now, the trespass of thy brethren, and their sin; for they did unto thee evil. At this point we must honestly pause: there is a long-standing scholarly discussion about the authenticity of this text. Not a few exegetes point out that the earlier record of Genesis nowhere records Jacob leaving such a dying charge; Jacob's long final words in chapters 48 and 49 are all recorded, and they are blessings and prophecies, with no such instruction about forgiveness. Therefore some scholars hold that this purported dying charge of their father was very likely composed by the brothers out of fear, spoken in their father's name, in order to add weight to their plea for pardon. Here we state this plainly: this is a scholarly view with grounds, but the text itself does not say clearly whether the words are true or false, and in preaching we make no final judgment, still less do we stand in the pulpit accusing the brothers of lying. Why? Because whether these words were truly spoken by Jacob or relayed by the brothers in their fear, what this scene wants us to see is the same: people in fear will use every means to find themselves support, even moving the words of a dead father out. And Joseph's response is the true center of this scene — he never checked at all whether the words were true.

Look at how Joseph responded. Before the messengers had even finished speaking, the text records only this: "Joseph wept." They came to beg pardon; he wept first. What do these tears mean? They mean that in Joseph's heart there never was the ledger the brothers imagined. On their side the brothers had rehearsed seventeen years of fear; on Joseph's side, not even a thought of revenge had ever been stored up. The person you have guarded against for half a lifetime never kept a grudge against you at all — such a discovery is half release and half heartache. Heartache, because only across their father's death did the brothers for the first time bring the matter to this point; release, because this weeping first melted a layer of seventeen years of suspicion. Then the brothers themselves came and fell down before his face and said, Behold, we be thy servants. This was their last card: if pardon could not be obtained, they would pay the debt with themselves — in that year they had sold their brother as a slave, and now they volunteered to be slaves before their brother. History had circled around, and the places of master and slave were wholly reversed.

At precisely the moment everyone thought Joseph would speak the sentence, Joseph spoke the keynote of the whole book of Genesis. First sentence: "Fear not: for am I in the place of God?" "Am I in the place of God?" — in the original, tachath elohim (תַּחַת אֱלֹהִים, H8478 plus H430), literally, am I in God's position, can I stand under God's place and take it in His stead [1]. Joseph was not being polite; he was drawing a boundary line: the authority to condemn and to pardon is with God, not with me; there is only one judgment seat, and I am not the one sitting on it. In his hand were grain, soldiers, the power of Egypt, and seventeen years of suffering as his reason — every condition for revenge a person could imagine, he had them all. Yet at the very moment he was most entitled to take revenge, he handed the authority of judgment back to God with his own hand. Brothers and sisters, the foundation of forgiveness is never who stands higher and who stands lower, nor who owes whom more or less. The foundation of forgiveness is this one sentence: I am not in God's place. Only a person willing not to sit in God's seat can lay down his own ledger; a person who clutches his ledger will sooner or later climb onto that seat, judge by himself and execute by himself, and in the end pay with himself as well.

The second sentence is the summary of the chapter, and of the whole book: "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." Here the original sets the same verb in parallel: chashav (חָשַׁב, H2803), to think, reckon, plan, intend; both "ye thought" and "God meant it" are finite (perfect) forms of this verb — you thought evil, but God meant it unto good [2]. Evil and good — in the original ra'ah (רָעָה, H7451) and tovah (טוֹבָה, H2896), one evil and one good, colliding head-on [3]. Please do not hear this sentence lightly. Joseph did not say the brothers actually had no ill intent; he did not say the dry pit, the Ishmaelite caravan, and the bloodied coat of many colors were all misunderstandings. He said with perfect clarity: as for you, you meant to harm me. Human evil Joseph does not whitewash, and God's goodness Joseph does not discount. Both stand at the same time — this is the Bible's honesty: the same stretch of experience, coming from human hands, is harm, and passing through God's hand becomes good. "God meant it for good" does not mean human intent is therefore made good; God overturned the outcome, but that does not mean God wants us to call evil good. Nor is forgiveness amnesia. Joseph remembered everything, and because he remembered, his forgiveness carries weight; a person who pretends nothing ever happened is not forgiving — he is only evading.

Where, then, was God's goodness shown? Joseph himself gave the answer: to save much people alive. Whom did it save? It saved the Egyptians, it saved those fleeing famine in Canaan and Syria, and it saved this very family that once sold him and now begged from him. Without that pit there would have been no storehouses in Egypt; without the storehouses Jacob's whole house would have been cut off at the root in the famine, and there would have been nothing to say about the Messiah's lineage. In the years no one could see, God used human malice as His material and wove a road that saved many people. This is exactly the settled theological form of what Joseph had already said in Genesis 45: God did send me before you to preserve life. And it is the root of Paul's later promise in Romans: all things work together for good to them that love God. We cite Romans 8:28 not as sticking a slogan onto a wound; Joseph's words came first, and he verified this sentence personally with seventeen years of tears and a lifetime of ups and downs, before it came to us to say it after him today.

The last sentence lands on Joseph's attitude toward his brothers from then on: "Now therefore fear ye not: I will nourish you, and your little ones. And he comforted them, and spake kindly unto them." "Comforted" — the original carries the root nichum (נִחַם, H5162), comfort and turning after repentance and grief [4]. The Chinese Union Version renders it as words of affection, words spoken to the heart. Joseph's forgiveness was not a single "let it go" and done; he gave two concrete things: first, provision — I will nourish you, and your children with you; forgiveness landed on grain, on three meals a day. Second, kind words — he in turn comforted the very men who ought to have comforted him. The wounded one opens his mouth to comfort — in human terms this makes no sense, but in God's intent it is exactly the shape of grace: the victorious side reaches out its hand first; the side in the right gives the grain first. In this moment Joseph most resembled that descendant of his who would come later: He does not wait until those who harmed Him deserve it before giving grace; while they are still prostrate and trembling, He hands the grace over.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Hope of a Set of Bones, and Our Application (50:22-26)

The storm passed and life returned to calm. Joseph dwelt in Egypt, he and his father's house with him, and he lived a hundred and ten years — in Egypt precisely the number of a complete life, the most blessed lifespan in Egyptian eyes. He saw Ephraim's children of the third generation, and Manasseh's grandchildren were brought up on his knees. A man sold at seventeen, drifting most of his life in a foreign land, in his old age had children and grandchildren around his knees — he had everything. Yet when the Bible comes to his death, it does not write how much property he left or how great a funeral Egypt gave him; it writes only the words he spoke. Those words are the closing stroke of Genesis.

"And Joseph said unto his brethren, I die: and God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land unto the land which he sware to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob." "Will surely visit" — in the original paqod yiphqod (פָּקֹד יִפְקֹד, H6485), the same verb used twice in succession, one of the heaviest emphatic constructions in Hebrew: not perhaps visit, but surely, certainly, personally visit, care for, and call by name [5]. This word "visit" later became the password of the whole Exodus: at the burning bush God said to Moses, I have surely seen, I will surely visit you — in the original the echo of this very word; when Moses later conveyed it to the elders of Israel, the people, hearing that God had visited them, bowed their heads and worshiped. And centuries later, on the night of the Exodus, Moses indeed, according to Joseph's charge, took Joseph's bones up with them. The end of Joshua records again that Joseph's bones were finally buried at Shechem, in the piece of ground Jacob had bought. Hebrews chapter 11, on faith, lists so many great figures, yet the single comment it gives Joseph is this one thing: by faith Joseph, when he died, made mention of the departing of the children of Israel, and gave commandment concerning his bones. When the Bible judges the peak of a person's faith, it does not look at how glorious he was in life; it looks at where, at his death, he staked his hope.

"Ye shall carry up my bones from hence." Note, "from hence." From where? Egypt. In Egypt Joseph had obtained everything: position, family, long life, honor. His coffin rested in Egypt — at the very end the text says his body was embalmed and put in a coffin in Egypt. The word "in Egypt / rested" is written with great weight. His body rested in the land of his success, but his heart did not make its home there. Before dying he was in effect saying to his brothers: my life in Egypt was a sojourn; my bones do not belong here; when the day comes that God visits you and you go, do not forget to take me with you — I will go home together with you. Brothers and sisters, this is the shape of an unfulfilled hope. Joseph did not see the Exodus with his own eyes; the thing he waited for happened nearly four hundred years after him. In his life he saw the beginning of the promise, not its completion; yet he left a set of bones as the token, holding the oath in advance on behalf of descendants yet to come. Faith does not always get to draw a complete full stop; there is a kind of faith that, unable to finish its full stop, makes itself into a comma, left in history, waiting for God to continue the sentence. And so Genesis closes: not with a triumph, not with a city, but with a coffin and one sentence — He will surely visit you. The next book opens, Pharaoh has changed, the people suffer, but the coffin is still there and the oath is still there. Anyone who turns the page into Exodus knows the story has not broken off, God has not forgotten, and the word "He will surely visit you" is waiting to be fulfilled.

Bringing this chapter back to ourselves, I want to speak to four kinds of people.

First, to those holding a ledger in their hand. The wounds you suffered are real — Joseph never denied "you meant evil against me," and you need not deny it either. But learn his boundary line: Am I in the place of God? Leave revenge to the One who sits on the judgment seat. What you hand over is not justice — justice is steadier in God's hand than in yours; what you hand over is the thing that gnaws at your heart daily and keeps you from sleeping. The brothers paid interest on that seventeen-year account every day; Joseph never collected it once, and his heart was the freest in the whole family.

Second, to those who have gained dignity in a foreign land. You have found your footing at work, bought a house and settled in a foreign land, and there will be times when you are mourned and honored. Joseph did not despise the good of Egypt — he nourished his whole family and comforted his brothers; but his bones knew where home was. Dignity may be received; your final resting place must not be mistaken. Do not let the land of your success slowly become the whole of your hope; remember your Machpelah clearly, and hold in advance, for your children, the land you are finally going to.

Third, to those whose hope has long gone unfulfilled. The thing you pray for has shown no movement in ten, twenty years; the person you wait for has not turned back; the promise you thought you would see with your own eyes in this life may only be fulfilled after you are gone. Joseph waited four hundred years, leaning not on sight but on one sentence: God will surely visit you. What you can do today is leave behind a bones-like token: a charge spoken to your children, a prayer not yet closed, a thing planted in the knowledge you will not see its fruit. An unfulfilled hope is not a failed hope; it too is faith, and often the most durable kind.

Fourth, to those who fear being repaid and whose conscience has never rested. The brothers feared for seventeen years and at last came prostrate. Do not cover your debt any longer with excuses, with other people's words, with busyness; while the person is still here and the door is still open, come yourself, bow down, and speak it plainly. You will discover that God has long prepared in the other person's heart a grace you never imagined. Joseph's tears were waiting for them, provision like gold was waiting for them, words of comfort were waiting for them. Many reconciliations lack only your one step out of fear.

One more thing — notice how many times Joseph's tears appear in this chapter. When his father breathed his last, he fell on his face and wept; when the brothers sent their message begging pardon, he wept before he had finished hearing it; at the end, when the brothers lay prostrate before him, after speaking his weightiest words he comforted them with kind words. Genesis records Joseph weeping many times: at the pit he had no chance to weep, in Potiphar's house he would not weep for others to see, in prison no one heard him weep; but the moment he was reunited with his family, his tears could not be held back — first he turned away and wept, then he wept embracing Benjamin, and later, in the chariot, he wept embracing his father. How many times a person weeps often says more about who he is than his power does. Joseph's greatness was not that he was never wounded, but that, wounded so heavily, his heart never hardened. Many people, once wounded by their closest kin, close their hearts from then on, left with nothing but guardedness toward everyone; Joseph let his tears wash his heart soft — soft enough still to prepare grain, land, and kind words for the very people who had harmed him. Brothers and sisters, tears here are not weakness; they are the road forgiveness has traveled. In a person willing to weep first before his enemies, the heart of revenge has already half died; in a person who will not weep at all and will only keep accounts, the account grows thicker and thicker until his whole life is reckoned into it. May God give us such tears too: not tears of self-pity, but the evidence of a heart that has not yet hardened.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today Genesis has closed before us. The first sentence of this book is "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth," and its last sentence is a coffin in Egypt. Between them lie the weakness of the patriarchs, the strife of brothers, and every kind of human intent; but the thread running through the whole book is God's intent: people mean harm, God means salvation; people scheme, God visits; people drive the road to a dead end, and on that dead end God saves much people alive. The summary of the whole book is not human intent; it is God's intent.

As we leave, may each of us keep one sentence in our hearts. Let the wounded keep this one: Am I in the place of God? I hand the account back. Let the successful keep this one: my resting place is not in Egypt. Let those who are waiting keep this one: God will surely visit you; even if the fruit ripens after I am gone, my hope will not fall empty.

Let us pray together: Faithful Lord, thank You — Genesis is finished, but Your word is not finished. Take from our hands the ledgers we have clutched for years; teach us to stand in our own place, leave judgment to You, and hand grace to people. Give us Joseph's deathbed faith: at death grasping not property but the promise; saying not "I am finished" but "God will surely visit you." May our children one day carry our bones toward the land You swore and promised. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Joseph said unto them, Fear not: for am I in the place of God? But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." (Genesis 50:19-20, KJV) "God will surely visit you; and ye shall carry up my bones from hence." (Genesis 50:25, excerpt, KJV)

Once their father died, the account the brothers had feared for seventeen years finally came due. They fell down and begged for pardon, but Joseph wept first. He did not whitewash the old evil: you meant to harm me. Nor did he clutch the right of revenge: am I in the place of God? Forgiveness is not forgetting; it is remembering everything, yet handing judgment back into God's hand. At his death, this old man who had everything in Egypt left only one sentence and one set of bones: God will surely visit you, and my bones must go home. What he waited for would take four hundred years to come to pass, yet he staked his hope on it in advance. Today, hand the ledger in your hand to God, and hand your unfinished hope to God as well. You are not in God's place, so you need not judge; you stand in God's promise, so you need not despair. Human intent will pass away; God's intent will surely be accomplished. The account you lay down today, God has not laid down; the fruit you cannot see today, God is already preparing on the way. Rest your whole life, in peace, in the hand of the God who will surely visit His people.

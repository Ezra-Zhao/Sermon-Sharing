# It Is the LORD Your God Who Fights for You — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 3

> [First translation — proofreading pending / 初译待校]

> **Verification of the title:** Five candidates were considered — (1) *It Is the LORD Your God Who Fights for You*, from the base-text wording of 3:22, "for it is the LORD your God who fights for you" [in the KJV anchor: "for the Lord your God he shall fight for you"]; (2) *Not One Was Left*, from 3:3, which covers only the battle against Og, not the allotment or Moses' plea; (3) *"Let It Suffice Thee; Speak No More unto Me of This Matter"*, from 3:26, which covers only the closing refusal, not the two kings' defeat; (4) *The Land Which Thou Shalt See*, from 3:28, which covers only the handing over, not the taking of the land; (5) *Threescore Cities*, from 3:4, which covers only the battle's fruit. Candidate (1) is adopted: all twenty-nine verses of the chapter — the defeat of Og (vv. 1–7), the boundary note and the iron bedstead (vv. 8–11), the allotment east of the Jordan (vv. 12–20), the charge to Joshua (vv. 21–22), and Moses' plea refused (vv. 23–29) — hang on this one sentence: verses 1–11 are its fact, verses 12–20 its distribution, verses 21–22 its charge, and verses 23–29 its other side — the God who fights for Israel is the same God who says to Moses with His own mouth, "Let it suffice thee." Chinese title: 《为你争战的是耶和华》.

Where the KJV and the Chinese base text differ, the difference is registered separately below and in the notes, and is not harmonised. Key registered points from the Chinese collation, with the KJV reading added: (i) 3:2 — the base text has an exclamation, "Fear him not!"; the KJV reads "Fear him not: for I will deliver him … into thy hand." (ii) 3:4 — the base text's wording rendered "this is all the region of Argob" is its own phrasing and is not rewritten; the KJV reads "threescore cities, all the region of Argob." (iii) 3:6 — the base text reads, in effect, "we destroyed every inhabited city, men, women and children"; the Union Version of 2010 reads "the men, women and children of every city"; the KJV reads "utterly destroying the men, women, and children, of every city" — the KJV here stands with the 2010 wording, not with the base text's "inhabited cities" phrasing; registered separately. (iv) 3:11 — the base text has no "Behold"; the 2010 version adds "Behold, his bed was an iron bed"; the KJV does have "behold his bedstead was a bedstead of iron; is it not in Rabbath of the children of Ammon?" — on this point the KJV stands with the 2010 version against the base text; registered separately. (v) 3:18 — the base text ends the clause with a semicolon, the modern-punctuation Union Version with a full stop; the KJV reads "The Lord your God hath given you this land to possess it: ye shall pass over armed." (vi) 3:22 — the base text is singular: "Ye shall not fear them" addressed in the singular, "for [He] fights for *you* (singular)"; the 2010 version is plural throughout; the KJV reads "Ye shall not fear them: for the Lord your God he shall fight for you" — its subject "Ye" and its "you" are plural in form and address, differing from the Chinese base text's singular addressed to Joshua alone; the difference is registered, not harmonised (see the first-translation record at the end). (vii) 3:24 — the base text has no word for "begun," though the Hebrew has hāḥillôtā ("Thou hast begun"); the 2010 version supplies "Thou hast begun to show…"; the KJV reads "thou hast begun to shew thy servant thy greatness" — the KJV, like the 2010 version, carries "begun," which the Chinese base text omits; registered separately (see Note 8). (viii) 3:26 — the base text reads "Let it suffice thee!"; the 2010 version, "Enough for you!"; the KJV reads "Let it suffice thee; speak no more unto me of this matter." (ix) 3:27 — the base text gives the directions in the order east, west, south, north; the Hebrew order is west, north, south, east, and the KJV follows the Hebrew order: "westward, and northward, and southward, and eastward"; registered only (see Note 9). (x) 3:28 — the base text's word order is "make him strong-hearted," the 2010 version's "strengthen his heart"; the KJV reads "charge Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him."

> **Format note:** The sermon is in five sections, each headed by a time code (a 60-minute sermon structure). Within each section, five layer markers are used — [Fact layer] [Theological-inference layer] [Summary layer] [New Testament reading layer] [Scholarly and historical discussion layer]. The fact layer records only facts and arithmetic verifiable in the chapter's base text; the inference layer makes theological inferences and marks them as inferences; the summary layer gives one governing sentence; the New Testament reading layer registers only the New Testament's echoes of this chapter's motifs, without reading them back as the Old Testament's original meaning; the discussion layer registers divergent readings in scholarship and tradition and divergences from the parallel accounts in Numbers, presenting them side by side and leaving the adjudication open. Hebrew word forms verified against the BHS text line on the wd.bible verse pages and standard lexica (BDB), with Strong's numbers: yārēʾ (to fear, H3372, 3:2, 22), nātan (to deliver / give, H5414, 3:2, 18), ḥāram (to devote to destruction, H2763, 3:6), ʿereś (bedstead, H6210, 3:11), barzel (iron, H1270, 3:11), ʾammâ (cubit, H520, 3:11), rəpāʾîm (Rephaim, H7497, 3:11, 13), ḥayil (valour / men of war, H2428, 3:18), ḥālaṣ (to be armed / equipped, H2502, 3:18), lāḥam (to fight, H3898, 3:22), ḥālal (to begin, H2490, 3:24), rav (enough / much, H7227, 3:26), ʿābar (to cross over / to be wroth, H5674, 3:26, 27, 28), ṣāp̄ôn (north, H6828, 3:27), ṣāwâ (to charge / command, H6680, 3:28), ḥāzaq (to strengthen, H2388, 3:28), ʾāmaṣ (to make strong / courageous, H553, 3:28) — seventeen items verified. ## The Sermon

Introduction (00:00–05:00). [Fact layer] Deuteronomy 3 is the last stretch of narrative in Moses' first discourse (1:1–4:43). Chapter 1 reviewed why the forty years were delayed; chapter 2 recorded the end of the wandering and the first victory, over Sihon; this chapter records the second victory and the first parcel of land given out: Og king of Bashan and all his army were wiped out, threescore cities changed hands, and the land east of the Jordan was there and then allotted to Reuben, Gad and the half-tribe of Manasseh. Immediately afterwards Moses made the matter of the two kings into Joshua's textbook and spoke the chapter's pivotal sentence — "for the Lord your God he shall fight for you" (3:22, KJV). But the chapter does not stop at the victory song: in its last seven verses the discourse suddenly turns to Moses' private affair — he pleaded to cross over, and God said, "Let it suffice thee." [Fact layer] The chapter has twenty-nine verses, and its structure is registered first: the battle against Og, seven verses (1–7); boundaries and the iron-bedstead parenthesis, four verses (8–11); the allotment, nine verses (12–20); the charge to Joshua, two verses (21–22); Moses' plea, seven verses (23–29). The speech account is counted separately: God's words in two blocks (v. 2; vv. 26–28), Moses' charges in two blocks (vv. 18–20 to the two and a half tribes; vv. 21–22 to Joshua), and Moses' prayer in one block (vv. 23–25); see Note 3. [Theological-inference layer] The chapter's position is worth seeing clearly at the start: at one end it is joined to the taking of the land, at the other to the handing over of leadership. Transjordan is not the destination; it is the model — how the land is gained, how it is allotted, how the battle is won and how the leader is succeeded are all acted out once in this chapter, in preparation for the second generation to repeat them across the river. When Moses preached this chapter he already knew he would not cross (the sentence had been recorded in 1:37); the more fully he tells the victories, the more plainly the hearers should understand that these victories have nothing to do with whether he personally stays or goes — it is the LORD who fights for Israel; so it was before, and so it will be in Joshua's days. [Summary layer] The chapter's governing line can be put like this: God fights, God allots, and God also bars the way. The same God put threescore cities into Israel's hand, allotted Transjordan to the two and a half tribes, and barred Moses on the east side of the Jordan — His sovereignty is not discounted by human merit, nor diverted by human pleading. What the chapter sets out, section by section, is how these three things stand side by side, none of them hidden.

### I. Og Defeated: A Second King, and Threescore Cities (3:1–7; 05:00–16:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 1 records the turn and the march to battle; the KJV in full: "Then we turned, and went up the way to Bashan: and Og the king of Bashan came out against us, he and all his people, to battle at Edrei." Items registered: first, "we turned" is the same word as the "turned" of 2:1 (registered paraphrase, mutually corroborating) — in chapter 2 Israel turned back from Kadesh into the wilderness; in this chapter they turn from Sihon's territory toward Bashan; two turnings, one a retreat into wandering, the other an advance northward. Second, it is Og who takes the initiative — "he and all his people came out" to meet them; the battlefield is at Edrei. This chapter records no Israelite envoys asking for passage (unlike the courtesy-before-arms approach to Sihon in 2:26–29; registered separately); the war is opened directly by Og's sally. [Fact layer] Verse 2 is God's word before the battle; the KJV in full: "And the Lord said unto me, Fear him not: for I will deliver him, and all his people, and his land, into thy hand; and thou shalt do unto him as thou didst unto Sihon king of the Amorites, which dwelt at Heshbon." Items registered: first, "Fear him not" occurs twice in the chapter; this is the first occurrence (v. 22 is the second, its object shifting from the singular king to the plural nations). Second, "into thy hand" — the account of "deliver / give" (nātan, H5414) in this chapter: in v. 2, delivered into thy (Moses' / Israel's) hand; in v. 3, delivered into our hands — two occurrences, joining the three in chapter 2 (2:24, 30, 36) into one line: God's "delivering" precedes man's "fighting"; before every victory there is first a completed act of delivery. Third, "as thou didst unto Sihon" — the Sihon affair is here set up as a precedent: the precedent is not Israel's battle record but God's manner of delivering. [Fact layer] Verse 3 records the outcome; the KJV in full: "So the Lord our God delivered into our hands Og also, the king of Bashan, and all his people: and we smote him until none was left to him remaining." The little word "also" is registered: Og is the second king to be "delivered." "None was left to him remaining" is phrased in the same pattern as chapter 2's account of the Sihon campaign (registered paraphrase). Verses 4–5 keep the account of the cities; v. 4 in full: "And we took all his cities at that time, there was not a city which we took not from them, threescore cities, all the region of Argob, the kingdom of Og in Bashan." Verse 5 in full: "All these cities were fenced with high walls, gates, and bars; beside unwalled towns a great many." Items registered: first, the number of cities is threescore — sixty, in the KJV's own wording — the chapter's only city count; for its scope see Note 13 and the cross-reading with Joshua 13:30. Second, the detail of the defences, "high walls, gates, and bars" — read it alongside 1:28 (registered paraphrase): what the first generation feared was precisely the report that the cities were "walled up to heaven"; this chapter writes the high walls into the list of spoils — however high the walls, all threescore cities were taken, not one missing. Third, the "unwalled towns" are counted separately from the sixty, and the text gives no number for them. [Fact layer] Verses 6–7 record the treatment; v. 6 in full: "And we utterly destroyed them, as we did unto Sihon king of Heshbon, utterly destroying the men, women, and children, of every city." Verse 7 in full: "But all the cattle, and the spoil of the cities, we took for a prey to ourselves." Registered: first, "utterly destroyed" is the causative stem of ḥāram (H2763), checked directly against the BHS text line on the verse page for 3:6 (the two forms wa-naḥărēm / haḥărēm in the same verse). Its scope is compared with the treatment account of the Sihon campaign in chapter 2 — there the city's people were all destroyed and the cattle and spoil taken (2:34–35, registered paraphrase); here the pattern is the same. Second, the Chinese base text's distinctive phrase "every inhabited city" was altered in the 2010 version to "the men, women and children of every city"; the KJV reads "the men, women, and children, of every city," agreeing with the 2010 phrasing; the versions are registered separately and not interchanged (Note 7). Third, the line between goods taken as prey and people destroyed is the same in both chapters; this sermon registers only the text's own stated scope, and leaves the ethical reading to the associated discussion in Section V and Note 13. [Theological-inference layer] The theological centre of this section is how the word "precedent" rewrites fear. The first generation heard of giants and high cities, and their heart melted (1:28); the second generation stands before the same kind of enemy, and what God gives them is not a new assurance but the old record: "as thou didst unto Sihon." Faith in this chapter is not courage conjured out of nothing; it is the use of memory — God filed away the last victory so that it could be drawn on in the next battle. The order of Moses' discourse is therefore also the order of a textbook: first Sihon (chapter 2), then Og (this chapter), and only then Joshua hearing "Thine eyes have seen" (v. 21): without victories that have been told, there is no courage handed down. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The battle against Og and the parallel account in Numbers 21:33–35 differ markedly in fullness; they are presented side by side, not harmonised: Numbers 21 records the turn toward Bashan, Og's sally, God's word (in wording nearly identical to v. 2 here, registered paraphrase), the striking down of Og, his sons and all his people, and the taking of his land — five verses in all, with no city count, no defences, no treatment detail and no parenthesis. This chapter has the sixty cities, the high walls, gates and bars, the account of destruction and spoil, and after it the allotment and the iron bedstead. The readings of scholarship and the commentary tradition are presented side by side: one reading takes Numbers 21 as the itinerary archive in brief and this chapter as the discourse's expansion — Moses re-tells the old events to the second generation for instruction, and so writes the defences and the treatment in detail, so that "fear not" has evidence. Another reading takes the two as accounts of common origin in different forms, each selecting according to its genre, with no need to align them item by item. A third reading notes that v. 3 here says only "we smote him until none was left to him remaining," while Numbers 21:35 expressly records the striking down of Og "and his sons" (registered paraphrase) — detail complementing detail rather than excluding it. The three readings stand together; the fact layer of this sermon registers only this: the divine word in the two texts is nearly identical, the battle's outcome points the same way, and the detail differs in fullness. No adjudication is made. [Summary layer] One governing sentence for the section: the second victory was fought according to the file of the first — God delivered first, men fought afterwards; there were threescore cities, with walls, gates and bars, and the result lacked not one. Moses kept the account this precisely in order to teach the second generation one thing: when your turn comes, open the file first, then take the field.


### II. Hermon's Three Names and an Iron Bedstead: The Full Stop of the Giants' Age (3:8–11; 16:00–25:00)

[Fact layer] Verses 8–10 are the post-battle summary of the boundaries; v. 8 opens (registered paraphrase): at that time we took, out of the hand of the two kings of the Amorites, the land on this side of the Jordan, from the river of Arnon unto Mount Hermon. Items registered: first, "the two kings of the Amorites" — Sihon and Og are here, for the first time in the chapter, named together as a pair ("these two kings" recurs in v. 21). Second, the land's two limits, south and north: from the river of Arnon in the south to Mount Hermon in the north; chapter 2's fruit (as far as Gilead) and this chapter's fruit (all Bashan) are here joined into one complete map of Transjordan. Verse 9 is a place-name parenthesis (registered paraphrase): this Hermon the Sidonians call Sirion, and the Amorites call it Shenir. One mountain, three names: the Israelites call it Hermon, the Sidonians by the sea call it Sirion, and the Amorites who lived there before call it Shenir — for the account of the names see Note 4: the three names stand side by side within a single verse, and the text does not adjudicate which is the "proper name." In the KJV the verse runs: "(Which Hermon the Sidonians call Sirion; and the Amorites call it Shenir;)". Verse 10 completes the boundaries (registered paraphrase): all the cities of the plain, and all Gilead, and all Bashan, unto Salchah and Edrei, cities of the kingdom of Og in Bashan — Edrei is both the battlefield of v. 1 and the eastern limit of v. 10, one name used twice, registered at head and tail. [Fact layer] Verse 11 is the strangest parenthesis in the chapter; the KJV in full: "For only Og king of Bashan remained of the remnant of giants; behold his bedstead was a bedstead of iron; is it not in Rabbath of the children of Ammon? nine cubits was the length thereof, and four cubits the breadth of it, after the cubit of a man." Items registered: first, "only Og … remained of the remnant" — Og is characterised as a survivor of the Rephaim (rəpāʾîm, H7497; the parenthesis in chapter 2 already registered this people as the giants of the former inhabitants; the KJV renders the word "giants" here and "the land of giants" in v. 13, where the Chinese base text reads "land of the Rephaim" — registered separately). Second, the bedstead's material is iron (barzel, H1270). Third, its dimensions: nine cubits long, four cubits broad, "after the cubit of a man" — the standard of measure is declared first: an ordinary man's cubit, not a "king's cubit." Fourth, the bedstead's whereabouts: "is it not in Rabbath of the children of Ammon?" — an enemy's relic, kept in the capital of a third country, and the narrator in a rhetorical question invites the reader to go and verify: "is it not in …?" Fifth, the parenthesis's position: verses 8–11 contain two parentheses — the parenthesis of Hermon's other names in v. 9 opens and closes within itself, while the iron-bedstead parenthesis opens at the end of v. 10 and closes at the end of v. 11; and the quotation marks of God's speech to Moses do not close until v. 28 (punctuation registered from the Chinese whole-chapter page) — a registration of boundaries and of a relic inserted into the body of the discourse. [Theological-inference layer] Placed in the discourse, this iron bedstead has a purpose aimed at chapter 1's panic: what crushed the first generation in chapter 1 was the "mighty people" and "the children of Anak" in the spies' mouths (1:28, registered paraphrase). In this chapter Moses gives a full account of the last king of the giants' age, bedstead and all — the king is killed, the bedstead remains, kept at Rabbath, where anyone may go and measure it: nine cubits long, four cubits broad — astonishingly large, and yet it has dimensions. What fear dreads most is dimensions: once measured, you know where the boundary lies. Moses does not evade the giant's size; he writes the size as numbers — this is the chapter's second lesson for the second generation: what your fathers dared not measure, we have measured for you. [Theological-inference layer] There is a further layer: the bedstead is kept in a foreign country as "evidence." The narrator did not have the iron bedstead carried back into the camp for display; he registers it in Rabbath of the Ammonites — Ammon being one of the peoples under the three prohibitions of chapter 2, a land Israel was not permitted to attack. The giants' last relic is preserved, of all places, on the very land Israel must not touch, kept by others: the nation that receives the land need not possess every piece of evidence; evidence in another's hand is still evidence. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three matters side by side. First, the nature of the iron bedstead: one reading takes "bedstead" as a bed (the usual meaning of ʿereś, H6210), its size — close to four metres long and two metres broad, in round terms — matching Og's stature; another reading takes it as a stone coffin or basalt sarcophagus — Bashan has much basalt, black and iron-like, and Joseph's simplified paraphrase version registers in a note at this verse "bed, or coffin" (per the translation line on the verse page for 3:11); a third reading takes it as a royal, monument-like display piece. The three readings stand together; the fact layer of this sermon registers only that the base text says "bed," its material "iron," and its dimensions and place of keeping as above, and makes no pronouncement. (The KJV's own wording is "bedstead … a bedstead of iron.") Second, Hermon's three names and the surrounding text: besides this chapter's three names (Hermon / Sirion / Shenir), later texts have other designations and spellings (as in Deut 4:48 and 1 Chronicles 5:23, registered paraphrase); the history of the names is organised differently in scholarship, and this sermon registers only this chapter's three names and who used each (Sidonians / Amorites), setting up no genealogy. Third, the keeping at Rabbath: why the iron bedstead should be in the Ammonite capital — one reading supposes the spoil passed from hand to hand, another an old connection between Og and Ammon, another that the parenthesis was composed in an age when its presence at Rabbath was common knowledge. This sermon registers only the rhetorical form of the text itself: it presupposes that the reader can verify it; that is the parenthesis's rhetorical function, and no origin need be supplied for it. [Summary layer] One governing sentence for the section: the boundaries are written as far as Mount Hermon, and the giant is written down to a single bedstead — the chapter handles "greatness" as two verifiable accounts: the mountain has three names, the bedstead has nine cubits. Moses' answer to fear is not "there is nothing to fear"; it is "the one you feared has already been measured."

### III. The Allotment East of the Jordan: Those Who Receive First, Cross First (3:12–20; 25:00–38:00)

[Fact layer] Verses 12–13 record the giving of the land; v. 12 (registered paraphrase): at that time we possessed this land; from Aroer, which is by the river Arnon, I gave half of Mount Gilead and its cities to the Reubenites and the Gadites. Verse 13 (registered paraphrase): the rest of Gilead, and all Bashan, the kingdom of Og, I gave to the half-tribe of Manasseh; all the region of Argob, with all Bashan, which was called the land of the Rephaim (KJV: "the land of giants"). The account of the recipients (Note 5): Reuben and Gad, two whole tribes, and the half-tribe of Manasseh — three recipient bodies in all. The verb of the giving is in Moses' first person, "gave I" — register this person carefully: the land was delivered by God (vv. 2–3), but the dividing was done by Moses; delivery belongs to God, distribution to man, and the chapter's division of persons is clear from the start. [Fact layer] Verses 14–15 record two men's land; v. 14 (registered paraphrase): Jair the son of Manasseh took all the country of Argob unto the coasts of Geshuri and Maachathi, and called them after his own name, Bashanhavothjair, unto this day (the KJV writes the name as the single compound "Bashanhavothjair"; the Chinese base text reads "Havoth Jair," "the villages of Jair" in sense — the meaning is registered by paraphrase). Verse 15 (registered paraphrase): and I gave Gilead unto Machir. Items registered: first, the two men — Jair and Machir, both descendants of Manasseh. Second, Jair's land is named after him (Havoth Jair, "the villages of Jair"), with the note "unto this day" — at the time of the discourse the name still stood. Third, Machir's receiving Gilead gets a single sentence, in marked contrast with the full record of Numbers 32:39–40 (Machir's descendants took Gilead, drove out the Amorites, and Moses gave Gilead to Machir's descendants, registered paraphrase); the difference in fullness is left to the discussion layer. [Fact layer] Verses 16–17 are the boundary account for Reuben and Gad (registered paraphrase): from Gilead even unto the river Arnon, half the valley, and the border even unto the river Jabbok, which is the border of the children of Ammon; the plain also, and Jordan, and the coast thereof, from Chinnereth even unto the sea of the plain, even the salt sea, under Ashdothpisgah eastward — all this was given to them. The account of the boundary place-names (Note 5): Gilead, the river Arnon valley, the Jabbok, the plain (the Arabah in the base text; the KJV renders it "the plain" — registered separately), the Jordan, Chinnereth, the sea of the plain / the salt sea, and Ashdothpisgah (the slopes of Pisgah) — eight places in all: the Arnon as the southern boundary, the Jabbok as the northern, and the Jordan line as the western. This joins directly onto 2:37, "we did not come near" the Ammonites' land: the Jabbok is at once the northern boundary of Sihon's land, the southern edge of Ammon's land, and the northern boundary of Reuben's and Gad's possession — one river, three identities. [Fact layer] Verses 18–20 are the charge after the allotment; v. 18 in full: "And I commanded you at that time, saying, The Lord your God hath given you this land to possess it: ye shall pass over armed before your brethren the children of Israel, all that are meet for the war." Verse 19 in full: "But your wives, and your little ones, and your cattle, (for I know that ye have much cattle,) shall abide in your cities which I have given you;" Verse 20 in full: "Until the Lord have given rest unto your brethren, as well as unto you, and until they also possess the land which the Lord your God hath given them beyond Jordan: and then shall ye return every man unto his possession, which I have given you." Items registered: first, the charge is addressed to the two and a half tribes who have just received land — "all that are meet for the war" (the original's "all the sons of valour," ḥayil, H2428; "armed" is ḥālûṣ, H2502, the same word as the "armed / equipped" of Numbers 32, registered cross-reference) must all cross over, ahead of their brethren. Second, those who stay are the wives, the little ones and the cattle, abiding in the cities already received. Third, the return is given no date, but a condition: until the brethren also possess the land and are also given rest, "as well as unto you," only then may they return. Fourth, "rest" — the original word is rest (registered paraphrase): the rest of Transjordan comes first, but may not be enjoyed alone; it must wait for the rest of the whole body. [Theological-inference layer] The order of this section is the chapter's ethical hinge: those who receive land first, cross the river first. In this chapter a possession is never a terminus; it is a mobilisation order — your land is in your hand, but your battle is not yet fought; wives and children may stay, the men of war must go; rest may be tasted first, but may not be finished first. Moses' handling of the two and a half tribes follows the same principle as the three prohibitions of chapter 2: in matters of land, God decides; what has been received may not be hoarded, and what has not yet been received may not be waited for idly. When the nine and a half tribes later cross the river, at the very front walk the men of war of this company that has already "come ashore" (registered from the surrounding text: Joshua 1:12–15 restates this command, Joshua 4:12–13 records their crossing at about forty thousand, and Joshua 22 records their return east) — this chapter's charge becomes the foreshadowing of the whole book that follows. [Theological-inference layer] The four words "as well as unto you" (v. 20) are the balancing sentence of the theology of allotment: the rest east and west of the Jordan must be of the same shape, the same quality, and under the same Lord. If those who receive first turn "first" into "alone," the possession becomes the seed of division; Moses lays down the condition of return on the very day of the giving, which is to bury a clause of unity inside the possession in advance. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The allotment account and the parallel in Numbers 32 differ; they are presented side by side, not harmonised. First: Numbers 32 records a request first — Reuben and Gad actively ask for the land east of the Jordan; Moses rebukes them and lays down conditions (cross armed; return only after the land is possessed); the two tribes agree, and the half-tribe of Manasseh appears only at the giving (Num 32:33, registered paraphrase). This chapter is throughout Moses' giving and charging, with no request, no rebuke, and no list of city names (Numbers 32 keeps an account of fourteen places of city-building; this chapter has not one city name in the allotment section). One reading takes this chapter as the discourse's abbreviation — the teaching's centre is "those who receive first, cross first," so only the charge is kept. Another reading takes the two narratives' moments as different — Numbers 32 records the event at the time, this chapter is the summary in retrospect at the end of the forty years, and genre decides the fullness. Second, Jair's receiving: Numbers 32:41 records that Jair took "villages" (within Gilead) and renamed them Havoth Jair; this chapter's v. 14 has him take "all the country of Argob" unto the coasts of Geshuri and Maachathi — the stated scope is small in one place and large in the other; registered side by side. Third, Joshua 13:30 records Jair's villages as sixty (registered from the surrounding text) — the same number as Og's sixty cities in v. 4 of this chapter, but a different referent (there, the villages Jair took; here, the cities taken from Og); on the relation of the two numbers, some in scholarship take them as the same territory differently described, others as each referring to its own matter; this sermon registers only the two texts and does not adjudicate. [Summary layer] One governing sentence for the section: in this chapter's allotment, names are written first, and conditions second — the names on the map, the conditions inside the possession: those who receive land first cross the river at the very front, and only when their brethren, like them, have been given rest, may they return to live in their own cities.


### IV. The Two Kings as a Textbook: Joshua's First Lesson (3:21–22; 38:00–45:00)

[Fact layer] Verses 21–22 are the chapter's shortest section, yet they are the discourse's hinge. Verse 21 in full: "And I commanded Joshua at that time, saying, Thine eyes have seen all that the Lord your God hath done unto these two kings: so shall the Lord do unto all the kingdoms whither thou passest." Verse 22 in full: "Ye shall not fear them: for the Lord your God he shall fight for you." Items registered: first, "at that time" — the fifth of the chapter's six occurrences of "at that time" (Note 3); the allotment has just ended, and the charge comes at once to the man who will take over. Second, "these two kings" — Sihon and Og are named together twice in this chapter (v. 8, "the two kings of the Amorites," and here, "these two kings"), and Sihon is additionally cited twice as a precedent (vv. 2, 6); the two kings' function in the chapter is by now plain: they are the textbook. Third, "Thine eyes have seen" — Joshua's qualification is not a curriculum vitae but eyewitness testimony: he was present at both kings' fall. Fourth, "all the kingdoms whither thou passest" — the kingdoms west of the river have not yet been engaged, and already they are brought within the scope of the promise: the two kingdoms of the past guarantee all the kingdoms to come. Fifth, the "Ye shall not fear them" of v. 22 is the chapter's second "fear not" (in v. 2 to Moses, its object the singular king; in v. 22 to Joshua — in the KJV its subject "Ye" and its "you" are plural in form, where the Chinese base text is singular; registered in the headnote and the final record, not harmonised); "he shall fight for you" is the participial form of lāḥam (H3898) — "the One who is fighting for you" — checked directly against the BHS text line on the verse page for 3:22. [Theological-inference layer] This section sets out the order of transmission: first eyewitness, then charge; first history, then courage. Moses does not simply say to Joshua, "Be brave," and finish; he first points it out to him: how the two kings fell, you saw with your own eyes. Courage in this chapter is not a temperament; it is the product of memory — answering, head to tail, the lesson of "precedent" in the first section: God's deeds, seen, told and remembered, and only then does the next man's turn come not to fear. The transmission of the church and of the family follows the same pattern: the next generation's faith is mostly not produced by hearing doctrines, but by watching, all along the way, how the generation before was led by God. [Theological-inference layer] Note also the charge's address: these words were not spoken on the battlefield, but after the allotment and before the crossing, on the occasion of a handover. Moses leaves his most important sentence to the man who takes over, and the subject of that sentence is not Joshua but the LORD — "the Lord your God he shall fight for you." What the successor needs most is not the predecessor's full set of methods, but the predecessor's God: methods change with circumstances; this One does not change. [New Testament reading layer] The New Testament's echoes of this chapter's motif, "He fights for you," are registered at the reading level only: in Romans 8, "If God be for us, who can be against us?" and "in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us" (registered paraphrase) — its logic is of the same shape as this chapter's: the root of victory lies in "God for us"; and in 1 Corinthians 15 the resurrection victory song declares that the victory is given by the Lord (registered paraphrase). The New Testament nowhere quotes Deuteronomy 3 verse by verse; this layer registers that absence as it stands and forces no correspondence. The chapter's own resting point is the historical guarantee of v. 21 and the theological pronouncement of v. 22. [Summary layer] One governing sentence for the section: the chapter's shortest two verses are its eye: Moses folds the two kings' fall into one sentence and hands it to Joshua — your eyes have seen; do not fear; it is the LORD who fights for you. Once history has been preached to this point, it is no longer history; it is a marching order.

### V. Moses' Plea: A Prayer Refused (3:23–29; 45:00–57:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 23 opens; its first sentence in full: "And I besought the Lord at that time, saying," — this "at that time" is the sixth of the chapter's six occurrences: after the allotment and the charge, it is Moses' own affair's turn. "Besought" is the hitpael of the root ḥānan (word form registered; number unverified, Note 11) — the root's basic sense is to be gracious, to show mercy: Moses' prayer, from its very first word, is a plea for grace — not a claim on merit, but a begging of a gracious God for grace. Verse 24 is the prayer's word of praise; the KJV in full: "O Lord God, thou hast begun to shew thy servant thy greatness, and thy mighty hand: for what God is there in heaven or in earth, that can do according to thy works, and according to thy might?" Items registered: first, the address is "O Lord God" — the chapter's only prayer opened with this address. Second, "thou hast begun to shew thy servant" — the KJV carries the word "begun" (ḥālal, H2490), which the Chinese base text does not translate and the 2010 version supplies as "Thou hast begun to show Thy greatness and Thy mighty hand to Thy servant"; registered separately (Note 8): Moses' meaning is that, in his eyes, the two kings' defeat was only the "beginning" of God's works — what he asks is to see how this beginning continues. Third, the praise's question, "for what God is there…?", is in the same key as the victory song's question in Exodus 15:11 (registered from the surrounding text): the prayer of Moses near his end uses the cadence he sang forty years before beside the Red Sea. [Fact layer] Verse 25 is the prayer's actual request; the KJV in full: "I pray thee, let me go over, and see the good land that is beyond Jordan, that goodly mountain, and Lebanon." Items registered: first, what is asked is one thing in two parts: to go over, and to see — "go over" is to cross the Jordan (the original ʿābar, of the same root as God's "was wroth" in v. 26; see the separate register in Note 10); "see" is to see with his own eyes, not to possess, not to dwell. Moses does not ask for the whole sentence to be revoked; he asks only to be allowed to step onto that land and have one look at it. Second, the land's designations are "the good land," "that goodly mountain, and Lebanon" — forty years of hope, landing on three phrases. Third, the prayer's close has no self-concluding "Amen"; what follows it is God's answer. [Fact layer] Verse 26 records the answer; the KJV in full: "But the Lord was wroth with me for your sakes, and would not hear me: and the Lord said unto me, Let it suffice thee; speak no more unto me of this matter." Items registered: first, the attribution is "for your sakes" — in the same pattern as 1:37, "the LORD was wroth with me for your sakes" (registered paraphrase); on its surface it differs from the attribution of Numbers 20:12 (that Moses and Aaron "believed not" God and did not sanctify Him before the children of Israel, registered paraphrase); the two are presented side by side and left to the discussion layer. Second, the original construction of "Let it suffice thee" is rav (much / enough, H7227) plus the preposition "to thee" — its idiomatic sense is "enough; stop here" (word-meaning registered). Third, "speak no more" is a prohibitive cutting-off: this matter ends here; even the prayer may not be continued. Verse 27 records the substitute God gave Moses; the KJV in full: "Get thee up into the top of Pisgah, and lift up thine eyes westward, and northward, and southward, and eastward, and behold it with thine eyes: for thou shalt not go over this Jordan." What was asked was "go over and see"; what was given was "go up and see": the seeing was granted; the going over was not. The order of the four directions in the KJV follows the Hebrew order (west, north, south, east), where the Chinese base text gives the Chinese conventional order (east, west, south, north) — already registered separately in the headnote (Note 9). Verse 28 records the errand God gave Moses; the KJV in full: "But charge Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him: for he shall go over before this people, and he shall cause them to inherit the land which thou shalt see." The words "But charge Joshua" are the nail of the turn: you cannot go over, but you are to send him over. The three commands — "charge" (ṣāwâ, H6680), "encourage," and "strengthen him" (ḥāzaq, H2388; ʾāmaṣ, H553 — the last word of the same root and number as the ʾāmaṣ used of Sihon's hardened temper in 2:30; the intertextual registration is in Note 10); "the land which thou shalt see" — Moses' portion is seeing, Joshua's portion is causing to inherit; one land, two portions. Verse 29 closes the chapter (registered paraphrase): so we abode in the valley over against Bethpeor — the discourse's location is named only here: the place where Moses spoke this whole address is the valley over against Bethpeor, east of the Jordan, on the bank from which one can see across but cannot enter. [Theological-inference layer] This section is the chapter's heaviest, because it is honest: Moses did not edit out his own failure. Before the second generation he told his most painful prayer exactly as it happened — how he asked, how God refused, and how blunt the refusal was. A leader's testimony is complete only at this point: not only how God used him to win battles, but also how God said no to him. A refused prayer written into the discourse is not self-pity; it is teaching material: the second generation must know that their leader is not an omnipotent man — he asked, he did not receive, and he is still standing here preaching. The definition of obedience is completed only at the end of this chapter: it is not obedience after every request is granted; it is, after "Let it suffice thee" has been said, still going up the mountain to look, and still charging Joshua. [Theological-inference layer] Within God's refusal there are two gifts, stated here with restraint at the inference level only, without going beyond the text. First, the grace of seeing — the account of "see" in this section: in v. 25 Moses asks to "see"; in v. 27 God gives "behold it with thine eyes"; in v. 28 He settles that Joshua will cause them to inherit "the land which thou shalt see." Moses' blessing to the eyes is not diminished but enlarged: he sees more than he asked — a panorama in four directions, and shown to him by God Himself. Second, the grace of an errand — the refused man is not left idle: his last stretch of road is to put courage into the successor's hand. A man's hardest obedience is often not accepting the "cannot," but, after accepting it, still personally bringing to completion the man who "can." [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three matters side by side. First, the attribution of Moses' exclusion from the land: within Deuteronomy the same pattern occurs twice — 1:37 and this chapter's v. 26 both read "for your sakes"; Numbers 20:12 attributes it to Moses and Aaron at Meribah, "ye believed me not, to sanctify me"; Psalm 106:32–33 speaks of the people's provoking and Moses' speaking rashly (registered paraphrase). The readings side by side: one reading takes Deuteronomy as Moses' self-account in a defensive key (his fault bound up with the people's sin, the attribution stressing solidarity); another takes Numbers 20 as the sentence at the time of the event and Deuteronomy as the emphasis in retrospect, the two read together giving the full picture (the Meribah affair itself arose from the people's strife); a third takes the two attributions as belonging to different genres (narrative sentence and discourse self-account), neither cancelling the other. This sermon registers only the attributive wording of the three texts and does not adjudicate. Second, the place of the mountain viewing: Numbers 27:12 reads "mount Abarim," this chapter's v. 27 "the top of Pisgah," Deuteronomy 32:49 Mount Nebo (in the Abarim range), and Deuteronomy 34:1 "Mount Nebo … the top of Pisgah" (registered paraphrase) — three forms of mountain name (Abarim / Nebo / Pisgah); tradition mostly reads them as different names for the same range, though some distinguish range and peak; this sermon registers only and makes no determination. Third, Moses' end after "Let it suffice thee": Deuteronomy 34 records Moses ascending the mountain, seeing the land, dying and being buried (registered paraphrase); how its verdict, "his eye was not dim, nor his natural force abated," answers this chapter's "seeing" motif belongs to a whole-scroll reading, and this sermon registers only that this chapter ends at v. 29, "abode in the valley" — it does not read chapter 34's ending forward as this chapter's comfort. The chapter's own ending is remaining in the valley. [Summary layer] One governing sentence for the section: what Moses asked for was the crossing; what God gave was the mountain. What Moses asked for was one look; what God gave was a panorama in four directions plus an errand — to charge the man who would cross in his place. The chapter's last lesson is the leader's lesson: when you have been told "Let it suffice thee," hand the courage over, dwell yourself in the valley, and watch another inherit.

Conclusion (57:00–60:00). [Theological-inference layer] Look back over the chapter: it begins with "we turned" and ends with "we abode in the valley"; between them stand threescore cities, an iron bedstead, the possessions of two and a half tribes, two verses spoken to Joshua, and a refused prayer. Chapter 1 asked why this generation could not enter; chapter 2 answered with restraint and a first victory. This chapter answers at a deeper level: the matter of receiving the land is, from beginning to end, God's matter — He fights, so the threescore cities lack not one; He allots, so those who receive first must cross first; He bars the way, so even Moses stops east of the Jordan. For today's hearers: we each have our own "two kings" in hand — battles God has already fought for us — and we must first learn to tell them, to our children and to those who will take over from us, as their evidence for not fearing. And we each have our own "Let it suffice thee" — things asked for and not received. The order of this chapter is the order of the road of faith: count the victories, without boasting; accept the barring, without leaving the field; put courage into the next person's hand, and dwell contentedly in the valley ourselves. May we, when we are afraid, remember who it is that fights for us; and may we, when we have been told "Let it suffice thee," still with our own hands encourage the one who is to go over, and strengthen him.

## Devotional Extract (for Daily Devotional Use)

"But charge Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him: for he shall go over before this people, and he shall cause them to inherit the land which thou shalt see." (Deut 3:28, KJV) Moses had pleaded, and God said: Let it suffice thee; speak no more of this matter. The land he had hoped for over forty years he was allowed only to see, not to enter. A refusal like that is hard for anyone to bear. Yet God did not let Moses leave that prayer empty-handed: He gave him a mountain, from which to look in all four directions; He gave him a man, to encourage with his own hands. So it is that when God says "no," it is often not a door closing but an errand changed — you cannot go over, but you are to send him over. Our lives have such moments too: that matter pleaded for so long, and He did not grant it; that step that simply cannot be crossed, and the heart is left with only "Why?" Moses' example stands in the valley east of the Jordan: he did not stop at the refusal; he went up the mountain and looked; he charged Joshua; he handed the courage over. Some lands we are only permitted, in this life, to see; but seeing is not wasted — what we have seen is to become another's courage when he inherits. If today you are standing on your own east bank of the Jordan, do not be quick to condemn yourself: go up the mountain, and see clearly what He will still accomplish; then turn round, encourage the "Joshua" beside you, and strengthen him. The step you cannot take may be exactly the step you are to send another across with your own hands. Prayer: Lord, thank You that even within refusal You leave an errand. Teach us, after "Let it suffice thee," not to lose heart; to go up the mountain and see what You will do, and with our own hands to encourage the one we are to bring to completion. May what we can see become another's courage to inherit. Amen.

### Devotional Extract (English, KJV-based)

Read Deuteronomy 3:28 (KJV): "But charge Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him: for he shall go over before this people, and he shall cause them to inherit the land which thou shalt see." Moses had pleaded to cross the Jordan, and God said no — "Let it suffice thee; speak no more unto me of this matter" (3:26, KJV). Yet the refusal came with an assignment: the man who could not go over was to strengthen the man who would. A denied prayer is not a dismissed life. Sometimes God closes the crossing and opens a mountain: you may see the land, and your seeing becomes someone else's courage. If you are standing on the east bank today, do what Moses did — climb, look, and then turn to your Joshua and put heart into him. The step you cannot take may be the step you are called to send another across.


## Endnotes (Accounts Rechecked by Hand)

Note 1 (The chapter's position and the discourse's divisions): This chapter belongs to the closing narrative section of Deuteronomy's first discourse (1:1–4:43): chapter 1 reviews the cause of the delay, chapter 2 records the first victory, this chapter the second victory and the first allotment, and chapter 4 turns to the review's conclusion and the exhortation to obey (registered paraphrase). The discourse's geographical point is named only at the chapter's end (v. 29, "the valley over against Bethpeor"), corroborating 1:5, "on this side Jordan, in the land of Moab" (registered paraphrase).

Note 2 (Structural account): The whole chapter has 29 verses: battle against Og, vv. 1–7 (7) + boundaries and parentheses, vv. 8–11 (4) + allotment, vv. 12–20 (9) + charge to Joshua, vv. 21–22 (2) + Moses' plea, vv. 23–29 (7) = 7 + 4 + 9 + 2 + 7 = 29. Two parentheses: the Hermon-alias parenthesis of v. 9 opens and closes within itself; the iron-bedstead parenthesis opens at the end of v. 10 and closes at the end of v. 11 (punctuation registered from the Chinese whole-chapter page; the KJV likewise sets vv. 9 and 11 in parentheses).

Note 3 (Speech and "at that time" account): God's words in two blocks — v. 2 (delivery before the battle) and vv. 26–28 (refusal of the prayer and the handing over). Moses' charges in two blocks — vv. 18–20 (to the two and a half tribes) and vv. 21–22 (to Joshua). Moses' prayer in one block — vv. 23–25 (praise, v. 24; request proper, v. 25). "At that time" occurs six times in the KJV text as fetched ("at that time" in vv. 4, 8, 12, 18, 21, 23) — exactly at each section's narrative pivot, with vv. 21 and 23 in immediate succession, marking the discourse's double turn from allotment to handover, and from handover to Moses' private affair. Joshua's name occurs twice: vv. 21, 28, both in charge contexts (God's / Moses').

Note 4 (Numerical account I — battle and bedstead): Cities, threescore — sixty — (v. 4, KJV "threescore cities"); bedstead nine cubits long, four cubits broad (v. 11), the standard of measure "after the cubit of a man"; Mount Hermon has 3 names: Hermon (the Israelites' name, registered from the text), Sirion (the Sidonians), Shenir (the Amorites), vv. 8–9 (KJV spellings "Sirion" and "Shenir," as in the Chinese base text's 西连 / 示尼珥). The "deliver" account: the delivery sentences of nātan (H5414) occur twice in this chapter — v. 2, "I will deliver … into thy hand," and v. 3, "delivered into our hands" (with chapter 2's three occurrences, the delivery sentences across the two chapters are five in all). "Fear not" occurs twice: v. 2 ("Fear him not," to Moses) and v. 22 ("Ye shall not fear them," to Joshua — plural in the KJV; see the headnote). Og's name occurs six times in the KJV: vv. 1, 3, 4, 10, 11, 13. Sihon's name occurs twice: vv. 2, 6 (both precedent-style mentions).

Note 5 (Numerical account II — allotment): Recipient bodies, 3: the Reubenites (vv. 12, 16), the Gadites (vv. 12, 16), and the half-tribe of Manasseh (v. 13). Individual recipients, 2: Jair (v. 14) and Machir (v. 15), both descendants of Manasseh. Boundary place-names, 8 (vv. 16–17, KJV forms): Gilead, the river Arnon (valley), the river Jabbok, the plain, Jordan, Chinnereth, the sea of the plain (the salt sea), Ashdothpisgah. "Possession / possess" in the allotment charge: v. 18, "hath given you this land to possess it," and v. 20, "his possession, which I have given you" — the base text's "for a possession / for an inheritance" occurs twice (vv. 18, 20). The eye motif occurs three times: v. 21, "Thine eyes have seen"; v. 27, "behold it with thine eyes"; v. 28, "the land which thou shalt see." Total of narrative place-names in the chapter, counted on the KJV text: 21 — Edrei, Heshbon, Bashan, Argob, Arnon, Hermon, Gilead, Salchah, Rabbath, Aroer, Geshuri, Maachathi, Bashanhavothjair, Jabbok, the plain, Chinnereth, the salt sea, Pisgah (including Ashdothpisgah in v. 17), Lebanon, Bethpeor, Jordan. The total agrees with the Chinese source manuscript's count of 21; the forms differ from the Chinese base text's list at four points — "Rabbath" (base text: Rabbah), the compound "Bashanhavothjair" written as one word (base text: Havoth Jair), "the plain" for the Arabah, and "Ashdothpisgah" in v. 17 where the base text has "the slopes of Pisgah" — registered separately in the first-translation record, the numbers not altered. (Heshbon enters the account as a place-reference, as in "which dwelt at Heshbon"; Rabbath as the place where the relic is kept. Sirion and Shenir are Hermon's alternate names and are not counted as separate places, exactly as in the source manuscript.)

Note 6 (Registration of 3:4): The Chinese base text's v. 4 wording, rendered "this is all the region of Argob," is reproduced as it stands in the source and registered here; it is not rewritten in the body as "this was all." The KJV reads "threescore cities, all the region of Argob, the kingdom of Og in Bashan," and the English quotations follow the KJV. Argob's scope is restated twice in vv. 13–14 (all the region of Argob = all Bashan); the three places corroborate one another (registered paraphrase).

Note 7 (Collated variants between base text and the 2010 version, summary; KJV readings added): (i) 3:6, "inhabited cities": the 2010 version reads "the men, women and children of every city"; the KJV reads "the men, women, and children, of every city," agreeing with the 2010 version. (ii) 3:11, no "Behold" in the base text: the 2010 version adds "Behold" at the head; the KJV has "behold." (iii) 3:18, clause punctuation: a semicolon in the base text, a full stop in the modern-punctuation Union Version; the KJV uses a colon after "to possess it." (iv) 3:22, person: singular "thou … for thee" in the base text, plural in the 2010 version — the base text's singular corresponds to the charge's addressee in v. 21 (Joshua personally); the KJV is plural ("Ye shall not fear them … for you"); this sermon narrates according to the base text and registers only, without adjudicating. (v) 3:24, "begun": absent from the base text, present in the 2010 version and in the KJV ("thou hast begun to shew") — see Note 8. (vi) 3:26, "Let it suffice thee": the 2010 version reads "Enough for you!"; the KJV reads "Let it suffice thee." (vii) 3:27, order of directions — see Note 9; the KJV follows the Hebrew order. (viii) 3:28, word order: the base text "make him strong-hearted," the 2010 version "strengthen his heart"; the KJV reads "encourage him, and strengthen him." In all eight places the Chinese source manuscript's quotations follow the base text; in this English version all quotations follow the KJV anchor, and the divergences are registered, not removed.

Note 8 (Note on "begun" in 3:24 in the original): The original hāḥillôtā is the causative-stem "begin" sense of ḥālal (H2490), seen directly in the BHS text line on the verse page for 3:24. The Chinese base text renders it "Thou hast shown Thy great power to Thy servant," without translating "begun"; the 2010 version renders "Thou hast begun to show Thy greatness and Thy mighty hand to Thy servant," supplying the word; the KJV likewise reads "thou hast begun to shew thy servant thy greatness, and thy mighty hand." The fact layer of the source manuscript registers the difference between the two Chinese versions: a reader of the base text does not see "begun," a reader of the original does — the rhetoric of Moses' prayer (God's works have only begun, and he wishes to see them continue) is built on this word in the original; where the inference layer builds on it, the basis is stated as the original, without pretending it is the base text's literal wording. In this English version the word stands in the KJV quotation itself.

Note 9 (Note on the order of directions in 3:27): The original's order is yāmmâ (toward the sea / west), ṣāp̄ôn (north), têmān (south), mizrāḥ (east) — the order of the four words seen directly in the BHS text line on the verse page for 3:27 (the three unverified forms yām, têmān, mizrāḥ are recorded by form only). The Chinese base text reads "east, west, south, north," the conventional Chinese order of the four quarters; English versions (such as the KJV, "westward, and northward, and southward, and eastward," quoted here from the anchor itself) mostly follow the original's order. This sermon registers only the difference of order and does not treat it as an error in the base text (ordering a translation by the target language's convention is standard translation practice). Genesis 13:14, Abram lifting up his eyes to look in the four directions (registered paraphrase) — the same "lift up your eyes, look in four directions, and the land is given" pattern has, in the Chinese base text, the same order in both places, an internal consistency of that version; the contrast between this chapter's "look, but not enter" and Genesis's "look, and receive the promise" is set side by side at the reading level only, with no pronouncement on original meaning.

Note 10 (Intertextual registration of ʾāmaṣ and ʿābar): First, ʾāmaṣ (H553): Deuteronomy 2:30 records that God made Sihon's spirit "stiff" (causative stem, registered paraphrase); this chapter's 3:28 records God's command that Moses "strengthen" Joshua (causative stem; the form wəʾamməṣēhû seen directly in the BHS text line on the verse page for 3:28) — the same word in the same stem, used in one place of an enemy king's hardening and in the other of a successor's strengthening; this sermon registers only the word form and the two contexts, and draws no inference that "same word" means "same intent." Second, ʿābar (H5674): in this chapter's v. 25 Moses asks to "go over" (ʿābar); in v. 26 God "was wroth" (the hitpael of ʿābar, glossed in the lexica as a posture of anger; the form wayyitʿabbēr seen directly in the BHS text line on the verse page for 3:26); in v. 27, "thou shalt not go over (ʿābar) this Jordan"; in v. 28 Joshua "shall go over" (ʿābar) — one word runs through the section: asking to cross, God's wrath, not crossing, the other man crossing. Scholarship often discusses this section's rhetoric by way of this common-root phenomenon; this sermon registers the distribution of the forms, leaves the reading open, and makes no lexicographical conclusion.

> Reading notes: Five things must be nailed down before teaching this chapter. First, the Amalekite young man's account does not match 1 Samuel 31: there Saul falls on his own sword and his armor-bearer does the same (cf. 1 Sam 31:4-6); here the young man says Saul was leaning on his spear, asked him to strike, and that he killed Saul. Two readings coexist among commentators and are registered here without adjudication: either the young man lied to claim credit, inventing the killing to win a reward, the crown and bracelet being trophies he picked up or seized on the battlefield; or Saul's self-inflicted wound had not yet killed him and the young man finished what Saul asked. This manuscript does not force the two together and does not decide between them; but on either reading, David condemns him solely on his own confession: you said with your own mouth, I have killed the LORD's anointed — your mouth testified against you. David judges not the hidden truth but what this man confessed and offered as merit. Second, David's weeping was not a performance: his only audience was his own followers; no elders of Israel were present, no ceremony of succession; he wept until evening and fasted before he had gained anything and before the news had even spread. He then executed the bearer of the news, composed a song to teach the people, and cursed Mount Gilboa; in the lament he records only Saul's kindness and achievements, not one word of his sin. Nowhere in the chapter are tears exchanged for advantage; the preacher must not turn David's grief into political craft. Third, David's execution of the messenger was a sentence pronounced, in his role as the anointed successor and judge, on a self-confessed act of regicide (details in the text); it is not a model for private revenge. By the young man's own account, the man he killed was Saul, who had hunted David for years — by private grudges David should have rewarded him; instead he killed him. Hearers today must not use this chapter to justify any private violence, revenge, or execution; its application lies in reverence for the LORD's anointed and reverence for confession and speech, not in imitating the sword. Fourth, the lament calls Jonathan's love wonderful, surpassing the love of women (cf. 2 Sam 1:26, condensed rendering): the original speaks of a covenant friend's loyalty exceeding the weight of ordinary affection, and commentators consistently read it as covenant loyalty and kindred friendship, not otherwise. Teach it as covenant friendship, recounting how David and Jonathan made a covenant and how Jonathan yielded and rescued him in 1 Samuel 18, 20, and 23; do not read anything else into it. Fifth, the Song of the Bow is a publicly taught song, yet it charges that the enemy's defeat-news not be proclaimed in Gath and Ashkelon: mourning has its object, its occasion, and its measure. When the church speaks of failure and death, learn this measure: teach, remember, and weep within; do not use another's fall as publicity material outside, and do not let the enemy make a festival of our tears.

## Synopsis

Second Samuel 1 is the threshold between the two books: the previous book ends with Saul falling on his sword and the men of Jabesh traveling by night to recover the bodies; this book begins not with a coronation but with news of a death. An Amalekite young man with torn clothes and dust on his head runs into Ziklag and presents Saul's crown and bracelet to David, hoping to trade an enemy's death for a future. David gives him three things he never expected: he tears his own clothes, he mourns and fasts until evening, and he sentences the young man to death on his own confession. Then David composes the Song of the Bow and teaches it to the people of Judah, singing "How the mighty have fallen" for Saul who had hunted him for years, and "I am distressed for you" for Jonathan his covenant friend. This sermon treats three things: how an opportunist dies by his own calculation and his own confession; how a successor takes over an era with tears rather than cheers; and how a lament teaches a nation to remember the fallen, honor an enemy, and weep for a friend. The change of dynasty in this book opens not with a victory song but with a lament; before the man after God's own heart takes the throne, he first learns to weep.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, today we stand on the threshold between two books: behind us the defeat at Mount Gilboa, before us a man not yet crowned who hears news of a death. Teach us to read David's tears: the day he had waited for over many years truly came, and he did not laugh — he tore his clothes, he fasted, he made a song. Restrain in us that small delight that waits to see others fall, and restrain the calculation that would use news, death-news, and other people's failures to pave our own way. For those among us who have just lost a dear friend or a family member, accompany them Yourself until the lament is sung to the end; for those among us who are about to take over and rise, teach them first reverence, first remembrance, first not to climb up by stepping on those who went before. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: The Third Day at Ziklag

Fix the time first. The chapter opens by telling us: after Saul's death, David had struck down the Amalekites and returned to Ziklag, where he stayed two days (cf. 2 Sam 1:1, condensed rendering). The striking down of the Amalekites is the event of 1 Samuel 30: David recovered the two households' people and goods that the Amalekites had carried off, and nothing was missing. On the third day after David returned to Ziklag, the messenger arrived: Gilboa is in the north, Ziklag in the south, and the whole of Israel lies between the battlefield and the exile's camp. The Bible hides a quiet contrast here: on the same third day, the people at Ziklag had just recovered everything from the ashes, while the people from Gilboa brought the crown of a king. Within a single day David was first made a victor and then pushed to the threshold of succession; his first act on that threshold was not to reach for the crown but to tear his clothes.

The chapter has three parts. Part one, the report and the sentence (vv. 1-16): the young man brings the crown and bracelet, David asks how he knows, the young man recounts the killing, David's company mourns, weeps, and fasts until evening, David ascertains the young man's origin and sentences him to death. Part two, the first half of the lament (vv. 17-22): David composes the Song of the Bow, orders it taught to the people of Judah, charges that it not be told in Gath and Ashkelon, wishes Mount Gilboa no rain and no fields, and sings that Saul and Jonathan were not divided in life or death, swifter than eagles, stronger than lions. Part three, the second half of the lament (vv. 23-27): David calls the daughters of Israel to weep for Saul, turns to his personal cry for Jonathan, and closes with "How the mighty have fallen in the midst of the battle" and "how the weapons of war have perished."

Three threads run through the chapter. The thread of the crown: the crown passes from Saul's head into the young man's hands and then before David, yet no verse says David put it on. In this chapter the crown is evidence, not a throne: it proves the king is dead, proves the messenger has the king's blood on him, and proves David took nothing of the throne in this chapter. The first thing the successor touches is not power but news of death; how he treats this delivered crown decides whether he is a usurper or the anointed. The thread of confession: every turn in the chapter hangs on words. The young man seeks merit with his mouth and is condemned by the same mouth: your own mouth has testified against you. From Genesis onward the Bible records how a person's words testify about himself; this chapter draws that thread tightest: what a person says, he must answer for — above all where the life and death of God's anointed is concerned, words must not be spoken carelessly and merit must not be claimed carelessly. The thread of tears: the previous book closes with the seven-day fast of the men of Jabesh; this book opens with David's company weeping until evening. The two mournings face each other across the books: the people fast for the king, and the king's successor also fasts for the king; the new era does not come cheering over the corpse of the old — two generations hand it over weeping together.

In reading this chapter, keep three measures. First, do not cite the young man's confession as supplementary history correcting 1 Samuel 31: the discrepancy between the two accounts is plain; the pulpit must register the divergence honestly, teach only by this chapter's own sentence, neither cover the young man's possible lie nor rewrite chapter 31 from this chapter. Second, do not preach David's lament as though his grievances with Saul were simply written off into warmth: David's later dealings with Saul's house, and Joab's killings of Abner and Amasa, have their own texts and judgments (cf. 2 Samuel 3 and 21, registered as a series); this chapter speaks only of this chapter — the David of this chapter truly wept, and the kindness recorded in the lament is true kindness; there is no need to defend or condemn later chapters in advance. Third, do not miss that the Song of the Bow was ordered to be taught to the people of Judah: mourning in this chapter is not private emotion but public education. A leader who teaches his people how to sing for the fallen is teaching the character of the nation: whether this nation will become a nation that gloats or a nation that weeps and remembers begins with this lesson.

## Part One: The Report and the Sentence (2 Samuel 1:1-16)

### 1. Clothes Torn, Dust on His Head (2 Samuel 1:2-4)

On the third day a man came from Saul's camp, his clothes torn and dust on his head; he came to David, fell to the ground, and bowed. David asked where he came from; he said he had escaped from the camp of Israel. David asked how the battle had gone; he said the people had fled, many had fallen and died, and Saul and his son Jonathan were also dead (cf. 2 Sam 1:2-4, condensed summary).

"Torn" is from the root קָרַע (qāraʿ, H7167), to tear, to rend. See BDB, s.v. קרע. [1] "Dust, earth" is עָפָר (ʿāpār, H6083), dust. See BDB, s.v. עפר. [2] Torn clothes and dust on the head were the customary dress of bereavement and lament: this young man wore mourning on his body when he came to David. Here the preacher must first help the hearers see the man clearly: the costume may be true grief, or it may be a facade performed for others — and the chapter soon shows this mourning dress worn by a man seeking merit. He ran fast, his courtesies were complete, and he reported first the people's flight and deaths, then landed on Saul and Jonathan — the order of his news was arranged with great measure, the speech of a man who had rehearsed it. David's first reaction deserves the chapter's full attention: he does not ask about the crown; he asks about the battle. He does not ask what he can gain; he asks how the people are. The exile of many years, hearing news of his homeland's defeat, asks first how many of the people fled and how many died — that is the ear of a king. We too have an ear when we hear news: some first ask what is in it for me, others first ask who is suffering. The first lesson of this chapter is not on the battlefield; it is in the order in which we listen to news.

### 2. How Do You Know Saul Is Dead? (2 Samuel 1:5-10)

David asked the young man who brought the news: How do you know that Saul and his son Jonathan are dead? The young man said: By chance I happened to be on Mount Gilboa, and there was Saul leaning on his spear, with the chariots and horsemen pressing close behind him. He looked behind him, saw me, and called to me; I said, Here I am. He asked who I was; I said, I am an Amalekite. He said to me: Stand beside me and kill me, for anguish has seized me and my life still lingers in me. And I was sure that he could not live after he had fallen, so I stood beside him and killed him, and I took the crown that was on his head and the bracelet that was on his arm and brought them to my lord (cf. 2 Sam 1:5-10, condensed summary).

"Crown" is נֵזֶר (nēzer, H5145), a crown, a mark of consecration. See BDB, s.v. נזר. [3] "Bracelet," the bracelet on the arm, is אֶצְעָדָה (ʾeṣʿādâ, H685), an arm bracelet. See BDB, s.v. אצעדה. [4] "Amalekite" is עֲמָלֵקִי (ʿĂmālēqî, H6003), a member of the Amalekite people. See BDB, s.v. עמלקי. [5] Hear David's question first: How do you know? These words are the calmest sentence in the chapter. A man who had waited many years hears that his enemy is dead and the throne is empty, and his first words are not cheers, not a reward, but a demand for evidence. The more fluently the young man answers, the bigger the cracks: he happened to be passing by, he answered without hesitation, he stood beside him, he killed him with his own hand, and he brought all the credentials. Every step is too complete — complete like a written petition for merit. Per reading note one, this manuscript does not decide whether he lied or finished the blow; it nails down only the logic of the verdict the text itself gives: he confessed to killing the LORD's anointed and offered it as merit. Twice, in 1 Samuel 24 and 26, David had the chance to kill Saul and would not stretch out his hand, and was even stricken in conscience for cutting off the corner of his robe (cf. 1 Samuel 24 and 26, treated in an earlier manuscript of this series); now a sojourning foreign young man runs in and says he has done for David what David for years refused to do, and presents it as a gift. He miscalculated one fundamental thing: David was waiting for God's time, not for news of Saul's death; what David wanted was what God established, not what he seized himself or what another seized for him. Preach this layer to everyone who wants to act on behalf of a leader, on behalf of God, on behalf of history: the merit you imagine may be your heaviest sin; God's anointed is not for opportunists to dispose of.

### 3. Mourning, Weeping, and Fasting Until Evening (2 Samuel 1:11-12)

David tore his own clothes, and so did the men who were with him; and they mourned, wept, and fasted until evening for Saul, for Jonathan, and for the people of the LORD, because they had fallen by the sword (cf. 2 Sam 1:11-12, condensed summary).

"Mourn" is from סָפַד (sāpad, H5594), to mourn, to wail, beating the breast. See BDB, s.v. ספד. [6] "Fast" is צוּם (ṣûm, H6684), to fast. See BDB, s.v. צום. [7] The messenger's torn clothes were worn in advance; David's torn clothes were torn on the spot — one is a facade, the other a reaction. More important still: the men with him did the same. David's grief was not a private tear; he led the whole camp in mourning together. Who were this company? People hunted by Saul for years, wandering in exile, whose families had just been carried off from Ziklag and just recovered. By human reckoning they had the most right to celebrate Saul's death: the man who oppressed them was dead. Yet when the leader tore his clothes, the whole camp tore theirs; when the leader wept, they wept with him until evening. A leader's character is the team's character — in this chapter, in the positive direction: with one afternoon of fasting, David taught a band of exiled fighters to be a mourning people rather than a revenge gang. He wept on three levels: for Saul, for Jonathan, and for the people of the LORD — for his enemy, for his friend, and for the many unnamed people who had fallen. The lament was not yet composed; he finished weeping first. The song was written after the weeping, which is why it contains not one false line — every word is underlaid with tears. When the church today composes songs, preaches, and publishes memorials for the departed, keep this order: truly weep first, then open your mouth; a memorial that has never been wept over is mostly material, not a lament.

### 4. Your Blood Be on Your Own Head (2 Samuel 1:13-16)

David asked the young man where he was from; he said: I am the son of an Amalekite, a sojourner. David said: How is it you were not afraid to stretch out your hand to destroy the LORD's anointed? David called one of his young men and ordered him to strike him down, and he struck him dead. David said to him: Your blood be on your own head, for your own mouth has testified against you, saying, I have killed the LORD's anointed (cf. 2 Sam 1:13-16, condensed summary).

"Anointed" is מָשִׁיחַ (māšîaḥ, H4899), the anointed one. See BDB, s.v. משיח. [8] "Blood" is דָּם (dām, H1818), blood. See BDB, s.v. דם. [9] The foundation of David's sentence is a single title: the LORD's anointed. However corrupt Saul had become, however relentlessly he had hunted David, he remained the king the LORD had anointed; before God Himself removed him and took his life (cf. 1 Samuel 31, where God closed the account through the Philistines and Saul's own sword), any hand stretched out to kill him was rebellion. The young man thought he had killed David's enemy; David told him: you killed the LORD's anointed. Notice that David did not say "you killed my enemy," nor "you saved me"; he lifted the whole matter out of private grievance and weighed it in God's sovereignty. Notice also the evidence of the sentence: your own mouth has testified against you. The young man's confession had been a petition for merit; David took it as testimony in court, unchanged, word for word, and sentenced on it. This lands as a heavy hammer on everyone who loves to claim credit, claim deeds, and profess loyalty with the tongue: the things you scramble to confess, you must answer for in full; the words you invent or claim for merit cannot be taken back, not one sentence, before the judgment seat. The preacher must also state the measure of reading note three: David here executed sentence in the public righteousness of one about to be king; the Bible records this to show his reverence for the anointed, not to authorize any person today to carry out private punishment. Our application today is reverence, caution of speech, and not touching unrighteous merit; matters of the sword belong to God and to the authority of justice, not to private hands.

### Ignition Point One: Do Not Build Your Future on News of Another's Death

I stop here to speak to one kind of person: do you also keep a hidden list in your heart, hoping certain people fall so your life will improve? Hoping the one who presses you is transferred, collapses, meets disaster; hoping a rival errs first; hoping the door that blocks you disappears by itself? The young man is the person who ran that hope into legs, spoke it into words, and did it into a deed. He did not wait for God; he carried out heaven's justice himself; he hugged the crown to his chest and ran all the way into Ziklag, expecting a reward — and a sentence was waiting for him. Today, in the name of the Lord, I call you directly: put that list away. Today — not after you have climbed up. A person who rises on news of an enemy's death will not sit steady once up, because his foundation is another's grave. Do not let another do for you, and then claim merit from you, the thing you for years refused to do with your own hand; the door you have waited years for, if it opens, God opens it — it does not open by anyone's blood. Learn David's first lesson in this chapter: if good news is stained with unrighteous blood, better to tear your clothes and weep than to reach out and take that crown. It does no harm for the crown to be worn a little later; once a hand is stained with the wrong blood, it cannot be washed clean in a lifetime.

## Part Two: The First Half of the Lament — Tell It Not in Gath (2 Samuel 1:17-22)

### 1. David Composed a Lament and Ordered It Taught to the People of Judah (2 Samuel 1:17-18)

David composed a lament over Saul and his son Jonathan, and ordered that the people of Judah be taught this song called the Bow; it is written in the Book of Jashar (cf. 2 Sam 1:17-18, condensed summary).

"Lament" is קִינָה (qînâ, H7015), a lament, a dirge. See BDB, s.v. קינה. [10] "Bow" is קֶשֶׁת (qešet, H7198), a bow. See BDB, s.v. קשת. [11] This verse hides the lament's origin and destination. Its origin is the bow: Jonathan's bow was the mark of his deeds in battle (the next verse sings of Jonathan's bow and arrows), and David titled the whole song "the Bow," setting his friend's most heroic aspect as the title of the song rather than the manner of his death. In making memorials for the departed, learn this thoughtfulness: remember the way they shone most in life, not the way they looked worst in falling. Its destination is teaching: this song was not sung by David behind a closed door; it was ordered to be taught to the people of Judah as teaching material. The Book of Jashar was a contemporary collection of the deeds of the righteous and of war songs (cf. Josh 10:13, registered as a series); that this song was entered into the book shows it was a public text from the beginning. The first song a leader teaches his people is not a victory song but a lament. The people of Judah would later fight many victorious battles with David, but before learning warfare they first learned this lesson: sing for the fallen, sing for your enemy, sing for the nation's defeat. If a nation's songbook holds only victories, it will be speechless at other people's funerals; David taught the lament first, and only afterward did Israel's psalms have room for so many laments (cf. the lament psalms, registered as a series). The church today should also ask itself: among the songs we teach the next generation, is there one sung for the failed, for the departed, for the enemy? If not, however lively our worship, it lacks the lesson of the Song of the Bow.

### 2. Tell It Not in Gath, Proclaim It Not in the Streets of Ashkelon (2 Samuel 1:19-20)

The glory of Israel lies slain on your high places; how the mighty have fallen. Tell it not in Gath, proclaim it not in the streets of Ashkelon, lest the daughters of the Philistines rejoice, lest the daughters of the uncircumcised exult (cf. 2 Sam 1:19-20, condensed summary).

"The glory" in this verse comes from צְבִי (ṣəḇî, H6643), beauty, glory — also a gazelle. See BDB, s.v. צבי. [12] "Fallen" is נָפַל (nāpal, H5307), to fall. See BDB, s.v. נפל. [13] The lament's first sound is not weeping but protection: do not tell it. David knew the chain by which defeat-news travels: once the news reached Gath and Ashkelon it would become the song, dance, and exultation of enemy daughters, and the shame of the fallen would be killed a second time at the enemy's festival. He could not stop the Philistines from celebrating on their own; what he could do was order his own people to close their mouths: Israel's defeat must not, from Israelite mouths, become the enemy's material. This is a deep collective dignity: one's own death-news is first mourned at home — not sent out, not sensationalized, not handed to the enemy as a knife. The shapes have changed today; the principle has not: the defeats, scandals, and deaths of church and family are most to be feared not when outsiders learn of them, but when our own people race to spread them, embellish them, and carry them to where the enemy can hold a celebration. Here the preacher must say one heavy sentence: to broadcast another's fall is never a testimony; it is handing over a knife. Learn from David: first close the gate of Gath, then go back up the mountain and weep.

### 3. Let There Be No Dew or Rain on Mount Gilboa (2 Samuel 1:21-22)

Mountains of Gilboa, let there be no dew or rain on you, and no fields yielding offerings for sacrifice, for there the shield of the mighty was defiled and cast away — the shield of Saul, as though he had never been anointed with oil. Jonathan's bow never turned back from the blood of enemies, and Saul's sword never returned empty from the fat of the mighty (cf. 2 Sam 1:21-22, condensed summary).

"Shield" is מָגֵן (māgēn, H4043), a shield. See BDB, s.v. מגן. [14] "Oil," anointing oil, is שֶׁמֶן (šemen, H8081), oil. See BDB, s.v. שמן. [15] "Mighty man, hero" is גִּבּוֹר (gibbôr, H1368), a mighty man, one of strength. See BDB, s.v. גבר. [16] Singing to this point, David pronounces a curse on a mountain. This is not venting anger on geography; the poet's grief and indignation have found their landing place: this mountain, which received the blood of a king and a prince, in his eyes deserves no more dew or rain and no more produce from its fields. The Bible is not afraid to let such emotion enter a song: laments have a place for imprecation (cf. Lamentations and the imprecatory psalms, registered as a series); faith is not the pressing of grief and anger down into calm, but the bringing of grief and anger before God and singing them out. The next line shows David's heart most: he sings Saul's shield as though he were singing his own father's shield. Saul's shield defiled and cast away, as though never anointed with oil — the mark of the anointed buried in dirt is his most painful stroke; immediately after, he sets Saul's and Jonathan's battle deeds side by side: the bow does not turn back, the sword does not return empty. In the lament, Saul has only battle merit and no charge sheet; the man who hunted David for years is, in the song, only a mighty warrior. This is not forgetfulness but choice: David remembered clearly what Saul had done — the chapters of 1 Samuel are the testimony; in the lament he simply lays down a rule: at a funeral, old accounts are not reopened. Whether a person can say only the good of an enemy at that enemy's funeral is the test paper of whether his heart has been colonized by hatred. David passed this test, and that is why he was fit to receive the kingdom that followed.

## Part Three: The Second Half of the Lament — I Am Distressed for You (2 Samuel 1:23-27)

### 1. Saul and Jonathan, Beloved and Lovely in Life (2 Samuel 1:23)

Saul and Jonathan, beloved and lovely in their lives, and in their death they were not divided; they were swifter than eagles, stronger than lions (cf. 2 Sam 1:23, condensed summary).

"Eagle" is נֶשֶׁר (nešer, H5404), an eagle, a vulture. See BDB, s.v. נשר. [17] "Lion" is אֲרִי (ʾărî, H738), a lion. See BDB, s.v. ארי. [18] The poet sings father and son in one place: beloved and lovely, not divided. Anyone who has read 1 Samuel knows this father-son relationship had cracks: Saul once hurled his spear at Jonathan and publicly cursed him (cf. 1 Samuel 20), and Jonathan had defied his father's command to make way for David. Yet the lament does not write the cracks; it writes the parallel. Father and son died together on one mountain, so the poet sings them, according to the fact of their shared death, as not divided. This line also does justice to Jonathan: he did not die by a wrong choice of his own; he was a son who accompanied his father to the end. He had a way to live — in 1 Samuel 20 he had already seen clearly that the throne would not remain in his own house — yet he still returned to his father's camp, went up to the battlefield, and died at his father's side. The cost of loyalty is sometimes exactly this: knowing the road leads to Gilboa, and still walking the last stretch with your father. The preacher should leave a seat here for those today who accompany parents and family through a hard final road: your companionship — the Bible has recorded it in a song.

### 2. Daughters of Israel, Weep over Saul (2 Samuel 1:24)

Daughters of Israel, weep over Saul, who clothed you in scarlet finery and put ornaments of gold on your clothing (cf. 2 Sam 1:24, condensed summary).

In the lament David names a group of people: the daughters of Israel. The reason is most concrete: in the years of Saul's reign, spoil and settled years once enabled ordinary women to wear scarlet finery and gold ornaments. The poet remembers a king not by singing his palace but by singing the change in the people's wardrobes: the good of his rule fell onto the bodies of ordinary women. This adds the other face of historical writing to the lament: to judge an era, look not only at the merit-and-fault ledger of the court but at whether the days of common people got a little better; to remember a leader, count not only his lost battles but the real benefits ordinary people received through him. When we remember predecessors today — departed pastors and elders — learn this concreteness: do not speak empty praise; be able to count the actual good he gave to a particular household and a particular person, and the remembrance will have weight. At the same time, this call returns mourning to the people: the lament is not the patent of a court band; women weeping at home and in the street are part of the whole nation's mourning. The democratization of tears is the Song of the Bow's second lesson to later generations.

### 3. How the Mighty Have Fallen in the Midst of the Battle; I Am Distressed for You (2 Samuel 1:25-27)

How the mighty have fallen in the midst of the battle; Jonathan lies slain on the high places. Jonathan my brother, I am distressed for you; you were greatly pleasing to me; the love you showed me was wonderful, surpassing the love of women. How the mighty have fallen, and the weapons of war perished (cf. 2 Sam 1:25-27, condensed summary).

"Love" is אַהֲבָה (ʾahăḇâ, H160), love. See BDB, s.v. אהבה. [19] "Wonderful" is from פָּלָא (pālāʾ, H6381), wonderful, extraordinary. See BDB, s.v. פלא. [20] "Sword" is חֶרֶב (ḥereb, H2719), a sword. See BDB, s.v. חרב. [21] In this section the whole song turns from a national anthem into a private letter: Jonathan my brother. By generation Jonathan was the king's eldest son and older than David; David calls him brother as a covenant address: in 1 Samuel 18 they made a covenant, and Jonathan gave David his robe, his sword, his bow, and his belt (cf. 1 Sam 18:3-4); in 1 Samuel 20 he risked his life to bring word; in 1 Samuel 23 he went into the wilderness to strengthen David's hand and said with his own mouth: you shall be king over Israel, and I shall be next to you. Such a man could have been the second person of the new dynasty; now he lies on Mount Gilboa. David's tears in this section therefore differ from the previous one: the tears for Saul are reverence, the tears for the nation are anguish, the tears for Jonathan are a heart-rending personal pain. He does not hide its weight: this covenant love was irreplaceable in his life, and afterward he sought out Jonathan's son Mephibosheth and showed kindness to Saul's house — that is the sequel of these tears (cf. 2 Samuel 9, registered). The whole song opens with the question "How the mighty have fallen" and closes with the same line, with one sentence set between: how the weapons of war have perished. The poet at last moves the lens from people to things — the common closing of all laments: however sharp the bow, however swift the sword, all stopped with their master on that day; what the living can carry away is only song and remembrance.

### Ignition Point Two: Today, Shed One True Tear for Your Saul

At this last place I speak to those whose hearts still keep an unsettled account. Do you have a Saul? Someone who wounded you, pressed you, hunted you; now he has fallen, failed, or is even gone, and the account in your heart is not yet closed — whenever he is mentioned, your words still hold only a charge sheet. David's lesson here is not that you pretend he never did wrong — not one page of 1 Samuel's testimony is torn out. He teaches you his choice at the funeral: first weep a while by the measure of the man's weight, first remember his kindness and merit; let God settle the account, and let the tears be yours to shed. Today — not when your feelings have been cultivated into place. If you are willing to shed one true tear for your Saul, you have graduated from his shadow: what hatred fears most is not revenge but mourning; a person who can sing a lament for an enemy can no longer be governed by that enemy. And to those who have just lost a dear friend: weep your Jonathan out fully; do not rush to pull yourself together; do not fear that tears look weak. Before David was a king he was a man who wept for a friend; your tears are not a breach in your faith but the evidence of your love. When you have wept, teach the song to those beside you, and let remembrance pass down generation to generation — that is how we overcome Gilboa.

## Cross-Reading: Four Passages Read Together

The matter of this chapter has echoes elsewhere in the Bible; four cross-readings nail it down. First, from the Old Testament: twice refusing to stretch out his hand — in the cave at En Gedi and beside the camp at the hill of Hachilah, David twice had the chance to take Saul's life and restrained his followers: far be it from me before the LORD to stretch out my hand against the LORD's anointed (cf. 1 Sam 24:6; 26:11, condensed rendering; key sentences checked against the Chinese Union Version). Read together with this chapter, the reason for the young man's death becomes clear: he did what David twice refused to do, and offered it as merit. The same knife: David would not use it, the young man scrambled to use it; the outcomes are an anointed one and an executed one. Second, from the Old Testament: the tradition of lament — Moses at the end of Deuteronomy sings a song for Israel as a witness (cf. Deut 31-32), and Jacob on his deathbed leaves words for his sons (cf. Gen 49); David's Song of the Bow continues precisely this tradition of preserving history in song: Israel's history is written not only in chronicles but also sung in laments. Read together, the weight of the Song of the Bow being ordered taught to the people of Judah becomes plain: it is the new dynasty's first national textbook. Third, from the Psalms: the heart that mourns for enemies — David testifies of himself: they repay me evil for the good I did as a friend, yet I prayed for them; when they were sick I wore sackcloth and fasted and humbled myself; I went about toward him as toward a friend or brother, bowed down in grief as one mourning for a mother (cf. Ps 35:12-14, condensed rendering; key sense checked against the Chinese Union Version). Psalm 35 is David's own footnote to his weeping for Saul's company: sorrowing and fasting for persecutors was not a momentary performance with him but his habitual heart. Fourth, from Proverbs: do not rejoice when your enemy falls, and let not your heart be glad when he stumbles, lest the LORD see it and be displeased (Prov 24:17-18, Chinese Union Version). The Song of the Bow is this proverb set to music: where the enemy fell, David set up a lament, not a celebration. And one from the New Testament: weep with those who weep; do not repay anyone evil for evil or take your own revenge, but leave room for God's wrath, for it is written: Vengeance is mine; I will repay, says the Lord (cf. Rom 12:15, 19, condensed rendering; key sense checked against the Chinese Union Version). Paul distills the ethics of David's life into a rule for the church: weeping with others and not taking revenge is the character the New Testament church inherits from David's lament. Read across both Testaments, this chapter is not a piece of palace gossip; it is a root of how the church today treats people and handles matters.

## Application: Six Kinds of People

First, for those waiting for someone else to fall so that their turn comes. That position, that opportunity you wait for — if it can only open because someone meets disaster, this chapter persuades you to wait differently: use the time of waiting to prepare yourself to be worthy of it, not to hope for the other's fall. David waited many years, and his waiting had content — he fought, he shepherded his followers, he twice let his enemy go — so when the opportunity truly came, he could weep first. Your concrete assignment today: delete from your heart the name of the one whose fall you hope for, and replace it with one prayer for him; once is enough. Once your enemy has appeared in your prayers, news of his death can never again become your good news.

Second, for those who carry news, pass messages, and make content. Half of the young man's death sentence was pronounced on his legs and half on his mouth: he delivered, as a petition for merit, unverified words in which he confessed to regicide. Everyone who lives by news today — from reporters and bloggers to the eager person who forwards fastest in the group chat — should examine himself before this chapter: am I passing on fact or a petition for merit? Have I, to seize the first release and the traffic, rushed another's defeat and death into Gath? Concrete assignment: for news involving another's fall, death, or scandal, first ask David's question — how do you know? If it cannot be verified, do not pass it on; if it must be passed on, first add mourning and measure, and not one word of your own delight.

Third, for successors and those newly risen. The order of David's succession is this chapter's rule for every successor: first mourn the predecessor, then talk about your own team; first publicly remember the predecessor's merit, and never lift yourself by belittling the predecessor; first deal with the bloodstained opportunist, showing everyone that the new era does not use such people or walk such a road. As you take over a team, a church, or a post today, you can do three things this week: publicly say one thing genuinely good about your predecessor; in the transition period leave your predecessor a dignified place; and refuse the first merit-seeker who runs to you with slander and another's handle against someone. A successor's prestige never comes from stepping on the predecessor; it comes from the lament he sings for him.

Fourth, for those who have just lost a dear friend or family member. This chapter permits you to weep, permits you to weep until evening, permits you to write your tears into a song. Do not listen to the voices urging you to pull yourself together before you listen to your own heart; David's tears held a nation, a friend, and an enemy, in distinct layers, and your grief may also flow slowly in layers. You have only one concrete assignment: write a paragraph for the person you have lost, recording the most shining thing he did and one concrete good he gave you — as the title of the Song of the Bow records Jonathan's bow — and set his best aspect in place. When you have written it, keep it safe; in days to come it will be your family's Book of Jashar.

Fifth, for those still keeping accounts with predecessors and opponents. The rule of the lament is that funerals do not reopen accounts, but this chapter never told David to destroy the testimony of 1 Samuel. Forgiveness and mourning are not amnesia; they are returning the right of judgment to God and releasing your own heart from the counting house. You can make an experiment today: for the person who wounded you, write down three true goods about him, or one real benefit he gave the world — as concretely as David counted that Saul made women wear scarlet. If you can write them, your heart has loosened an inch; if you cannot, first pray about this matter, asking God to give you David's eyes first, and then David's tears.

Sixth, for parents and teachers. The Song of the Bow was ordered to be taught to the people of Judah: mourning is taught. However adults at home talk about the fallen, the departed, and the failed, children will learn that kind of national character. If children only ever hear adults mock and gossip about the defeated, they will grow up to be Gath's messengers; if they have heard adults sigh for an opponent, shed tears for the departed, and say one just word for a predecessor, they will grow up to be makers of the Song of the Bow. This week's concrete assignment: at the dinner table, once, tell of the good of a departed elder of the family or the church, concrete to one event and one object, with no empty words; your family's lament tradition begins with this telling.

## Closing Prayer of Response

Lord, we gather 2 Samuel 1 into a response. We confess to You the list in our hearts that hopes for people's fall; exchange it for prayer. We confess to You every mouth that seeks merit by death-news and defeat-news; teach us to ask first "how do you know?", and to learn silence and verification first. We pray for the members who are about to succeed and are now rising: let them first sing a lament for their predecessors and not climb up by stepping on others. We pray for those who have just lost their Jonathan: accompany them as they weep until evening, and gather their tears into a song. We pray for those still keeping accounts with the living and the dead: let them today shed one true tear for their Saul and walk out of hatred's counting house. May our generation be a people who make laments, not a people who carry news for merit. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Extract (for daily devotion, 300-500 words)

Second Samuel 1: the news of Saul's death reaches Ziklag. The Amalekite young man who carries the news brings Saul's crown and bracelet and confesses that he killed the king with his own hand, hoping to claim merit; David instead tears his clothes, mourns and fasts with his followers until evening for Saul and Jonathan, sentences the young man to death on his own confession, and then composes the Song of the Bow and teaches the people of Judah to sing: How the mighty have fallen (cf. 2 Samuel 1, condensed rendering). The man David had waited for over many years was dead, and his first reaction was not to take the crown but to weep; for Saul who had hunted him he recorded only battle merit and no charge sheet, and for Jonathan his covenant friend he wept heart-rendingly. The same death-news is a future to the opportunist and a lament to David. Three questions for meditation today: Do I keep in my heart a list of people whose fall I hope for? When I pass on news, have I first asked "how do you know?" Can I name one true good of a person who has wounded me? Prayer: Lord, teach me not to climb up on other people's graves; teach me to mourn first and speak afterward; teach me to leave one true tear even for an enemy.

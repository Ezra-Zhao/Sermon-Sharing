# Strange Fire and Silence

Preacher: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries

Text: Leviticus 10:1-20 (KJV)

Abstract: Leviticus 10 follows immediately after the glory and the descending fire. Aaron's two sons, Nadab and Abihu, each took his censer, put strange fire in it, and offered incense which the LORD had not commanded them; and fire came out from before the LORD and devoured them. Moses said, This is it that the LORD spake, saying, I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me; and Aaron held his peace. The chapter then records that priests on duty must not mourn, the prohibition of wine and the office of distinguishing the holy from the common and the clean from the unclean, and, in the dispute over the goat, a precedent of appeal heard and accepted with understanding.

---

## I. The First Day After the Glory 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, when we read Leviticus 10, we must first remember that it stands immediately after Leviticus 9. The end of chapter 9 is one of the most glorious scenes in the whole Exodus narrative: Aaron had finished offering the sacrifices for the people; Moses and Aaron went into the tabernacle and came out again and blessed the people, and the glory of the LORD appeared unto all the people. "And there came a fire out from before the LORD, and consumed upon the altar the burnt offering and the fat: which when all the people saw, they shouted, and fell on their faces" (Lev 9:24, KJV). Fire came down, and the people fell on their faces — that was the height of worship, the moment when the newly established priesthood received its confirmation. Then, with no interval at all, the first verse of chapter 10 says: "And Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, took either of them his censer, and put fire therein, and put incense thereon, and offered strange fire before the LORD, which he commanded them not" (Lev 10:1, KJV). The very next line after the summit is the beginning of tragedy. That Scripture places these two events so close together is itself the first lesson the narrative gives us: the moment nearest to glory may also be the moment that most requires caution.

First, let the persons be stated plainly, without embellishment. Nadab and Abihu were Aaron's eldest and second sons. In chapter 8 they had just been anointed together with their father, had received the priesthood, had put on the holy garments, and had passed through the seven days of consecration; they were the two eldest of the newly appointed priestly company. Their identity has an even earlier layer: Exodus 24 records that Moses took Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel up the mountain, and they saw the God of Israel, and under his feet there was, as it were, a paved work of sapphire stone, clear as the heaven itself; they saw God, and ate and drank. That is to say, these two men were among the very few in all Israel who had with their own eyes seen the glory of God on the mountain and eaten and drunk there, and who were afterwards the first to be ordained as priests. Their identity is recorded here as the text gives it, without drawing moral speculation from it: the text does not say they were proud, does not say they were careless, and does not say what motive was in their hearts. If a sermon weaves their inner life into a story, that is the preacher's word, not the word of the text. This sermon stands only on the fact: those who fell were not outsiders, but those nearest; it was not a novice erring at the periphery, but men who had just received the priesthood and had once gone up the mountain and seen God, who came to grief at the very front line of the office. That fact by itself is weighty enough; it needs nothing added.

Let the time also be stated plainly. By the order of the narrative, the event took place at the very first stage of the priesthood's formal operation — indeed, on the same day as the offerings of chapter 9, or immediately after. On the first day of duty, at the first censer of incense, strange fire appeared. This reminds us that spiritual danger does not always appear in the wilderness lowlands, in seasons of weak faith; it can also appear when everything is smooth, everything glorious, and the rite has just been confirmed. The people's shouts of prostration had not yet faded, the fire on the altar had not yet cooled, and the trouble came. Those who come nigh to God are not far from danger, because every day they handle holy things and every day they stand on the boundary between the holy and the common. A person on a boundary must walk more steadily than a person far away.

The content of this chapter may be read in three parts. The first part, verses 1-7, is the event of the strange fire itself: the offering of strange fire, the fire coming out and devouring the two men, Moses declaring the word of God, Aaron holding his peace, the bodies carried out of the camp by their kinsmen, and the priests on duty forbidden to mourn. The second part, verses 8-11, is God speaking directly to Aaron, establishing the prohibition of wine, and making plain the office of distinguishing the holy from the common and the clean from the unclean, and of teaching the people. The third part, verses 12-20, returns to the daily service: the remains of the meat offering, the portions of the wave breast and the heave thigh, and the dispute over whether the goat of the sin offering had been eaten — Moses asking in anger, Aaron answering in calm appeal, and Moses hearing it and being content. Taken together, the chapter does not record only a calamity; it records how, after the calamity, the priestly office was spoken clear again: what must not be done, what must be done, and how, when there is a difference among fellow workers, the matter is to be spoken through between truth and understanding.

The reading followed in this sermon is the layering used throughout this series: first the layer of fact — what the text expressly says; then the layer of commentary — the differing views of interpreters through the ages on the difficulties, set side by side, and where there is no settled conclusion, none is made; and only at the end the New Testament reading and homiletical use, with the layer marked, and the later reading never filled back in as the plain meaning of the original text. There is a real difficulty in chapter 10: two young men died before the altar. That fact is not pleasant to hear, and it ought not to be preached lightly. This sermon will not sensationalise it, nor use their death as an example to frighten people; neither will it evade it, nor smooth away the seriousness of the text. We seek only to set the text straight: how the word of God speaks, how Aaron responds, and how the system goes forward amid grief. When we have understood this chapter, we shall understand that reverence is not distance from God; it is precisely the lesson most necessary for those who come nigh to God.

## II. Incense That Was Not Commanded 08:00-24:00

The event itself is told in only two verses, yet every word carries weight. "And Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, took either of them his censer, and put fire therein, and put incense thereon, and offered strange fire before the LORD, which he commanded them not. And there went out fire from the LORD, and devoured them, and they died before the LORD" (Lev 10:1-2, KJV). Look first at the action: each took his own censer. The censer was the vessel each man ordinarily used for burning incense; the difficulty of this chapter does not lie in the vessel itself. They put fire in it, put incense on it, and went before the LORD to offer. Offering incense in itself was no amateur's act — in the tabernacle there was a golden altar of incense, and burning incense daily was originally the priest's office. The problem lies in two expressions: strange fire, and commanded them not. Strange fire, in the Hebrew, is fire that is foreign, not according to rule; and commanded them not is the chapter's own characterisation of the whole matter: this censer of incense was not within what God had commanded. The core of the violation, the text itself points out, lies not in attitude and not in motive, but in not commanded — God had not commanded it so, and yet they did it so.

What exactly the strange fire refers to has no settled conclusion in scholarship to this day; this sermon sets four views side by side honestly, and decides for none of them. The first view: the source of the fire was wrong. The fire on the altar was fire God himself had sent down, to be kept burning on the altar continually and never to go out; the fire for incense ought properly to be taken from the altar; if they took ordinary fire from elsewhere, that fire was strange fire. The second view: the hour was wrong. The burning of incense had appointed times, once in the morning and once in the evening, or when the high priest entered the most holy place on the Day of Atonement; if they offered incense presumptuously at a time when incense should not be offered, the hour was wrong and the rite was wrong. The third view: it was unauthorised. To enter and offer incense — especially to approach rashly just after the glory had appeared — may not at that moment have been the office assigned to these two men; they overstepped their assignment and did what had not been entrusted to them. The fourth view: foreign incense was mixed in. The formula of the incense has express words in Exodus 30: no man shall make its like for himself, and no strange incense shall be offered; if they tampered with the spices, the incense was not according to rule. Each of the four views has supporting texts beside it, and each has something it cannot explain. An honest sermon can only say: the text has not left us a checklist of violations; it has left us one characterisation — not commanded. Perhaps that is precisely the point the text wants us to remember: the root of the problem does not lie in some technical detail, but in man's treating what he himself wishes to do as what God wants him to do.

Then comes the outcome, and the wording must be read exactly: "And there went out fire from the LORD, and devoured them, and they died before the LORD" (Lev 10:2, KJV). Here an observation of narrative structure is made, and marked as observation, not as a further doctrine: in Leviticus 9:24, fire came out from before the LORD and consumed the offering on the altar; in Leviticus 10:2, in almost the same wording, fire came out from before the LORD and devoured the men who had offered wrongly. The same fire, from the same source, falls once on the offering and once on the men. The narrative places the two sentences so close together that the reader is meant to see the contrast for himself: the fire of God accepts the offering that is according to rule, and also judges the approach that is not according to rule. The fire did not change; what changed was the manner in which man stood before the fire. The fire on the altar is not an atmosphere that may be borrowed at will; it is the sign of God's presence. Whoever comes nigh to this fire must come nigh according to the word of God, not according to his own impulse.

Then comes the handling of the bodies, calm in a way that tightens the heart. Moses called Mishael and Elzaphan, the sons of Uzziel, Aaron's uncle, and said to them: "Come near, carry your brethren from before the sanctuary out of the camp. So they went near, and carried them in their coats out of the camp; as Moses had said" (Lev 10:4-5, KJV). Several facts are to be set straight here. First, those who carried the bodies were not Aaron and his two remaining sons, but the cousins Mishael and Elzaphan; priests on duty do not touch a corpse — that is the boundary of the office, not coldness of affection. Second, they carried them out together with the priestly coats the bodies wore, with no pause or handling within the sanctuary; the cleanness of the sanctuary cannot be polluted by death, and the rule stands even in the most grievous hour. Third, the place was outside the camp, carried out beyond the whole encampment, in line with the place Leviticus assigns throughout for uncleanness and refuse. Those who did this were their kinsmen; the text calls Mishael and Elzaphan their brethren, suggesting that within this arrangement there is still family consideration: those who carried them out were not strangers, but their own people. A solemn rule and family consideration stand in the same verse, side by side, neither cancelling the other.

Immediately after, Moses said to Aaron and to his remaining sons Eleazar and Ithamar: "Uncover not your heads, neither rend your clothes; lest ye die, and lest wrath come upon all the people: but let your brethren, the whole house of Israel, bewail the burning which the LORD hath kindled" (Lev 10:6, KJV). That priests on duty must not mourn sounds severe; it must be read exactly at the layer of fact. Uncovering the head and rending the clothes were the ordinary rites of mourning; Moses was not forbidding Aaron to grieve in his heart — from beginning to end the text never forbids grief — he forbade priests on duty to withdraw from the office by the rites of mourning. The rule for the high priest is written out later: the anointing oil is upon his head, he is consecrated to God, he shall not uncover his head nor rend his clothes, nor go in to any dead body, nor defile himself even for his father or his mother. The reason likewise stands in the text: they had just been anointed and had just put on the holy garments, and were in the midst of the office; if the priest became unclean for the dead, the work of the sanctuary would stop, and the congregation would lose its mediator for that day. So the matter of weeping was not cancelled; it was divided: the whole house of Israel wept in their place, while the three men on duty stood before the altar and finished their office. That is the system's arrangement on the day of calamity: grief is not allowed to crush the office, and the office does not pretend there is no grief; on one side, the tears of the whole house, on the other, the uninterrupted service at the altar. Many years later, parents who, in the pain of losing a child, must still stand at their post, may find their own shadow in this verse — not that it does not hurt, but that, hurting, one must still keep the watch one is assigned to keep, while for the weeping, there are brethren to weep in your place. But the heaviest sentence of this chapter and its quietest action have not yet appeared. In the next section we stop at verse 3: the word Moses declared, and Aaron's silence.

## III. Sanctified — and Aaron Held His Peace 24:00-42:00

The fire of verse 2 had scarcely fallen when, in verse 3, Moses spoke to Aaron a word that is the centre of the whole chapter: "This is it that the LORD spake, saying, I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me, and before all the people I will be glorified" (Lev 10:3, KJV). The source of this saying is not pointed elsewhere by the text; Moses here spoke out, in this moment, what had always been God's mind. To be sanctified — the Hebrew verb is the word from the root for holiness, for being set apart as holy. Them that come nigh me refers precisely to the priests — those who enter before God every day, handle the holy things, and stand between the people and God. The logic of this sentence must be read exactly: God is sanctified not only among those far away; he is sanctified precisely among those who come nigh to him. The nearer, the more manifest. Nearness is not exemption; nearness is the weightening of responsibility: the nearer a man is to God, the more clearly God's holiness is manifested in him — manifested as glory through his service according to rule, or manifested as righteousness through his judgment when he transgresses. Moses' word is not a comment made with hindsight; it is pastoral care for the high priest at the scene of disaster: he set what had just happened back within the holiness of God for Aaron to understand, so that Aaron would know this was no chance accident, but the holy God keeping his own holiness among those who come nigh to him. The second half of the saying runs: before all the people I will be glorified. The people, in chapter 9, saw fire fall on the offering, and glorified God; in chapter 10 they see fire fall on men, and must likewise learn who this God is. Glory runs through both chapters in one line; the manner of its being glorified makes a man stand in awe.

Aaron's response is the quietest scene in the whole chapter, and the one later readers have returned to most often: "And Aaron held his peace" (Lev 10:3, KJV). The original is a single verb, meaning to be silent, to cease, to make no sound. The text records only silence; it does not record that Aaron said a single word — no defence, no question, no wail, and no spiritual platitude either. The meaning of this silence has been read differently by readers through the ages; this sermon sets the views side by side and decides for none. The first: a silence of submission. Aaron accepted the word Moses declared, knew that there was God's holiness in God's judgment, and, having nothing to plead before God, was silent and left the judgment to God. The second: a grief beyond words. A father who in one day loses two sons who have just put on the holy garments grieves too deeply to speak; the silence is the very shape of mourning, and needs no further label. The third: restraint within the office. He was on duty, even the rites of weeping were forbidden him, and with silence he kept his post; his tears flowed inward, he spoke not a word — that too is a kind of bearing. The three views do not exclude one another: one and the same silence may hold submission, grief, and restraint together. The preacher must restrain his own desire to explain: where the text leaves a blank, preaching may point it out and may set the views side by side, but must not preach any one view as a monologue from Aaron's heart. What we know for certain is only the action — he held his peace. Many parents, reading this verse, will stop here, because this silence so resembles the deepest moments of real life: not that there is nothing to say, but that every word is too light, and to speak it would profane the pain. The honesty of Scripture here commands respect: it does not arrange for Aaron to say one fitting word to round the scene off; it records only that he was silent. That silence itself is a posture of prayer, as a later psalm says, I was dumb with silence, I held my peace, even from good; and as the prophet says, he sits alone and keeps silence before the LORD and waits for him. When these cross-references are cited, it must be marked that they are later echoes, not the words of this chapter; yet the echo is real: throughout, Scripture honours such moments when a man can say nothing before God — it does not hurry him, does not explain him, does not cover him over.

Then the voice turns, unexpectedly and yet fittingly: "And the LORD spake unto Aaron, saying, Do not drink wine nor strong drink" (Lev 10:8-9, KJV). Note the object and the manner of the speaking: in this chapter, when God himself opens his mouth, he speaks directly to Aaron, not, as on most days, through Moses. At the very time when the son has just come to grief and the high priest is silent, God himself establishes a rule for him: when you go into the tabernacle of the congregation, neither wine nor strong drink shall you drink, thou nor thy sons with thee, lest ye die; it shall be a statute for ever throughout your generations. Why is the prohibition of wine promulgated at this very moment? Here the layers must be kept, and honesty guarded. From beginning to end the text does not say that Nadab and Abihu drank wine, nor that they offered wrongly because they were drunk. To read the prohibition of wine as evidence that they offended while drunk is an inference at the commentary layer, not an express statement of the text; this sermon marks it as inference and does not adopt it as fact. The reason the text itself gives is another, written out clearly just after: "That ye may put difference between holy and unholy, and between unclean and clean; and that ye may teach the children of Israel all the statutes which the LORD hath spoken unto them by the hand of Moses" (Lev 10:10-11, KJV). That is to say, the prohibition of wine looks to distinguishing: the core of the priestly office is distinction — distinguishing the holy from the common, the clean from the unclean, and teaching the result of that distinction to the people. A man whose mind has been robbed of sobriety by wine cannot distinguish. That God re-states this rule on the day of disaster is to nail down again the root of the office: the priest's danger lies not only in a hand offering one wrong censer of incense, but also in a heart that has lost distinction. Wine here is a representative thing — it represents everything that makes a man lose sobriety and measure. In the hour of duty, a man must be sober enough to distinguish, because the people's holy and common, clean and unclean, are guarded by his distinction.

This passage states the priestly office with great completeness, and it is worth remembering word by word: to put difference between the holy and the unholy, and between the clean and the unclean, and to teach the people all the statutes. The priest is not merely an operator of sacrifices; he is the people's teacher — teaching as office is stated this expressly in this chapter, and later, when the prophetic books rebuke priests for failure of office, it is precisely this measure they use: they have put no difference between the holy and the profane, neither have they shewed difference between the unclean and the clean. The office has three layers: to draw near and offer, in the middle to distinguish, and outwardly to teach. The tragedy of Nadab and Abihu happened at the first layer, yet the rule of distinction was stressed doubly on the very day of disaster, as if God were saying: a man may hold a censer in his hand and still need to draw the line between holy and common clear in his heart; for a man who cannot draw it clear, the nearer his hand is to the altar, the greater the danger. For anyone standing today at a pulpit, or at any spiritual post, this rule is likewise a rule for the hour of duty: you may be busy, you may be practised, you may be deep in seniority, but if you have lost distinction — preaching the holy as common, and pushing the common as holy — the office is hollowed out from within. Sobriety is the daily exercise of those who come nigh to God.

## IV. The Remaining Portions and an Appeal 42:00-56:00

After the calamity, this chapter does not stop in the ashes; it returns to the daily service, and that itself is a teaching: the life of the sanctuary cannot halt for one tragedy; the system must go on working amid grief, and within the details of its working lies the warmest conversation of the whole chapter. Look first at the portions. Moses spoke to Aaron and to his remaining sons Eleazar and Ithamar: take the meat offering that remains of the offerings of the LORD made by fire, and eat it without leaven beside the altar, for it is most holy; eat it in the holy place, as commanded. Then he spoke again of the wave breast and the heave shoulder: they and their children may eat them in a clean place, because they are their due portion for ever from the peace offerings. This passage looks like a ledger, yet it carries two meanings. First, supply does not break: two sons have died, and the remaining priests must still eat; God, by the portions, provides for them, and office and life are cared for together on the same day. Second, the remains are to be eaten in the holy place and in a clean place: calamity does not lower the standard of the rule; what should be eaten in the holy place is still eaten in the holy place, and what must be clean must still be clean. Sorrow is no reason for relaxing holiness; the dignity of the system shows itself precisely in that on its hardest days it still proceeds by rule. Reading this passage, note also the repeated names: Eleazar, Ithamar — each time Moses names them, he confirms that these two remaining sons are still within the office. The household has been reduced by two persons, and the list must still be read on; to read the names is to set the living back in their posts.

Then comes the dispute over the goat, the ending of the whole chapter, and the first precedent-like dialogue within the priestly system; this sermon sets out its course as the text gives it. Moses diligently sought the goat of the sin offering — the very one offered for the people in chapter 9 — and found that it had been burnt, not eaten. By the rule, if the blood of a sin offering was not brought into the sanctuary, the flesh belonged to the priests, to be eaten in the holy place; that eating was part of the priests' bearing of the people's iniquity. If the blood was brought into the sanctuary, the flesh might not be eaten and had to be burnt. The blood of this goat had not been brought into the sanctuary, so by rule it should have been eaten by the priests; now it had been burnt instead — a link that should have been borne by the priests had not been completed. Moses was angry with Eleazar and Ithamar, and asked them: "Wherefore have ye not eaten the sin offering in the holy place, seeing it is most holy, and God hath given it you to bear the iniquity of the congregation, to make atonement for them before the LORD?" (Lev 10:17, KJV). Moses' anger has the rule behind it — that is the layer of fact: he was guarding the rule and asking after the office; it was not unreasonable rage.

Aaron's appeal is the most weighty speech in the whole chapter. He said to Moses: "Behold, this day have they offered their sin offering and their burnt offering before the LORD; and such things have befallen me: and if I had eaten the sin offering to day, should it have been accepted in the sight of the LORD?" (Lev 10:19, KJV). The scriptural logic of the appeal may be read in two layers. First, the layer of fact: this very day this household has suffered bereavement; two sons have died in the sanctuary; as a father, as one freshly bereaved, to eat on such a day the sacrificial flesh whose meaning is the bearing of iniquity, with heart and state both at odds with what that offering requires — if he had eaten it by constraint, it would likely not have been acceptance, but another kind of irregularity. Second, a deeper reading found in many commentators: Aaron is saying that, being today in the grief of bereavement, by rite he ought not to treat himself by the ordinary rule; that he did not eat was not contempt for the rule, but fear that, in an unclean and unfitting state, he might eat of the most holy thing and repeat the offence in another form. The two readings are set side by side in this sermon, and together they point to one thing: Aaron's appeal is no excuse to evade; he sets out the intent behind the rule — the meaning of eating the sacrificial flesh lies in bearing and in acceptance, and today's calamity made that bearing impossible to complete by the ordinary rule; better to be questioned for not eating than to eat in a disordered state by constraint. Within this appeal there is the pain of a bereaved father, and there is also a priest's caution: in the morning, men had died because of what was not commanded; in the afternoon, Aaron would rather be questioned by Moses than do one more thing of which his heart was unsure. This layer is the sermon's application, and its level is marked: the text does not say outright that he was afraid, but the tone of his whole speech is restrained, and returns the right of decision to God — should it have been accepted in the sight of the LORD? — he asks not whether Moses is satisfied, but whether God would regard it as good.

The ending has one sentence only: "And when Moses heard that, he was content" (Lev 10:20, KJV). The elder brother who had asked in anger stopped, heard it through, and accepted it. That acceptance carries great weight: it established the first precedent within the priestly system — the rule must be kept, but when the keeping of the rule meets real calamity and the state of a man's heart, there is room for an appeal to be heard, and ground for understanding to stand. Moses did not change the rule; the rule that the goat should be eaten was not abolished. He acknowledged only that, on a day such as today, Aaron's choice was right. This is not muddling through; it is setting the seriousness of the law and the understanding of pastoral care on the same table: the morning's fire teaches us that the word of God must not be altered presumptuously; the afternoon's appeal teaches us that the word of God is to be carried out faithfully, and also with understanding, within real human life. A system with only the morning and no afternoon will be so hard that it shatters; with only the afternoon and no morning, it will be so soft that it scatters. Leviticus 10 has given us both: the fire, and he was content, within one chapter. Let those who lead remember this stop of Moses: he was angry, he called to account, yet after hearing the appeal through, he acknowledged openly that the other was right — to be angry in guarding the rule is not hard; the hard thing is, having heard the appeal, to lay anger down and say, this word is right. In this chapter Moses both declared the most severe sentence and made the most understanding decision; both are the duty of a leader.

## V. Beginning at the House of God: The New Testament Reading and Drawing Near Today 58:00-60:00

What follows belongs to the layer of New Testament reading and homiletical use, and is not filled back in as the plain meaning of Leviticus 10. Two passages in the New Testament echo this chapter from afar; preaching may use them, but must mark the layer. The first: Hebrews 12 exhorts believers to worship God with thanksgiving and not to despise him: "Let us have grace, whereby we may serve God acceptably with reverence and godly fear: for our God is a consuming fire" (Heb 12:28-29, KJV). The wording of consuming fire forms an interpretive echo with the fire that came out before the altar: the New Testament has not abolished reverence; it has brought reverence from the tabernacle into the daily worship of the church — drawing near to God remains grace, and grace never cancels reverence. The second: "For the time is come that judgment must begin at the house of God" (1 Pet 4:17, KJV). Peter there treats of the sufferings of believers and the judgment of God; when preaching uses this sentence, it takes its sense of order: the holiness of God comes first to those nearest to him, just as the fire of this chapter fell first in the priest's household. Both passages are restatements, by New Testament authors after the event of Christ, of the Old Testament theme of holiness; Leviticus 10 is not, in its literal sense, already speaking of the church. With the layers marked, the use stands firm.

We return to this chapter itself to conclude. Leviticus 10 leaves four exercises for those who draw near to God today. First, nearness does not mean presumption: to offer incense is a good thing, yet its hour, its manner, and its authorisation must all be according to the word of God; if zeal oversteps the boundary of what was not commanded, zeal itself becomes danger. Second, sobriety is the duty of the hour of service: distinguishing the holy from the common and the clean from the unclean, and teaching those beside us, begins with the clearness of our own minds; whatever steals our distinction, those on duty must keep far from. Third, grief has its place: Aaron's silence is honoured by the text, and the weeping of the whole house is received by the system; faith does not require a man to pretend he does not hurt; it teaches him, hurting, still to keep his post, and allows brethren to weep in his place. Fourth, an appeal has room to be heard: Moses, who guarded the rule, heard Aaron the bereaved father through, and was content; a healthy spiritual community has both the seriousness of the fire and the understanding that hears before it decides. May we, this generation of those who draw near to God, learn Aaron's silence and learn Moses' being content; hold the censer in our hands, and keep reverence in our hearts; stand on the boundary between holy and common — sober, careful, and full of mercy. Amen.

## Endnotes

1. esh zarah (strange fire): Hebrew אֵשׁ זָרָה, transliterated esh zarah, a noun phrase: strange fire, fire not according to rule, 10:1. esh אֵשׁ is fire, Strong H784; zarah זָרָה is a feminine adjective: strange, foreign, not according to rule, of the root z-w-r, Strong H2114. Lexical sources: BDB, strange, foreign; HALOT, strange, alien, unauthorised, and related senses. In Leviticus zarah is also used of unauthorised incense and of an outsider approaching the holy things without right; its core sense is what does not belong to this system and has not been permitted. What exactly the strange fire refers to has no settled conclusion in scholarship; the views are set side by side: wrong source of fire (not taken from the continually burning fire of the altar); wrong hour (not the appointed time for offering incense); unauthorised (overstepping the assignment and offering presumptuously); foreign incense mixed in (the spices not according to Exodus 30:9, 34-38). Milgrom tends to take not commanded as the governing line, with the questions of source and authorisation together; Wenham stresses the definition by absence of command; Hartley sets the views side by side without forcing a verdict. This sermon follows them.

2. hiqrivu asher lo-tzivvah otam: a Hebrew expression, transliterated hiqrivu asher lo-tzivvah otam, meaning they offered that which the LORD had not commanded them, 10:1. hiqrivu is the perfect of the verb to present, to bring near; tzivvah is to command, to order, root tz-v-h, Strong H6680. The negative lo together with the relative asher forms the characterising clause of the whole verse: the problem of what was offered is summed up by the text as not commanded. Milgrom notes that this construction echoes the construction in Deuteronomy concerning a false prophet who presumptuously speaks a word that was not commanded; presumptuously is the defining word of this verse.

3. biqrovai eqqadesh: Hebrew בִּקְרֹבַי אֶקָּדֵשׁ, transliterated biqrovai eqqadesh, meaning I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me, 10:3. qrovai is those who come nigh, those who draw near, root q-r-v, the same root as offering and bringing near, Strong H7138; eqqadesh is the verb to be shown holy, to be treated as holy, root qadash, holy, set apart as holy, Strong H6942. Lexical sources: BDB, be holy, be treated as holy; HALOT, be holy, be sanctified. Those who come nigh, in Numbers, also denotes those Levites chosen to draw near for service; in this verse those who come nigh refers first to the priests.

4. vayyidom Aharon: Hebrew וַיִּדֹּם אַהֲרֹן, transliterated vayyidom Aharon, meaning and Aaron held his peace, 10:3. The verb damam: to be silent, to cease, to be without sound, Strong H1826. Lexical sources: BDB, be silent, be still, wait in silence; HALOT, be silent, be dumbfounded. The same verb is used of silent waiting before God in Psalm 37:7, Psalm 62, and Lamentations 3:28, as cross-referential echoes. The meaning of Aaron's silence is set side by side in several views — silence of submission accepting the judgment, grief beyond words, and restraint within the office; the text records only the action, not the inner state, and this sermon reaches no settled conclusion.

5. Uncover not your heads, neither rend your clothes: 10:6, the ordinary rites of mourning, comprising the uncovering of the head with the hair let loose, and the rending of garments. The parallel rule forbidding the high priest to defile himself for the dead is found in Leviticus 21:10-12, the reason being his consecrated state by the anointing oil and the holy garments. Mishael and Elzaphan were sons of Uzziel, Aaron's uncle, see Exodus 6:22, and thus Aaron's cousins; in 10:4-5 they carry the bodies out of the camp — a matter of the factual layer.

6. yayin veshekhar: Hebrew יַיִן וְשֵׁכָר, transliterated yayin veshekhar, wine and strong drink, 10:9. yayin יַיִן is wine, Strong H3196; shekhar שֵׁכָר is strong drink, mostly a potent drink brewed from grain or fruit, Strong H7941. Lexical sources: BDB, strong drink, intoxicating drink; HALOT, beer, strong drink, and related senses. The view that the two men offended while drunk belongs to commentary inference: Hartley and Wenham both mention the possible connection and both note that the text itself does not state it expressly; this sermon marks it at the level of inference and does not adopt it as fact.

7. lehavdil: Hebrew לְהַבְדִּיל, transliterated lehavdil, an infinitive: that ye may put difference, that ye may distinguish, 10:10. The causative form of the verb badal: to separate, to distinguish, to set apart, Strong H914. Lexical sources: BDB, separate, distinguish, make a distinction; HALOT, divide, separate, distinguish. To distinguish the holy (qodesh, H6944) from the common (chol, H2455), and the clean (tahor, H2889) from the unclean (tame, H2931), is the core verb-group of the priestly office, standing together with teaching in 10:11 (the root yarah, the verbal source of torah as teaching), and constituting the priest's twofold office of distinguishing and teaching; Ezekiel 22:26 rebukes priests for putting no difference between the holy and the profane, using precisely this measure.

8. The wording of statute and generations: the statute for ever in 10:7, 9 is, in the original, an ordinance for generations, in the same usage as noted in earlier sermons of this series. Both the prohibition of wine and the rule against leaving the tabernacle are bound to this wording: their scope is not limited to that day; they are standing rules of the priestly system, not temporary measures, a point Milgrom stresses.

9. The remains of the meat offering and the wave breast and heave thigh: 10:12-15. The remains of the meat offering brought into the tabernacle are most holy, to be eaten beside the altar in the holy place, without leaven; see Leviticus 2 and 6:14-18. The breast of the wave offering (tenufah, waving in presentation, H8573) and the right thigh of the heave offering (terumah, lifting in presentation, H8641) are the priests' perpetual portion; see Leviticus 7:28-36. That the portions are re-stated on the day of calamity, item by item in Moses' words of command, belongs to the factual layer of the system's continued operation.

10. se'ir hachattat: Hebrew שְׂעִיר הַחַטָּאת, transliterated se'ir hachattat, the goat of the sin offering, 10:16. se'ir is a he-goat, Strong H8163; chattat is sin offering, sin, Strong H2403. Lexical sources: BDB, sin, sin-offering; HALOT, sin offering. The detail in dispute: this goat is the sin offering goat offered for the people in Leviticus 9:3, 15, whose blood was not brought into the sanctuary; by the division in 6:26, 30, the flesh of an offering whose blood was not brought in was to be eaten by the priests in the holy place, while one whose blood was brought in was to be burnt. Hence Moses questions the burning instead of the eating as a transgression; Aaron appeals on the ground that bereavement had befallen him that day and that he had not eaten; and Moses was content, 10:16-20. Milgrom treats the precedent character of this passage at length; Wenham and Hartley both treat the logic in Aaron's appeal of the conflict between a mourner's state and the most holy sacrificial flesh.

11. He was content: 10:20, in the original an expression that, when Moses heard it, it was good in his eyes, of the same evaluative word-family as the it was good of Genesis — an observation of narrative. This sentence is the first record within the priestly system of an appeal accepted: the rule was not changed, the case was decided; later traditions of legal precedent often cite this passage on weighing principle and circumstance. The sermon text marks this as narrative observation and homiletical application, and erects no further doctrine upon it.

12. Sources at the layer of New Testament reading: Hebrews 12:28-29, serving God acceptably with reverence and godly fear, for our God is a consuming fire; 1 Peter 4:17, judgment must begin at the house of God. See also Hebrews 10 on the once-for-all offering and the cleansing of conscience, and the institutional background of Leviticus 16, where on the Day of Atonement the high priest alone enters the most holy place. All belong to the layer of New Testament reading and homiletical use, and are not filled back in as the plain meaning of Leviticus 10.

## Devotional Excerpt

Reading Leviticus 10 today, I stop at Aaron's silence. In one day he lost two sons, and he said not a word. There truly are moments in life when it is not that we have nothing to say, but that every word feels too light. If I draw near to God, I must learn reverence: what He has commanded, I will not alter on my own; what is not mine to do, I will not add. I will distinguish the holy from the common with a sober mind, keep my post in quietness, allow myself silence in grief, and allow my brothers to weep in my place. And when someone answers me in my anger, may I learn from Moses — to listen first, and then to judge, and to say, this is good in my sight. This morning, as I offer, I will ask first: is this what You have commanded, or what I have stirred up myself? Having asked it clear, then I will act — slower by one step, rather than presumptuous by one step. Lord, teach me to draw near to You without presumption, to serve You with a clear mind, to be still before Your holiness and gentle toward human weakness, so that the nearer I come to You, the steadier my steps and the softer my heart will be. I will put my censer within Your word, and my days within Your order: in shouting, not frivolous; in wounding, not fleeing; until the day I see Your face, still standing among those who come nigh to You, hearing You say Yourself that that first fire will refine me, and not consume me.

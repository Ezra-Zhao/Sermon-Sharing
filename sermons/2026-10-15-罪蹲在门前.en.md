## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace be with you!

Yesterday, we finished Genesis chapter 3. Adam and Eve, clothed in the tunics of skin made by God's own hands, departed from the Garden of Eden. As they left, they carried two things upon them: one was a covering, and the other was a promise—that the Seed of the woman would bruise the serpent's head.

Today, turning to chapter 4, we will look at the first scene of this promise touching the ground. To be honest, this scene is not pleasant to look upon. The first child born outside the garden killed the second child. The Bible does not portray humanity's beginnings with polite respectability; it writes with unflinching honesty: once sin went out the garden gate, it entered the household door; first it was shifting blame between husband and wife, and then it was shedding blood between brothers.

Yet please notice, God's appearances in this chapter are no fewer than in chapter 3. The questions He asks Cain are even more numerous than those He asked Adam. He warns before the deed is done, He interrogates after the blood is shed, and He sets a mark at the time of judgment. From beginning to end, this chapter depicts a Father who refuses to let go.

Please turn to Genesis 4:1-16; I will read a few key passages:

"Now Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived and bore Cain, and said, “I have acquired a man from the LORD.” Then she bore again, this time his brother Abel. Now Abel was a keeper of sheep, but Cain was a tiller of the ground. And in the process of time it came to pass that Cain brought an offering of the fruit of the ground to the LORD. Abel also brought of the firstborn of his flock and of their fat. And the LORD respected Abel and his offering, but He did not respect Cain and his offering. And Cain was very angry, and his countenance fell." (Gen. 4:1-5)

"So the LORD said to Cain, “Why are you angry? And why has your countenance fallen? If you do well, will you not be accepted? And if you do not do well, sin lies at the door. And its desire is for you, but you should rule over it.”" (Gen. 4:6-7)

"Now Cain talked with Abel his brother; and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him. Then the LORD said to Cain, “Where is Abel your brother?” He said, “I do not know. Am I my brother’s keeper?” And He said, “What have you done? The voice of your brother’s blood cries out to Me from the ground." (Gen. 4:8-10)

"And the LORD set a mark on Cain, lest anyone finding him should kill him. Then Cain went out from the presence of the LORD and dwelt in the land of Nod on the east of Eden." (Gen. 4:15-16)

Sixteen verses, three acts:

First, 4:1-5: The two brothers sacrifice—God looks first at the person, then at the offering. If the heart is not right, no matter how complete the gift, it will not pass;
Second, 4:6-7: God intercepts man at the door—sin crouches waiting at the door, yet God steps up first to speak at the door; the warning itself is grace;
Third, 4:8-16: The voice of blood in the field and the mark of Cain—man killed his brother, yet God still pursues with questions and still protects, leaving a lifeline in judgment so man does not despair.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. After man left the garden, You did not leave man. We pray that today, through this lesson of Cain, You would illuminate those unspoken rages, comparisons, and jealousies within our hearts, so that before we raise our hands, we may first hear Your voice at the door. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: God Looks First at the Person, Then at the Offering—The Two Brothers' Sacrifices (4:1-5)

### 1.1 Eve's First Words Were Still Directed Toward the Promise

At the beginning of chapter 4, we first listen to the mother speak.

When Eve gave birth to her first son, she said: "I have acquired a man from the LORD." (Gen. 4:1)

These words connect back to yesterday's message. In 3:20, she received the name "the mother of all living," and in 3:15, she heard the promise of the Seed. Now, as the first child arrives, she immediately attributes this child to God: the LORD has caused me to acquire him. What she hoped for was very likely that very Seed who would bruise the serpent's head.

The child was named Cain. In Hebrew it is *Qayin* (קַיִן), which sounds similar to words meaning "to get" or "to acquire," perfectly matching the mother's phrase, "I have acquired a man."

Then she bore a second son, named Abel. In Hebrew it is *Hevel* (הֶבֶל), meaning breath or vapor, which is the exact word for "vanity" repeated throughout Ecclesiastes. A breath of air—blown out and gone.

Placing these two names side by side pierces the heart. The eldest is named "Acquired," and the second is named "A Breath." The one the parents took pride in later raised his hand; the one regarded as a mere breath had his blood spilled in the field, yet his name was personally proclaimed by the Lord Jesus as righteous Abel (cf. Matt. 23:35). Man looks at birth order and strength, but God has always kept a completely different ledger.

### 1.2 On the Day of Sacrifice, the Difference Lay First in the Person, Then in the Gift

The two children grew up; one tilled the ground, the other kept sheep. There is no hierarchy between occupations; the issue did not lie there.

"And in the process of time it came to pass that Cain brought an offering of the fruit of the ground to the LORD. Abel also brought of the firstborn of his flock and of their fat." (Gen. 4:3-4)

In describing Abel, the text adds two details: the firstborn, and their fat. The firstborn represents first in order of priority; the fat is the very best portion of the entire sheep. What Abel offered was giving God the best first. On Cain's side, the text merely says "the fruit of the ground"—no "firstfruits," no "choicest." Let us not add words the Bible does not say—it does not explicitly say Cain brought defective goods; but by intentionally mentioning Abel's "firstborn" and "fat," the contrast is already clear between the lines.

Even more critical is the order in the next sentence:

"And the LORD respected Abel and his offering, but He did not respect Cain and his offering." (Gen. 4:4-5)

Please notice: it mentions the person first, and then the offering. First it is "Abel and his offering," and then "Cain and his offering." God's order of acceptance is to look first at the person, and then at the gift. When the person stands upright, only then does the gift count; if a person's heart is already crooked, no matter how high the gifts are piled, they merely cover up the crookedness.

The New Testament makes this layer explicit. Hebrews 11:4 says: "By faith Abel offered to God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain, through which he obtained witness that he was righteous." The difference lies first in that single word, "faith." First John 3:12 puts it even more severely: Cain "was of the wicked one and murdered his brother. And why did he murder him? Because his works were evil and his brother’s righteous."

In the Old Testament, God had long since articulated this principle thoroughly. First Samuel 15:22: "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to heed than the fat of rams." In Psalm 51, after David committed his great sin, he said: "For You do not desire sacrifice, or else I would give it; You do not delight in burnt offering. The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit, a broken and a contrite heart—these, O God, You will not despise." (Ps. 51:16-17)

Therefore, worship is never just about pushing a gift forward and calling it done. Worship is about first presenting the person. Whether you are willing to let God deal with you first, whether your heart is willing to be leveled before Him, comes before how much you give or how loudly you sing.

### 1.3 Cain's Anger Was Directed in the Wrong Direction

When his offering was not respected, Cain's reaction was: "And Cain was very angry, and his countenance fell." (Gen. 4:5)

Brothers and sisters, we must see this step clearly. Having one's offering not accepted was originally an opportunity to return before God and examine oneself: "Lord, where was I wrong? Show me." The door was open.

Yet Cain's anger was not directed toward his own heart; it was directed toward his brother. It was not "What is wrong with me?" but "By what right does he have this?" The moment comparison enters, worship is corrupted: originally the eyes were looking at God, but now the eyes are fixed on the person beside him—he was accepted, I was not; he was commended, I was left out. Anger takes root most easily in such soil, because it wears a cloak that looks very much like righteousness: "I worked hard too; what I offered came from what I painstakingly cultivated by my own toil. By what right?"

Proverbs says: “A fool vents all his feelings, / But a wise man holds them back” (Prov. 29:11). And again: “He who is slow to anger is better than the mighty, / And he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city” (Prov. 16:32). Scripture never denies that people get angry; it asks: On whom does your anger fall? When you go home and ponder through the night, are you thinking about how to return before God, or how to outdo and crush that other person?

To wrap up this first part in one sentence: **God looks at the person first, and then at the offering. When something is wrong with the offering, do not rush to hate the person next to you; return first before God, and examine your own heart.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Sin Crouches at the Door—God Speaks Before Striking (4:6-7)

### 2.1 God’s Two Questions Are an Outlet for Anger

Cain’s countenance fell, and God spoke immediately. Notice that God did not wait until tragedy struck before coming to settle accounts; He came while the matter was still only showing on Cain’s face.

“So the LORD said to Cain, ‘Why are you angry? And why has your countenance fallen?’” (Gen. 4:6).

Just as in Genesis 3, God takes the initiative to ask. He asked Adam, “Where are you?” and He asks Cain, “Why are you angry?” God’s questions are not an interrogation; they are an invitation: Speak out what is in your heart, before it turns into an act of your hands.

What people in their anger are least willing to do is bring that anger out into the light and speak it aloud. Because once it is spoken, they themselves hear how utterly untenable it is. Kept bottled up, it only grows larger; spoken to God, it has a chance to be dismantled. The psalmists understood this; they poured their grievances, their anger, and their indignation into prayer (cf. Asaph’s turning point in Psalm 73: it was when he went into the sanctuary of God that he understood). If only Cain had been willing to pause at this question, the second half of chapter 4 would never have needed to be written.

God then pointed out the way forward: “If you do well, will you not be accepted? And if you do not do well, sin lies at the door” (Gen. 4:7).

The words “will you not be accepted” are vital. God did not shut the door. He was effectively saying: Cain, the path is still there. If you do well, you too will be accepted; acceptance is not reserved exclusively for Abel. You do not need to fight for it; just return and walk the right path, and you will have it. Those listening to this sermon today need to hear this clearly: God’s acceptance is not a limited supply; someone else receiving it does not mean there is less for you. A comparing heart always assumes grace is a sliced pie—if someone gets a larger bite, I get less. That is not true. “If you do well, will you not be accepted?”—these words are spoken to everyone who feels left behind.

### 2.2 “Crouching at the Door”: Sin’s Posture and What It Desires

Then comes the most famous, and most difficult to translate, sentence in the entire chapter:

“Sin lies at the door. And its desire is for you, but you should rule over it” (Gen. 4:7).

“Lies” or “crouches”—the Hebrew word is *rovets*, originally meaning to crouch or lie down, like a wild beast lying in wait. Sin here is not an abstract concept; it is a beast crouching at the door. Not noisy, not thrashing, not rushing to pounce—just lying beside the door you must pass through every day, waiting for your heart to soften, waiting for you to grow weary, waiting for you to say, “Just this once.”

English translations frame this verse with different phrasing, yet the meaning aligns:
- KJV: “sin lieth at the door. And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.”
- NIV: “sin is crouching at your door; it desires to have you, but you must rule over it.”
- NRSV: “sin is lurking at the door; its desire is for you, but you must master it.”

The Lu Zhenzhong version struggles to translate this verse, which precisely demonstrates how compressed and difficult the original Hebrew is; the scripture quotations in this manuscript are based on the Chinese Union Version, while original word meanings are referenced for explanatory purposes only.

Look further at the word “desire.” In Hebrew, it is *teshuqah*, to rush toward, to long for. It was just used once in Genesis 3:16, where God says to the woman, “Your desire shall be for your husband.” It is the exact same word. Sin’s craving for man carries a force that seeks total possession: it is not content with making you stumble occasionally; it wants to move in permanently, have the final say, and make you obey it from then on.

God’s command follows immediately: “But you should rule over it.” To rule over—the Hebrew is *mashal*, to govern, to master. God’s words are unmistakable: as for the beast at the door, if you do not master it, it will master you. There is no middle ground. Feed anger, jealousy, and bitterness for one day, and they grow larger by a day; cut them off for one day, and they grow weaker by a day. How you treat that thing crouching at the door today determines whether you rule it tomorrow, or it rules you.

In his commentary on Genesis, Warren W. Wiersbe, when expounding this verse, reminds readers to pay attention to the timing of God’s warning to Cain. The main point is that God issues a warning before the sinful act takes place, showing that God leaves room for man to repent, and man cannot blame his subsequent choices on circumstances or others. (Source: Warren W. Wiersbe, *The Bible Exposition Commentary*, main teaching of Genesis 4; this manuscript paraphrases and is not a direct quotation.)

### 2.3 The Warning Is Grace, but Tragically Cain Did Not Grasp It

The Book of James breaks down this chain for us: “Then, when desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death” (Jas. 1:15). Conceived, full-grown, bringing forth death—there is a process in between. What God was intercepting in Genesis 4:6-7 was precisely this step of conception. A fallen countenance is still only conception; words spoken and hands set in motion are the birth.

The words of Proverbs 4:23 are most fitting when read alongside this chapter: “Keep your heart with all diligence, / For out of it spring the issues of life.” Guarding the heart is not about crushing emotions to death; it is about guarding the doorway: which thoughts are allowed in to sit down, and which thoughts are shown the door on the spot. Abel died in the field, but the root lay at the door—in that place where no human saw, where only God asked the question, Cain had already lost the first battle.

Let me press this point home here.

Brothers and sisters, God is still speaking at the door today. He does not wait until you have brought about disaster before seeking you out. At the very moment your face begins to fall, before the words leave your mouth, before your hand is raised, He asks you: Why are you angry?

Blessed is the one who hears this question! Do not wait. Settle it today, at the door; do not drag it out to be settled in the field. Repenting at the door costs a single prayer; repenting in the field costs a lifetime of wandering. While sin is still crouching, before it pounces, return before God and lay your heart bare before Him—He questions you not to condemn you, but to hold back your hand.

To wrap up this second part: **Sin is crouching at the door, but God is also standing at the door. Whomever you listen to first determines which door you walk through.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: Blood Has a Voice, the Mark Bears Mercy—From the Field to Nod (4:8-16)

### 3.1 In the Field, God Asked the Most Piercing Question

The warning went unheeded.

“Now Cain talked with Abel his brother; and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him” (Gen. 4:8).

When Scripture records murder, it uses only a single sentence, so plain that it chills the heart. It does not embellish, because it needs no embellishment: an older brother led his younger brother into an empty field and struck him down. At first, it was a fallen countenance during sacrifice, then it was words, and finally it was the field. The path of sin becoming full-grown is walked step by step; not a single step happens out of nowhere.

God came again, and once again He asked: “Where is Abel your brother?” (Gen. 4:9).

When Adam hid, God asked, “Where are you?” When Cain murdered, God asked, “Where is your brother?” What God asks is always the very thing man least dares to answer.

Cain’s reply is the hardest, most defiant statement in Scripture: “I do not know. Am I my brother’s keeper?” (Gen. 4:9).

“Keeper”—the Hebrew is *shomer*, the very same word for keeping and protecting used in Psalm 121: “The LORD is your keeper.” He was effectively saying: He is not my responsibility. Brothers and sisters, so many relationships die on this one sentence: “He is not my responsibility.” Parents toward children, husbands toward wives, church members toward one another—once this sentence is erected in the heart, what happens in the field is only a matter of time.

God’s answer brought the very earth into the reckoning: “The voice of your brother’s blood cries out to Me from the ground. So now you are cursed from the earth, which has opened its mouth to receive your brother’s blood from your hand” (Gen. 4:10-11).

Blood has a voice. Man assumes that once it is buried in the dirt, no one will know; God says the dirt itself is speaking on his behalf. Abel’s name means “a breath”; the breath vanished, but the voice did not. Thousands of years later, when the Lord Jesus spoke of martyrs, He still began with “the blood of righteous Abel” (cf. Matt. 23:35). What man regarded as a fleeting breath, God remembered for thousands of years.

The judgment immediately falls: "When you till the ground, it shall no longer yield its strength to you. A fugitive and a vagabond you shall be on the earth" (Gen. 4:12). The tiller of the ground finds that the ground refuses to yield its strength to him; the one who coveted the produce of the soil is destined to wander over the earth. What sin promises and what sin ultimately delivers have always been two entirely different things.

### 3.2 Cain’s Fear, and the Mark God Set on the Murderer

Only now does Cain speak, and his words are filled with "I" after "I": "My punishment is greater than I can bear! Surely You have driven me out this day from the face of the ground; I shall be hidden from Your face; I shall be a fugitive and a vagabond on the earth, and it will happen that anyone who finds me will kill me" (Gen. 4:13-14).

Notice: what he feared was the punishment falling upon himself. From beginning to end, he did not utter a single word of grief for Abel. There is a vast difference in direction between regret and repentance. Regret is aching over the consequences to oneself; repentance is aching over the person one has wounded and the God one has offended. Cain had regret, but he had no repentance. This lesson holds true for every one of us: when you pray, do not rush to count how much you have suffered; first ask, "How is the person I wounded? How is the God I have offended?"

Yet the next scene is the most breathtaking, speechless moment of the entire chapter.

God says to this man who murdered his brother: "Therefore, whoever kills Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him sevenfold." And the LORD set a mark on Cain, lest anyone finding him should kill him (Gen. 4:15).

"Mark," in Hebrew *'owth* (אוֹת)—a sign, a miraculous sign. It is the very same word used for the rainbow as a sign in Genesis 9, and circumcision as a sign in Genesis 17. Scripture does not describe what this mark looked like, and we should not guess. What Scripture makes crystal clear is its purpose: protection. God Himself issued a restraining order against vigilantism, stopping the cycle of blood vengeance. This mark was not a medal of honor; it did not mean Cain had done anything right. It was God’s sovereign declaration placed upon the face of a murderer: "Vengeance belongs to Me, not to you." The words of Romans 12:19 are foreshadowed right here: "Beloved, do not avenge yourselves, but rather give place to wrath; for it is written, 'Vengeance is Mine, I will repay,' says the Lord" (Rom. 12:19).

The judgment was real: the wandering was real, being driven from God’s face was real. Yet the mercy was also real: his life was spared, and the mark was set. In that very same verse, God’s justice and God’s mercy do not yield a single inch to one another.

### 3.3 The Land of Nod, and the Gleam of Light at the Chapter's End

"Then Cain went out from the presence of the LORD and dwelt in the land of Nod on the east of Eden" (Gen. 4:16).

Nod, in Hebrew *Nod* (נוֹד), means wandering. The place where he went to live was literally named "Wandering." East of Eden, and farther east still—man drifts farther and farther away from the garden. Adam was driven to the east of the garden; Cain walked even further east. The directional pull of sin is remarkably consistent: always heading east, always moving farther from His face.

Yet at the end of chapter 4, Scripture does not leave us with Cain. The close of the chapter records that Eve bore Seth, and Seth had Enosh: "Then men began to call on the name of the LORD" (Gen. 4:26). Abel was dead, but the line of promise was not broken; God appointed another son to carry it forward. And humanity’s way of responding to God shifted from comparison at the altar of sacrifice to calling upon His name.

That line leads all the way to the cross. Hebrews 12:24 says that the blood of Jesus "speaks better things than that of Abel." Abel’s blood cried out from the ground, crying for justice; the blood of Jesus speaks, proclaiming forgiveness. One man’s blood cried out demanding retribution; another Man’s blood cried out putting a full stop to retribution. The vigilantism restrained by the mark on Cain’s face was brought to the cross, where God, through the blood of His own Son, severed the entire chain of vengeance once and for all.

To conclude our third part: **Abel’s blood has a voice, and Jesus’ blood also has a voice. What you hear today is the latter: your sins are forgiven, and your family no longer has to keep walking east.**

## (52:00 - 60:00) Applications for Six Groups of People · Altar Call · Closing Prayer

### Application 1: To Seekers Who Have Not Yet Believed in the Lord

My friend, this lesson offers you a word today: God speaks before disaster strikes, not only judging after it happens.

Even after hearing the warning, Cain still chose that path. That was his choice, not because God had failed to stop him. In your life, is there a recurring word—spoken by parents, urged by friends, or known deep down within your own conscience—that you keep pushing away? God asks questions in many ways. Your hearing this sermon today is itself a question from Him. While the matter is still crouching at the door, do not let it walk out into the field.

### Application 2: To Husbands, Wives, Siblings, and Parents in Marriages and Families

The victim in this chapter was not a stranger, but a brother. The deepest wounds often occur in the closest relationships.

When you go home, change one sentence first. Erase "He is not my responsibility" from your home. Between husband and wife, between parents and children, between brothers and sisters, God’s question to Cain—"Where is your brother?"—must be answered at home first: Do I know how he has been lately? Did I ask when his countenance fell, instead of just waiting? If Cain had learned to keep his brother at home, what happened in the field would never have taken place. Keeping is not controlling; it is caring.

### Application 3: To Those in the Workplace and in Competition

The workplace is where the sacrificial altar is most easily re-enacted: a colleague’s proposal was accepted, while mine was not; he was promoted, while I stayed in place.

The formula of the Cain-like reaction is: when someone else is accepted, I become angry; once I am angry, I pin the blame on them. Change that formula today: when your offering is not accepted, first return before God to deal with your own heart, and then faithfully till the ground in your hands. Proverbs 14:30 says, "A sound heart is life to the body, But envy is rottenness to the bones." Envy rots your own bones; the first one it harms is yourself. Those who live by comparison ruin themselves before they even begin to till the soil.

### Application 4: To New Believers

New believers must learn to listen to the sound at the door. After coming to faith, temptation does not vanish; it simply changes how it crouches: a recurring thought, something you should not click on, a harsh word right at the tip of your tongue.

Remember three things: First, crouching does not mean it has won; the fact that it is crouching outside means it has not yet entered. Second, do not negotiate with it; negotiating is feeding it. Third, pray immediately, remove yourself from that environment, and confide in a mature brother or sister. Mastering sin rarely happens by toughing it out alone in a room; most often, it comes by stepping immediately back into the light.

### Application 5: To Leaders, Parents, and Those in Ministry

Those who lead can learn three steps from God’s dealings with Cain: first ask, then warn, and leave a way of life.

God did not condemn Cain right out of the gate. He first asked, "Why are you angry?", giving him space to speak. Then He clearly laid out the consequences, fully exposing the posture of sin. Finally, even when the judgment fell, He set a mark to leave a way of life. When we correct others today, we should do the same: clarify the issue, make boundaries clear, and provide a way forward. Leadership that offers only condemnation without a way out will only produce another person walking east.

### Application 6: To the Entire Church

The church must be a people who call on the name of the Lord, not people competing over whose offering is bigger on the sacrificial ground.

The sentence at the end of Genesis 4 is one of the church’s birth certificates: "Then men began to call on the name of the LORD" (Gen. 4:26). Abel’s place was empty, Seth stepped in, and the calling began. This is how the church has survived for thousands of years: not because people never stumble, but because after stumbling, they are still willing to call upon Him. If someone among us is currently walking east, the church’s responsibility is not to point fingers behind their back, but to walk over and ask: "Where are you? Where is your brother? Let us come back together."

### The Spark Moment

*(Pause, slowing down the pace)*

Brothers and sisters, I want you to remember this door today.

Sin is crouching at the door, but God is also standing at the door. Cain did not lose to Abel; he lost because he had two opportunities to turn back, and both times he refused. After the sacrifice, he refused to examine himself; after the warning, he refused to stay his hand.

*(Pause, speaking with increased emphasis)*

What about you? Where has that matter in your heart stalled? Is it in a fallen countenance, in a spoken word, or have you already made plans to go out into the field?

It is today, not tomorrow. Stop at the door. Before your hand is raised, before the words become irrevocable, before the relationship is severed, listen first as God asks you: "Why are you angry? Where is your brother?"

This question is the final step He has left for you, and it is also the first way of life He offers you. Step down, and you find home; walk past, and you find Nod.

### Altar Call

I am making two calls:

**The first call**: You are carrying suppressed anger, jealousy, or bitterness toward someone in your heart, and you know that taking one more step forward will bring disaster. If you are willing today to surrender it at the door and ask God to master it, please stand up, and we will pray together for you. There is no shame in this; the one who stands up is the very first one to triumph today.

**The second call**: You have lost touch with a family member or a brother, and that phrase in your heart, "He is not my responsibility," has been spoken for far too long. If you are willing today to take that first step back, please raise your hand, and I want to pray for you. And for our friends who have not yet believed in the Lord, if you are willing to receive Jesus and let His blood speak on your behalf, please also raise your hand.

*(Waiting for response, praying for those standing and raising their hands)*

### Closing Response Prayer

Let us bow our heads and pray together:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. You questioned Cain the moment his countenance fell; You still pursued him after blood was spilled in the field; and You even set a mark upon him when judgment fell. Your justice was never compromised, and Your mercy was never absent.

Lord, we confess that something is crouching in our hearts as well. There is comparison, there is envy, and there is that phrase we have spoken for years: *He is not my responsibility.* We bring it before You; we will no longer feed it, and we will no longer hide it. We pray that You would remind us according to Your Word: 'If you do well, will you not be accepted?' Bring us back to the right path to be accepted, rather than grasping for what belongs to others.

We pray that You would hold back our hands—hold them back at the door, not after we reach the field. Heal the wounds in our families, between brothers and sisters, and among coworkers. May those who ought to keep their brother become his keeper once more; may those heading east turn around and come back.

We thank You that while Abel’s blood cried out, the blood of Your Son speaks better things for us. Relying on this blood, we will not go to Nod today; we are coming home.

In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

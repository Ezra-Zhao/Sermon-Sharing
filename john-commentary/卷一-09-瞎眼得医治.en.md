# Commentary on the Gospel of John, Volume One, Chapter 9: The Man Born Blind Has Seen

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «生来瞎眼的人，看见了».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

- Volume One, Chapter 9
- Author: Guangyi Zhao (Guangyi Zhao)
- Scripture: John chapter 9

---

This chapter is a little special in the Gospel of John. Elsewhere, it is mostly Jesus speaking; in this chapter, Jesus did one thing, and then yielded the stage to a man who had just become able to see. This man had not read many books and had no status; when he appeared, he was begging by the roadside. Yet the most solid sentence in the whole chapter was spoken by him.

John wrote this chapter as though writing a record of questioning: the neighbors questioned him, the religious leaders examined him, and even his parents were called in to be confronted. And from beginning to end he spoke of only one thing — his own experience. In this chapter, we too want to follow this path: not in a hurry to speak great doctrines first, but to look first at a real person, and how he met Jesus.

## I. The Scripture

The man by the roadside had no name when he appeared. After reading the whole chapter through, you still cannot find his name; you can find only two labels: born blind, and sitting and begging.

> Now as Jesus passed by, He saw a man who was blind from birth. And His disciples asked Him, saying, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" Jesus answered, "Neither this man nor his parents sinned, but that the works of God should be revealed in him. ... When I am in the world, I am the light of the world." When He had said these things, He spat on the ground and made clay with the saliva; and He anointed the eyes of the blind man with the clay. And He said to him, "Go, wash in the pool of Siloam" (which is translated, Sent). So he went and washed, and came back seeing. (John 9:1-7, NKJV)

Two small points need to be explained clearly first.

The Pool of Siloam was in the south of the city of Jerusalem; its water was brought in from the Gihon Spring outside the city through an underground water channel. John specifically gave this name a note in parentheses: Siloam is "Sent." This blind man, in effect, was sent — dispatched — to go and wash. He heard, went, washed, and came back seeing. There was no other secret to his healing; he simply did according to that word.

Another word is "begging." Later, when the neighbors recognized him, what they recognized was also this label: "Is not this he who sat and begged?" (9:8) In that era, for a person born blind it meant being unable to work all his life — no trade, no income, able only to sit by the roadside and rely on people's alms. When people mentioned him, the first thing they thought of was not his name, but his affliction. He lived in the city, yet it was as though he stood outside the city.

At this point, the matter could have ended: a man was healed, and went home to live his life. But John used a whole chapter to record what followed, because the real story lies afterward. First, put two key sayings here.

When he was summoned a second time by the religious leaders for questioning, he was pressed to declare his position: Was Jesus a sinner or not? His answer was:

> "Whether He is a sinner or not I do not know. One thing I know: that though I was blind, now I see." (John 9:25, NKJV)

Later, because of words like these, he was cast out. When Jesus heard of this matter, He personally came to find him:

> Jesus heard that they had cast him out; and when He had found him, He said to him, "Do you believe in the Son of God?" He answered and said, "Who is He, Lord, that I may believe in Him?" And Jesus said to him, "You have both seen Him and it is He who is talking with you." Then he said, "Lord, I believe!" And he worshiped Him. (John 9:35-38, NKJV)

## II. Exposition

That question of the disciples asked, in fact, the question in everyone's heart: When something this big has happened, someone has to be responsible, right?

In their thinking, between suffering and sin there was a straight, direct line. The Old Testament had said that God visits the iniquity of the fathers upon the children to the third and fourth generations (Exodus 20:5); the calculation the disciples took for granted was this one: either this man himself, or his parents — one party must have done wrong, for retribution to fall upon his eyes. With one sentence Jesus cut this line: Neither this man nor his parents sinned. This must be read carefully. Jesus was not saying that God, in order to display a miracle today, had deliberately made this man blind for several decades. He replaced the whole question: the disciples asked "why," pursuing responsibility; Jesus answered, "but that the works of God should be revealed in him," looking at the way out. Where suffering comes from, He did not explain; where this man's story would go, He rewrote on the spot.

"When I am in the world, I am the light of the world" — this sentence was not a new phrase either. Just in the previous chapter, Jesus had already publicly declared: "I am the light of the world. He who follows Me shall not walk in darkness, but have the light of life" (John 8:12, NKJV). Not long after those words were spoken, He met this man born blind on the road. The declaration was followed immediately by an instance. This word light, in His mouth, was not merely a figure of speech; it was to fall upon a specific person.

Then came that clay. Jesus could perfectly well have spoken one sentence and made him see; it was not that He could not do so. But this time He stooped down, spat on the ground, made clay, and anointed those eyes that could not see. Those who have read the beginning of the Bible will feel a stir in their hearts here: in the beginning, God formed man from the dust of the ground (Genesis 2:7). Clay, a man's hands, the Lord who formed man — this scene was like a small re-creation. And He also wanted this blind man to walk the last stretch himself: feeling along the wall, groping his way, going down to the side of the pool, and washing the clay off. The light entered in the action of his doing according to what was said.

The disturbance arose over the Sabbath. The trouble lay in that clay: in the rabbis' rules, making clay belonged to work, and work was not permitted on the Sabbath. So the Pharisees first argued among themselves; some said, "This Man is not from God, because He does not keep the Sabbath" (9:16, NKJV). The Old Testament prophet Isaiah had long ago said that the One who was to come would open the eyes of the blind (Isaiah 35:5). Now the evidence stood before them — a living, seeing person — yet they first had to deal with the question of regulations. The most sobering contrast in this chapter lies here: people whose eyes were intact checked the procedures against the light; the person who had never seen the light walked, step by step, into the light.

There is another background, not far away. At the Feast of Tabernacles not long before, Jesus had stood among the crowd and cried out: "If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink" (John 7:37, NKJV). During that feast, the priests drew water every day from the Pool of Siloam and poured it on the altar. Now the pool to which Jesus sent this blind man to wash was the very same pool. The water drawn during the feast was for ceremony; this time, the pool water washed open a pair of real eyes.

Lay out the order of questioning in the whole chapter, and you will see how this man changed. After he washed and returned, the neighbors first argued: Was this really that man or not? Some said it was he, others said it was not; in the end he himself spoke up: "I am he" (9:9, NKJV). When a person changes too completely, those who first cannot recognize him are often the acquaintances. The neighbors pressed him on how he had come to see, and he said: "A Man called Jesus..." (9:11) In his mouth, Jesus was first "that Man." Later he was brought before the Pharisees; asked one level deeper, he changed his wording: "He is a prophet" (9:17, NKJV). At the second examination, the other side pressed him to change his words and draw a clear line from Jesus; he instead summed up a line of reasoning: "If this Man were not from God, He could do nothing" (9:33, NKJV). By the end of the chapter, standing before Jesus, what he cried out was: "Lord, I believe!" The address rose level by level. Note this order: it was not that he first learned a whole set of doctrines completely, and only then began to speak; he understood as he spoke. Understanding grew in the process of his telling his own experience, time after time. Examined to the end, he even spoke a passage of his own reasoning: "Now we know that God does not hear sinners; but if anyone is a worshiper of God and does His will, He hears him. Since the world began it has been unheard of that anyone opened the eyes of one who was born blind" (John 9:31-32, NKJV). A begging man, standing before a room full of learned men, spoke the clearest passage in the whole scene.

In the middle there is a passage that makes one feel distressed to read. The Pharisees called his parents in; his parents were terrified and pushed the words away completely clean: "He is of age; ask him. He will speak for himself" (9:21, NKJV, cf.). John explained the reason: at that time the Jews had already agreed that if anyone confessed that Jesus was the Christ, he would be put out of the synagogue (9:22). The synagogue was the center of Jewish life; relatives and neighbors, business dealings, weddings and funerals were all tied to it; to be cast out meant being struck off by the whole community. The parents' weakness does not look good, but it is all too real. In the end this man was still cast out (9:34); he paid the full price for that sentence, "One thing I know."

The ending is the warmest stroke in the whole chapter. Those who cast him out were the religious authorities; the One who came to find him was Jesus Himself. In fact, from beginning to end, the one taking the initiative was Jesus: at the opening, it was Jesus who first saw him (9:1), when he still could see nothing; at the end, it was Jesus who heard he had been cast out and specially came to meet him. This man bowed down and worshiped Jesus — "worshiped" is the action of prostrating oneself in worship; the One he worshiped was already no longer merely a benefactor. And the Pharisees standing nearby asked: "Are we blind also?" Jesus answered: "If you were blind, you would have no sin; but now you say, 'We see.' Therefore your sin remains" (John 9:41, NKJV). Here the chapter closes: the blind have seen, and those who thought they could see are, on the contrary, blind. The dividing line is not in the eyes, but in whether one is willing to admit that one needs light.

## III. Application

Is suffering fate? The entry point of this chapter begins right here.

When Chinese people meet with a sudden calamity, they often say one sentence — "bad fate" — or trace it upward: Was it that the ancestors did not accumulate virtue, that something went wrong with the family's feng shui? The disciples' way of asking is exactly the same as ours, only asked more bluntly: Was it this man, or his parents? Hidden behind this way of asking is a little comfort: as long as the responsible party is found, suffering seems to have an account settled. But for the person lying right in the suffering, this comfort is cold; it amounts to judging the wounded person once more. Jesus did not walk down this road. He did not issue an accident report to this family; He stretched out His hand. If you are in an inexplicable situation — ill for a long time, something has happened at home, your days inexplicably stuck — the first thing this chapter hands you is not an explanation, but an order: you do not have to think through "why" first before you deserve help.

Next, speak of that label. Note the first action in this chapter: what Jesus saw, Scripture writes, was "a man," not "a blind man." The neighbors mentioning him called him "that beggar"; the disciples mentioning him first asked about his sin; the Pharisees mentioning him checked his record. Only Jesus first saw a person. Perhaps on you too there is a label that has been pasted on until it is taken for granted: that sick person, that unemployed person, that divorced person, that person buried in debt. When a label has been pasted on long enough, even oneself almost believes it, thinking that is one's name. This chapter says: that is not your name.

Next, speak of that sentence, "One thing I know." Compared with the Pharisees, this man had no advantage in anything: no learning, no status, he could not even fully explain Jesus' origin. He could not win an argument, and he could not recite Scripture as well as they could. But in his hand there was one thing the other side did not have — his own story. "Though I was blind, now I see" — this sentence cannot be refuted, because it is not an opinion; it is a fact. Many people's plan concerning faith is to wait until they have thought through all the questions before believing. The difficulty is that questions cannot be thought through to the end: the Pharisees were the most familiar with Scripture, and were farthest from the answer. This chapter gives the opposite order: first grasp that one small thing you truly know, even if it is as small as a comfort you cannot explain, a turn that cannot be dismissed as coincidence. Acknowledge it, and begin walking from it.

There is also: cast out, and found again. Perhaps you have had unpleasant experiences with the church or with believers, and have even been hurt. In this chapter, those who hurt people were religious people: they had Scripture, they had regulations, they had enthusiasm for condemning people; the only thing they lacked was mercy. Please do not mix them up together with Jesus: those who cast people out are them; the One who goes out to find people is Jesus. To this day, this distinction still holds.

Finally, that sentence, "We see." This sentence is spoken to those who think themselves clear-sighted, including you and me who are reading this chapter. Our generation has much information and quick judgments, and ready-made views prepared for everything. Too many views sometimes block the eyes: when a new thing is placed before one, it is first processed by the old conclusion. This blind man, on the contrary, had an advantage: he clearly knew that he could not see, so he was willing to go and wash, willing to acknowledge, willing to believe. Admitting, "In this matter, I am blind," is not admitting defeat; that is the place where light begins to enter the eyes.

## IV. Response

Having read this far, you might pause and think about a few questions. There is no need to hurry to answer with standard answers; thinking itself is a beginning.

- If you had been standing beside that man at the time, what would you most want to ask him? Would you ask him whether, blind for so many years, there was hatred in his heart? Or would you ask him what color this world was, that he saw for the first time?
- "One thing I know" — setting aside for now all the questions you have not thought through, among the things you have personally experienced, is there even one that you cannot easily dismiss with the two words "by chance"?
- The Pharisees' difficulty — do you have it? Is there something you have in fact not yet understood, yet have already drawn a conclusion about?
- Jesus' instruction to that blind man was simple, only one sentence: Go, wash. And he went. If today there is one small thing you know in your heart you should do, yet have been putting off, what would it be?

If you are willing, you may follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, I admit that there are many things I cannot see clearly, including my own days. If You truly are the light of the world, please first let me see a little, beginning right from that one thing I truly know, and lead me forward. Amen."

There are also entrances for continuing onward. In the next chapter we will read John chapter 10, where Jesus says that He Himself is the "good shepherd" — the very One in this chapter who went out to find the man. You may also find a trusted Christian friend, point out to him the sentence in this chapter you are least convinced by, and hear how he answers. Questions need not be stored up; asking them is the beginning.

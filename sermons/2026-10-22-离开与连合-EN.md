## Summary (about 100 words)

The LORD God said that it is not good for the man to be alone, and that He would make a helper fit for him. A man leaves his father and mother and cleaves to his wife, and the two become one flesh. A wedding is not two people holding a ceremony; it is the order God established in the beginning being fulfilled today in two people: leaving is the order, cleaving is the covenant, and becoming one is grace. May the couple take these three words with them into their marriage.

---

## (00:00 - 03:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Dear relatives and friends, dear guests, today we have not come to join a festivity; we have come to bear witness. Witness to what? Witness that, before these two people even knew each other, God had already prepared His purpose for marriage; witness that a man and a woman, before everyone, say the words "I do" to each other, and also to God.

Let us reverently read Genesis chapter 2 together:

"And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him." (Gen 2:18, KJV)

"Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh." (Gen 2:24, KJV)

Now hear the echo in Ephesians chapter 5: "For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother, and shall be joined unto his wife, and they two shall be one flesh. This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church." (Eph 5:31-32, excerpt, KJV)

Let us bow our heads in prayer: Dear heavenly Father, thank You for instituting marriage, and thank You for bringing this couple before You today. Through Your word, teach them to understand leaving, to learn to cleave, and to become one in You. And let every person here, married or unmarried, hear once again Your purpose from the beginning. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (03:00 - 10:00) Part One: It Is Not Good to Be Alone — Marriage Begins with God's Purpose (2:18-23)

The starting point of marriage is not that two people first fell for each other; it is that God first spoke a word: it is not good for the man to be alone.

At that time Adam lacked nothing. The Garden of Eden was a garden God planted with His own hand, his work was given by God, his food was prepared by God, and he spoke with God face to face. He lacked no resources and no mission; he lacked only one thing: a person fit for him. God said it was not good — this is the first time in Genesis that God says something is not good. Before this He saw everything He had made, and it was very good; but when it came to the man being alone, He said, it is not good. Loneliness is not a feeling that comes only when a person is weak; loneliness is a judgment God Himself made: human beings were created not to live alone.

So God said: I will make a helper fit for him. The words "helper fit for him" are in the original 'ezer kenegdô [1] — 'ezer is a helper, and kenegdô means opposite him, corresponding to him, fit for him. Please hear this clearly: in the Old Testament the word 'ezer is most often used of God Himself: He is our help, the help of Israel. A helper is not a person of lower rank; a helper is a person who comes with strength to uphold another. Kenegdô is even more beautiful: face to face, eye to eye, a worthy counterpart, completing each other. God did not make Adam a follower; He made a person who could talk with him, remind him, and stand shoulder to shoulder with him.

Bride and groom, I give this sentence to you today: you have not come to find a person to order around, nor a person to support you; you are receiving an 'ezer kenegdô — a helper with strength, fit for you, who will walk face to face with you for a lifetime. Respect should be the air of your marriage from the first day.

God made Eve not from another handful of dust, but from Adam's rib. When Adam woke and saw her, his first words were poetry: This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh. The intimacy of marriage, from the very beginning, carries this kind of claiming: you are not an outsider; you are part of my life. In a moment, when you exchange your vows, that will not be reciting lines; it will be, like Adam, claiming each other before everyone: from today, your affairs are my affairs, your home is my home, and your tomorrow includes a share that is mine.

There is another layer of meaning to say to the couple today. God brought Eve to Adam — the word "brought" in the text is very tender. In marriage there is God's own leading, there are also the many years of your parents' nurturing, and there are your own choices, step by step. You did not grab a random person from a vast sea of people; in prayer, in getting to know each other, and in the blessing of your elders, you recognized each other. So the order of gratitude at a wedding must not be confused: thank God first, then thank your parents, then thank each other. A new home that knows gratitude has half of its foundation already firm. In the days ahead, when you meet difficulties and look back on today, remember this: this person is the one God brought to me, the one my parents, with tears, blessed and entrusted to me; I must cherish this person all my life, I must not say "whatever" in a moment of anger, and I must not belittle the other's worth in times of trouble. Being fit for each other does not mean having no differences; being fit means that when differences are placed together, they can exactly complete each other. His rationality complements your sensitivity, your attentiveness complements his carelessness — this is precisely the beauty of kenegdô: not a copy of yourself, but receiving a person different from me, yet prepared for me. Learning to appreciate difference is far more important than rushing to remake the other person.

## (10:00 - 17:00) Part Two: Leaving — Not a Severing of Kinship, but a Rearranging of Order (2:24a)

Verse 24 begins with the word "therefore." Because there is such a fitting helper, because there is such bone-of-bone intimacy, therefore a man shall leave his father and mother.

"Leave" is in the original 'azav [2], to leave, to forsake, to lay down one's former dependence. The word is used with weight. The Bible does not say that a person should despise his parents; the Ten Commandments clearly say, Honour thy father and thy mother. Leaving does not speak of an emotional severing; it speaks of a rearranging of order: from today, the first place in your life is no longer your father and mother; it is your spouse.

I have seen many difficulties in marriage that were not because the two people were bad, but because the order was not set right. The parents' words always outweigh the spouse's words, the door of the small home is always open to the family of origin, and money, time, and decisions — not one of them is discussed first by the two of them. In such a marriage, the person is still there, but the heart has stayed at the parents' home, and cleaving has nowhere to begin.

So today, before both sets of parents, I will say this plainly. Leaving, to the couple: you must learn to make your own decisions, bear your own responsibility, and give your own account to God. When something happens, your first sentence should not be a phone call asking, "Mom, what do you say?" It should be to close the door, and for the two of you to pray together and discuss together. Your parents will always be your support, but a support is not a steering wheel.

Leaving, to the parents: today is also your day to let go. You raised your children and brought them here; most of your assignment is already done. From now on, the deepest love is not to keep managing, but to step back one pace — to bless, to watch, and to pray. When a child calls to pour out grievances, first persuade toward reconciliation; do not pour oil on the fire. When a child has not asked for help, do not reach in on your own to change the rules of their small home. Letting go is not losing a child; it is gaining a son, gaining a daughter, and gaining a new home that will rise up.

Leaving also sets a hope for the couple's future: one day your children will also leave you, and then you will understand your parents' hearts today. While you can now, honour them well and go home often to see them — leaving never means going far away; it means, with gratitude, building a new, independent home.

Leaving must also land on a few concrete small matters. I will say them plainly before everyone today, so you do not have to grope your way slowly after the wedding. First, manage money together. How much you earn, how much you spend, how much you give to both sets of parents, how much you offer — lay it all out on one table, the two of you, and speak openly, hiding no private thoughts; openness about money is the first lesson of being one. Second, arrange time together. However busy work is, leave clear boundaries for your spouse, for your parents, and for yourselves; do not let phones and social obligations take up all the evenings of your small home. Third, speak words first at home. If you have an opinion about the other, close the door first and speak well; do not run first to your own family or your in-laws' family to complain. If parents ask, report only good news and ask for prayer; do not let a momentary emotion become a lasting knot between two families. These things sound trivial, yet they are exactly the daily practice of leaving. When the order is right, the home is at peace; when the order is disordered, even deep affection will be worn away. If you are willing to learn from these three small matters, your leaving will not be a slogan; it will be the mark that a new home has truly become independent.

## (17:00 - 21:00) Part Three: Cleaving and Becoming One Flesh — A Covenant, Not a Contract (2:24b; Eph 5:31-32)

After leaving comes cleaving. "Cleave" is in the original davaq [3], to cling closely, to adhere, to follow tightly, like flesh stuck to bone and unable to be separated. In Deuteronomy this word is used for Israel cleaving to the LORD and following Him tightly — used of marriage, its weight comes out: cleaving is not living under the same roof, not keeping house as partners; it is two people making a covenant before God, pressing their whole selves in, leaving no way back.

A contract speaks of conditions: only if you do it will I do it. A covenant speaks of commitment: in prosperity or in adversity, I am here. The vows you are about to speak are the words of a covenant. A wedding lasts one day; a covenant is kept for a lifetime. As days pass you will discover that the hardest part of cleaving is not the big things, but the small things: after a tiring day, are you still willing to speak well? When there is a disagreement, do you first win over the other person, or first win back the relationship? When you have done wrong, are you willing to be the first to say sorry? The cleaving of davaq is, in these small things, pressed closer day by day.

The two become one flesh — in the original basar echad [4], one flesh, one body. Being one does not mean two people become one person, with one swallowing the other. Being one means: joys are shared together, difficulties are borne together; your weakness is covered by me, my lack is supplied by you; no outsider can insert himself into this cleaving again, and not even one's own wilfulness can. Being one also means openness — the text says that at that time the husband and wife were both naked, and were not ashamed. Before each other you need not perform and need not hide; such candour is the most precious gift in marriage, to be guarded for a lifetime.

In Ephesians Paul raises this verse one level higher: This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church. It turns out marriage is not only a matter of two people; marriage is a picture: as Christ loved the church and gave Himself for it, so a groom loves his bride and gives himself for her; as the church reverences Christ, so a bride reverences her husband. Your wedding today therefore has a mission of witness — in the days ahead, when neighbours see how you live, they will be able to glimpse a little of how Christ loves people. This burden is not light, but grace is sufficient: only because you have first been loved by Christ in this way can you learn to love each other in this way.

Cleaving must also withstand the test of conflict, so let me say a few more honest words. What couple does not bicker? The key is not not quarrelling, but how you come back after a quarrel. I set three rules for you: do not say the word divorce — angry words wound the covenant most; do not dig up old accounts at night — when you are tired, sleep first, and talk when it is light; do not let a third person judge the matter — pray first, and first admit your own share of fault. Whoever bows the head first is not the loser; whoever bows the head first is the one who first remembered the covenant. Being one also means upholding each other before outsiders: do not belittle your spouse before friends, and do not make the other's shortcomings into a joke; this is the dignity of being one. If you keep this, cleaving will not be a verse at a wedding; it will be grace still visible thirty, fifty years later, when children and grandchildren gather around the table. Then your hair will be white, your hands still holding each other, and young people will understand at a glance: so this is what the Bible means by becoming one flesh.

## (21:00 - 24:00) Application: Everyone Present Receives One Sentence

To the couple. Remember today's three words: leaving, cleaving, one. I give you three concrete assignments: first, every day keep a little time that belongs only to the two of you, and speak what is in your hearts, even if only for ten minutes; second, for the three big matters of money, children, and both sets of parents, set the rules first and discuss first, and do not let small things snowball into big knots; third, pray together. A couple who pray hand in hand cannot quarrel far. If you put God in the middle of your marriage, this cord is threefold, and not quickly broken.

To both sets of parents. Thank you. Today you sit in the front row, with tears in your eyes, reluctance in your hearts, and also comfort. Please receive this sentence: your assignment changes from discipline to blessing. From today, pray more and worry less; affirm more and compare less; be there when they need you, and let go with a smile when they can walk on their own. The road you have walked in your marriage, the hardships you have eaten, the grace you have received — these are the richest inheritance you leave to this new home; when there is opportunity, tell them slowly.

To all the guests. Today you are not spectators; you are witnesses. Witnesses have a responsibility: in the days ahead, pray for this couple, lend a hand when they are weak, speak words that build up, and do not speak words that tear apart. If one day you hear one of them say they want to give up, please remind them of today — remind them that in this hall, before everyone and before God, they said, "I do." And married guests, please, when you go home today, hold the hand of the one beside you; unmarried guests, do not fear marriage — what God instituted is, in the end, good.

Finally, I leave one practice for all the married guests, and also for the couple as homework after the wedding: after you go home, find a quiet evening, and the two of you read today's text again, Genesis chapter 2, read slowly; after reading, each tell the other one thing you thank them for, and then one thing you yourself need to change; you must not accuse each other, you may only admit your own share. After speaking, hold hands and make a short prayer, placing tomorrow, work, both sets of parents, and the children who have not yet appeared, all into God's hands. If there is such an evening once a month, your cleaving will be renewed again and again in God's word. Marriage does not live a lifetime on the emotion of the wedding day; it lives on these ordinary evenings, returning again and again to the purpose of the beginning, leaving the old self again, cleaving to each other again, becoming one again. As the couple go out from here today, may you bring this practice into your new home, let it become your family's tradition, and not lose it in ten, twenty years. When you look back then, you will find that the most romantic thing was not today's flowers and applause, but those ordinary nights of praying hand in hand, night after night, truly joining two people into one.

## (24:00 - 25:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Everyone, in the beginning God said it is not good to be alone, and made for man a helper fit for him; man leaves his father and mother, cleaves to his wife, and the two become one flesh. Today, this word written in Genesis is to become fact in a couple. May they leave with gratitude, cleave with a covenant, and become one with grace.

Will the couple please stand, and let us pray for them together: Heavenly Father, You are the God who instituted marriage, and the God who keeps covenant and shows steadfast love. We place this couple in Your hands. Guard their hearts, that all their lives they open only to each other; guard their home, that it take Your word as its foundation; guard their days, that there be laughter, and also the witness of supporting each other in difficulty. If You give them children, may they grow up in love; if there are storms in their days, may they remember today, and remember You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who is Himself the Lamb of God, and who is also the Bridegroom of the church, Amen.

Next, will the couple please face each other and exchange your vows.

## Endnotes

[1] 'ezer kenegdô: Hebrew עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ, 'ezer (עֵזֶר, H5828, help, helper) plus kenegdô (כְּנֶגְדּוֹ, from neged, H5048, in front of, opposite, corresponding to), literally rendered "a helper opposite him" or "a helper fit for him"; Gen 2:18, 20; the meanings follow BDB and HALOT. 'Ezer is used many times in the Old Testament to describe God as the help of His people (cf. Exod 18:4; Ps 33:20), so a helper carries the sense of upholding with strength, not of inferiority; Part One of this manuscript develops this point.

[2] 'azav: Hebrew עָזַב (H5800), to leave, forsake, lay down dependence; Gen 2:24, "a man shall leave his father and mother"; the meaning follows BDB and HALOT. This manuscript stresses that leaving is a rearranging of order, not an abolition of filial duty, and is to be read together with Exod 20:12, "Honour thy father and thy mother"; this is noted here expressly.

[3] davaq: Hebrew דָּבַק (H1692), to cling closely, adhere, follow tightly; Gen 2:24, "and shall cleave unto his wife"; the same word is used in Deut 10:20 and 13:4 for a person cleaving tightly to the LORD; the meaning follows BDB and HALOT. The distinction between covenant and contract in this manuscript is a homiletic development from the word "cleave."

[4] basar echad: Hebrew בָּשָׂר אֶחָד, basar (בָּשָׂר, H1320, flesh, body) plus echad (אֶחָד, H259, one), that is, one flesh, one piece of flesh; Gen 2:24, "they shall be one flesh"; the meanings follow BDB and HALOT. Eph 5:31-32 quotes this verse and points it to the mystery of Christ and the church; Part Three of this manuscript develops this point.

## Devotional Excerpt

The surest foundation of a marriage is not how well suited two people seem, but that they stand together in the word of God. Leaving means putting the order right, giving your spouse the first place, and letting your parents rest in blessing; cleaving means holding the heart close, not letting go in small things, and refusing wounding words in conflict; becoming one flesh means counting joys and burdens as ours, not yours and mine, and facing money, time, and decisions openly together. Whether you are married, single, or praying today for a child's marriage, begin with one small thing: pray earnestly once for the person beside you — your spouse, your parents, or the partner God may one day give — and give thanks for the relationship itself. Ask God to teach you to see the other as a helper, to uphold before you criticize, to keep the bond with a covenant heart that does not quickly give up, and to speak words that build up rather than words that chill a heart. God said it is not good to be alone, and He has promised to be with us. Whom will you reach toward today, what life-giving word will you speak first, and where will you be the first to humble yourself and admit fault? Entrust it to prayer, and God Himself will fulfill the good purpose He had from the beginning, shaping two people, day by day, into one.

## Summary (approx. 100 words)

The LORD appeared to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre. Three guests arrived at the tent, and Abraham prepared a lavish feast. During the meal, the promise was reiterated: at this time next year, Sarah shall have a son. Sarah laughed secretly behind the tent, and God brought it to light: Is anything too hard for the LORD? As the guests arose to depart toward Sodom, God revealed His intent of judgment, and Abraham drew near to intercede, pleading all the way from fifty righteous people down to ten. This sermon examines three realities: God drawing near to human dinner tables; cynical laughter transformed into joy; and those who walk closely with God learning to stand before Him on behalf of others.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Introduction · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

At the close of our previous sermon, Abraham was ninety-nine years old. On that very day, he circumcised his entire household, and his name was changed. Changing names, entering into a covenant, receiving the sign—these were all momentous events. According to our way of thinking, after such grand matters, God ought to be worshipped from afar in the heavens. Yet as chapter 18 opens, God does something that exceeds our expectations: He comes to the oaks of Mamre, standing at the tent door in the heat of the day. Abraham lifted up his eyes and saw three men standing opposite him; he ran to meet them, bowed himself to the ground, and his first words were an invitation to stay for a meal.

Brothers and sisters, this is one of the most vital marks of our faith: God is not a God who merely issues decrees from on high; He is pleased to step down, draw near to mankind, and sit at human tables. This Almighty God declared His great name in the previous chapter, yet in this chapter He takes up a dining bowl.

However, this chapter is about more than just a meal. Halfway through the meal, the conversation turns to Sarah’s secret laughter; when the guests arise to depart, the conversation turns again to the judgment of Sodom. Abraham walked along to see them off, and as he walked, he came to stand before God, opening his mouth to intercede for a city with which he had no kinship ties—apart from his nephew Lot. A meal, an intercession, and between them stands the very same God: He is pleased to draw near to man, and He is pleased to listen to man.

Let us first read the pivotal passages of Scripture:

"Then the LORD appeared to him by the terebinth trees of Mamre, as he was sitting in the tent door in the heat of the day. So he lifted his eyes and looked, and behold, three men were standing by him; and when he saw them, he ran from the tent door to meet them, and bowed himself to the ground." (Gen. 18:1-2, excerpt) [1]

"And He said, 'I will certainly return to you according to the time of life, and behold, Sarah your wife shall have a son.' (Sarah was listening in the tent door which was behind him.)" (Gen. 18:10, excerpt)

"And the LORD said, 'Shall I hide from Abraham what I am doing?'" (Gen. 18:17, excerpt)

"And Abraham came near and said, '...Far be it from You to do such a thing as this, to slay the righteous with the wicked...'" (Gen. 18:23, 25, excerpt)

Let us pray: Lord, we thank You. You are the Almighty God, yet You are pleased to walk to our tent doors and sit at our tables. We pray that through this chapter today, You would first nourish our hearts, and then teach us, like Abraham, to draw near to You and intercede for others. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The God at the Table—He Sits at the Tent Door to Eat (18:1-8)

First, consider the manner of the manifestation. Scripture states, "the LORD appeared to him by the terebinth trees of Mamre," and immediately following, what Abraham saw was "three men." Commentators have discussed this extensively: one was the personal manifestation of the LORD Himself, while the other two were angels—this corresponds with the beginning of chapter 19: "Now the two angels came to Sodom in the evening." Two proceeded toward Sodom, while one remained to converse with Abraham, and the One who remained is referred to throughout Scripture as the LORD. In other words, this was not God sending messengers to deliver a word; this was God coming in person.

The timing of His coming is also noteworthy: "in the heat of the day." Under the midday sun, it was a time for rest, not a time for work. God chose to come at man's most relaxed and domestic moment. He did not wait for Abraham to ascend an altar; He did not wait for him to set up a formal religious display. Brothers and sisters, God often steps into our lives not during our well-prepared religious moments, but at noon, at the dining table, in the ordinary routine of our days. The question has never been whether God comes; the question is, when He comes, do we recognize Him, and are we willing to run and welcome Him?

Abraham's response is the highlight of this passage: he "ran" to meet them—a ninety-nine-year-old man, having only recently been circumcised, running to welcome guests; he bowed himself to the ground and addressed the visitor as "my Lord"; he pleaded with them to stay, speaking with profound humility: "do not pass by Your servant," let a little water be brought, wash your feet, and let me fetch a morsel of bread. Yet what was actually served at the table? Cakes made from three seahs of fine flour—that is several kilograms of flour [7]; a tender and good calf—freshly slaughtered and prepared whole; along with butter and milk. He promised "a morsel of bread," but laid out a lavish banquet.

This is the pattern of receiving God: humble in speech, generous in hand. The standard of our hospitality often reveals our inward estimation of the guest. Abraham did not yet fully know who had arrived, but he employed the standard reserved for the Most High. The Epistle to the Hebrews later looked back on this passage, saying: "Do not forget to entertain strangers, for by so doing some have unwittingly entertained angels." (Heb. 13:2). What Abraham entertained was far more than angels.

There is another detail: Abraham stood by them under the tree and waited on them while they ate, not sitting down at the feast himself. He stood by their side. Brothers and sisters, intimacy with God does not mean informality before God. This man, whom God had just renamed "father of many nations," assumed the posture of a servant beside God's dining table. The closer one is to God, the more clearly one knows where to stand.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Laughter Behind the Tent—Is Anything Too Hard for the LORD? (18:9-15)

Midway through the meal, the guest asked: "Where is Sarah your wife?" Is this not an unusual question? This was a guest arriving for the first time, yet how could he call the mistress of the house by name? Abraham answered: "Here, in the tent." The guest then uttered the statement that brought the entire narrative to its climax: "I will certainly return to you according to the time of life, and behold, Sarah your wife shall have a son." The One speaking was none other than the LORD manifesting Himself in person (see note [1]).

Sarah was listening behind the tent. Scripture reveals her condition: Abraham and Sarah were old, well advanced in age, and Sarah had passed the age of childbearing. In other words, from the perspective of human biology, the door to this possibility was firmly shut. Therefore, Sarah laughed within herself, saying: "After I have grown old, shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"

Please note that Sarah's laughter was inward; it was in her heart, making no sound. Yet God brought it to light: "And the LORD said to Abraham, 'Why did Sarah laugh, saying, "Shall I surely bear a child, since I am old?"'" Then comes the weightiest question in this chapter, indeed in the entire Book of Genesis: "Is anything too hard for the LORD?"

The word translated "too hard" has as its root the Hebrew word *pele* (H6381), which means wonderful, beyond human strength, unsearchable by man—this is the very word used in Psalms when it speaks of Him doing "wonderful things."[2] God's question was not so much a tone of rebuke as one of assurance: He meant, "Is there such a category as 'too hard' for Me? You are calculating the ledger of the body, but I am doing the work of creation." Then He pinned down the exact time: "At the appointed time I will return to you, according to the time of life, and Sarah shall have a son."

Sarah was afraid and denied it, saying, "I did not laugh." God's reply had the meaning of just a few words: "No, but you did laugh!" The story comes to a gentle pause here—no punishment, no lengthy denunciation. God both exposed it and did not hold it against her; He recorded her laughter and fulfilled His word all the same. Later, when the child was born, he was named Isaac—which means "he laughs" (*tzachaq*, H6711).[3] God did not erase Sarah's inward laugh; rather, He wrote it into the child's name, turning it into the public joy and laughter of the entire household. Brothers and sisters, those difficulties and doubts that you keep in the dark, too ashamed to let anyone see—God has heard them all. When He exposes them, it is not to humiliate you, but to transform your secret, inward laugh into the laughter of joy held in your arms at this time next year.

Do not miss the way God asked the question either: He asked Abraham, yet the words were spoken for Sarah, who was behind the tent, to hear. God often works this way: through one particular setting, He speaks to another person. What you are sitting here listening to today may very well be the exact word God wants to say to you.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: Intercession by the Roadside—From Fifty Down to Ten (18:16-33)

The men rose up and looked toward Sodom, and Abraham went with them to send them on the way—and halfway through sending his guests off, the story took a turn. God had a thought in His heart, which Scripture deliberately records: "Shall I hide from Abraham what I am doing?" God was about to judge Sodom, and He would not hide this matter from His friend. The words immediately following are crucial: "since Abraham shall surely become a great and mighty nation, and all the nations of the earth shall be blessed in him? For I have known him, in order that he may command his children and his household after him, that they keep the way of the Lord, to do righteousness and justice" (18:18-19, excerpted).[6] God revealed His heart to Abraham not because Abraham was curious, but because he was to teach his descendants in the future to "do righteousness and justice"—in the original text, *mishpat* (H4941, justice, judgment) and *tzedaqah* (H6666, righteousness, uprightness) are used together.[4] God first let His friend witness a lesson firsthand: how a righteous God deals with an evil city. The first scene of this lesson was spoken by God Himself: "Because the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great, and because their sin is very grave"—that "outcry," in the original text *za'aqah* (H2201), is the wailing of the oppressed, the cry of an appeal for justice.[5] God judges not out of temper, but because the outcry has reached Him. Then God said: "I will go down now and see whether they have done altogether according to the outcry against it that has come to Me." The omniscient God said He would "go down and see"—this was a posture demonstrated for man's sake: before God executes judgment, He first investigates the actual facts. If even God does not condemn based on rumor, how much less should we.

After Abraham heard this, he did the most famous deed of his life: he came near—Scripture says he "still stood before the Lord," and then he opened his mouth. His prayer began with a theological question: "Far be it from You to do such a thing as this, to slay the righteous with the wicked, so that the righteous should be as the wicked; far be it from You!" Notice that his argument was not his own feelings, but God's attributes: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" He took God's righteousness as the basis for praying to God. Then the numbers went down all the way: fifty, forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty, ten.[8] In the midst of this, he repeatedly humbled himself: "Indeed now, which am but dust and ashes, have taken it upon myself to speak to the Lord."

How should we view this bargaining? This was not haggling in a marketplace; this was an intercessor probing the depths of God's mercy step by step. With every drop of a tier, God said: I will do it. God was never impatient; He never said "enough." Finally it stopped at ten—not because God was unwilling to lower it further, but because Abraham stopped asking. Perhaps he thought: if there are not even ten righteous people, he had nothing left to say about the condition of this city. When the prayer was ended, "the Lord went His way," and Abraham returned to his place.

This passage gives us two foundations for prayer: First, interceding for others does not depend on how tight our personal friendship with God is, but on the fact that God's righteousness and mercy are inherently who He is—we speak to Him according to His very nature. Second, for those who draw near to God, the scope of their prayers will expand. Abraham could completely have asked for just one thing at this point: save my nephew Lot. He did not. He pleaded for the entire city, including those he did not know, even those who deserved judgment. The heart of God's friend will become more and more like God's heart.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Types of People

The first type: those in the exhaustion of noonday. You feel that God only comes when your condition is good—yet He knocks on the tent door when you are at your most lax, when you least want to see anyone. Do not hide today; run out to meet Him.

The second type: those laughing secretly behind the tent. You have heard God's promises many times, and that inner murmur of "impossible" has become so quiet even you can barely hear it. God has heard it. Today He wants to say to you: Is anything too hard for the Lord?

The third type: Sarah, who once denied it. "I did not laugh"—we often do this before God as well, denying it first. God's reply is gentle yet firm: No, but you did laugh. Admitting your lack of faith brings you closer to blessing than pretending to be strong.

The fourth type: those who have a Lot in their family. Your loved one lives in a dangerous city; you have pleaded with them, you have been disappointed. This chapter tells you: you can still stand before God. You cannot make decisions for them, but you can intercede for them, and even for that city.

The fifth type: those who pray only for themselves. Try expanding your prayer list outward by one circle: coworkers, neighbors, this city. The mark of drawing near to God is that the radius of your prayers grows larger.

The sixth type: those who feel that judgment has nothing to do with them. God told Abraham what He was about to do so that he would teach his descendants to do righteousness and justice. Those who hear God's heart must live out righteousness before their children; this is far more important than merely explaining it to them.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Responsive Prayer

Today we have seen a God who is willing to sit beside a dining table, a God who turns secret laughter into shouts of joy, a God who is willing to listen to man's inquiry down to the tenth person. He came in person to eat, so that we might know He delights to draw near; He paused for Sodom to listen to prayer, so that we might learn to stand before Him on behalf of others.

Let us pray together: Almighty God, we thank You. You do not despise our humble tents; You come in person to dine with us. Lord, You have heard all of our Sarah-like secret laughter; please fulfill Your promise according to Your word, turning our impossibilities into laughter held in our arms at this time next year. We also ask that You give us the heart of Abraham: to stand before You in intercession for our families, for this city, and for those we do not know, until You declare that it is accomplished. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Chapter Notes

[1] The manifestation and the three guests: Gen. 18:1 states that the Lord appeared, and in 18:2 Abraham saw "three men"; the one in chapter 18 who converses with Abraham and is addressed as "the Lord" is the one who remained, while the other two are seen in Gen. 19:1 as "the two angels." This manuscript adopts the exegetical position of "the Lord appearing in person + two angels," a reading shared by many commentaries (such as the Genesis commentaries by Wenham and Hamilton).

[2] *pele*: Hebrew פָּלָא (*pālā'*, H6381), wonderful, unsearchable, beyond human capability; Gen. 18:14, "Is anything too hard for the Lord?"; definitions based on BDB, HALOT. This word is frequently used in Psalms for God's wonderful deeds (e.g., Ps. 139:6, translated "wonderful" in the Chinese Union Version).

[3] *tzachaq*: Hebrew צָחַק (*tsāchaq*, H6711), to laugh, to rejoice; Gen. 18:12, Sarah "laughed within herself"; the same word as Abraham's joyful laughter in Gen. 17:17 and Sarah's joyful laughter in Gen. 21:6; definitions based on BDB, HALOT; the name Isaac (יִצְחָק, H3327) shares this same root.

[4] *mishpat*: Hebrew מִשְׁפָּט (H4941), justice, judgment, ordinance; *tzedaqah*: Hebrew צְדָקָה (H6666), righteousness, uprightness; in Gen. 18:19, "to do righteousness and justice," the original text pairs these two words; in 18:25, "Judge" in "the Judge of all the earth" also shares the same root with *mishpat* (the participle form of H8199). Definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

[5] *za'aqah*: Hebrew זְעָקָה (H2201), an outcry, crying out, sound of complaint; Gen. 18:20, "has reached Me"; definitions based on BDB, HALOT. In Exod. 3:7–9, the outcry of the Israelites in Egypt also uses a related word (a cognate word family of H6818); this is not expanded in the sermon body and is noted only here.

[6] *yada*: Hebrew יָדַע (H3045), to know, acknowledge, show elect care; Gen. 18:19, "I have known him" (the Chinese Union Version translates this as "cared for him," while the KJV reads "I know him"); definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

[7] Three seahs of fine flour: The amount in Gen. 18:6 equals several kilograms of flour in modern measurements, which, together with an entire calf (18:7), far exceeds the modest phrase "a morsel of bread" (18:5); this manuscript uses this to preach "humble in speech, generous in hand." The measurement conversion is an approximation, following standard commentary conventions.

[8] Regarding the numbers in Abraham's intercession in Gen. 18: The decrease from fifty to ten is found in 18:24–32; the interpretation in this manuscript—that "stopping at ten was not God being unwilling to lower it further, but Abraham ceasing to ask"—is a homiletical reading based on the text's narrative flow (in 18:32 Abraham says, "Let not the Lord be angry," and the narrative shifts immediately in 18:33), not a lexical conclusion, noted specifically here.

Title verification: The Chinese title is taken from the base text of 35:6 — among the cities given to the Levites there are to be six cities of refuge, in the Chinese wording "that the manslayer may flee there." The English title follows the KJV's own wording of the same institution: at 35:6 the KJV reads "which ye shall appoint for the manslayer, that he may flee thither"; at 35:11 the exact clause stands as "that the slayer may flee thither, which killeth any person at unawares"; and at 35:15 the chapter closes its appointment with "may flee thither" once more. The title therefore takes the 35:11 form, "That the Slayer May Flee Thither," the KJV's own words for the one who kills at unawares; the 35:6 form ("for the manslayer, that he may flee thither") is registered alongside it, and the two are one appointment in two wordings, not two institutions. "City of refuge" is this chapter's proper name for the institution (the KJV has the refuge-city wording at 35:6, 11, 13, 14, 15, 25, 26, 27, 28, 32, ten verses), and this sermon is built on the cities of refuge as its frame: the cities of the Levites (35:1-8), the appointment of the cities of refuge (35:9-15), the distinction between the murderer and the slayer (35:16-24), residence in the city and the death of the high priest (35:25-29), and witnesses, satisfaction, and the cleansing of the land (35:30-34).

Form: The main text is divided into five parts, each headed with a time code (a 60-minute sermon structure); within each part the material is sorted by five layer marks — (Fact) / (Argument) / (Summary) / (New Testament Reading) / (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) — with material from the chapters before and after marked (Cross-Reference). ## Main Text

Introduction (00:00-04:00). (Fact) By Numbers 35 the allotment of the land has already been commanded: the four borders of Canaan are drawn (34:1-12) and the leaders of the twelve tribes are named (34:16-29). But before the people cross the river and enter the land, two matters concerning "cities" must still be settled: the cities of the Levites, and the cities of refuge for the slayer. The setting of verse 1 is registered from the base text: the LORD speaks to Moses in the plains of Moab, by Jordan near Jericho (registered paraphrase, not checked as a full quotation). The order is not accidental. (Argument) Before a piece of land can become a holy land, the first question it must answer is not the question of borders but the question of blood: innocent blood shed on the ground pollutes the land (35:33); and if the one who killed at unawares is himself killed by private vengeance, that is one more innocent life. So before the people enter the land, God sets the "cities" in place first — cities of lodging for the tribe that has no inheritance of land, and cities of escape for the man who killed without intent. (Summary) The governing sentence of the chapter can be put this way: in the promised land God first sets up the coordinates of mercy, and then draws the boundaries of righteousness; the city of refuge is mercy, judgment is righteousness, and both rest on one sentence — "for I the LORD dwell among the children of Israel" (35:34). The five parts that follow take the chapter section by section. One further piece of background must be made plain first. (Fact) This chapter contains no narrative at all: the whole chapter is law — from the speaking of the first verse to the seal of the last, nothing "happens"; everything is a command of how things "shall be." And the commands are given before the entry into the land: the land is not yet possessed, and the cities are already set up on paper. Law walking ahead of history is the standing pattern of legislation in the Pentateuch, and it is especially marked in this chapter: even the cubits of the suburbs and the distribution of the cities of refuge on the two banks are measured out while Israel is still east of Jordan. (Argument) This means that among the first pieces of baggage the people carry into Canaan are the blueprints of mercy and righteousness — the land may be taken and divided, but how the cities are to be used and how a life is to be reckoned was written down before the entry. To gain the land is not the hard part; the hard part is living on the land in an order worthy of the One who dwells there. What this chapter establishes is the hardest link in that order: the account of human life, and how it alone can be settled.

### Part One: Cities for the Levites — Forty-Eight Cities, the Cities with Their Suburbs (35:1-8) (04:00-15:00)

(Fact) The first command of this chapter is not about the cities of refuge but about the Levites. Verse 2, from the base text: "Command the children of Israel, that they give unto the Levites of the inheritance of their possession cities to dwell in; and ye shall give also unto the Levites suburbs for the cities round about them." (35:2) The tribe of Levi is arranged uniquely in this book: they are numbered for the service of the priests and the tabernacle (Numbers 3-4), and at the division of the land they do not receive one whole territorial inheritance tribe by tribe — this chapter does not restate the reason; it gives only the disposition: each tribe is to set apart cities from the inheritance it has received, for the Levites to dwell in. How many? Verses 6-7, from the base text: "And among the cities which ye shall give unto the Levites there shall be six cities for refuge, which ye shall appoint for the manslayer, that he may flee thither: and to them ye shall add forty and two cities. So all the cities which ye shall give to the Levites shall be forty and eight cities: them shall ye give with their suburbs." (35:6-7) The arithmetic recounted by hand: six plus forty-two equals forty-eight, agreeing exactly with the total of verse 7 (see Endnote 2). Beyond the cities there are also the suburbs. Verses 4-5, from the base text: "And the suburbs of the cities, which ye shall give unto the Levites, shall reach from the wall of the city and outward a thousand cubits round about. And ye shall measure from without the city on the east side two thousand cubits, and on the south side two thousand cubits, and on the west side two thousand cubits, and on the north side two thousand cubits; and the city shall be in the midst: this shall be to them the suburbs of the cities." (35:4-5) There are two sets of numbers here: one thousand cubits (v. 4) and two thousand cubits (v. 5, two thousand on each of the four sides). The principle of the giving stands in verse 8: "And the cities which ye shall give shall be of the possession of the children of Israel: from them that have many ye shall give many; but from them that have few ye shall give few: every one shall give of his cities unto the Levites according to his inheritance which he inheriteth." (35:8) (Argument) This principle is worth stopping over: the cities are not levied evenly, tribe by tribe, but in proportion to the inheritance received — those with much land give many, those with little give few. Righteousness here is not arithmetical equality but proportionate fairness. The Levites have no inheritance of their own; their livelihood hangs on the supply of their brother tribes, and God writes that supply into law rather than leaving it to men's good moods. (Fact) Verse 3 registers the use of the suburbs: the cities are for dwelling, and the suburbs for their cattle, for their goods, and for all their beasts (registered paraphrase: "And the cities shall they have to dwell in; and the suburbs of them shall be for their cattle, and for their goods, and for all their beasts," 35:3, wording from the anchor, carried as registration in the same register as the Chinese manuscript). (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The tension between the thousand cubits and the two thousand cubits is set out in parallel, without adjudication. One reading takes the thousand cubits as the suburbs proper and the two thousand as the outer boundary including the fields beyond the city (the rabbinic tradition mostly reads it this way, dividing the two numbers into two belts of land). A second reading takes the two verses as two methods of measurement — one a radius measured from the city, the other the total of the boundary on the four sides. Other commentators hold that the two numbers come from different layers of tradition, preserved side by side in the editing. This sermon sets the readings side by side and adjudicates none of them; the fact layer can settle only this: both numbers stand in the base text, and the text itself makes no reconciliation. (New Testament Reading) The Levites are scattered through all Israel — Joshua 21 afterwards registers the fulfilment of these forty-eight cities one by one (cross-reference layer). Scattering means that the teachers of the law are placed in the midst of the people rather than gathered in one place as a state within a state. In the New Testament's echo of this pattern the reading layer registers one item only: believers are called "a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9, registered paraphrase of the key point) — a priestly existence is likewise scattered among the nations. (Argument) Look one layer deeper: why do the Levites exchange land for cities? The register of the surrounding text is that the Levites' "inheritance" is spoken of in another way — Numbers 18 registers the LORD's word to Aaron, that the Levites have no inheritance among the children of Israel, and the tenth of all is their inheritance (registered paraphrase). The forty-eight cities of this chapter are therefore not compensation but provision: a tribe whose inheritance is service needs not field boundaries but dwellings and pasture; not one whole fief but forty-eight points of settlement scattered among the tribes. When Moses blesses the tribe of Levi in Deuteronomy 33, their office is registered as teaching Jacob the judgments and Israel the law (registered paraphrase) — the arrangement of scattered cities fits that teaching office exactly: when the teacher lives in the midst of the people, the law is not a scroll kept in the tabernacle but a daily voice in every city. (Fact) The later fulfilment of these forty-eight cities is registered in Joshua 21 city by city, tribe by tribe (cross-reference layer): Judah and Simeon receive nine cities, Benjamin four, and Ephraim, Dan, the half-tribe of Manasseh, Gad and the rest each receive their share, the six cities of refuge included, the total agreeing with this chapter's forty-eight (the chapter's own closing register gives the total as forty-eight, registered paraphrase). (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The actual historical form of the Levitical cities is discussed in scholarship: some researchers hold that the complete fulfilment of the forty-eight cities was not necessarily realized in a single period, and that the list may carry an idealized editorial colouring; others discuss the transmission of the list on the basis of the agreements and differences between Joshua 21 and 1 Chronicles 6. Both views are registered side by side; what this sermon can settle is only the text's own account: Numbers commands forty-eight, Joshua registers forty-eight, and the number is self-consistent within the text. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: the tribe with no inheritance of land received the best position in all the land — dwelling in the midst of everyone. God settles the men who serve first, and then the men who must flee, because the six cities of the next part are set apart from these very forty-eight cities.


### Part Two: Six Cities of Refuge — Three on This Side Jordan, Three in Canaan (35:9-15) (15:00-26:00)

(Fact) Verses 9-10 are the opening of the second command: the LORD speaks to Moses, and when the people have come over Jordan into the land of Canaan, they are to appoint cities of refuge (registered paraphrase). Verse 11, from the base text: "Then ye shall appoint you cities to be cities of refuge for you; that the slayer may flee thither, which killeth any person at unawares." (35:11) Note that the base text reads "cities of refuge," the institution's own name. Whom do the cities of refuge receive? The base text's definition is a single term: the one who kills at unawares. Verse 12 states the cities' function: "And they shall be unto you cities for refuge from the avenger; that the manslayer die not, until he stand before the congregation in judgment." (35:12) A city of refuge is not a pardon; it is a shelter for the time being. It is not the end of the matter; it is a waiting for judgment. The man who flees there has his life preserved first — and his case still has to be tried. Verses 13-14, from the base text: "And of these cities which ye shall give six cities shall ye have for refuge. Ye shall give three cities on this side Jordan, and three cities shall ye give in the land of Canaan, which shall be cities of refuge." (35:13-14) The distribution counted by hand: three east of Jordan, three in Canaan, six in all, agreeing with verses 6 and 13. Verse 15 gives the full range of those received: "These six cities shall be a refuge, both for the children of Israel, and for the stranger, and for the sojourner among them: that every one that killeth any person unawares may flee thither." (35:15) (Argument) Attend to this range: the children of Israel, the stranger, and the sojourner are listed together. The door of the city of refuge does not open by identity; it opens by fact — the question is whether you killed at unawares, not who you are. The equality the law draws here lies not in favoured treatment but in sameness of procedure: the same refuge, the same judgment, the same standard. (Fact) This chapter commands only the number six and the distribution on the two banks; it does not record the names of the six cities (see Endnote 5). The names of the three cities east of Jordan stand at Deuteronomy 4:43 — Bezer, Ramoth in Gilead, and Golan in Bashan; the names of the three cities in Canaan stand at Joshua 20:7 — Kedesh, Shechem, and Hebron (both places are cross-reference registrations; this chapter does not carry the names, and they are not read back into this chapter as its facts). That Moses appointed three cities first on the east of Jordan is registered separately in Deuteronomy 4; Joshua 20 registers the formal appointment of the cities of refuge after the crossing. (Cross-Reference) The cities-of-refuge legislation is not an isolated instance in the Pentateuch: Exodus 21:13 first lays down the principle that the one who kills not by lying in wait but at unawares may flee to a place God appoints (registered paraphrase); Deuteronomy 19 adds the commands to prepare the road and, in later days, to add three more cities (registered paraphrase). Numbers 35 is the most complete in structure of the three passages: it states appointment (35:9-15), distinction (35:16-24), residence (35:25-29), and the general rules (35:30-34) all at once. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) "Revenging blood" was a widespread family duty in the ancient Near East: the blood-relative bore the responsibility of exacting blood for the dead. The historical discussion of the cities of refuge registers two points. First, scholarship widely observes that the cities of refuge do not abolish the revenging of blood; they confine it within procedure — the revenger of blood may not enter the city to kill, but must wait for the congregation's judgment (35:12, 24). Second, the cities of refuge have a formal kinship with the ancient custom of sanctuary at a holy place, but this chapter's cities are not set beside an altar; they are set in the cities of the Levites, and their outcome is the congregation's judgment, not the priest's shelter. This answers to the register of Exodus 21:14, that the man who kills presumptuously is to be taken even from the altar to die (registered paraphrase): refuge never means escape from guilt. (New Testament Reading) Hebrews 6:18 uses an image of flight to refuge: we "have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us" (Heb 6:18, the base text's wording, registered). The author borrows the picture of the Old Testament city of refuge to describe believers laying hold on the hope set before them. The reading layer registers only this one borrowing: what the New Testament author prizes is the direction of the "fleeing" — not flight away from God, but flight toward the place God has appointed. (Cross-Reference) The picture of a city of refuge actually at work is not spelled out in this chapter; Joshua 20 supplies one corner of the procedure: the man who flees to the city is first to stand at the gate of the city and declare his cause to the elders of the city, and the elders receive him into the city and give him a place to dwell among them (registered paraphrase). This register matters: a city of refuge is not unguarded open ground; at its gate there is a first hearing — the elders hear his declaration, receive him to dwell first, and the formal judgment returns afterwards to the congregation of his own place (35:12, 24). Shelter first, judgment afterwards — the order cannot be reversed: judge first and shelter afterwards, and the man is already dead at the revenger's hand; shelter without judgment, and the city of refuge becomes a criminal's hiding hole. The two orders together make the complete institution. (Argument) One more item deserves notice: the cities of refuge are set in the cities of the Levites. Why should the shelter stand precisely in the cities of the men who serve? This chapter gives no express reason; at the argument layer it may be felt this way: the Levites are the company in Israel devoted to the law and to worship; to settle the one who killed at unawares in their midst is to place the man who most needs to be recovered by the law in the hands of the men who know the law best. And since the Levitical cities are spread through every tribe (Part One), the six cities of refuge are spread with them across the two banks — the network of shelter and the network of teaching are one and the same net. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) On the geographical distribution of the six cities, the commentary tradition often speaks of "even spacing, within reach of flight": the six cities are distributed north and south on the two banks of Jordan, so that a man anywhere in the territory can reach one within a short journey (registered as a traditional reading; the measured distances between the cities are variously stated, and this sermon cites no figures). A further layer of historical discussion: the actual operation of the cities-of-refuge system in the later period of Israel's settlement is very lightly touched in the biblical narrative — apart from the appointment registered in Joshua, the books of Kings and the Prophets rarely record a cities-of-refuge case, and scholarship estimates the system's actual duration in different ways; all of this belongs to the register layer, and this sermon makes no assertion. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: the door of the city of refuge stands open to the one who killed at unawares, and what has never defended the murderer is not a city wall but a judgment; the six cities are distributed on the two banks so that the fleeing man, wherever he is, has a city he can reach.

### Part Three: The Distinction Between the Murderer and the Slayer — Instruments, Hatred, and Suddenness (35:16-24) (26:00-38:00)

(Fact) The cities of refuge receive only the one who killed at unawares; so there must be a judgeable standard for who is a murderer and who is a slayer. Verses 16-21 list the group of murder cases. Verse 16, quoted in full from the base text: "And if he smite him with an instrument of iron, so that he die, he is a murderer: the murderer shall surely be put to death." (35:16) Verse 17 is registered: a man smites him "with throwing a stone, wherewith he may die," and he dies (registered paraphrase, not a full quotation). Verse 18 is registered: a man smites him with "an hand weapon of wood, wherewith he may die," and he dies (registered paraphrase). Verse 19, from the base text: "The revenger of blood himself shall slay the murderer: when he meeteth him, he shall slay him." (35:19) Verse 20 is registered as two circumstances: thrusting a man "of hatred," and hurling at him "by laying of wait," so that he dies (registered paraphrase). Verse 21, quoted in full from the base text: "Or in enmity smite him with his hand, that he die: he that smote him shall surely be put to death; for he is a murderer: the revenger of blood shall slay the murderer, when he meeteth him." (35:21) The case group counted by hand: three classes of deadly instrument (an instrument of iron, a stone wherewith he may die, a hand weapon of wood), two circumstances of hatred (thrusting of hatred, hurling by laying of wait), and one further case of smiting with the hand in enmity — six case-sections in all (35:16-21, one case to a verse, with verse 20 containing two circumstances). (Argument) The logic by which this group of cases is judged must be read along two lines. The first line is the instrument: iron, a stone that can kill, wood — to do the deed holding in one's hand a thing sufficient to kill is, for the law, to presume intent, because a man knows in his own heart how dangerous the thing in his hand is. The second line is hatred: thrusting, hurling, the bare hand need not in themselves be deadly, but if they proceed from hatred and laying of wait, they are judged murder all the same. The determination of murder looks not only at the outcome but at the heart with which the instrument was taken up, and at the hatred of ordinary days. Here the law does something extremely difficult: it judges the inner man, yet judges him only by outward evidence — instrument and hatred are both verifiable facts, not guessed confessions. (Fact) Verses 22-24 turn to the group of manslaughter cases. Verse 22, quoted in full from the base text: "But if he thrust him suddenly without enmity, or have cast upon him any thing without laying of wait," (35:22) Verse 23, in full: "Or with any stone, wherewith a man may die, seeing him not, and cast it upon him, that he die, and was not his enemy, neither sought his harm:" (35:23) Read the two groups against each other: the same thrusting (v. 20 against v. 22), the same hurling (v. 20 against v. 22), the same stone (v. 17 against v. 23) — the actions are almost identical, the determinations wholly opposite. The base text gives the boundary line clearly: whether there was enmity, whether there was laying of wait, whether he saw him, whether he sought his harm. Verse 24, from the base text: "Then the congregation shall judge between the slayer and the revenger of blood according to these judgments:" (35:24) (Argument) The position in "judge between" is worth magnifying: the congregation stands in the middle, between the two sides — on one side the revenger of blood, who has lost a kinsman, and whose anger is real; on the other side the slayer, who caused the disaster without intent, and whose life hangs in the balance. The function of judgment is to separate private blood with public determination. Without this middle layer, blood-vengeance kills its way onward: kill the slayer, and a new feud is formed. The law does not deny the legitimacy of vengeance outright — in a murder case the revenger is still the executor (35:19, 21); what it denies is the limitlessness of vengeance: the feud is revenged only as far as the judgment, and no further. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) On the institution of the go'el (the revenger of blood, H1350, checked against the BHS text on this chapter's comparison page), the scholarly discussion registers two points side by side. First, the same word elsewhere also denotes the near kinsman, the redeemer — as in Leviticus 25, the kinsman who redeems land and redeems persons (cross-reference register). Revenging blood and redeeming property come from one word, and scholarship mostly points to the underlying conception as the maintenance of the family's wholeness: what the family has lost — land, persons, blood — the nearest kinsman steps forward to recover. Second, this chapter limits the go'el's action to two occasions: after the congregation has judged the case a murder (35:19, 21), and after the slayer has left the city of his own accord (35:27) — at all other times his hatred must halt outside the city. The two readings (the revenger of blood as an old custom progressively taken over by the law, or as an executive office within the law from the start) coexist in scholarship; this sermon sets them side by side only. (New Testament Reading) In the Sermon on the Mount the Lord Jesus pushes judgment deeper still: whoever is angry with his brother shall be in danger of the judgment (Matt 5:22, registered paraphrase of the key point). Numbers 35 presumes the inner state from outward evidence; the Sermon on the Mount speaks to the inner state directly — the reading layer registers this line of deepening and does not confuse the two passages' forms and audiences: one is the law of adjudication, the other a discourse on the kingdom of heaven. (Fact) Set the verdict-sentences of the two case groups side by side once more: on the murder side, every case closes with "shall surely be put to death" — "the murderer shall surely be put to death" at verse 16 and "he that smote him shall surely be put to death" at verse 21 can be checked in the base text, and the remaining verses follow the same sentence-form (registered). On the manslaughter side (vv. 22-23) there is no word of punishment at all, only the statement of the facts, running straight into the judgment of verse 24. With the two groups side by side, the law is training the people in a kind of eyesight: at a death, do not at once cry murder; ask three things first — what was in his hand? What was stored in his heart? In what circumstances did it happen? Instrument, hatred, and laying of wait or its absence are all checkable. (Argument) Set this method of judging before today's reader and it still has an edge. Later jurisprudence divides the inner states of killing into intent, negligence, and accident, clarifying layer by layer — this chapter's case group was doing the same work more than three thousand years ago, except that it writes no definitions, only cases: with six murder cases and three manslaughter cases the law draws an abstract boundary into a shape a man can step on. This is precisely the wisdom of case-law legislation: the people do not need to understand the jurists first; let them remember the stories of the iron and the stone, and the boundary stands in their hearts. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The word "laying of wait" (35:20) is this chapter's key circumstance-word for determining murder, standing in positive and negative contrast with "without laying of wait" at verse 22; its original word-form was not checked against the source page in this sermon and is entered as pending verification (see the supplementary note at Endnote 13). Further, the ancient Near Eastern law codes (such as the Code of Hammurabi) likewise contain provisions distinguishing the circumstances of a death, and scholarship often discusses them in comparison with this chapter: not a few of the Near Eastern provisions close the matter with compensation and fines, while this chapter's murder cases are uniformly "shall surely be put to death," with satisfaction expressly forbidden (35:31) — its distinctiveness shows up in the comparison. This belongs to the discussion register; the bearing of the comparison is gathered up at the satisfaction passage of Part Five. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: the same stone can be a murder weapon or an accident; the law does not convict by the action but by the hatred and the laying of wait — and it takes this right of judgment out of the hands of the dead man's family and places it on the congregation's judgment seat.


### Part Four: Abiding in the City of Refuge — Until the Death of the Anointed High Priest (35:25-29) (38:00-48:00)

(Fact) After the judgment, the slayer's disposition stands in verse 25: "And the congregation shall deliver the slayer out of the hand of the revenger of blood, and the congregation shall restore him to the city of his refuge, whither he was fled: and he shall abide in it unto the death of the high priest, which was anointed with the holy oil." (35:25) Attend to the two verbs: "deliver" and "restore." The congregation does not merely judge him not a murderer; it actively delivers him out of the revenger's hand and restores him to the city of refuge — because outside the city he is still in danger. (On "anointed with the holy oil": the KJV has "oil" at this place, where the Chinese base text has no word for oil; see the base-text notes at the head of this sermon.) Verses 26-27, quoted in full from the base text: "But if the slayer shall at any time come without the border of the city of his refuge, whither he was fled; And the revenger of blood find him without the borders of the city of his refuge, and the revenger of blood kill the slayer; he shall not be guilty of blood:" (35:26-27) Verse 28, in full: "Because he should have remained in the city of his refuge until the death of the high priest: but after the death of the high priest the slayer shall return into the land of his possession." (35:28) Verse 29, in full: "So these things shall be for a statute of judgment unto you throughout your generations in all your dwellings." (35:29) (Fact) The death of the high priest occurs three times in this chapter, verse by verse: verse 25, "unto the death of the high priest"; verse 28, "until the death of the high priest" and "after the death of the high priest"; verse 32, "until the death of the priest" (v. 32 belongs to Part Five and is registered here in advance). (Argument) Why is the day of release tied to one man's death? The text gives no reason; it gives only an institution. At the argument layer it may be felt this way: the slayer is no murderer, yet after all a life was cut off because of him; his freedom cannot be bought back by himself (v. 32 expressly forbids satisfaction), it can only be waited for. And the release he waits for is bound to the death-day of another man who is set apart as holy — this is not something he can calculate, hasten, or arrange; he can only abide in the city and wait in quietness. This "waiting" is itself the discipline this law gives the slayer: life is heavy; land on which a man's blood is shed cannot be cleansed (35:33); even the one without intent cannot treat the matter as though nothing had happened. (New Testament Reading) Hebrews, speaking of high priests, says that the high priests of the old covenant were many, because they were not suffered to continue by reason of death (Heb 7:23, registered paraphrase of the key points); and it says that Christ, having offered Himself once, made the matter complete (Heb 7:27, registered paraphrase of the key points). Reading the two places together, the reading layer registers one contrast: the man in the city of refuge waits for the death of a mortal high priest and is released; the people of the New Testament are released because an ever-living High Priest died for them and lives to make intercession continually. The mechanism of release is of the same construction in the two covenants — both are tied to a high priest — but the directions are opposite: in one place a death-notice is awaited; in the other, a death already accomplished and a life that does not die. This is a registration of the reading layer, not an assertion about the Old Testament's original sense. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) That the death of the high priest is joined to release is discussed in scholarship under two readings, set side by side. One reading explains it by the turn of an era: the high priest's death symbolizes the end of a term of office, and old cases are closed with it, as a general amnesty is tied to the succession of a king. A second reading explains it by substitutionary bearing: the high priest is the representative of the whole people, and his death takes up, in a ritual sense, the guilt that had been left suspended. The two readings coexist in the commentary tradition; this sermon adjudicates neither. One further historical register: Josephus, in the Jewish Antiquities, records the provisions on the cities of refuge and the death of the high priest in a way that basically restates the law's own meaning (registered paraphrase, with no verbatim quotation). (Fact) The "throughout your generations" of verse 29 closes this part: this is no temporary arrangement for the wilderness period, but a statute of judgment permanently in force after the entry into the land, in all their dwellings. (Fact) The four words "at any time" in verse 26 can be checked in the base text, and their weight is very heavy: the risk of the slayer's leaving the city has no term and no exceptions — it is not that after some years of residence he is automatically safe, nor that the matter is reckoned differently when the revenger is old and feeble. There is one boundary only: within the border of the city, the congregation's judgment shelters him; outside the border of the city, the law no longer restrains the revenger's hand. This austere stroke is in fact the most practical protection of the slayer: if hope carries a gamble with it, a man will test the boundary; with the boundary drawn without any ambiguity, he instead settles down in the city and lives out the "waiting" as his days. (Argument) Notice also that abiding in the city of refuge is not a prison sentence. The text does not say that he must labour, be kept under guard, or be isolated from the market-place — he dwells in a city of the Levites, living among its inhabitants (Josh 20, registered paraphrase), living a received life, not an imprisoned one. To him this city is both a soft confinement and a home: his possession lies at a distance (v. 28 still registers "the land of his possession" in his name; it has not been confiscated), his family and his future are all still there — only, they must wait. This arrangement of "possession retained, freedom suspended" weighs the measures of punishment and mercy very exactly: what he loses is years, not identity; freedom of movement, not his standing as one of the people. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The death of the high priest opening the door of release has a structural counterpart in the release of the jubilee in Leviticus 25 (registered paraphrase: in the jubilee, men and land each return to their own) — both are "the time has come, all are released," reckoned not case by case according to personal merit. Scholarship has much discussion of this institution of "time as the unit of pardon," and some researchers compare it with the general pardons proclaimed at the succession of a dynasty (registered). This sermon registers the comparison only and sets up no new theory; what the fact layer can settle is still that one sentence: the day of release is fixed on the death of the high priest, not on the slayer's own performance. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: the city of refuge is not a place of ease but a place of waiting; in the city the slayer learns one thing — a man's life cannot be priced, and freedom cannot be bought by oneself; it can only wait for the news of that one man's death to arrive.

### Part Five: Witnesses, Satisfaction, and the Cleansing of the Land — Blood Pollutes the Land (35:30-34) (48:00-57:00)

(Fact) The last five verses are the general rules of the whole chapter, in three articles. The first article: witnesses. Verse 30, quoted in full from the base text: "Whoso killeth any person, the murderer shall be put to death by the mouth of witnesses: but one witness shall not testify against any person to cause him to die." (35:30) For a capital conviction there must be the mouth of witnesses — plural; one witness is not enough. (Cross-Reference) This principle is registered at two places in Deuteronomy, 17:6 and 19:15 (registered paraphrase: a matter is established by the mouth of two or three witnesses); the New Testament continues in the same register — see the registrations at Matthew 18:16, 2 Corinthians 13:1, and 1 Timothy 5:19 (all registered paraphrase). The second article: satisfaction. Verse 31, quoted in full from the base text: "Moreover ye shall take no satisfaction for the life of a murderer, which is guilty of death: but he shall be surely put to death." (35:31) Verse 32, in full: "And ye shall take no satisfaction for him that is fled to the city of his refuge, that he should come again to dwell in the land, until the death of the priest." (35:32) The two "ye shall take no satisfaction" counted by hand: the first, satisfaction for a murderer's life in place of death; the second, satisfaction for the city-of-refuge man's early return — on the account of life, money is of no use at either end. (The KJV's word at both places is "satisfaction," rendering the Hebrew koper, H3724; the Chinese base text's word at both places is the word for ransom-price, and the two registers stand in parallel columns — see Endnote 11.) (Argument) In the ancient world this prohibition was glaring. Many ancient law codes allowed a life to be redeemed with silver, and negotiation of a blood-price was the standing practice; this chapter takes life off the market altogether: a murderer's life cannot be bought, and a slayer's freedom cannot be bought either. The rich man cannot exchange property for a capital charge, and the poor man need not sit one day longer for want of money. The unpriceability of life is the hardest bone this law leaves to later generations. (Cross-Reference) Compare Exodus 21:30: where an ox gores a man to death and its owner, having been warned that the ox was wont to push, is liable, there is a provision registered that his life may be given over to a satisfaction laid upon him (registered paraphrase) — satisfaction is not wholly unused within the legal system; it is precisely at the two places of murder and the city of refuge that it is expressly shut off. Scholarship has much discussion of this opening and this shutting set against each other; it is registered here without expansion. The third article: the cleansing of the land. Verse 33, quoted in full from the base text: "So ye shall not pollute the land wherein ye are: for blood it defileth the land: and the land cannot be cleansed of the blood that is shed therein, but by the blood of him that shed it." (35:33) The companion manuscript's note at this place follows the Chinese base text's own parenthesis — that its word for "cleansed" is in the original the word for atonement made — and it is registered here in the same sense: the account the land owes can be settled only by the blood of the one who shed blood; nothing else counts. Verse 34, in full, closes the whole chapter: "Defile not therefore the land which ye shall inhabit, wherein I dwell: for I the LORD dwell among the children of Israel." (35:34) (Argument) The last sentence of the whole chapter pulls the root of every provision out into the open: why does blood pollute the land? Why must the cities of refuge, the judgments, and the prohibition of satisfaction be so elaborate? Because this land is not only the land where the people dwell; it is the land in whose midst God dwells. The ultimate reason for righteousness is not social order but the holiness of the One who dwells there. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The conception that "blood pollutes the land" is registered at the discussion layer under two points. First, in Old Testament ritual thought blood is both the carrier of life (Lev 17:11, registered paraphrase: the life is in the blood) and one of the heaviest sources of pollution: where it is shed, there it must be dealt with. Second, commentators discuss the relation between verse 33's "the land cannot be cleansed but by the blood of him that shed it" and later judicial practice — the actual enforcement of this provision in Israel's history is thinly documented, and it is mostly registered without assertion. (Cross-Reference) The witness system has its other half as well, not written in this chapter but written in Deuteronomy 19: if a man bears false witness to accuse his brother, the judges are to inquire diligently, and the false witness is to be dealt with as he had thought to have done to his brother (registered paraphrase). Read the two places together and the witness system is complete: on one side, "one witness shall not testify against any person to cause him to die," guarding against mob unjust killing and one-sided accusation; on the other side, the penalty that repays the false witness in his own coin, guarding against the witness seat itself being bought and used. With both faces of the evidence rules present, the judgment seat can sit steady. (Argument) The seal of verse 34 must also find its echo within this book. Numbers 5 once commanded that everyone unclean be put out of the camp, and the reason given was precisely that the LORD dwells in the midst of the camp and the camp in which He dwells must not be defiled (registered paraphrase). From camp to land, the same principle is enlarged by one circle: in the wilderness, God dwells in the camp, and the camp must be clean; once settled, God dwells among the children of Israel, and the land must be clean. All the elaboration of the cities of refuge — the settlement of the six cities and the forty-eight, the distinguishing of the cases, the threshold of witnesses, the prohibition of satisfaction — traced to its root, is for this one sentence, "Defile not therefore": not that the land is so remarkable, but that the One who dwells in its midst must not be treated lightly. (Fact) Finally, count the structure of the whole chapter: among its thirty-four verses, the provisions directly concerning "death" run from verse 12 through verse 33, with blood in nearly every verse; but the chapter's first sentence gives cities, and its last sentence is God's dwelling in the midst. Blood is in the middle, cities at the two ends, and God over the whole chapter — that is the shape of Numbers 35. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: witnesses guard against unjust killing, the prohibition of satisfaction guards against the powerful buying life, and the cleansing of blood guards against the whole land being dragged down — the whole chapter is sealed with "for I the LORD dwell among the children of Israel": at the end of the law stands the God who dwells there.

Conclusion (57:00-60:00). (Argument) Look back over the whole chapter: before the people enter the land, God does three things first — He gives cities to the men who have no inheritance of land, a road to the men who killed without intent, and a boundary to the men who would revenge blood. In the institution of the cities of refuge, righteousness and mercy do not each yield a step; each goes to its extreme: toward the murderer, severity to the point of no satisfaction; toward the slayer, protection to the point that the congregation delivers him back to the city with its own hands. (New Testament Reading) Here too is the lesson the church must ask itself: a healthy community ought to be both a city of refuge and a judgment seat — for the man who has fallen without intent, a refuge he can reach; for evil knowingly committed, a boundary that does not sell favours. At the end, hand the whole chapter back to its sealing sentence: all our judgments of right and wrong are made for no other reason than that the Lord who dwells in our midst is holy; and the day of our release likewise hangs on a High Priest — not waiting for Him to die, but because He has died, and lives. May we both flee in, and abide in peace. (Argument) The sermon closes here: the institution of the cities of refuge did not remain on paper for us to muse over — Joshua registers its fulfilment, Hebrews borrows its image, and every healthy church and every believer's household still lives on this institution's extended line: keep a city for the man who has fallen without intent, keep a line against evil knowingly committed, and keep, for all right and wrong, a judgment seat that does not sell favours. As the land becomes a holy land through God's dwelling there, so a man, by fleeing into Christ, becomes a new creature.


## Devotional Extract (for daily devotional use)

The door of the city of refuge is opened for one kind of person: blood on his hands, and no hatred in his heart. After the congregation has judged his case clear, it does not merely acquit him; it personally delivers him out of the hand of the revenger of blood and restores him to the city. He abides in the city; he is not counted a prisoner, yet neither can he go home — until the death of the high priest, which was anointed with the holy oil. Before that day comes, there is nothing he can do: he cannot redeem himself with money (35:32), he cannot declare the matter closed for himself; he can only abide, and wait. Hidden in this waiting is a gentle reminder: some releases are not earned, but waited for; it is not that we have cleansed ourselves, but that the news of another's death arrives, and only then does the door open. Our salvation is even more so — we have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us (Heb 6:18), not because we are innocent, but because a High Priest died for us and lives for ever. Today, if in your heart there is an old matter you cannot lay down, a mark you cannot wash away, do not go on redeeming yourself with busyness and money; the gate of the city stands open — run in, abide there: the release has already been accomplished; only wait and rest. Prayer: Lord, we confess that on our hands there are marks that cannot be washed clean, and in our hearts debts that cannot be fully told; thank You that You have not made us redeem ourselves with money, with busyness, and with performance, but have only called us to flee into the city You have appointed. We are willing to abide there, no longer testing the boundary, no longer reckoning the old accounts backwards, waiting for Your time, abiding in Your release, until the day we go home. Amen.

# I Cannot Reverse It — A Sermon on Numbers 23

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Retranslated in full from the finalized Chinese manuscript (as corrected in Rolling Batch 21, 25 October 2026); draft translation pending native-speaker proofreading
Text: Numbers chapter 23 (30 verses in full, in the Chinese, English, and Hebrew canonical numbering alike; the Hebrew canonical chapter division has been checked and is the same chapter and the same verses, see Endnote 1)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), taken from the double-checked anchor text of Numbers 23 held for this batch and verified verse by verse against it; the companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (Simplified, "Shen" edition; hereafter "the Chinese base text"), verified verse by verse on 24 October 2026 and corrected in Rolling Batch 21 on 25 October 2026. The English text is not a back-translation of the Chinese quotations: every quotation below is KJV wording, and where the two base texts differ in wording, the difference is registered, not harmonized. The Devotional Excerpt's English paragraph likewise embeds KJV wording and is not rendered back from the Chinese.
Title verification: The title is taken from Balaam's own statement of the irreversibility of blessing in the second parable, verse 20, in the base text's own wording, read continuously: "Behold, I have received commandment to bless: and he hath blessed; and I cannot reverse it." (23:20) The chapter's three movements — the first utterance at the high places of Baal (23:1-12), the second utterance in the field of Zophim (23:13-26), and the third building of altars on the top of Peor (23:27-30) — are held together by one fact: Balak changes the place three times and builds seven altars three times, to buy one curse, and what he buys is blessing every time; and a blessing once given cannot be reversed. Candidates checked verse by verse and set aside: "How shall I curse, whom God hath not cursed?" (23:8) covers only the helplessness of the first parable; "Surely there is no enchantment against Jacob" (23:23) governs only the second half of the second parable; "God is not a man, that he should lie" (23:19) is the theological ground of the second parable, and as a title would cover only the second utterance. Verse 20 alone is at once a command ("I have received commandment"), a fact ("he hath blessed"), and a boundary ("I cannot reverse it"), and the failures of all three movements are gathered into it; it is therefore the title. The Chinese title is taken from the same verse in its own base text; each manuscript follows its own base text.
Base-text notes (where the two base texts or common paraphrase differ, each base text is recorded as it stands): At 23:8 the KJV reads "How shall I curse, whom God hath not cursed? or how shall I defy, whom the LORD hath not defied?" — the Chinese base text likewise has God not cursing and the LORD not defying; paraphrases that soften either clause are not followed. At 23:9 the KJV reads "lo, the people shall dwell alone, and shall not be reckoned among the nations" — "dwell alone" is recorded as it stands, not "be set apart." At 23:12 the KJV reads "Must I not take heed to speak that which the LORD hath put in my mouth?" — the operative phrase is "take heed to speak"; the Chinese base text's verb at this verse likewise denotes careful speaking-forth, and neither text is rendered as a bare "say." At 23:16 the KJV reads "And the LORD met Balaam, and put a word in his mouth," where the Chinese base text at this verse reads “临到” (the sense "the LORD came to Balaam"; its wording is distinct from its “迎见” wording, "meet," at 23:3, 4, 15) — the two texts are set side by side here: the KJV keeps the "met" wording, the Chinese base text varies it, and neither is harmonized to the other; the underlying verb family is the Hebrew qārâ in all four places (23:3, 4, 15, 16), see the counts in Endnote 4. At 23:19 the KJV reads "he should lie" and "he should repent," with the two rhetorical questions "hath he said, and shall he not do it? or hath he spoken, and shall he not make it good?" — recorded as it stands. At 23:20 the KJV reads "and I cannot reverse it" — not "I cannot revoke it" or "I cannot turn it back" in paraphrase; the title follows the KJV wording exactly. At 23:21 the KJV reads "He hath not beheld iniquity in Jacob, neither hath he seen perverseness in Israel" — "iniquity" and "perverseness" are the KJV's words; the Chinese base text's pair at this verse carries the sense "iniquity / evil-doing," and the renderings of the 2010 Chinese revision ("calamity / trouble") belong to another version and are registered only here. At 23:23 the KJV reads "Surely there is no enchantment against Jacob, neither is there any divination against Israel" — recorded as it stands, not in the form of common oral paraphrase. At 23:24 the KJV reads "he shall not lie down until he eat of the prey, and drink the blood of the slain" — "the prey" and "the slain" are the KJV's words. At 23:28 the KJV reads "the top of Peor, that looketh toward Jeshimon," where the Chinese base text writes the place-name as “毗珥” (a variant Chinese orthography for Peor, distinct from its form in Numbers 25) — each manuscript records its own text's form for this verse (see Endnote 3). At 23:3 the KJV reads only that Balaam "went to an high place," where the Chinese base text carries the further detail of a bare high place; the detail is registered here and in Endnote 3, and is not imported into the KJV quotations.

## Summary

At the end of chapter 22 Balaam stands at the high places of Baal and sees only the utmost part of Israel's camp (22:41). In chapter 23 the project of cursing formally begins: Balaam directs that seven altars be built and seven oxen and seven rams prepared, and himself goes forward to meet the LORD (23:1-4). The LORD puts a word in his mouth, and what comes out is the first parable of blessing: "How shall I curse, whom God hath not cursed?" (23:5-10) Balak protests, and Balaam answers with the declaration of chapter 22: "Must I not take heed to speak that which the LORD hath put in my mouth?" (23:11-12) Balak does not give up: he changes the place to the field of Zophim, to the top of Pisgah, lets Balaam see only the utmost part of Israel, and builds seven altars again (23:13-15). The second parable is harder than the first: "God is not a man, that he should lie"; "I have received commandment to bless... and I cannot reverse it"; "Surely there is no enchantment against Jacob" (23:16-24). Balak calls a halt: curse not at all, bless not at all (23:25). Balaam answers: "All that the LORD speaketh, that I must do" (23:26). At the end of the chapter Balak changes the place a third time, brings him to the top of Peor, that looketh toward Jeshimon, and builds seven altars a third time, offering a bullock and a ram on every altar (23:27-30) — the word has not yet come out, and the chapter closes first before a fresh row of altars: the only thing man can change is the place; what he cannot change is the blessing God has already given.

## Main Text

### Part One: Seven Altars — the Curse Project Begins (Numbers 23:1-6) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) In verses 1-2 the first procedure of the project is the building of altars, quoted verbatim from the base text: "And Balaam said unto Balak, Build me here seven altars, and prepare me here seven oxen and seven rams. And Balak did as Balaam had spoken; and Balak and Balaam offered on every altar a bullock and a ram." (23:1-2) The items registered one by one: the specification is laid down by Balaam, the execution borne by Balak — the king who is paying, once he reaches the altar, must instead take orders from the man he has bought; the number "seven" appears in this form three times in the chapter (23:1, 14, 29) and is the fixed pattern of this rite; the chapter itself does not state its meaning, and this sermon registers the number only. In verse 3 Balaam sends Balak aside and goes alone, quoted verbatim: "And Balaam said unto Balak, Stand by thy burnt offering, and I will go: peradventure the LORD will come to meet me: and whatsoever he sheweth me I will tell thee. And he went to an high place." (23:3) The word "peradventure" is registered: whether there will be a meeting at all, Balaam himself is not sure — what he prepares is a meeting that may come to nothing. In verse 4 the meeting takes place, quoted verbatim: "And God met Balaam: and he said unto him, I have prepared seven altars, and I have offered upon every altar a bullock and a ram." (23:4) The first sentence Balaam reports to God is his own bill of quantities: the altars counted, the offerings offered. God does not answer this bill of works. Verse 5 is quoted verbatim in full: "And the LORD put a word in Balaam's mouth, and said, Return unto Balak, and thus thou shalt speak." (23:5) "Put a word in... mouth" is recorded as it stands — the word is placed into this mouth; it is not produced by this mouth; and "thus thou shalt speak" is a command to speak word for word as given, leaving not even the sovereignty of phrasing to the prophet. In verse 6 Balaam returns to the waiting company: "And he returned unto him, and, lo, he stood by his burnt sacrifice, he, and all the princes of Moab." (23:6) The king and his princes stand by the sacrifice waiting for one curse; what they will receive is this chapter's first parable.

(Argument) In this opening movement, look first at the displacement of the rite. Building altars and offering sacrifice are the language of worship; here they have become the preliminary procedures of cursing: Balak supposes that if the scene is made grand enough and the price paid in full, God's word will be delivered to order. Balaam's report shows the human heart still more: his first sentence on meeting God is not inquiry but a claim of merit — seven altars, a bullock and a ram on every altar, like a certificate of completion. The mistake men most easily make is to treat preparation as merit and ritual as leverage, and to suppose that God owes a receipt. But God's answer in verse 5 sets the order straight: God does not certify the works; God gives the word directly. The altar is built by man, the word is put by God, and the two are never cause and effect of each other. The church today has the same certificate-of-completion mentality: the meeting has been held, the offering given, the programme filled out, and the blessing is then felt to be due for delivery on schedule. The first act of this chapter tears up that certificate: God met Balaam not because of the seven altars but because He would speak; and the word He speaks is the exact opposite of what those seven altars were built to buy.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Altar" is mizbēaḥ (H4196); "burnt offering" is ʿōlâ (H5930). The verb family behind "meet" is qārâ (H7136 / H7125), to encounter, to meet by coming upon; it occurs four times in this chapter (23:3, 4, 15, 16). On the KJV letter the four are all rendered with "meet / met"; in the Chinese base text three places read with its "meet" wording and 23:16 reads with its "came to" wording — the count of four rests on the Hebrew, and each text's renderings are registered separately (see Endnote 4). The construction behind "put a word in... mouth" is literally "put a word in his mouth," the same image as the "put in my mouth" of 22:38; this sermon registers it by the base text's wording. The numbers above were each checked before writing (for the method see Endnote 5).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The exact origin and purpose of the "seven altars" pattern in the ritual world of the ancient Near East are variously explained in scholarship: some explain "seven" as a number of completeness, others read it alongside the sacrificial customs of neighbouring peoples. The text of this chapter itself records the pattern only and does not explain its meaning; the accounts are set out here without adjudication. The main text establishes one fact only: this rite was specified by Balaam and served the cursing, and its output was decided by God's word, not by the rite.

(Fact) The "peradventure" of verse 3 and the "met" of verse 4 gain their weight only when read together. In the base text Balaam's own words are: "peradventure the LORD will come to meet me: and whatsoever he sheweth me I will tell thee." (23:3) He does not say "the LORD will surely come," nor "I shall surely obtain" — the man who practises divination, before the true God, does not dare to guarantee even whether he will receive an answer. Yet the narrative of verse 4 settles the outcome in a few words: "And God met Balaam." On man's side a "peradventure"; on God's side, fact. This sequence is repeated twice in the chapter (23:15-16 in the same form): it is not man's procedure that summons God; it is God who meets man according to His own will. Register further: at both meetings, the first thing God does on speaking is not to answer Balaam's question but to put a word and to direct — "thus thou shalt speak" (23:5, 16). Balaam asks whether cursing is possible; God gives a parable. God never answers in the columns of the order form; He speaks according to His own heart. The details of the meeting this chapter does not state (vision, dream, or another means — none is recorded; see Endnote 8); the main text registers the fact of the meeting itself only, and does not supply a scene for the narrative.

(Argument) The word "peradventure" also shows one honesty and one sorrow in Balaam's profession. The honesty: he knows that the One he faces is not a supplier from the spirit world who can be summoned at will — the diviner's fee can move his feet, but it cannot move God's coming; that he is willing to say "peradventure" in front of his employer is an admission that one stretch of this business lies outside his control. The sorrow: he plainly knows that God cannot be sent for, yet he keeps coming back to the altar, running errands for a business that cannot send for God. Knowing that God cannot be bought, and refusing to stop being bought, are two different things. How many servants are stuck at the same place: the theology is all correct — God does not lie, does not repent, cannot be bribed; yet life goes on travelling between altar and wages as before. Balaam's problem was never a lack of knowledge; what he lacked was to live his knowledge into his footsteps: having known the "peradventure," he should have stopped the first time the "peradventure" fell empty into blessing, instead of treating the "peradventure" as a licence to begin the next round.

### Part Two: A People Dwelling Alone — the First Parable (Numbers 23:7-10, 11-12) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) In verse 7 the parable begins; its introduction is quoted verbatim in full: "And he took up his parable, and said, Balak the king of Moab hath brought me from Aram, out of the mountains of the east, saying, Come, curse me Jacob, and come, defy Israel." (23:7) The parable first restates the order: there was one purpose in bringing me here — to curse and to defy. Verse 8 is the hinge of the whole poem, quoted verbatim in full: "How shall I curse, whom God hath not cursed? or how shall I defy, whom the LORD hath not defied?" (23:8) The logic is a double impossibility: for a curse to take effect, God's curse must stand before it; since God has not cursed, man's curse has nothing to follow. Here Balaam states the boundary of chapter 22 as a principle: it is not that I am unwilling; it is that water without a source cannot flow. In verse 9 the parable turns to what is seen, quoted verbatim: "For from the top of the rocks I see him, and from the hills I behold him: lo, the people shall dwell alone, and shall not be reckoned among the nations." (23:9) "See" and "behold" are Balaam's professional acts — he stands on the heights, and his trade is watching; but what he sees is not the weakness Balak wants him to see: it is a people "dwelling alone" — dwelling on the earth alongside the nations, yet not reckoned on the nations' account. Verse 10 is the climax of the poem and its aside, quoted verbatim in full: "Who can count the dust of Jacob, and the number of the fourth part of Israel? Let me die the death of the righteous, and let my last end be like his!" (23:10) The first half is number: the dust cannot be counted, the fourth part cannot be numbered — Balaam has seen only the utmost part of the camp (the border in 22:41, the top of the rocks here), and even the fourth part of that utmost part he cannot reckon to the end. The second half is the only words Balaam speaks for himself in the whole chapter: he wishes his own death and last end to be like the righteous'. Registered distinctly: this wish is the self-statement inside the parable, not the narrative's verdict on his end; he envies the end of the righteous while standing on the work-site of cursing the righteous. In verse 11 Balak's account collapses, quoted verbatim: "And Balak said unto Balaam, What hast thou done unto me? I took thee to curse mine enemies, and, behold, thou hast blessed them altogether." (23:11) Balaam's answer in verse 12 is the echo of the declaration of chapter 22, quoted verbatim in full: "And he answered and said, Must I not take heed to speak that which the LORD hath put in my mouth?" (23:12) The phrase "take heed to speak" is registered: his office is stated to its limit here — the word is not his; in speaking it forth he may only take heed, neither adding nor taking away.

(Argument) The first parable must make one word plain: alone. This is not a geographical description — Israel is at this moment encamped opposite Moab, living as neighbours with the nations. Dwelling alone is a fact of identity: they are not reckoned among the nations, because their account is not kept in the nations' ledger, and their blessing and curse are not decided by the nations' rules. Balak's whole calculation treats Israel as one nation among the nations, to be dealt with as such: it can be reconnoitred, it can be priced, an expert can be hired to curse it. What Balaam sees from the top of the rocks overturns exactly this premise: this people's origin is the exodus (taken up in the second parable, 23:22), this people's number is the covenant of dust, this people's standing is to dwell alone. The church should also hear this sentence: the church may dwell among the nations, yet it does not live by the nations' system of evaluation — it does not prove its blessedness by the world's applause, and it does not count itself to have lost the blessing because the world curses it. Dwelling alone is not aloofness; it is a different belonging: there is no page for us in the nations' ledger; our names are recorded in another book.

(Argument) The aside in the second half of verse 10 needs a pause of its own. "Let me die the death of the righteous" — the man who says this has just been running errands for an order to curse. He envies the end of the righteous, yet his feet stand on his employer's heights; he knows which death is good, yet he has not turned the direction of his life toward that death. This is the truest crack in Balaam's whole person: the wish is real, the obedience is false; he wants the outcome of the righteous, not the way of the righteous. A man can be moved to tears by the death of the righteous and at the same time go on receiving the wages of cursing — if being moved does not change the road, it is only decoration on the work-site. Examine ourselves by the same measure: is the end I envy, and the direction I am walking, the same road? Balaam's tragedy was never that he did not understand; he understood that the death of the righteous is enviable; he simply would not turn around now. The parable ended, and he did not go down the mountain and home; he went with Balak to a second place.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The word behind "parable" in "took up his parable" is māšāl (H4912), a proverb-like utterance, a saying in figure; it occurs twice in this chapter (23:7, 18) — in the KJV both verses read identically, "And he took up his parable"; in the Chinese base text the two verses differ slightly in wording (the first carrying an extra verb element, the second without it), and each manuscript records its own text verse by verse without conforming one to the other. "Curse" in verse 7 is ʾārar (H779); "defy" is zāʿam (H2194), the same word in verse 8. The "curse" of "How shall I curse" is by a different verb, qāḇaḇ (H6895), of the same root as the qābâ of chapter 22. "Dust" is ʿāpār (H6083); "the fourth part" is rōḇaʿ (H7255), whose precise reference is discussed in scholarship and is kept at the discussion layer.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The reading of "the fourth part" in verse 10 has long been divided: some take it literally as a fourth of Israel's total (answering to the disposition of the four camps), others, on the basis of ancient versions and etymology, read it otherwise (a "dust-cloud" family of readings, for example). This sermon registers the base text's "the fourth part" as it stands, and the main text uses only its poetic sense of the uncountable; no doctrine of number is built on it, and the accounts are set out without adjudication. The intertextual relation between "dwell alone" in verse 9 and "Israel then shall dwell in safety alone" in Deuteronomy 33:28 likewise belongs to the discussion layer; the main text registers this verse's own wording only.

(Fact) What is seen in verses 9-10 has one plain echo with the old words of the covenant (cross-reference layer): Balaam says, "Who can count the dust of Jacob" (23:10), and God's promise to Abram in Genesis runs, in key points, that his seed will be made as the dust of the earth, so that if a man can number the dust of the earth, then shall his seed also be numbered (Gen 13:16, key points) — from the beginning, the number of the dust has been a figure for the uncountable, and now it is spoken as it stands out of the mouth of a foreign prophet. Whether Balaam knew what he was restating the chapter does not say (it does not state what he had read); the narrative lets the reader hear it: a promise more than four hundred years old is being personally confirmed, on the heights of Moab, by a hired curser. Register also a contrast of vantage: Balaam states his own standing in the poem — "For from the top of the rocks I see him, and from the hills I behold him" (23:9) — he looks at Israel always from above; yet the content of what he sees lowers him: a people dwelling alone, uncountable dust, an enviable righteous end. The height gave him no vantage over this people; it gave him only a post from which to witness for this people. A man who stands high does not necessarily see large; Balaam's lesson here is this: God may set you on a height so that you will carry His word farther, not so that you will think yourself higher.

### Part Three: Try Another Place — the Field of Zophim and Pisgah (Numbers 23:13-17) (24:00-44:00)

(Fact) When the first parable is sung, Balak's countermeasure is to change the place, quoted verbatim: "And Balak said unto him, Come, I pray thee, with me unto another place, from whence thou mayest see them: thou shalt see but the utmost part of them, and shalt not see them all: and curse me them from thence." (23:13) Balak's new tactics are registered in three items: change the location, limit the view, keep the target — he judges that the first failure was a problem of standing: having seen them all makes it hard to strike; see only the utmost part, and perhaps the curse will go out. In verse 14 the new work-site opens, quoted verbatim: "And he brought him into the field of Zophim, to the top of Pisgah, and built seven altars, and offered a bullock and a ram on every altar." (23:14) The "field of Zophim" is a place-name, its sense that of a watchmen's field (Summary layer); the standing has changed from the high places of Baal to the top of Pisgah, and the specification of the rite is not reduced by a fraction. In verse 15 the procedure is repeated, quoted verbatim: "And he said unto Balak, Stand here by thy burnt offering, while I meet the LORD yonder." (23:15) Read against verse 3, the two meetings differ subtly in posture: the first was "peradventure the LORD will come to meet me," carrying uncertainty; the second Balaam states with assurance — "while I meet the LORD yonder" — as if God had become a fixture of the procedure, available on call. In verse 16 God nevertheless comes again according to His own sovereignty, quoted verbatim: "And the LORD met Balaam, and put a word in his mouth, and said, Go again unto Balak, and say thus." (23:16) "Put a word in his mouth" is in the same form as verse 5, and "say thus" is reissued as it stood — the procedure may be repeated; the sovereignty of the word yields not an inch. In verse 17 Balaam returns to the place of waiting, quoted verbatim: "And when he came to him, behold, he stood by his burnt offering, and the princes of Moab with him. And Balak said unto him, What hath the LORD spoken?" (23:17) Balak's question is registered: he asks not "What pleases the LORD?" but "What hath the LORD spoken?" — by this moment, even this king who is paying is waiting for God's word, though the sentence he waits for is another one.

(Argument) This movement must make Balak's "change the place" plain, because it is man's most common way of dealing with God's word. When the word is unwelcome, the first reaction is not to turn back but to change the conditions: change the location, change the angle, change to a view that sees only the utmost part, in the hope that the same God will say a different sentence in a different place. Balak's logic is the world's common sense: when the result is unfavourable, the parameters are probably wrong — the standing, the view, the occasion; adjust and try again. But God's word is not experimental data; it does not change with the point of observation. Before the second parable has even come out, this movement has already set down the verdict in advance: the second time, God still "put a word in his mouth," still says "say thus" — the place has changed, the procedure has been performed, the altars have been built again, and only the manner in which the word is produced has not changed by a letter. Believers have their Pisgah-like temptations too: this verse does not apply in this situation — ask again in another situation; this preacher's word is too hard — listen to another instead; this boundary is too narrow here — it is enough to see only the utmost part. God does not forbid us to change places; He simply never changes His sentence in a new place. To look for a second location for a word you have already heard clearly is this chapter's warning to everyone who would "try once more": what awaits at the second meeting may be a harder word than the first.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The "Zophim" of the "field of Zophim," Ṣōpîm, comes from the ṣāpâ (H6822) family of watching; the word-form is registered as such, its precise reference pending verification. The Strong's numbers for "Pisgah," Pisgâ, and for "Peor," Pəʿôr (the place of 23:28; on its Chinese base-text form see Endnote 3), are marked pending verification in this sermon and are not supplied from memory; see Endnote 5. The "utmost part" rests on qāṣeh (H7097), of the family of end and extremity; the KJV at 23:13 reads "the utmost part of them," and at 22:41 likewise of seeing the utmost part of the people — both places speaking of the view that sees only a portion — and this sermon registers each verse by its own base text's wording.

(Fact) Set the procedures of the two meetings side by side and register them (the chapter's own comparison): in both, Balaam specifies seven altars, seven oxen, and seven rams (23:1, 14); in both, Balak stands by the sacrifice waiting (23:3, 15); in both, God meets, puts a word, and commands him to return and speak as given (23:4-5, 16); in both, Balaam returns and Balak with his princes stands to receive him (23:6, 17). The only variable between the two is place and view: the utmost part of the whole camp against the utmost part of a portion. This strict comparison is the experimental design the narrative itself lays out — only one variable is allowed to move; if the result changes, the cause is assigned to the place; if the result does not change, the cause can only rest on the God who does not change. Beginning at verse 18, the second parable comes precisely to announce the result of this experiment.

(Argument) The tactic of "thou shalt see but the utmost part of them" (23:13) must be pushed one layer further into the human heart. Balak wants to narrow the view not only as a military trick but as a spiritual self-deception: see them all, and you cannot but admit that this people is a blessed whole; see only the utmost part, and you can treat that part as the whole and curse it. Man's customary way of dealing with an unwelcome truth is exactly this move — not to deny it head-on, but only to shrink the range of looking: look only at the few in the church who have fallen, only at the few prayers that went unanswered, only at your own weakest corner, and then say: you see, it is nothing much. Balaam is brought up to Pisgah the second time with his view cut down to a portion, yet the parable that comes out sees more than the first time: the first time he saw only dwelling alone and dust; the second time he sees the shout of a king, the strength as of a unicorn, and a lion that has risen. The view is cut by man; what is seen is given by God. The man who allows only a portion thinks he controls the conclusion; God sets the whole picture down within the portion. If we are willing to be honest, we should examine ourselves in reverse: in what matters do I consent to see only a portion? Beyond that portion, God has long since spoken the blessing in full; I simply do not wish to turn my head and see the whole camp.

### Part Four: God Is Not a Man — the Second Parable (Numbers 23:18-24) (44:00-52:00)

(Fact) In verse 18 the second utterance begins, and the specification rises first, quoted verbatim: "And he took up his parable, and said, Rise up, Balak, and hear; hearken unto me, thou son of Zippor:" (23:18) The first time, Balaam reported what he saw; the second time, the prophet commands the king to rise and hear judgment — employer and prophet have exchanged places inside the parable. Verse 19 is the theological summit of the whole chapter, quoted verbatim in full: "God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent: hath he said, and shall he not do it? or hath he spoken, and shall he not make it good?" (23:19) Registered sentence by sentence: God is not a man — He has neither man's lying (discarding deed with word) nor man's repenting (discarding an old word with a new intention); the two rhetorical questions nail the fact down: what He has said, He will do; what He has spoken, He will make good. Verse 20 is the verdict in which the summit lands on this case, and the verse on which this sermon's title rests, quoted verbatim in full: "Behold, I have received commandment to bless: and he hath blessed; and I cannot reverse it." (23:20) "I have received commandment" answers to "he hath blessed": the command rests on the prophet, the blessing rests in God's hand; "I cannot reverse it" is not Balaam's modesty but the nature of the matter itself — a blessing already given does not stand among the things that can be revoked. Verse 21 gives Israel as God sees them, quoted verbatim in full: "He hath not beheld iniquity in Jacob, neither hath he seen perverseness in Israel: the LORD his God is with him, and the shout of a king is among them." (23:21) The subject of "He hath not beheld" is God — this is not a statement that Israel is without sin (the fact layer of this book's earlier narrative has its own register; see the discussion layer); it is a statement of God's viewing as fixed by His covenant with this people: what He sees is the people with whom He is present, the camp in which there is the shout of a king. "The shout of a king" is registered: there is a king in the camp — not a king Israel has set up (there is no such event in this chapter), but the shout for the LORD as King in their midst. In verse 22 the exodus is named again, quoted verbatim: "God brought them out of Egypt; he hath as it were the strength of an unicorn." (23:22) Verse 23 is the death sentence of the enchantment trade, quoted verbatim in full: "Surely there is no enchantment against Jacob, neither is there any divination against Israel: according to this time it shall be said of Jacob and of Israel, What hath God wrought!" (23:23) The man who says this is Balaam himself, who lives by enchantment and divination (the diviner's fee of 22:7) — God borrows the mouth of a man of the trade to declare the trade ineffective against this people; and what will finally be said of this people is not a word of cursing but a word of wonder. Verse 24 closes the second utterance with the image of the lion, quoted verbatim in full: "Behold, the people shall rise up as a great lion, and lift up himself as a young lion: he shall not lie down until he eat of the prey, and drink the blood of the slain." (23:24) The curse Balak wanted to buy did not come; what came was a lion already risen.

(Argument) Verse 19 must be preached slowly, and preached through. Balak changes the place on the assumption that God is as man is: a man can be moved by a new setting, softened by repeated asking, won over by a higher specification. In this verse Balaam lifts the foundation open: God is not a man. He does not lie, so His word needs no revision; He does not repent, so His blessing needs no renegotiation. For Israel encamped opposite Moab this is a safeguard; for us standing by the altar it is likewise a safeguard and a warning. The safeguard: a blessing God has given does not fail because the enemy raises his bid, is not discounted because the place is changed, and does not shrink because of the prophet's motives. The warning: the words God has spoken do not reverse for us either — His promises come to pass, and His warnings also come to pass. What man most wants from God is often a negotiable attitude: an exception this once, an indulgence this time, a boundary moved a little. Verse 19 bars this road: God's non-negotiability is precisely the reason all His promises can be trusted. If He would reverse a blessing for Balak, He would reverse for someone else the blessing given to you; since He reverses for no bid, this blessing stands on you as firm as He Himself.

(Argument) Verse 21, "hath not beheld iniquity," and verse 23, "no enchantment," must be read together if they are not to go astray. That God has not beheld iniquity in Jacob does not mean this people's conduct is spotless — the reader of this book has just read their murmurings, idolatry, and rebellion (cross-reference layer; see the register in the discussion layer); this is the viewing of the covenant: God views them according to the covenant He made and the grace by which He redeemed them, as a father views a son by his sonship, not as an enemy prosecutes a sinner. Precisely for this reason enchantment has nowhere to take hold: for a curse to take effect, a valid accusation must first be found in God's court; and since God covers by the covenant, the accusation has nowhere to land. This is not an indulgent covering — this book's later narrative shows God's discipline of this people to be just as severe (discussion layer); it is a covering of order: the right to deal with this people rests in God's hand and is not outsourced to an order from Moab. The church should paste these two verses on its heart: your sin God disciplines Himself, and it is not the enemy's turn to condemn you; your blessing God gives Himself, and it is no one's turn to reverse it. Within God's covenant, the failure of accusation and the reality of discipline can stand at the same time.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Lie" is kāzaḇ (H3576); "repent" is nāḥam (H5162), to turn in heart, to take back what was said before. The word behind "reverse" is of the šûḇ family in its causative use (H7725), to cause to turn back, to revoke. "Iniquity" is ʾāwen (H205); "perverseness" is ʿāmāl (H5999), evil of trouble and toil — the difference in rendering of these two words between versions (as, in the Chinese tradition, the base text's pair carrying the sense "iniquity / evil-doing" against the 2010 revision's "calamity / trouble") belongs to translation discussion; this sermon registers the pair by its own base text and builds no argument on a single translated word. "Shout" is tərûʿâ (H8643), the sound of the trumpet blast and of shouting. "Enchantment" is naḥaš (H5173); "divination" is qesem (H7081), the same word as the "diviner's fee" (rendered "rewards of divination") of 22:7. "Unicorn" renders rəʾēm (H7214); "great lion" renders lāḇîʾ (H3833); "young lion" renders ʾărî (H738).

(New Testament Reading) The New Testament echoes this passage at two places, with the same tenor: on God's calling of Israel, Romans says, in key points, that the gifts and calling of God are without repentance (Rom 11:29, key points) — the "not repent" of 23:19 here becomes, from a sentence in a parable, a theorem of the history of salvation; and Titus speaks, in key points, of the eternal life which God, that cannot lie, promised before the world began (Titus 1:2, key points), pointing with the "that he should lie" denied in 23:19 to the same covenant-keeping God. Both places are set out at the reading layer: the New Testament's unfolding is not read back into this chapter, and this chapter's facts do not need the New Testament in order to stand — that a given blessing cannot be reversed was already said in full on the heights of Moab.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The tension between verse 21 and this book's facts is set out side by side without harmonization. Fact one: this chapter's parable says that God "hath not beheld iniquity in Jacob" (23:21). Fact two: this book's earlier narrative repeatedly records Israel's sin — within this book itself there are the lust and murmuring of chapter 11, the refusal to enter Canaan in chapter 14, the rebellion of Korah's company in chapter 16, the strife of Meribah in chapter 20, and the complaining and the fiery serpents of chapter 21 (the murmuring three days after the exodus does not stand in this book). The two registers coexist in one book, and this chapter does not adjudicate between them. The paths of explanation in the commentarial tradition are left to this layer: some explain the parable's viewing as the viewing of the covenant (God views this people by the covenant's declaration); some explain it by the semantics of ʾāwen and ʿāmāl as belonging to the family of calamity (God has not brought calamity to completion upon this people); some explain it by the declarative character of the poetic genre. The accounts are set out without adjudication; the main text establishes only the base text's own wording and the narrative facts, and neither cancels the other. Further, the tension between "that he should repent" denied in verse 19 and expressions of the type "the LORD repented" in Genesis 6:6 and 1 Samuel 15:11 is likewise an old topic of this layer: within the same book, 1 Samuel 15:29 itself carries the sentence, in key points, that He will not lie nor repent, coexisting in one chapter with 15:11 and 15:35 — the text itself sets them side by side, and this sermon, as its rule is, registers only and does not harmonize, keeping the accounts at this layer.

(Fact) The inner march of the second parable, registered verse by verse in its order from the base text: first the nature of God is established (23:19 — not a man, no lying, no repenting); next the verdict is given (23:20 — commandment received, blessing already given, cannot be reversed); then the people as God sees them is set out (23:21 — no iniquity beheld, God with him, the shout of a king); their origin is traced (23:22 — brought out of Egypt, strength as of a unicorn); enchantment is declared ineffective (23:23); and it ends with the image of the lion fixing their future posture (23:24). This order is not casual arrangement; it is an argument: because God is this kind of God (19), therefore the blessing cannot be reversed (20); because this people, in God's eyes, is this kind of people (21-22), therefore enchantment has nowhere to take hold (23); because both of the foregoing are true, therefore this people's rising cannot be stopped (24). What Balak hears by the altar is a parable; its skeleton is a complete theology: the doctrine of God first, the doctrine of the chosen people in the middle, and the sentence on the enemies last. Register one further point: "God brought them out of Egypt" in verse 22 is the common anchor of the two parables (in the first parable, the number of the dust in 23:10 comes from the same stretch of covenant history) — when Balaam speaks of this people's present and future, the root is sunk in that one event of the exodus; apart from that event this people is nothing, and holding to that event this people fears no curse. This chapter's theology can therefore be gathered into one sentence: as God brought them out, so God keeps them to the end — the bringing out is the beginning of grace, the keeping is the continuation of faithfulness, and both ends are God's, neither end in man's hand.

### Part Five: The Top of Peor — a Third Building of Altars and a Look Back over the Whole Chapter (Numbers 23:25-30 and the Whole Chapter) (52:00-60:00)

(Fact) In verse 25 Balak calls a halt, quoted verbatim: "And Balak said unto Balaam, Neither curse them at all, nor bless them at all." (23:25) The order has changed: if a curse cannot be bought, at least buy a silence — neither curse nor bless is the buyer's last stop-loss line. In verse 26 Balaam's answer strikes out the stop-loss line as well, quoted verbatim in full: "But Balaam answered and said unto Balak, Told not I thee, saying, All that the LORD speaketh, that I must do?" (23:26) Registered: Balaam here appeals to the position he has held from the beginning (22:18, 38; 23:12) — he cannot promise silence, just as he cannot promise a curse; the switch of this mouth is not in his hand, and its opening and its closing must alike be done in obedience. In verse 27 Balak, his stop-loss failed, wagers a third time, quoted verbatim: "And Balak said unto Balaam, Come, I pray thee, I will bring thee unto another place; peradventure it will please God that thou mayest curse me them from thence." (23:27) The phrase "peradventure it will please God" is registered: after two verdicts, Balak still treats God as a variable to be tried by luck — this is not ignorance; it is fear arrived at the point of refusing to admit the account. In verse 28 comes the third height, quoted verbatim: "And Balak brought Balaam unto the top of Peor, that looketh toward Jeshimon." (23:28) The view changes again: from the utmost part of the camp (22:41), to the utmost part of a portion (23:13), to Peor looking toward Jeshimon (the wilderness) — the looking is changed ever further aside, and the door of the answer is shut ever tighter. In verses 29-30 the third beginning of work, quoted verbatim: "And Balaam said unto Balak, Build me here seven altars, and prepare me here seven bullocks and seven rams. And Balak did as Balaam had said, and offered a bullock and a ram on every altar." (23:29-30) The chapter closes before a third row of new altars: the third parable is left to the next chapter; this chapter records only that the project is as before and the specification as before — and one premise which, after two utterances, no one can any longer overturn: the blessing has been given, and it cannot be reversed.

(Argument) The chapter closes by leaving one sentence for each of three kinds of people. First, for those who want to change the place and ask once more: Balak changed his place three times and received a harder word each time. Some answers you already have; what is lacking is not a second location but a turning back in accordance with the first answer. Neither Pisgah nor Peor was the wrong place; what was wrong was carrying a settled question to a new place to try one's luck. Second, for those repeatedly tempted by a raised bid: Balaam's mouth held through two utterances — this was not his virtue but the sovereignty of God's putting the word into his mouth; yet his person still stood beside a third altar. Holding one sentence is not hard; what is hard is, having held it, being willing to go down the mountain. If in some matter God has already governed your mouth, the next step is not to wait for a third beginning of work but to leave the altar-side while you can still walk. Third, for those encamped on the other side: Israel still has not spoken a single sentence in the whole chapter (fact layer: this chapter has not one line of Israelite speech); yet the battle was fought entirely outside their camp — twenty-one altars, two meetings, two parables: the whole might of Moab bought, for them, a louder blessing. You do not need to hear every curse prepared for you, nor to dismantle with your own hands every altar built for you; your duty is to encamp, as usual. While God finishes speaking His word for you on the heights, you attend to living your days well on the plain.

(Fact) The chapter's data are gathered in closing, every item already counted (for the details see Endnote 4): the whole chapter has 30 verses; the building of altars runs in three rounds (23:1-2, 14, 29-30), each round seven altars, seven oxen, and seven rams — for the whole chapter, twenty-one altars, twenty-one oxen, and twenty-one rams in all; the meeting occurs at four places (23:3, 4, 15, 16, on the Hebrew qārâ family; on each base text's renderings see Endnote 4); "put a word" occurs three times (23:5, 12, 16); "thus thou shalt speak / say thus" occurs twice (23:5, 16); "took up his parable" occurs twice (23:7, 18; in the KJV the wording is identical at both verses); the height is changed three times (the high places of Baal, carried over from 22:41; the top of Pisgah, 23:14; the top of Peor, 23:28). The narrative's geographical arc: the view narrows step by step from the utmost part of the whole camp to a portion, and then to looking toward Jeshimon; the verdict of blessing is raised step by step: the first utterance says a curse is impossible (23:8), the second says reversal is impossible (23:20); man's devices are used ever more to exhaustion, and God's word is spoken ever more to fullness.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The date of composition, sources, and redaction of Balaam's two parables are much discussed in scholarship: some regard them as ancient poems embedded in the narrative, others as the pen of the narrative's author. This sermon establishes only the facts of the canonical text: the two parables' narrative position and function in this chapter — the first answers "can a curse be made?", the second answers "can it be reversed?" — and adopts no chronological conclusion as a premise; the accounts are kept at this layer for reference. Likewise, the relation of Pisgah to Mount Nebo, and the question of the identity of Peor with the place of Beth-peor (Numbers 25), are registered only as the co-occurrence of place-names, with no assertion of identity; they are left for treatment in the sermon on chapter 25.

(Fact) The chapter's account of speeches is settled in one last entry: there are only three speaking parties — Balak, Balaam, and God (the two parables through Balaam's mouth). Israel has not one line of speech in the whole chapter, in the same form as chapter 22; the princes of Moab are present at two places (23:6, 17) and likewise have not a word. God's direct speech in this chapter is two sentences only, both of them words of direction: "Return unto Balak, and thus thou shalt speak" and "Go again unto Balak, and say thus" (23:5, 16, key points of the direction) — all the rest is parable through Balaam's mouth. This distribution itself is the chapter's shape: the party discussed, watched, and cursed is silent; the party paying speaks the most, changing his order twice and his place three times; the party in authority speaks the least, yet every sentence settles the matter. The quantity of speech and the weight of sovereignty in this chapter stand in inverse proportion — and this too is a measure the chapter leaves to the reader: do not judge who is in authority by whose voice is loudest. The heights of Moab were noisy for a whole chapter; the camp on the plain was quiet as usual; and when day breaks, what counts is those two shortest sentences.

## Devotional Excerpt

Balak changed the place three times and built twenty-one altars, to buy one curse, and what he bought was blessing every time. The most piercing sentence is Balaam's: "Behold, I have received commandment to bless: and he hath blessed; and I cannot reverse it." (23:20) If even the man hired to curse you cannot reverse the blessing God has given you — then what is left that can reverse it? Not the changing of the place, not the enemy's raising of his bid, not the prophet's mouth. "God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent" (23:19). He does not bless on a passing impulse, nor take back on a passing displeasure; what He has said, He will do accordingly; what He has spoken, He will make good. In our hearts there often lives a little Balak: when things go badly, we want to change the place, change the method, change the person and ask once more, hoping God will change His sentence somewhere else. In fact God's not changing His sentence is exactly our greatest comfort — if His blessing could be bought away by a curse, it could also be taken back by a mood, and we would never have rest. Encamp today in the word He has already spoken: the blessing has been given, and it cannot be reversed. However high the enemy's altars are built, they are not higher than this one sentence. And there is one further layer, spoken to you who are now being discussed and plotted against: Israel did not speak one sentence in the whole chapter, yet God spoke two whole parables for them on the heights. You do not need to prove yourself, nor to curse back, nor to dismantle altars through the night; the God who views you according to the covenant has not beheld your iniquity so as to condemn you as the enemy accuses; what He sees is the camp of His presence. Quietness is not inability; it is handing the right to speak back to the God who, having spoken, will surely make it good.

English (KJV-based):

"God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent: hath he said, and shall he not do it? or hath he spoken, and shall he not make it good? Behold, I have received commandment to bless: and he hath blessed; and I cannot reverse it." (Num 23:19-20, KJV) Balak changed the place three times and built twenty-one altars, but a spoken blessing from God does not come with a return policy. The God who does not lie also does not take back. Rest today in the word He has already spoken over you — it cannot be reversed.

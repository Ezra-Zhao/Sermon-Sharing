## Summary (about 100 words)

Jacob, one hundred and forty-seven years old and knowing his days were few, summoned his twelve sons to his bedside and spoke of "what shall befall you in the last days." Reuben lost the birthright; the anger of Simeon and Levi was cursed; Judah received the blessing of the lion and the sceptre, until Shiloh comes; Joseph received the longest blessing, and Benjamin closed the poem as a ravening wolf. A father's words became a map for his descendants: there are warnings, there are blessings, and grace still leaves a place inside the sentences of judgment.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

What we read today is a father's last words. Not a will from an accountant's office, not a document from a lawyer's firm, but a poem. Nearly the whole of Genesis chapter 49 consists of poetic lines: short sentences, parallel phrases, image upon image coming down like drumbeats. This chapter cannot be preached at the rhythm of narrative prose; it must be preached at the rhythm of poetry — one line, then a pause, letting the image settle into the heart before moving on. For a dying person has no wasted words; and the one speaking here is Jacob, who wrestled with God through the night, came away limping, and had his name changed.

The scene at the opening of this chapter is very quiet. An old man lies on a bed, his eyes already dim, his strength already failing, and he calls all twelve of his sons to gather around the bed. The eldest son Reuben is over forty; the youngest, Benjamin, is already grown. Twelve men, twelve temperaments, twelve roads already traveled and still to be traveled. The old man's first sentence is not an accounting of property but an announcement about time: gather yourselves together, that I may tell you what shall befall you in the last days. He is looking not at yesterday but at tomorrow; not at this family's grievances, but at the destiny of these twelve tribes hundreds and thousands of years from now.

Let us read together several key passages of today's Scripture — slowly, reading out the rhythm of the poem:

"And Jacob called unto his sons, and said, Gather yourselves together, that I may tell you that which shall befall you in the last days. Gather yourselves together, and hear, ye sons of Jacob; and hearken unto Israel your father." (Genesis 49:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"Judah, thou art he whom thy brethren shall praise: thy hand shall be in the neck of thine enemies; thy father's children shall bow down before thee. Judah is a lion's whelp... The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be." (Genesis 49:8-10, excerpt, KJV)

"Joseph is a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough by a well; whose branches run over the wall. The archers have sorely grieved him, and shot at him, and hated him: but his bow abode in strength, and the arms of his hands were made strong by the hands of the mighty God of Jacob; (from thence is the shepherd, the stone of Israel:)" (Genesis 49:22-24, excerpt, KJV)

Finally, read the close of the Scripture: "And when Jacob had made an end of commanding his sons, he gathered up his feet into the bed, and yielded up the ghost, and was gathered unto his people." (Genesis 49:33, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, let us sit quietly today beside Jacob's bed and hear a father finish his weightiest words. For the words of warning, keep us from hiding; for the words of blessing, make us bold to receive them; and for that sentence which speaks of Shiloh, which speaks of Yourself, let us hear the hope within it. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Words at the Bedside, and a Warning to Three Sons (49:1-7)

First, hold fast to one phrase in the first sentence: "that which shall befall you in the last days." In the original it is 'acharit hayyamim (אַחֲרִית הַיָּמִים, H319 + H3117), literally "the latter part of the days, the end of the days" [1]. This phrase carries great weight in the Old Testament; when the prophets later spoke of the last days and of the kingdom of the Messiah, this is the phrase they used. By opening with this phrase, Jacob lifted a family's bedside matter to the height of salvation history from the very first sentence: what he spoke of was not the household affairs of twelve sons, but what God would accomplish in the latter part of history. A father's last words were, from the beginning, a prophet's prophecy.

But notice the distinctive way this prophecy is spoken: it does not float in the sky; it lands, sentence by sentence, on each son's temperament and past. Jacob's method was first to see what kind of person this man had been all his life, and then to say what kind of tribe this tribe would become in the future. Character is the seed of destiny, and yesterday's choices are tomorrow's map. This is exactly the most piercing place in this chapter: the father's words are accurate not because he could tell fortunes, but because he knew his sons too well — he knew which son's inner water was still boiling, which son's anger had never yet been restrained, and which son had already learned, through suffering, to turn back.

The first to be named is the eldest son, Reuben.

"Reuben, thou art my firstborn, my might, and the beginning of my strength, the excellency of dignity, and the excellency of power: unstable as water, thou shalt not excel; because thou wentest up to thy father's bed; then defiledst thou it: he went up to my couch." (Genesis 49:3-4, KJV)

The father first counts out, one by one, what he should have had: firstborn, born when his father's strength was strong, excellency of dignity, excellency of power. The birthright was no small thing: it meant a double portion of the inheritance, the leadership of the family, a priest-like standing. Reuben should have stood at the front of his eleven brothers. But the poem's next line turns sharply: "unstable as water." What does boiling water look like? Tumbling, unsettled, uncontrolled, spilling over at the first surge. With these few words Jacob measured his eldest son's whole life: he had strength, but no restraint; he had standing, but no steadiness; whenever emotion and desire rose, every boundary was swept away.

Why say this? The Scripture itself gives the ground: "because thou wentest up to thy father's bed." Genesis 35:22 records that Reuben lay with Bilhah, his father's concubine — this manuscript states this fact only as the Scripture states it, without embellishment and without speculating on details. The Bible records this matter in a single sentence, and the father, settling this account at his deathbed, also uses a single sentence; but that one sentence decided a tribe's place for centuries: the tribe of Reuben afterward never produced a king, a prophet, or a judge in Israel, and the birthright passed to the two sons of Joseph (see 1 Chronicles 5:1-2). Brothers and sisters, this passage must be preached slowly: between one's portion and loss of control there is only a boundary line. A person may have the best starting point, the thickest resources, the most honored title; but if the water inside keeps boiling and is never restrained, the title can be lost. The terror of sin lies not only in the one thing it accomplished, but in how it quietly rewrites the map of a person and a family for generations to come. Jacob did not cover up for his eldest son, nor did he curse him to the ground — he simply laid out the fact and stated the consequence clearly. Even a dying father's rebuke is restrained, because he knew that once words are spoken, they become a history his descendants can hear.

Next come Simeon and Levi, and the father speaks of the two of them together:

"Simeon and Levi are brethren; instruments of cruelty are in their habitations. O my soul, come not thou into their secret; unto their assembly, mine honour, be not thou united: for in their anger they slew a man, and in their selfwill they digged down a wall. Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce; and their wrath, for it was cruel: I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel." (Genesis 49:5-7, KJV)

The background of these lines is the matter of the city of Shechem in Genesis 34: their sister Dinah had been dishonored, and the two of them, while all the men of the city were sore from circumcision, entered the city, killed all the males, and plundered the whole city. The text here does not retell the plot; it only characterizes two things: their "anger" and their "wrath." Please note the object of the father's curse — he cursed not these two sons themselves, but their anger: "Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce." Words spoken and decisions made in anger still had to be brought to the bedside for settlement decades later, because the price of anger is never paid by anger itself; it is paid by sons and grandsons.

The land the tribe of Simeon later received lay inside the territory of Judah, and it was gradually absorbed, almost fading out of history; the tribe of Levi received no single block of inheritance, but was scattered among forty-eight cities. The same word "scatter" eventually carried a different flavor for the two tribes: at the time of the Exodus, when Moses called out, "Who is on the LORD's side?", the Levites stood forth (Exodus 32), and that sentence of "scattering" was turned in God's hand into being set apart as holy, scattered among the cities to teach the law. This manuscript makes only this point here: a sentence of judgment, in God's hand, does not have only one way to unfold; human anger invites a curse, yet God's grace can still leave a place, inside the sentence of the curse, for the one who turns back. This is the undertone of the whole chapter, and when we come to Judah we will return to it.

By this point the atmosphere at the bedside is heavy. Three sons: one has lost the birthright, two have had their anger cursed. If this poem stopped here, it would be a written verdict. But the father's next breath turns to the fourth son — Judah. The summit of the whole chapter begins with Judah.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Judah's Lion and Sceptre — Until Shiloh Comes (49:8-21)

"Judah, thou art he whom thy brethren shall praise: thy hand shall be in the neck of thine enemies; thy father's children shall bow down before thee." (Genesis 49:8, KJV)

Listen to the sound of this first line. The name Judah itself is associated by wordplay with "praise" — when his mother Leah bore him, she said, "Now will I praise the LORD." Now the father turns that name around: it is not only that Judah praises God; it is that his brethren shall praise Judah. From being praised, to being bowed down to, these three lines rise one level above another: his brethren praise him, his hand is in the neck of his enemies, and his father's children bow down before him. Judah is to stand at the front of his brothers. But please do not understand this as Jacob's favoritism. Judah did not reach this place in a single leap: in his early years he sold his brother Joseph, and that was the stain of his life; but by Genesis chapter 44, standing before that Egyptian official, he stepped forward and said he was willing to remain as a slave in Benjamin's place — the man who had sold his brother later became the man who stood in his brother's place. This blessing at the father's bedside recognizes precisely the man after that turning back. Grace is not the erasure of the past; grace is that, after the sentences of judgment have been spoken, God is still willing to leave a foremost place for the one who turns back.

Then comes the most famous image in the whole chapter: "Judah is a lion's whelp: from the prey, my son, thou art gone up: he stooped down, he couched as a lion, and as an old lion; who shall rouse him up?" (Genesis 49:9, KJV) Here the poem does not argue; it only sets down pictures: a lion's whelp, a lion, an old lion — a tribe's might and dignity all pressed into these three images. The lion is the king of beasts, and a lion couching is more fearsome than a lion roaring, because its strength is gathered, steady, and in no need of proof. Revelation 5:5 looks back to this line and calls the Lord Jesus "the Lion of the tribe of Juda, the Root of David." From one line of poetry at a bedside to the throne in Revelation there lie more than a thousand years: David became king and fulfilled the lion's kingship; David's descendant Jesus came and fulfilled the lion's final outcome. Typology should go this far and no further; there is no need to force every detail into correspondence — Judah's lion speaks first of the royal destiny of the tribe of Judah, and only then, at the far end of history, points to that true Son of David.

Then comes one of the weightiest verses in the whole Old Testament:

"The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be." (Genesis 49:10, KJV)

First look at two words. "Sceptre," in the original shevet (שֵׁבֶט, H7626), whose basic sense is a rod or staff, also the sceptre in a king's hand, and which can also denote a tribe [2]. A staff, a stick for driving sheep in a shepherd's hand, becomes in a king's hand the sign of authority. The staff that "shall not depart from between his feet" is in the original mechoqeq (מְחֹקֵק, H2710), derived from a verb meaning "to inscribe, to decree," denoting the ruler, the lawgiver; his staff is the lawgiver's staff [3]. The two staffs stand in parallel, and the poem's meaning advances layer upon layer: kingship is nowhere else but in Judah; the authority of lawgiving and rule does not depart from Judah's descendants. In Numbers 24:17 Balaam saw "there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel"; Ezekiel 21:27 speaks of the one "whose right it is" coming; Hebrews 7:14 states plainly that "our Lord sprang out of Juda" — this line runs from Jacob's bedside all the way to the New Testament.

The whole problem rests on one word: "Shiloh." In the original shiloh (שִׁילֹה, H7886) [4]. This manuscript must be honest: the reading of this word has no settled conclusion in scholarship to this day; three main views stand side by side, and this sermon makes no final ruling. First, it is read as the One who is to come — "until Shiloh himself comes," that is, the One who brings peace and owns the kingship comes; many interpreters across the ages and the church's tradition have received it this way, and the hope of this chapter arises from here. Second, a re-division of the phrase along the lines of "until tribute is brought to him": the word is re-read as "tribute to him," that is, the peoples come and submit to him bearing tribute, which corresponds exactly to the second half of the verse, "unto him shall the gathering of the people be"; the Greek of Ezekiel 21:27 and the handling of some ancient versions lie close to this direction. Third, it is connected with the place-name "Shiloh," read as "until (the descendants) come to Shiloh" — and Shiloh did indeed later become the place of Israel's tabernacle. Among the three views the versions also make their own choices: the Septuagint (LXX) renders this place differently from the Masoretic text, some renderings having the sense of "until that which is reserved for him comes"; the KJV and the Chinese Union Version keep the transliteration "Shiloh / 细罗," while the NIV leans toward "until he comes to whom it belongs." Fact and exposition must be kept separate: that the word's meaning is unsettled is a fact; but whichever view is taken, the historical direction of this verse is equally clear — kingship belongs to Judah, until One who is worthy of that kingship comes, and the peoples gather to Him. That the church has heard the footsteps of the Messiah in this verse for two thousand years is not because it seized upon a settled solution for one word, but because the whole weight of the verse leans in that direction: the sceptre shall not depart, the lawgiver shall not depart, and the peoples shall gather. This is not an ending that one tribe can bear; it is a hope pointing to a greater King. The title of this sermon is taken from here: *Until Shiloh Comes* — Jacob's blessing gazed from the bedside all the way to the end of history.

Judah's passage closes with pictures of abundance: he binds his foal to the vine, washes his garments in wine; his eyes are red with wine and his teeth white with milk (Genesis 49:11-12, paraphrased). That is a picture of plenty in a time of peace: grapes so many that a foal can be tied to them, wine so much that clothes can be washed in it. Preaching poetry, it is enough to pause here — the final outcome of kingship is not war, but abundance and rest.

After Judah, the father's words suddenly become short. The six sons in the middle each receive only one or two lines of poetry. Preaching must not pad this section with verse-by-verse exposition; it is enough to select what has a basis and to make clear the destiny of each tribe behind its line of poetry.

Zebulun: "Zebulun shall dwell at the haven of the sea; and he shall be for an haven of ships; and his border shall be unto Zidon." (Genesis 49:13, KJV) One sentence fixed this tribe's place: by the sea, in commerce, extending outward. The word for the tribe of Dan is more winding: "Dan shall be a serpent by the way, an adder in the path, that biteth the horse heels, so that his rider shall fall backward." The father immediately added the most unexpected interjection in the whole chapter: "I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD." (Genesis 49:17-18, KJV) Speaking of Dan, Jacob suddenly looked first toward God's salvation — as if he saw danger on this tribe's road and first entrusted the hope to God. Gad's word is a tug of war: "Gad, a troop shall overcome him: but he shall overcome at the last." (Genesis 49:19, KJV) The one overcome by a troop overcomes at the last — this is the resilience of a border tribe. Asher receives only one line of abundance: "Out of Asher his bread shall be fat, and he shall yield royal dainties." (Genesis 49:20, KJV) Naphtali is a light picture: "Naphtali is a hind let loose: he giveth goodly words." (Genesis 49:21, KJV) Issachar's word is the most thought-provoking: "Issachar is a strong ass couching down between two burdens: and he saw that rest was good, and the land that it was pleasant; and bowed his shoulder to bear, and became a servant unto tribute." (Genesis 49:14-15, KJV) A tribe that prized ease and was willing to bow its shoulder and serve under tribute — the poem affirms his strength and also carries a note of regret for his love of comfort; the proportion is all there, and nothing more needs to be added.

Read together, these six lines are precisely the wisdom of poetry: not every son needs a whole sermon. Some men's lives are fully told in one accurate sentence. A father's words became a map for his descendants: some sons received a whole panorama, some only a road marker; the marker is small, but when the tribe arrived there centuries later and looked, it matched exactly. Preachers should also learn this proportion: preach as much as the Scripture gives; where the Scripture is silent, do not speak in its place.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Joseph's Long Blessing, Benjamin's Morning Wolf, and the Old Man's Burial (49:22-33)

The longest blessing in the whole chapter is given to Joseph. In length, Joseph's passage is second only to Judah's, and its feeling is the thickest. The image with which the father opens is the most beautiful in the whole chapter:

"Joseph is a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough by a well; whose branches run over the wall." (Genesis 49:22, KJV)

"Fruitful," in the original porat (פּוֹרָת, H6509), bearing fruit abundantly, flourishing and productive [5]. Joseph is a tree planted by a well: its roots have water, its branches run over the wall. A wall is a boundary — the high walls of Egypt, the high walls of prison, the high walls of a foreign land — yet his branches ran over the wall: his life overflowed the range others had drawn for him. The father then replays his life: "The archers have sorely grieved him, and shot at him, and hated him: but his bow abode in strength, and the arms of his hands were made strong by the hands of the mighty God of Jacob; (from thence is the shepherd, the stone of Israel:)" (Genesis 49:23-24, KJV) Who are the archers? The brothers who sold him, the mistress who falsely accused him, the butler who forgot him. The arrows flew for so many years, and this bow did not break. Why? The poem itself gives the answer: it is not that Joseph's bow was good; it is that the hand steadying him was firm — the mighty God of Jacob, the Shepherd and the Stone of Israel. Joseph's resilience was never Joseph's resilience alone.

Then the blessing surges up like spring water, one level higher than the next: the blessings of his father prevailed above the blessings of his ancestors, unto the utmost bound of the everlasting hills (Genesis 49:26, paraphrased). Here the text itself pushes the source of the blessing upward step by step: from the blessings of heaven above and of the deep that lieth under, up to the utmost bound of the everlasting hills — "everlasting" here renders 'olam (עוֹלָם, H5769) [6]; hills outlast men, and blessing outlasts the hills. The father poured out upon Joseph's head, all at once, the blessings received through the generations, because this son was the savior of the whole family in the famine. The rhythm of the poem is fastest here, blessing stacked upon blessing, as if the father feared there was not enough time and wanted to press all his remaining words into this son who was lost and found again.

The last is Benjamin: "Benjamin shall ravin as a wolf: in the morning he shall devour the prey, and at night he shall divide the spoil." (Genesis 49:27, KJV) A morning wolf closes the poem, and the picture is fierce. This youngest son's tribe was afterward known for its valor. The father did not write him soft, nor did he write him wicked — a wolf is a wolf, valor is valor — placed at the end of the poem like a final heavy accent.

When the twelve sons had all been spoken of, the Scripture gives one summary sentence: "All these are the twelve tribes of Israel: and this is it that their father spake unto them and blessed them; every one according to his blessing he blessed them." (Genesis 49:28, KJV) "Every one according to his blessing" — please fix this sentence firmly in mind. Jacob's blessings were not wholesale; they were tailored: the one who needed warning received warning, the one whose anger needed cursing had his anger cursed, the one to whom the sceptre was to be handed received it. A father's words can become a map precisely because they are honest. A father who only says pleasant things cannot give his children a map; a father who only says heavy things cannot either. Jacob had both, and that is what a true father is.

Then the old man turned his words from his children to himself, and gave only one instruction: his burial. "Bury me with my fathers in the cave that is in the field of Ephron the Hittite, in the cave that is in the field of Machpelah... There they buried Abraham and Sarah his wife; there they buried Isaac and Rebekah his wife; and there I buried Leah." (Genesis 49:29-31, excerpt, KJV) He counted out, one by one, the people in that cave: grandfather, grandmother, father, mother, and Leah. In Egypt, Pharaoh could have given him a royal funeral; he did not want it. He wanted to return to Canaan, to lie in the soil of the promised land, among his ancestors. This is not nostalgia; this is faith's last lesson: most of his life had been spent in flight and sojourning, yet at his death he was perfectly clear — my belonging is not in Egypt's splendor, but in the land God promised. When he had finished, "And when Jacob had made an end of commanding his sons, he gathered up his feet into the bed, and yielded up the ghost, and was gathered unto his people." (Genesis 49:33, KJV) "He gathered up his feet into the bed" — what a peaceful way to die. The words were finished, the entrusting was finished, and he gathered up his own feet and departed in peace. The man who had wrestled with God through a whole night went in the end like this: without struggle, because everything that needed to be entrusted had been entrusted, and everything that needed to be hoped for had been seen — including that one sentence that gazed centuries ahead: "until Shiloh come."

For the application, we take hold of only two kinds of people, and take hold tightly.

The first kind: those who are parents and those who lead others. Jacob's bedside words set a rule for our speaking: toward children and toward those who come after us, speak truth, and also speak blessing; let the warning be specific down to that particular past event, and let the blessing be specific down to that particular name. Do not only praise all your life, and do not only scold all your life. The words you speak today are becoming the map of your children's tomorrow — at the crossroads of life, what they will recall is very likely one sentence of yours. Decide now what you hope that sentence will be, and say it now; do not wait until the bedside.

The second kind: those who feel they have been wounded by a father's words, or fixed and defined by them. What Reuben heard was a rebuke; what Simeon and Levi heard was a curse upon their anger — but please note that the tribe of Levi later turned a page in God's hand. A father's words are a map, not a railway track; a map points out a direction, but if the one walking is willing to turn back, God can open another road inside a sentence of judgment. More importantly, the words of the Father that we hear in Christ stand higher than the words of any earthly father: the sceptre was finally placed in Shiloh's hand, and the peoples gather to him — you and I are among those gathering peoples. An earthly definition is not the final outcome; the heavenly blessing is.

The third kind: those standing at a crossroads in life who feel it is "too late." When Jacob spoke these words he was one hundred and forty-seven years old, his eyes dim, lying on a bed, without even the strength to rise; yet it was at exactly this moment that he spoke the most far-seeing words in the whole chapter — he saw the King centuries later, he saw the day when the peoples would gather. In God's hand there is no such thing as "too late": Moses was eighty when he left Egypt, Caleb at eighty-five still said, "I am as strong this day," and Jacob finished the weightiest words of his life just before his last breath. If you feel you are old, that your chances have passed, that you can no longer manage your children's affairs, that your own situation is already fixed — please hear the lesson of Jacob's bedside: as long as there is one breath left, you can still bless; as long as you are willing to open your mouth, the words of old age can still become a map for descendants. Do not use "too late" to decline what God wants you to do today: the blessing that should be spoken to your children, speak it today; the wrong that should be admitted to someone, admit it today; the hope that should be entrusted, entrust it today. Shiloh has already come; the task of our generation is not to lie down and wait for the end, but, like Jacob, before gathering up our feet, to speak the words rightly, bless fully, and hand people over into God's hands. Those who finish the last stretch of the road this way die in peace.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we sat beside Jacob's bed and heard a dying poem to its end. Reuben's water was still boiling, and the birthright was lost; the anger of Simeon and Levi was cursed, and its price passed to their descendants; Judah received the lion and the sceptre, and the blessing gazed all the way to Shiloh's coming and the gathering of the peoples; Joseph's branches ran over the wall, and the blessing reached to the everlasting hills; Benjamin closed the poem as a morning wolf, and the old man gathered up his feet into the bed and was gathered to his people. A father's words became a map for his descendants — there are warnings, there are blessings, and grace always leaves a place inside the sentences of judgment.

Let us pray together: Heavenly Father, thank You for reminding us through Jacob's bedside words: character is the seed of destiny, and the choices we make today are drawing the map of our children's tomorrow. Restrain the boiling water within us, stop our fierce anger, and teach us to speak honestly and to bless specifically. Thank You most of all that the sceptre did not fall empty — Shiloh has already come, the Lion of the tribe of Judah has already overcome, and the peoples are gathering to Him. Let us also walk on Your map all our lives, until we see Your face. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[4] shiloh: Hebrew שִׁילֹה (H7886), Genesis 49:10, "until Shiloh come." Three scholarly views stand side by side, and this manuscript makes no final ruling: (1) "until Shiloh himself comes," referring to the One who owns the kingship and brings peace; (2) a re-division of the phrase as "until tribute is brought to him / until he comes to whom it belongs," corresponding to the second half of the verse, "unto him shall the gathering of the people be"; see Ezekiel 21:27; (3) a connection with the place-name "Shiloh," read as "until (coming) to Shiloh." The differences among the versions are noted as they stand: the LXX renders this place differently from the Masoretic text, some renderings having the sense of "until that which is reserved for him comes"; the KJV and the Chinese Union Version keep the transliteration Shiloh / 细罗, while the NIV leans toward "until he comes to whom it belongs." The meanings are discussed according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the whole weight of the verse leans toward the messianic hope" is homiletical exposition, noted separately from the fact that the word's meaning is unsettled.

[5] porat: Hebrew פּוֹרָת (H6509), bearing fruit, flourishing and productive, derived from the verb parah (to bear fruit, to flourish); Genesis 49:22, "Joseph is a fruitful bough"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[6] 'olam and the structure of the blessing: 'olam, Hebrew עוֹלָם (H5769), everlasting, perpetual; in Genesis 49:26, "unto the utmost bound of the everlasting hills," the source of the blessing is pushed upward from "the blessings of heaven above and of the deep that lieth under" all the way to the everlasting hills. Note: Deuteronomy 33:15, Moses' blessing on Joseph, uses a different word, qedem (קֶדֶם, H6924, ancient, aforetime), in "the chief things of the ancient mountains"; the two words are close in imagery but are not the same word, and are distinguished here. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript uses this to explain the structure of 49:22-26, in which the blessing rises "one level higher than the next, and outlasts the hills"; this is structural exposition.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be." (Genesis 49:10, KJV) "Joseph is a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough by a well; whose branches run over the wall." (Genesis 49:22, KJV)

Jacob's dying words became a map for his descendants. On the map there are warnings: Reuben's water boiled up, and he lost the birthright; the anger of Simeon and Levi was cursed, reminding us that unrestrained emotion passes its cost on to our children. On the map there are also blessings, and the blessings are longer than the warnings: Judah's sceptre points all the way to the coming of Shiloh, Joseph's branches run over the wall, and the blessing reaches to the everlasting hills. What is the heaviest sentence you have ever heard — a father's rebuke, or someone's definition of you? Remember: a map is not a railway track. In God's hand, Levi's "scattering" later became being set apart as holy, and Judah, who once sold his brother, later stood in his brother's place. More than that, the One who holds the sceptre has come, the peoples gather to Him, and you are among them. Hand your life back to this greater map today: let the boiling water settle, and receive the blessing sentence by sentence. A father's words pass away; God's word endures forever.

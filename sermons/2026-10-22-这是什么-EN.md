## Summary (about 100 words)

Exactly one month out of Egypt, the people in the wilderness of Sin longed for the flesh pots of Egypt, preferring to be slaves with full stomachs. God sent quail and manna, teaching them in the very act of provision to know Him: gather daily according to need; much gathered no surplus, little suffered no lack; what greed kept overnight bred worms; the Sabbath was grounded before the Ten Commandments were given. One jar of manna was stored for later generations, showing that grace has discipline, and that gathering one day's bread one day at a time is precisely the training of faith.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter what we heard was still singing. The Red Sea was divided, Pharaoh's chariots sank to the bottom of the sea, and Moses and all the people stood on the shore and sang: The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation. Miriam took a timbrel, and all the women followed her out with dancing. What an exalted chapter that was. But the Bible never flatters us when it turns the page. As soon as Exodus chapter 16 opens, the singing breaks off, and in its place come the growling of stomachs and the complaining of the whole camp. How much time passed between the singing and the complaining? The Scripture remembers it clearly: one month.

Let us work out the days carefully. The Israelites set out from Rameses on the fifteenth day of the first month when they left Egypt; by chapter 16, on the fifteenth day of the second month, they had come into the wilderness of Sin, which is between Elim and Sinai. From the fifteenth day of the first month to the fifteenth day of the second month — no more, no less — exactly one month. One month earlier they were still slaves; one month later they were already a delivered people, who had seen the ten plagues with their own eyes, walked through the Red Sea with their own feet, and drunk the sweetened bitter water with their own mouths. Every piece of grace was still warm, yet human memory cannot stay warmer than the stomach. As soon as the stomach was empty, all past grace went cold.

In this chapter there is no foreign enemy and no pursuing army; the only thing that tests the people is one question: what shall we eat? And this is also the real battlefield of faith for most of us. It is not whether we believe at the point of life and death, but whether we still believe in that Provider when the days are ordinary and the rice bowl is a worry. Today we will stand with the people in the wilderness, hear them utter a complaint, and watch how God answers; we will watch how a food that fell from heaven, whose name no one could say, fed a whole discipline of grace, day by day, into the body of this nation. That discipline has not been abolished to this day. When the Lord Jesus taught us to pray, "Give us this day our daily bread," the root of that prayer is sunk in this chapter.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And the whole congregation of the children of Israel murmured against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness: And the children of Israel said unto them, Would to God we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to the full; for ye have brought us forth into this wilderness, to kill this whole assembly with hunger." (Exodus 16:2-3, excerpt, KJV)

"Then said the LORD unto Moses, Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you; and the people shall go out and gather a certain rate every day, that I may prove them, whether they will walk in my law, or no." (Exodus 16:4, excerpt, KJV)

"At even, then ye shall know that the LORD hath brought you out from the land of Egypt: And in the morning, then ye shall see the glory of the LORD; for that he heareth your murmurings against the LORD." (Exodus 16:6-7, excerpt, KJV)

"And when they did mete it with an omer, he that gathered much had nothing over, and he that gathered little had no lack; they gathered every man according to his eating." (Exodus 16:18, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, we too are people who easily forget grace after walking for a month, people who want to turn back as soon as hunger comes. Through this chapter, teach us to know You afresh as the Provider, and teach us also to learn the rules of Your provision: do not grasp for more, do not hoard in secret, receive in peace, and rest in peace. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: On the Full-Month Day, Thoughts of the Flesh Pots — How Memory Beautifies Slavery (16:1-3)

First, set the scene straight. The people did not complain in Egypt, nor did they complain beside the Red Sea; they complained in the wilderness of Sin after they had already been delivered and had walked for a full month. The place name Sin (Hebrew סִין) merely resembles the English word "sin" in spelling; in the original it has no etymological connection with sin at all, and we will not make an article of it — only remember one point: this was a wilderness with no grain, no water market, and no prospect, where a person standing there and looking around saw nothing but sand and soil. What faith finds hardest to endure is never the one night of great wind and waves, but the ordinary days after the storm, when day after day no way out can be seen. The Red Sea could be divided in one night; the wilderness must be walked step by step to the end.

We must listen to the people's words word by word, without whitewashing them: "Would to God we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to the full." There are three alarming layers in this sentence.

The first layer is that they would rather die in Egypt than live in the wilderness. What they said was not "Would to God we could go back to Egypt," but "Would to God we had died in Egypt earlier." That is to say, in the moment of hunger, looking back, even dying in the land of slavery seemed better to them than living on the road of freedom. When a person is pressed by present lack to a certain point, he writes off all the bitterness of the past in one stroke and keeps only his hatred of the present. Brothers and sisters, this is not a disease unique to the Israelites; this is human nature. How many people walk out of a bondage, gain freedom not long ago, and as soon as they meet difficulty say: if only I had known, I would never have come out. The whip of those days is forgotten, the tears of those days are forgotten; all that is remembered is that at least there was a steady meal in those days.

The second layer is that beautified flesh pot. "When we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to the full" — did they really have such days in Egypt? Let us go back to Exodus chapter 1 and check the account: in Egypt they were slaves who made bricks, overseers pressed them every day, heavy labor bore down on them, and they were not even allowed to let their baby boys live. In chapter 5 Pharaoh would not even give straw, yet the number of bricks was not reduced by one, and their groaning filled the land. In such days, could slaves sit around flesh pots and eat to the full? Later, in Numbers chapter 11, their list of longings for Egypt grew even longer: fish, cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions, and garlic, counted out item by item, as if Egypt were a canteen that fed people well and filled them up. This is how memory works: it remembers the menu of the land of slavery in perfect detail, and forgets the whip of the land of slavery completely. Once a person wants to turn back, he first paints the past with a coat of grease. The nostalgia for leeks, onions, and flesh pots looks like nostalgia on the surface, but in its bones it is a trace of servility: for a person who has been a slave a long time, the stomach adapts to bondage before the heart does — the stomach remembers the master's pot, while the heart forgets the master's whip. Delivered for one month, the appetite of a slave had not yet been changed. This is the first mirror this chapter holds up to us.

The third layer is the charge they laid against Moses: "Ye have brought us forth into this wilderness, to kill this whole assembly with hunger." The great event of the exodus, in their mouths, became a premeditated killing by starvation. The God who led them out became a God who meant to harm them. When people complain deeply enough, they will always rewrite the story: they call deliverance a trap, and leading abandonment. How are we any different? In smooth times we say God is leading; in hard times we say God has abandoned us; when we are full we say God's grace, and when we lack we say God has treated us unfairly. The same God, the same road — only because the stomach's fullness or emptiness differs, the verdict is turned upside down. The people's problem was never that they had not experienced God; it was that the God they had experienced could not stand against their present feelings. When feeling reigns as king, memory usurps the throne, and grace is put on trial again.

Note also one detail: on the surface, the object of their complaining was Moses and Aaron — the Scripture says they murmured against Moses and Aaron — but God's answer was: your murmurings are not against us, but against the LORD. Leaders stand in the middle, often taking the people's complaints in God's place, and also bringing the people's complaints before God. Moses did not curse back, nor throw down the load; he carried the words up exactly as they were, and brought God's words down exactly as they were. This is a lesson for those who serve people: when complaints come, do not rush to defend yourself, and do not join in complaining either; first bring the people before the Lord who truly hears. Because God heard — from beginning to end in this chapter, God's ears were at work before the people's mouths. They thought they were talking to themselves in the wilderness, but God said: I have heard the murmurings you uttered, not one word missing.

And what did God do after He heard? He did not send fire, He did not send plague; He sent bread. People complained, and God sent food; people said they would starve to death, and God said there will be flesh in the evening and bread in the morning until you are full. God's way of dealing with complaining is beyond everyone's expectation: He does not begin with a great lecture, but first serves a meal. He knew that the problem behind this crowd was not only an empty stomach, but a heart that did not know Him. So the provision that immediately follows carries a purpose in every item. In the next part we will see it: knowledge is carried inside provision, and a lesson is hidden inside the food.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: At Twilight You Shall Know the LORD, in the Morning You Shall See His Glory — Knowledge and Fairness Carried Inside Provision (16:4-21)

God's answer came in two steps, one at dusk and one at dawn, with a night between them, splitting one lesson into two halves, teaching it slowly.

First look at God's words to Moses: "Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you." Bread raining down from heaven — this expression appears here for the first time in the Bible [1]. For bread that grows from the ground, people know the seed, know the seasons, and know their own sweat; for bread that comes down from heaven, people know nothing at all — they can only receive, and only give thanks. With His first sentence God made the sovereignty of provision clear: this bread is not produced by the wilderness, nor conjured by Moses; it is "I will rain it down." The source of provision is in heaven, not in human hands. The people thought the problem was that the wilderness had no bread; God let them see that the problem was never the barrenness of the wilderness, but that they did not know the God who can open a canteen in the wilderness.

Immediately after, God Himself named the purpose of this provision, in verse 6: "At even, then ye shall know that the LORD hath brought you out from the land of Egypt." And in God's own words in verse 12: "At even ye shall eat flesh, and in the morning ye shall be filled with bread; and ye shall know that I am the LORD your God." Brothers and sisters, please circle this word "know." God rained down bread not only to fill stomachs, but even more to make people know Him. A filled stomach is a matter of a moment; knowing God is a matter of a lifetime. If the people were merely full and the complaining stopped, God's purpose would only be half achieved; what God wanted was that, while they ate, they would know: the One who brought us out of Egypt has not thrown us into the wilderness to die; He is the LORD who feeds us day by day. So the provision in this chapter carries knowledge inside it; every meal is a lesson in knowing God. The quail came flying in at dusk and covered the camp — that was the flesh they could not stop longing for, and God gave it, in overwhelming measure; the manna came down with the dew at dawn — that was a new bread they had never seen, and God gave that too, without ceasing, day after day. One old and one new, one evening and one morning: God outmatched the nostalgic flesh pot — the pot in Egypt had to be exchanged for slavery, while the flesh in the wilderness flew in freely; fullness in Egypt ended in chains, while fullness in the wilderness came from a gracious hand.

Moses also announced in advance: in the morning you shall see the glory of the LORD, for He hears your murmurings. Then the Scripture writes that while Moses was speaking, Aaron spoke to the whole congregation, and they looked toward the wilderness, and behold, the glory of the LORD appeared in the cloud. The people uttered their complaints toward the wilderness, and God's glory also appeared from within the cloud on the wilderness side. The complainers thought the wilderness was empty of everything; God let them see with their own eyes: the wilderness is not empty; the wilderness holds glory in the pillar of cloud, and a Host who can lay a table at any time. Many times we are like the people: we stare only at the lack and cannot see the Presence. God did not first rebuke their eyes; He first set His glory before their eyes, and then set the food beside their feet. Knowing God always begins with His self-revelation — with His taking the initiative to show Himself.

The next morning, after the dew had gone up, on the surface of the wilderness there lay a small round substance, as fine as frost on the ground. The people asked one another: "What is it?" In the original, man hu — a question blurted out [4]. Moses answered: this is the bread which the LORD has given you to eat. People usually name a food by what it is; this food's name, however, was taken from "we do not know what it is at all." The name manna is itself a question mark, reminding everyone who eats it: you are alive not because you control everything, but because a God whom you cannot fathom places undeserved and incomprehensible grace at your doorstep every day. They ate it for forty years and still called it "What is it?" If grace is eaten until it becomes routine, until one no longer even asks, that is the real danger.

Next came the rules. God commanded: gather according to each man's eating, one omer for each person, according to the number of persons in each tent [2][3]. The omer was a measure; one omer is roughly about 2.2 liters, which is the commonly used estimate. The rules look simple, but inside them is hidden a complete discipline of grace. Let us look at them one by one.

First: according to mouths and persons, take each according to need, and do not take extra. The gatherer was to count the people in the tent and gather that many portions: as many mouths, that many portions. This means the unit of provision is not the individual's appetite, but the whole family's need; the hand that gathers must first think of the people in the same tent. When grace comes to a person, it is never so that he alone can stuff himself, but so that he can become a channel of supply for his household. It is the same today: with the time, gifts, and income God gives you, the first question is not how much more I can demand, but whether the people in my tent have enough.

Second: he that gathered much had nothing over, and he that gathered little had no lack. The people went out and gathered, some more and some less; when they came back and measured it with an omer, the one who gathered much had no surplus, and the one who gathered little had no shortage. This sentence was later quoted word for word by Paul in 2 Corinthians chapter 8, teaching the fairness between churches: your abundance supplies their lack. So this is no idle bookkeeper's remark; this is fairness established by God's own hand. This fairness is not cutting everyone into blocks of exactly the same size; it is each person being filled according to his own eating. Fairness is not equal division; fairness is each according to need, no one hungry, and no one hoarding. In God's kingdom, the measure is set by Him and the fullness is given by Him; all people can do is gather as instructed, eat with joy, and not compare whose basket holds more. Comparison is the most unnecessary burden in the wilderness: your omer measures your eating, my omer measures my eating, and when measured, both are exactly enough.

Third: do not keep it overnight in private hoarding. Yet Moses had said it plainly: let no man leave any of it until the morning. But some did not listen; they left some of it until the morning, and it bred worms and stank. Moses was angry with them. Please note: the worms and the stink were not an extra magic trick of punishment sent down by God; they were the natural outcome of greed itself, and God merely let it show itself for what it was. Yesterday's grace, stored into today in order to possess it, rots first in the receiver's heart. It was bread; kept in the wrong way it became worms. Hidden here is a whole study of the corruption of greed: why do people insist on keeping extra? In the end, because they do not believe there will be more tomorrow. Because they do not believe, they clutch; the more they clutch, the more afraid they become; the more afraid, the more they clutch. God's provision, however, is deliberately issued by the day, precisely to dismantle this unbelief day by day. If you snatch tomorrow's portion into your hands today, far from making you secure, it makes you guard all night a jar that is already stinking. What can be kept is only the portion gathered in obedience; what greed stores rots first. The people learned this lesson on the very first day, and learned it with a smell filling the whole camp that no one could forget.

Fourth, and this is the undertone of this whole passage: all of this was a test. Before raining down the bread, God had already told Moses that the people should go out and gather a certain rate every day, "that I may prove them, whether they will walk in my law, or no" [2]. This word "prove," nisah in the original, means to test, to try, to verify. It is not that God did not know their hearts; God was using the small daily matter of gathering bread to let their own hearts show themselves, and also to let them, in small obedience repeated day after day, train their faith into being. Faith is not trained into being by one shout beside the Red Sea; faith grows, inch by inch, in the small matters of bending down each morning to gather, stopping when it is enough, and sleeping in peace at night. God hides the greatest lessons inside the smallest routines. Whether a person can be trusted by God with great things is seen first in whether, in the small matter of daily bread, he can gather according to God's rules and stop at God's limit.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: A Double Portion on the Sixth Day, Rest on the Seventh; One Jar Stored, the Beginning of Forty Years (16:22-36)

On the sixth day, things changed. The people went out and gathered twice as much, two omers for each person. All the rulers came and told Moses, as if something had gone wrong. But Moses said this was exactly what the LORD had spoken: tomorrow is the rest of the holy sabbath unto the LORD [5]. Bake what you will bake, and boil what you will boil, and what remains over lay up for yourselves until the morning. The people did so, and the manna laid up until the morning did not stink, nor were there any worms in it. On the seventh day, Moses said: eat what was laid up today, because today is a sabbath unto the LORD; today you will not find it in the field. Six days you shall gather it, but on the seventh day do not go out to gather, because the seventh day is the sabbath. As it turned out, some people still went out to gather on the seventh day and found nothing. The LORD said to Moses: How long do you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws? See, because the LORD has given you the sabbath, therefore He gives you on the sixth day the bread of two days; stay every man in his place, and let no man go out on the seventh day. So the people rested on the seventh day.

The weight of this passage cannot be overestimated. Please note the order: the institutional practice of the Sabbath was grounded in this chapter, in time before the giving of the Ten Commandments at Sinai. Only in Exodus chapter 20 was "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy" written into the Ten Commandments; but already in chapter 16, through the gathering and the not-gathering of manna, God let the people first learn rest with their bodies, and only then hear the commandment with their ears. This shows that in God's intention, rest is first a rhythm of receiving, and only afterward a written law. Rest is not a reward for finishing six days of work — a reward must be earned by man, but rest is purely received: the double portion on the sixth day was prepared by God, staying home on the seventh day was man's cooperation, and the preparation came first, the cooperation afterward. God first places the double portion in your house, and only then tells you to rest in peace; He never asks anyone to keep the sabbath on an empty stomach. Many people live rest in reverse: either they hoard desperately and dare to rest only once they have hoarded enough — that is resting on inventory, and as soon as the inventory thins, the heart panics; or they use rest as an excuse for laziness, failing to gather on the six days when they should gather, and hoping on the seventh day for a double portion to fall from heaven — that is using grace as a cover for laziness. The rest in this chapter is neither of these: gather diligently for six days, and stop candidly on the seventh. Being able to stop is because one believes the double portion on the sixth day came from God's hand; being able to gather diligently is because one knows God's provision is accomplished through man's bending down. Before His law, rest was already there, inside the daily life of grace.

As for those few people who went out on the seventh day and found nothing, we need not laugh at them; they are our miniature. The hands can stop, but the heart cannot stop; the camp gate is shut, but the legs still want to walk out. The greatest unbelief of our generation is often not doubting whether God can provide, but being unable to be quiet, always feeling that browsing a little longer, making one more trip, hoarding a little more, is what makes life secure. But in this chapter God with His own hand emptied the field on the seventh day: go out and it is in vain, search and you will not find. He uses "not finding" to treat our anxiety, forcing us to admit: if a person's security is built on ceaseless gathering, then the gathering itself is bondage. True rest is, on a day when nothing is gathered, still eating one's fill and sleeping well, because one knows the Provider does not rest, and His care does not stop working.

Then the Scripture lifts the camera up from the wilderness and looks toward decades later. The Israelites called the bread from heaven manna; its appearance was like coriander seed, white, and its taste like wafers made with honey. God also commanded Moses: fill an omer with manna and lay it up for your generations, so that they may see the bread with which God fed them in the wilderness. Moses told Aaron to take a pot, put an omer of manna in it, and lay it up before the LORD, before the Testimony — in the area of the ark — to be kept for later generations. This one jar of manna is the quietest and also the farthest-reaching action in the whole chapter. The daily bread was not allowed to stay overnight; only this one jar was to be kept across decades and centuries. Why? Because people forget too easily, and their children never saw it with their own eyes. The grace the fathers ate, the sons can only hear about; and heard long enough, a story becomes a legend. God insisted on leaving one jar of the real thing as a credential, so that later people could point at it and ask: What is it? And so that parents could answer again and again: this is the bread the LORD rained down from heaven to feed us when we had no food in the wilderness. What can be kept is not only bread; what can be kept is testimony. The bread greed stored bred worms; the jar stored in obedience entered the sanctuary. The difference is not in storing itself, but in for whom one stores and by whose word one stores. What is stored greedily for oneself rots in the tent; what is stored as testimony for later generations is placed before God. We too should ask today: what I leave to my children — is it hoarding that will stink, or a credential by which God's works can be pointed to and told?

At the end the Scripture says: the Israelites ate manna for forty years, until they came to an inhabited land, until they came to the border of the land of Canaan. Later, in Deuteronomy chapter 8, Moses looked back on these forty years and said it thoroughly: God humbled you and let you hunger, and fed you with manna which neither you nor your fathers knew, to make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of the LORD; in the wilderness for forty years He fed you with manna, to prove you and to humble you, and in the end to do you good. The bread of forty years began with the first handful of manna in this chapter. One bread gathered for forty years, one question — "What is it?" — asked for forty years. God's provision was never a one-time surprise; it was long, long faithfulness. What people grew tired of, God did not grow tired of; where people wanted variety, God used the same bread and the same faithfulness to feed a nation from slaves into children. The Psalms also echo this chapter: He commanded the skies above and opened the doors of heaven, and rained down manna upon them to eat, and gave them of the corn of heaven; man ate angels' food; He sent them meat to the full; they asked, and He brought quails, and satisfied them. Layer upon layer, the echoes of the Old Testament all point to the same fact: the Provider is more precious than the provision.

In the New Testament, the Lord Jesus Himself took up this chapter and taught from it in John chapter 6. The crowd asked Him for a sign, saying our fathers ate manna in the wilderness. Jesus answered: Moses did not give you that bread from heaven, but my Father gives you the true bread from heaven; I am the bread of life: he that comes to me shall never hunger, and he that believes on me shall never thirst; the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world. This is the New Testament reading of this chapter, and we should mark it honestly: the people in Exodus chapter 16 received, on that day, real bodily bread, which saved their lives; and the Lord Jesus, by means of this historical fact, pointed out that manna was in the end only a shadow, and that the true bread which comes down from heaven and gives people life forever is Himself. Those who ate manna still died; those who eat of the Lord, this bread, will live forever. A shadow is not false; a shadow is real — only the One to whom the shadow points with outstretched hand is the substance. When reading the Old Testament brings us here, our eyes should move from the bread to the Person: God fed Israel for forty years, and at last gave His only Son down as bread for the whole world. The discipline of grace is not abolished in the New Testament, but the center of grace is fully revealed: gathering one day's bread one day at a time is, in the end, drawing on Christ day by day.

Gather this chapter into application, and I leave you four sentences. The first is for those who love nostalgia: stop beautifying your Egypt. Count again the whips beside that flesh pot, and you will know that nostalgia often lies. Do not, because of a moment's lack, want to go back to the place from which God brought you out. The second is for those who love to hoard: pour out the bread kept overnight. What greed stores rots first; what unbelief holds suffers first; receive today's sufficient grace with joy today, and leave tomorrow's grace to tomorrow's Lord. The third is for those who cannot stop: learn the empty field of the seventh day. Exert yourself for six days, rest on the seventh; rest is not something you deserve only after you have hoarded enough — God first gives the double portion, and you, by faith, withdraw your hands. The fourth is for parents and leaders: keep one jar of manna. Tell the next generation carefully the story of how God provided for your household in its hardest days, and best of all leave a credential behind, so that in the future, when they point and ask "What is it?", you will have something to say. Grace has discipline, and daily bread teaches faith — these words are the general account of this chapter's forty years.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through one chapter from the full-month day of the exodus. The people longed for flesh pots, and God rained down bread from heaven; the people wanted to gather extra and hoard in private, and God set the rule of taking according to need and of worms for what was kept overnight; the people still wanted to go out and gather on the seventh day, and God taught them rest before an empty field; at last, one jar of manna was stored for later generations, and the bread of forty years began to be counted from the first handful. What this chapter teaches us is not how to stockpile grain, but how to believe: believe that the Provider is enough day by day, believe that rest comes before achievement, and believe that the best inheritance to leave later generations is a credential of God's faithfulness. The question "What is it?" the people asked all their lives, and we too will ask it all our lives. Every time we bow down to receive grace, let us first wonder, first give thanks, and only then eat our fill.

Let us pray together: Lord who rains bread from heaven, thank You: when we complained You provided first, when we were greedy You set rules, and when we could not stop You gave rest. Forgive our nostalgia that beautifies Egypt, heal our unbelief that hoards overnight, and teach us to gather each day's portion each day — not boasting when we gather much, not panicking when we gather little; diligent for six days, at ease on the seventh. And let us leave our children one jar of testimony, so that they may know You, just as at twilight You wanted us to know that You are the LORD. In the name of the Bread of Life, the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[1] lekhem min-hashamayim: Hebrew לֶחֶם מִן־הַשָּׁמַיִם, meaning "bread / food (rained down) from heaven"; lekhem (לֶחֶם, H3899) is bread, food; in Exodus 16:4 the LORD said, "I will rain bread from heaven for you," using this expression; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Psalm 78:24-25 calls manna "the corn of heaven" and "angels' food," a scriptural echo of this manuscript's language about heavenly bread; "the source of provision is in heaven" is a homiletical exposition drawn from this, stated separately from the lexical facts.

[2] nisah: Hebrew נִסָּה (H5254), to test, to try, to prove; in Exodus 16:4 the LORD said, "that I may prove them, whether they will walk in my law, or no," using this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Deuteronomy 8:2-3 and 16, where Moses reviews the forty years of testing and the feeding with manna; this manuscript's "God hides the greatest lessons inside the smallest routines" is a homiletical exposition drawn from that intertext.

[3] 'omer: Hebrew עֹמֶר (H6016), omer, the name of a measure; Exodus 16:16, "an omer for every man," and 16:18, "when they did mete it with an omer," both use this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Its capacity, about 2.2 liters, is the commonly used scholarly estimate, not a precise figure given by the text itself, and this manuscript has noted it as an estimate. See Exodus 16:36, "an omer is the tenth part of an ephah," the text's own internal explanation of the measure.

[4] man and man hu: manna, Hebrew מָן (man, H4478); in Exodus 16:31 the Israelites named this food manna; its naming is commonly associated with the man hu (מָן הוּא), "What is it?", which the people asked one another in 16:15; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This explanation of the naming is the usual reading within the text's own context, but the etymology of manna is also discussed in scholarship, and this manuscript does not treat it as a settled case, taking only the association presented by the narrative itself; "the name itself is a question mark" is a homiletical exposition drawn from this.

[5] shabbaton shabbat: Hebrew שַׁבָּתוֹן שַׁבַּת, shabbaton (H7677, a complete rest, a solemn rest) used together with shabbat (H7676, sabbath); in Exodus 16:23 Moses said, "To morrow is the rest of the holy sabbath unto the LORD" (KJV); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The sabbath in this chapter precedes the giving of the Ten Commandments in Exodus chapter 20 and already appears as an institutional practice (gathering for six days, stopping on the seventh), which is the scriptural basis for this manuscript's "rest was grounded before His law"; "rest is a rhythm of receiving, not a reward" is a homiletical exposition drawn from this.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Then said the LORD unto Moses, Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you; and the people shall go out and gather a certain rate every day, that I may prove them, whether they will walk in my law, or no." (Exodus 16:4, KJV) "And when they did mete it with an omer, he that gathered much had nothing over, and he that gathered little had no lack; they gathered every man according to his eating." (Exodus 16:18, KJV)

Only one month out of Egypt, the people were already longing for the flesh pots of Egypt. Our memory often works the same way: grace fades quickly, while lack feels unforgettable. God did not begin by rebuking them; He first rained down bread, and in the bread He set a gentle discipline: gather each day's portion each day; much gathers no surplus, little suffers no shortage; what greed keeps overnight breeds worms and stinks; on the sixth day there is a double portion, and on the seventh day a restful Sabbath. Grace is not given so that we can stockpile a lifetime of security at once; it invites us to trust day by day — receive today's bread today, thank Him for today's mercy today, and leave tomorrow's supply to the One who will be there tomorrow morning. Today, hand back to God that jar of anxiety you have been keeping overnight. Only what obedience gathers can be kept; what greed stores rots first. Gather your one omer for today in peace. Enough is the truest shape of grace.

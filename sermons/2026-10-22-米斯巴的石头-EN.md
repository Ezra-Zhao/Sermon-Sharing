## Summary (about 100 words)

Jacob sojourned in Haran for twenty years and his household grew greatly, yet the faces of Laban and his sons all changed toward him. God told him to return to his homeland; he left with his whole family, Laban pursued for seven days but was stopped by God in a dream. Jacob recounted twenty years of hardship to his face: his wages had been changed ten times, yet God never once allowed Laban to harm him. In the end the two set up a heap of stones as a witness, called it Galeed and Mizpah, and swore not to harm each other.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

We continue Jacob's story. At the end of the previous chapter, Jacob had increased exceedingly under Laban (Genesis 30:43); Leah holding Judah and saying, "Now will I praise the LORD," belongs to the end of the chapter before that (Genesis 29:35). As this chapter opens, the atmosphere has completely changed. Jacob's household in Haran has grown greatly: flocks covering the hills, servants in groups, camels and donkeys as well. But the faces of Laban and his sons have all changed. In the past Laban embraced Jacob and said, thou art my bone and my flesh; now his sons mutter behind his back that Jacob has taken away all that belonged to their father. A man who has lived under another's roof for twenty years suddenly discovers that every face around him has changed, and even the air feels unsafe. Many people have lived such days: the longer you stay in a place, the more you feel you cannot stay; the more you do, the more others feel you have taken advantage.

This chapter is about leaving. Not leaving on impulse, not leaving in a fit of anger, but, after hearing God's word, taking the whole household, slipping away unnoticed, crossing the Euphrates, and heading for the hill country of Gilead. Behind him is Laban, who will pursue for seven days and seven nights; ahead of him is a homeland he has not returned to for twenty years, and a brother, Esau, whom he has not seen for twenty years and who once wanted to kill him. Jacob is caught in the middle: going forward is hard, turning back is harder. Yet he still went. Today we will learn two things from this chapter: one is the courage to leave, and the other is God's hidden protection. People changed your wages ten times, God never once allowed them to harm you — this sentence is the heart of the whole chapter.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And he heard the words of Laban's sons, saying, Jacob hath taken away all that was our father's; and of that which was our father's hath he gotten all this glory. And Jacob beheld the countenance of Laban, and, behold, it was not toward him as before. And the LORD said unto Jacob, Return unto the land of thy fathers, and to thy kindred; and I will be with thee." (Genesis 31:1-3, excerpt, KJV)

"Your father hath deceived me, and changed my wages ten times; but God suffered him not to hurt me." (Genesis 31:7, excerpt, KJV)

"And God came to Laban the Syrian in a dream by night, and said unto him, Take heed that thou speak not to Jacob either good or bad." (Genesis 31:24, excerpt, KJV)

"This heap is a witness between me and thee this day. Therefore was the name of it called Galeed; And Mizpah; for he said, The LORD watch between me and thee, when we are absent one from another." (Genesis 31:48-49, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, many among us are standing where Jacob stood: in an environment where faces have changed, not knowing whether to stay or to go; or already on the road of leaving, with hearts full of fear. Through this chapter today, let us hear Your word, see Your hidden protection, and receive the courage to take the step forward. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Faces Changed, God's Word Came — The Decision to Leave (31:1-21)

First look at the three signals for Jacob's decision to leave, each clearer than the one before.

The first signal is that people's faces changed. The Scripture says Jacob heard the words of Laban's sons, and beheld that the countenance of Laban was not toward him as before. One "heard," one "beheld": his ears heard gossip, his eyes saw faces. Brothers and sisters, in the workplace, among relatives, in any place of sojourning, danger often does not begin with an open knife; it begins with faces. The words are still the same words, the smile is still the same smile, but the temperature has dropped. The words of Laban's sons especially deserve a close listen: "Jacob hath taken away all that was our father's." Is that true? No. Jacob's wealth was exchanged for six years of shepherding wages; it was what God Himself had made plain in the previous chapter and what Laban had acknowledged on the spot: the speckled and spotted sheep would be Jacob's. Laban himself had said, I have learned that the LORD hath blessed me for thy sake. His sons, however, called God's blessing Jacob's robbery, and twenty years of toil a sudden seizure. This is how jealousy works: it never admits what price you have paid; it only stares at what you now have. It erases all of your cost, and labels all of your gain as ill-gotten. If Jacob had waited for them to round out their words and for the faces to turn back, he would only have waited for worse faces. Once the human heart turns jealous, it does not heal itself; it only deepens.

The second signal is that God's word came. "And the LORD said unto Jacob, Return unto the land of thy fathers, and to thy kindred; and I will be with thee." Note the order of God's speaking: first the command, then the promise; first "Return," then "I will be with thee." God did not first explain how Jacob would face Esau on his return, nor first guarantee that Laban would not pursue; He gave only one command and one promise of presence. Many times we wait for God to unfold the map of the whole journey before we are willing to move, but God gives only the next step. The promise Jacob received at Bethel was, I will keep thee, and will bring thee again into this land; now, twenty years later, the time has come for the promise to be fulfilled, and the way it is fulfilled is to take the step first, and then gradually to see. The order of faith has always been like this: it is not that we walk only after we see clearly; it is that we see clearly only as we walk.

The third signal is hidden in Jacob's consultation with his two wives. Jacob sent and called Rachel and Leah to the field to his flock, and laid the matter out plainly: I see the countenance of your father, that it is not toward me as before; but the God of my father hath been with me. And ye know that with all my power I have served your father. And your father hath deceived me, and changed my wages ten times; but God suffered him not to hurt me. This consultation is a rare moment of dignity in Jacob's life. He did not act first and report later, nor pack up his wives and children like luggage; he first explained the reasons clearly, explained God's leading clearly, and let his wives judge for themselves. The answer of Rachel and Leah is the most heartbreaking and also the most satisfying passage in the whole chapter: "Whatsoever God hath said unto thee, do." They also said something even heavier: Is there yet any portion or inheritance for us in our father's house? Are we not counted of him strangers? For he hath sold us, and hath quite devoured also our money. Two daughters said of their own father: he has sold us. This was not something Jacob taught them to say; this was what they themselves had tasted in twenty years in that house. Laban had used his daughters as bargaining chips for a bride price, and now his daughters confirmed in person: that house had long ceased to be a home. The decision to leave was thus made in the unanimity of the whole family. Brothers and sisters, a truly God-led leaving is not a husband deciding alone while the whole family is forced to follow; it is laying God's leading on the table, so that those affected can discern it together and nod together. Courage without consensus underneath it will scatter before seven days are out.

The decision was made, but the action required wisdom. They set out while Laban was away shearing his sheep. Some say this was Jacob deceiving again, fleeing again. The word the Scripture uses does indeed carry the sense of "stealing away" [3]: Jacob stole away unawares to Laban the Syrian, taking his whole family and all that he had across the river. We need not whitewash every one of Jacob's methods, but we must also see the situation in full: if he had stayed to confront Laban face to face, would Laban have let them go? Would a man who changed wages ten times in twenty years suddenly speak of fairness at a farewell? Jacob knew Laban too well: a face-to-face farewell would be handing the lives of his wives and children into the other party's hand. Some leavings need not be made with drums and gongs; some farewells, once spoken, can no longer be carried out. In an unrighteous environment, to leave quietly and cleanly is wisdom, not cowardice. Of course, Jacob's leaving also contained one failure: Rachel stole her father's household gods. We will examine this matter in detail in Part Two; here we record only one note: a person can set out with the right decision, yet still hide the wrong thing in his luggage. That God leads a person to leave does not mean the person has done everything right; on the road of leaving, God will keep clearing out our luggage all along the way.

Before setting out, there is one more word that must be heard, and that is God's self-introduction at Bethel. When God told Jacob to set out, He specially recalled Bethel: "I am the God of Bethel, where thou anointedst the pillar, and where thou vowedst a vow unto me: now arise, get thee out from this land, and return unto the land of thy kindred." The dream of twenty years ago and the vow of twenty years ago — God had forgotten none of it. When people travel too long, they easily treat their first touch from God as a thing of youth; God does not see it that way. He brings the words of twenty years ago verbatim into today, reminding Jacob: you are not a man with nowhere to go; you are a man with a covenant upon him. Your homeland is not only Canaan in geography, but also Canaan in promise. Brothers and sisters, when you have stayed in a place until your heart is grey and cold, what God reminds you of is often not a new doctrine, but the very sentence you first received, spoken to you again. As He said it at first, so it still counts today.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Stolen Gods and the Seven Days' Pursuit — God Restrains in Secret (31:22-42)

On the third day after the leaving, the news reached Laban: Jacob had fled. What Laban did was wholly unsurprising: he took his brethren with him and pursued, following for seven days' journey, and overtook him in Mount Gilead. What does seven days mean? Jacob was traveling with his whole household: pregnant women, infants, great herds of sheep and cattle that could not move fast; Laban traveled light, all able-bodied men. One fleeing, one pursuing, the distance shrinking day by day. Judged by human circumstances alone, Jacob was bound to lose: the pursuers were strong in men and horses, while he had only the old, the weak, women and children; the pursuers came in anger, while he had no stronghold to defend. Brothers and sisters, many nights in life are like this: you are clearly walking a road at God's word, yet the pursuers draw nearer and nearer, and you begin to doubt whether you heard wrong, whether you walked wrong. The answer this chapter gives is: pursuers drawing near does not mean God is far away. God's protection often does not restrain where you can see it, but restrains where you cannot see it.

How did God restrain? "And God came to Laban the Syrian in a dream by night, and said unto him, Take heed that thou speak not to Jacob either good or bad." Neither good nor bad was to be spoken, meaning: you shall not press him with words, shall not threaten, shall not entice; this matter is not yours to dispose of. The words of accusation Laban had prepared before sleep were all voided when he woke. The next day, when he overtook Jacob, his first words still carried fire: Why didst thou steal away unawares to me, and carry away my daughters, as captives taken with the sword? Why didst thou flee away secretly, and steal away from me; and didst not tell me, that I might have sent thee away with mirth? But at the most crucial point he had to confess it himself: "It is in the power of my hand to do you hurt: but the God of your father spake unto me yesternight, saying, Take thou heed that thou speak not to Jacob either good or bad." Please hear the weight of this sentence clearly: it was not that Laban suddenly softened, nor that Laban began to speak of family feeling; it was that God had arrived at Laban's pillow the night before. While Jacob was still anxious in his tent, God had already finished the negotiation in his enemy's dream. This is God's hidden protection: He does not always make the pursuers disappear; He often goes first into the pursuers' hearts and turns the direction of the knife. Looking back after many years, you will discover that many harms that should have fallen on you softened, veered away, or stopped halfway — not because you were lucky, but because Someone kept watch for you through the night while you were asleep.

Along the pursuit, there was also an embarrassing matter lying across the road: the gods were missing. After overtaking them, besides the accusation, Laban added one more sentence: "And now, though thou wouldest needs be gone, because thou sore longedst after thy father's house, yet wherefore hast thou stolen my gods?" Only then was Rachel's theft of the gods brought to light. Why did Rachel steal them? The Scripture does not say plainly, and interpreters through the ages have offered several views: some say she feared her father would use the gods for divination to track their whereabouts, and stealing them cut off his eyes and ears; some say she had grown up in her father's house, steeped in what she saw and heard, and truly regarded those small household gods as a protecting reliance, and, on the point of leaving, wanted to carry a blessing from her parental home with her; others say those gods were connected with a title to the family inheritance, and stealing them secured a claim for her husband. Whichever it was, the undertone is the same: the person who left still had Haran in her heart though her feet had left Haran. Rachel believed God's promise, and also stole her father's idols; she followed her husband toward the land of promise, yet hid the gods of the old house in her bundle. This is not Rachel's problem alone; it is the problem of every one of us: when we leave an environment, we often move only our bodies, while the old environment's way of judging value, its way of seeking peace, its way of grasping security, are packed up unchanged and carried along. God leads us out of Egypt, and our pockets still hold the gods of Egypt. Fortunately the story does not stop there: Jacob, not knowing, angrily made a heavy vow, that with whomsoever the gods were found, that person should not live. Laban searched tent by tent, and entered Rachel's tent. Rachel had put the gods in the camel's furniture and sat upon them, and excused herself from rising on account of her bodily condition; Laban searched the whole tent and found nothing. This scene is both laughable and sad: the thing worshipped as a god ends up sat upon; the man searching for his god rummages through the whole tent, while the god is hidden right before his eyes. The Bible writes with restraint, yet writes the emptiness of idols through and through: how can a god that cannot even hide itself protect anyone? And the God who truly protected this family was not in the camel's furniture, not in the tent; last night He was in the enemy's dream, and this morning He was between the two camps. Brothers and sisters, will you open your luggage today and take a look at what is hidden inside? After many years of believing in the Lord, what do you still secretly rely on for peace? Is it the number in your savings, a word from a certain person, a fortune you have calculated, or an anxious ritual you cannot change? The person sitting on an idol must in the end stand up and hand it over, and only then can the road be walked cleanly.

The search came up empty, and attack and defense changed places. It was Jacob's turn to speak. The Bible says Jacob was wroth, and chode with Laban. This speech is Jacob's longest and firmest utterance in twenty years; the man who had ordinarily swallowed his anger before Laban reckoned the account item by item: "These twenty years have I been with thee; thy ewes and thy she goats have not cast their young, and the rams of thy flock have I not eaten. That which was torn of beasts I brought not unto thee; I bare the loss of it; of my hand didst thou require it, whether stolen by day, or stolen by night. Thus I was; in the day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night; and my sleep departed from mine eyes." Brothers and sisters, please read these sentences slowly. Twenty years, in three stretches: the first fourteen years serving for two daughters, then six years serving for the flock. Drought by day, frost by night, sleep departed — all the hardship of a shepherd is in there. What was torn by beasts could by rule have been excused, yet he bore the loss himself; the rams of the flock a shepherd might by custom have eaten, yet he did not touch them. In twenty years, his work was beyond reproach and his suffering beyond telling, while his wages were changed ten times. If he had spoken these words first, they would have sounded like boasting; because he endured twenty years and spoke only after the search came up empty and his innocence was established, these words became evidence presented in court. A wronged person need not hurry to defend himself on the first day; innocence can withstand a search, and accounts can withstand an inspection. When the time comes, laying twenty years out in the sunlight has more power than any quarrel.

Jacob's words finally landed on God, and this is the highest point of the whole passage: "These twenty years have I been in thy house; I served thee fourteen years for thy two daughters, and six years for thy cattle: and thou hast changed my wages ten times. Except the God of my father, the God of Abraham, and the fear of Isaac, had been with me, surely thou hadst sent me away now empty. God hath seen mine affliction and the labour of my hands, and rebuked thee yesternight." "Sent away empty" — those words name the most likely ending of twenty years: by Laban's arithmetic, a son-in-law's labor was free, his daughters were sold, the flock was his own, and Jacob ought to leave with empty hands. Endings that did not happen often reveal God's hand most clearly: surely thou hadst sent me away now empty — except that God had been with me. Brothers and sisters, as you look back on the road you have walked, please also learn to count the things that did not happen: that disaster which should have fallen on your head did not fall; that person who should have emptied you out did not succeed; those years which should have ended empty-handed instead stored up a household. God's protection is written half in the things He accomplished for you, and half in the things He restrained for you. The second half you ordinarily cannot see; only at a moment of confrontation like Mount Gilead do you suddenly see clearly: through these twenty years, I did not endure alone.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Setting Up Stones as a Witness — Galeed and Mizpah (31:43-55)

After the confrontation, Laban was the first to relent. His words are very interesting: "These daughters are my daughters, and these children are my children, and these cattle are my cattle, and all that thou seest is mine: and what can I do this day unto these my daughters, or unto their children which they have born?" Going around in a large circle, it amounted to an admission: the people are all on your side, the goods are all on your side, and there is nothing I can take away. Then he made a surprising proposal: "Now therefore come thou, let us make a covenant, I and thou; and let it be for a witness between me and thee." The man who had pursued for seven days finally wanted to make a covenant. This was not Laban suddenly becoming noble; this was the best way out that the situation, after God's restraint, forced into being: what cannot be taken back by force will be bounded by a covenant. God's protection is not only restraining a moment's harm; it often also pushes people toward a lasting arrangement, so that harm has a boundary from then on and the past is sealed from then on.

Jacob's response was to take a stone and set it up for a pillar, and to tell his brethren to gather stones, which they heaped up, and they ate there upon the heap. Eating a meal before speaking of the covenant was the rule for making covenants in ancient times: sharing a table meant the reconciliation had taken effect. Then came the naming. Laban called the heap in Aramaic Jegarsahadutha, and Jacob called it in Hebrew Galeed, and both mean the same: this heap is a witness. One heap of stones, two languages, two names — precisely the portrait of these two men's lives: living together for twenty years, even in naming a heap of stones each spoke his own language, yet together before this same heap they confessed one thing: here there is a witness; here God is watching.

The name Galeed is worth pausing over carefully [1]. It is compounded of two words: a heap of stones, plus a witness, testimony. Stones themselves cannot speak, yet in the tradition of the holy covenant, stones are the most durable witnesses: people die, words are forgotten, the stones still stand there. Jacob had a lifelong connection with stones: at Bethel he had pillowed on a stone and poured oil on it; now at Mount Gilead he again heaped stones and set up a pillar. A man on a road of flight had no notary's office, no lawyer, no contract on paper to file away; what he had was only stones and God. He turned every instance of God's presence into a landmark, so that in days to come his children, passing by, could point and say: God was with us here. Brothers and sisters, your life also needs such stones: not superstition about a stone, but solemnly recording and setting up the things God has done for you, lest, when hardship comes, you forget how you ever made it through.

Laban gave this place a second name: Mizpah, for he said, The LORD watch between me and thee, when we are absent one from another [2]. This sentence was later often copied out and given to relatives and friends setting out on a journey; it sounds beautiful, like a blessing. But put back into its original context, it was at first not a blessing, but a sentence carrying wariness. What Laban said next was: If thou shalt afflict my daughters, or if thou shalt take other wives beside my daughters, no man is with us; see, God is witness betwixt me and thee. He said Mizpah because he did not trust Jacob: when I cannot see you, may God watch you in my place. This is a covenant made for the suspicious: trust between people has gone bankrupt, so let God stand guard instead. The wonder is that God does not disdain a covenant that begins this way. For people whose trust has broken to still be willing to invite God into the middle is better than inviting the knife into the middle. Mizpah began in wariness, yet in later ages it became a byword for watching over one another, because its core is right: where people cannot see each other, God can see; the covenant people cannot keep, God will keep. When husband and wife are separated in two places, when parents send children on a far journey, when co-workers go their separate ways, who can guarantee that the other will never change for a lifetime? What cannot be guaranteed should be handed over to that sentence, Mizpah: may the LORD watch between me and thee. It is not using God to frighten the other party; it is placing both parties under God's eyes, which is both a restraint and a comfort.

The content of the covenant was spoken plainly by both sides. Laban swore and drew the boundary: "This heap be witness, and this pillar be witness, that I will not pass over this heap to thee, and that thou shalt not pass over this heap and this pillar unto me, for harm." One boundary line governs two men: from now on, pursuit stops here, and harm stops here. Jacob also swore by the fear of his father Isaac. At the end Laban said: "The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the God of their father, judge betwixt us." Early the next morning, Laban rose up, kissed his sons and his daughters, and blessed them, and returned to his place. A confrontation pursued over seven days ended in a meal, a heap of stones, several kisses, and blessings. There was no sentimental embrace of reconciliation, no tearful confession of wrong; it was drawing the boundary clearly, setting up the witness firmly, and each going home. This gives us a very realistic model for handling broken relationships: not every relationship can be restored as it was at first; the best ending for some relationships is to draw a boundary with dignity, not to harm each other, and then each to walk forward. Drawing a boundary is not hatred; setting up a stone is not a curse; to speak "we shall not harm each other" aloud and make it a covenant is itself a kind of completion. Some people are destined to retreat from your life to the other side of the boundary; your lesson is not to pull them back, but to bless them as they return to their place, and to walk in peace yourself toward the land of promise.

What matters even more is that this covenant contains a clause set specially for the weak. The sentence Laban contended for on behalf of his daughters — if thou shalt afflict my daughters, or take other wives beside them — was a father, before letting go, discharging a father's duty one last time [4]. However badly he had failed as a father, this one closing sentence at least stood on his daughters' side. And the executor of this sentence was not he, but God: though no man knows, God is witness. Brothers and sisters, there is much suffering in the world that no one knows: suffering behind closed doors at home, suffering in marriage that cannot be spoken, with no one to witness and no one to uphold. The covenant of Mizpah tells such people: that no one knows does not mean there is no witness. God is in the middle; God sees it all. He rebuked the strong in Laban's dream, and He will also, in corners no one knows, preserve evidence for the suffering, and in His time judge personally.

For the application of this chapter, I would land on four concrete places. First, about leaving. If you are now in an environment where faces have all changed and wages are changed again and again, do not first rush to leave in a fit of anger; do the three things Jacob did: hear God's word clearly, explain the leading clearly to the family members who will be affected, and choose the time to set out with wisdom. When God moves you to go, He will give you a word, He will give you companions for the road, and He will give you the time when the door opens. Second, about luggage. Before you go, inspect your own camel's furniture once: the idols, old habits, and old securities the old environment left with you — hand them over today, and do not let Rachel's embarrassment become your embarrassment. Third, about reckoning accounts. A person who has been wronged should learn Jacob's kind of reckoning: no exaggeration, no fabrication, laying out the facts item by item, and in the end ascribing the credit to God. Such reckoning is not to humiliate the other party; it is to let yourself graduate from bitterness: once the account is settled and the stone is set up, a person can walk forward and need not recount it year after year. Fourth, about drawing boundaries. With those who have harmed you and can no longer be close, learn the way of Mizpah: set one plain boundary, do not cross it to harm, and let God watch over each side. If forgiveness cannot yet be achieved for the moment, first achieve not harming; if a blessing cannot yet be spoken for the moment, first achieve handing judgment over to God. After the boundary is drawn well, the road runs in the direction of your own land of promise; do not turn back to count the other party's footsteps.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked a road of seven days: from Haran, where faces had changed, to Mount Gilead, where stones were set up as a witness. Jacob's courage to leave did not come from his being bold; it came from God's word. The safety of Jacob's whole family did not come from his running fast; it came from God's restraint in his enemy's dream. People changed his wages ten times, God never once allowed them to harm him; people pursued for seven days' journey, and God drew the boundary with a single dream. What finally remained on the mountain was not a grave, but a heap of stones bearing witness: Galeed says, here there is a witness; Mizpah says, may the LORD watch between me and thee.

Let us pray together: Lord who watches between people at Mount Gilead, thank You; while we were still asleep, You went first to our enemy to restrain him; before the ending of being sent away empty-handed could arrive, You reached out first to rewrite it. Give those who should go the courage to leave, give those who should stay a heart to wait, give those who have been wronged the innocence to reckon accounts, and give those whose relationships are broken the wisdom to draw boundaries. Search also what is hidden in our camel's furniture, so that we may walk cleanly and walk at ease. When we are separated from one another and cannot see one another, may You watch between us, and also keep watch among us. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] gal'ed: Galeed, Hebrew גַּלְעֵד (H1567), Genesis 31:47-48 Jacob called the heap Galeed, while Laban called it in Aramaic Jegarsahadutha, with the same meaning; the name is compounded of gal (גַּל, H1530, heap, pile) and 'ed (עֵד, H5707, witness, testimony), literally "heap of witness"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The third part of this manuscript, "stones are the most durable witnesses," is a homiletical exposition based on this compound meaning; the factual portion is the etymological composition.

[2] mitzpah: Mizpah, Hebrew מִצְפָּה (H4709), Genesis 31:49 Laban called the place of the covenant Mizpah, saying, "The LORD watch between me and thee, when we are absent one from another"; the name derives from the verb tsaphah (צָפָה, H6822, to watch, keep watch, observe); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript notes that the saying in its original context carried wariness and in later usage became a byword for watching over one another; the two belong respectively to the historical context and to later usage.

[3] ganav: Hebrew גָּנַב (H1589), to steal, take by stealth, and by extension to deceive, to act stealthily; Genesis 31:20, "Jacob stole away unawares to Laban the Syrian," and 31:27, where Laban charged Jacob, "wherefore didst thou steal away from me," both use forms of this word, while 31:30 and 31:39, concerning Rachel's stealing of the gods and losses apart from what was torn by beasts, have distinct contexts; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Parts One and Two of this manuscript render it with both "stealing away" and "deceiving," on the basis of this double sense.

[4] 'anah: Hebrew עָנָה (H6031), to afflict, cause to suffer, oppress, humble; Genesis 31:50 Laban said in the covenant, "If thou shalt afflict ('anah) my daughters," meaning to treat them harshly and oppress them in marriage; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Genesis 15:13 and Deuteronomy 26:6, where the same word is used for Israel's affliction in Egypt, as a cross-reference for this manuscript's "suffering no one knows also has God as its witness."

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Your father hath deceived me, and changed my wages ten times; but God suffered him not to hurt me." (Genesis 31:7, KJV) "Except the God of my father, the God of Abraham, and the fear of Isaac, had been with me, surely thou hadst sent me away now empty. God hath seen mine affliction and the labour of my hands, and rebuked thee yesternight." (Genesis 31:42, KJV)

Over twenty years, Jacob's wages were changed ten times. Each time he bore it, and each time he went back to work. Only on the day of reckoning at Gilead did he see something more real than his grievance: people changed his wages ten times, and God blocked the harm ten times; people meant to send him away empty-handed, and God never once allowed it. If today you feel that your labor is always repriced and undervalued, do not rush to break relations. Keep the account honestly, do the work faithfully, and entrust your days to the God who rebukes by night. He has seen your hardship and the toil of your hands; His restraining hand often reaches out while you are still asleep. When the day comes to set up your own stone, you will understand: you did not survive by endurance alone; you survived because God said, not allowed. Today, learn to do two things as Jacob did: first, lay the account of twenty years quietly before God, without exaggeration and without bitterness, stating only the facts; second, set up the stone of Gilead in your heart, reminding yourself that every pursuit has an end, and that the day will come when the boundary is drawn. May the LORD watch between me and thee — this sentence was not only for Laban; it is also for you today: where people cannot see, God has been watching all along; the covenant people cannot keep, God keeps in your place. Take this sentence with you on the road, and your steps will be steady.

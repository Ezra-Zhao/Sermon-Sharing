> Reading reminders: Five things must be nailed down when teaching this chapter. First, Uzzah's death must not be preached as God being violent-tempered and killing at whim: the manner of transporting the ark had long been laid down in the law of Moses — the Kohathites were to carry it on their shoulders, not touch the holy things, not even look at them (cf. Num 4:15; 7:9). The new cart David used this time was exactly the method the Philistines had used when returning the ark (cf. 1 Sam 6) — a foreign expedient substituted for God's own command. Keep the focus on holiness and reverence: zeal cannot replace obedience; a well-meant hand that crosses the line God drew is still transgression. Second, this chapter must not be used to teach people to keep away from God out of fear: after his fear, David stopped, inquired and corrected himself — three months later he carried the ark on shoulders according to the law (cf. the parallel account, 1 Chr 15:13-15), sacrificed after six steps, and brought the ark into the city with gladness. Fear in Scripture is the beginning of reverence, not the end of relationship; to preach this chapter as "do not go near God" is to preach medicine as poison. Third, on David's dancing, Michal's accusation of exposure is registered, not adjudicated: the narrative itself records that David wore a linen ephod — a ministering garment, not nakedness — and both Hebrew words for his dancing mean leaping and whirling, with no obscene sense. The preacher may neither use this chapter to endorse every disorder or exposure in worship, nor follow Michal in dismissing embodied worship wholesale as unseemly. Fourth, Michal must not be preached as a mere negative stereotype: she was Saul's daughter, first promised to David, then given by her father to Palti, then demanded back by David by name (cf. 1 Sam 18; 25; 2 Sam 3) — all her life she was a person moved back and forth in the change of dynasties. Her contempt at the window has a charge that can be examined, and a sorrow that can be pitied; this manuscript judges her contempt according to the text while registering her life story — no mockery, no extra blows. Fifth, that Michal had no children all her life is only juxtaposed in the narrative, with no stated causation; commentators differ (one reading: divine punishment; another: the natural result of the couple's estrangement). It is registered, not adjudicated; the preacher must never use this chapter to declare any childless person cursed, and must never pronounce judgment from the pulpit on women's bodies or childbearing.

## Summary

2 Samuel 6 is the chapter of the ark entering the city, and the most costly lesson in worship in David's life. In the previous chapter he had just received the crown of all Israel; in this chapter his first great act is to bring into his new capital the ark that had stayed for decades in the house of Abinadab at Kiriath-jearim (cf. 1 Sam 7:1-2). Thirty thousand men, a band, a new cart — a spectacle at its height — yet it failed over one small thing: they hauled the ark on an ox cart, which was the Philistines' method, not the law of Moses. At the threshing floor the oxen stumbled, Uzzah stretched out his hand to steady the ark and died beside it, and the singing stopped on the spot. The king was first troubled, then afraid, and unloaded the ark halfway at the house of a Gittite — and for three months that household's blessing grew too large to hide. David had learned: the second time, no cart, but shoulders; six steps, then a sacrifice; the king himself took off his royal robe, wore a linen ephod, and danced with all his might before the ark, while the whole city shouted and blew trumpets. Only one person watched from a window: Michal. What she saw was not the ark, but a king who did not look like a king. Husband and wife confront each other at the chapter's end: she says, how undignified you were today; he says, I will become even more lowly. This sermon treats three things: in welcoming God's presence, neither zeal nor spectacle can replace God's method; after holiness has frightened a person away, reverence must lead him back — the three months' pause was not giving up, but making up the missed lesson; and the dispute over dignity in worship: when God's glory and human face can only be chosen one at a time, David chose to be lowly to the end.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, today we stand on the threshing floor of Nacon, at the door of Obed-edom's house, and beside the window in the city of David. First hold back in us the well-meant hand of Uzzah — the hand that reaches out before You have commanded — and teach us to place our zeal inside obedience. Heal also our state of being stalled by fear: we may stop, but after making up the lesson we must bring You back by Your method. For ordinary households visited by blessing, whose whole families receive grace, we thank You; and for the person at the window we pray: open her eyes Yourself, that heart which sees only dignity and not Your presence. Finally, give us the courage of David's one sentence — before You, I will become even more lowly. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: The King's First Great Act Was to Fetch the Ark

Read chapter 5 together with this chapter. At the end of chapter 5 David won two victories in the Valley of Rephaim and his throne was secure; at the opening of this chapter he does not expand the army or build a palace, but does one thing first: fetch the ark. The order must be made clear: for David, the new capital must first have God's presence, and only then does it deserve to be a capital. Where was the ark then? In the house of Abinadab on the hill at Kiriath-jearim — sent there from Beth-shemesh in 1 Samuel 7, it had remained a long time: the twenty years recorded in 1 Samuel 7:2 are the years up to the point when Israel mourned and sought after the LORD, and after that it passed through the years of Saul as well, so that in total it stayed for several decades. The sensation of the ark's capture and return in Eli's days had long become the previous generation's story; during Saul's reign (for its length cf. Acts 13:21) Scripture nowhere records that he inquired about the ark (cf. the parallel wording in 1 Chr 13:3: "we did not seek it during the days of Saul"). David's first lesson as king was to bring back to the center of the nation the presence of God that had been neglected for decades. This chapter has three parts. Part one, the new cart and the threshing floor (vv. 1-11a): thirty thousand men go to welcome the ark, the new cart and new songs, Uzzah's outstretched hand, Perez-uzzah, the king's trouble and fear. Part two, three months and six steps (vv. 11b-15): the ark stays at Obed-edom's house, the whole family is blessed, the king makes up the lesson and starts again, a sacrifice after six steps, dancing with all his might. Part three, the window and the confrontation (vv. 16-23): Michal's contempt, the setting up of the ark, blessing and distribution of cakes, the dispute at home, and becoming even more lowly. The chapter has three inner threads. The thread of method: the same ark, the same king, the same people — the first time hauled by cart, ending in death; the second time carried on shoulders, entering the city with song and dance. This chapter is Scripture's clearest lesson that method is theology: how one treats God's presence is itself the content of faith. The thread of blessing: where the ark is, there is blessing — three months in Obed-edom's house, blessing reaching the whole family and all that he had; the reverse of blessing is not curse but Uzzah's death on the threshing floor: the presence is true blessing and true holiness, and the dividing line is only whether people approach Him according to God's boundary. The thread of eyes: the whole chapter is a contest of seeing — the people see the ark, the king sees the LORD, Michal sees the king; different objects of sight, utterly different conclusions. This chapter asks every hearer: when you worship, whom are your eyes actually looking at?

Before reading the chapter, set three limits. First, do not preach the first procession as sheer hypocrisy: the zeal of the thirty thousand was real, the band was real, David's longing was real; the tragedy of this chapter lies not in motive but in method — and that is precisely the most piercing lesson: motive entirely right, method wrong, and a life is still lost. Second, do not overlook the weight of Abinadab's family: Uzzah and Ahio were men of the household that guarded the ark; Uzzah's outstretched hand has often since been read as an act of rashness, but he guarded the ark all his life and died beside his post; preach his transgression according to the text, and also remember his duty with fairness — never make sport of the dead from the pulpit. Third, do not preach the couple's dispute at the chapter's end as household gossip: this is a head-on collision of two kingdoms — the Saul-house view of kingship (a king must look like a king) and David's view of worship (a king is first a worshiper). In Michal's words is the shadow of her father's whole life; in David's answer, the root of his being chosen all his life. Preach it from the pulpit as a theological dispute, not as a marital quarrel.

## Part One: The New Cart, the Band, and the Hand on the Threshing Floor (2 Sam 6:1-11)

### 1. Thirty Thousand Men Go to Fetch a Neglected God (2 Sam 6:1-2)

David again gathered all the chosen men of Israel, thirty thousand, and arose and went with them to bring up the ark of God from Baale-judah — the ark called by the name of the LORD of hosts who sits enthroned on the cherubim (cf. 2 Sam 6:1-2, condensed rendering in substance).

"Called by the name": in the original, נִקְרָא שֵׁם (niqrāʾ šēm), the name is called, proclaimed, over it. See BDB, s.v. קרא, s.v. שם. [1] Cherubim: in the original, כְּרוּבִים (kərûḇîm, H3742), cherubim. See BDB, s.v. כרוב. [2] First look at the number thirty thousand: Israel's chosen men, thirty thousand — this is no moving crew; it is a national ceremony. Then look at how solemnly the narrative titles the ark: the LORD of hosts who sits enthroned on the cherubim — the ark is not a piece of sacred furniture; it is the footstool and the sign of the throne of the invisible King (cf. Ps 99:1; 132:7-8, cross-read in exposition). What David was bringing into the city was not an antique, but a King neglected by His own nation for decades. Preach this verse first to the church: the first sign of decline in a nation, a church, a family is often not moral collapse, but the presence of God being taken up to a little house on the hill and left there unasked after — Saul did not inquire about the ark for forty years; the ark did not protest, did not leave; it simply stayed quietly in Abinadab's house, waiting for the next king to remember it. God's presence does not punish those who neglect Him with absence; He simply waits. The waiting God is the first portrait in this chapter.

### 2. The New Cart Was a Borrowed Method (2 Sam 6:3-5)

They carried the ark of God on a new cart from the house of Abinadab on the hill, and Uzzah and Ahio, the sons of Abinadab, drove the cart; David and all the house of Israel made merry before the LORD with all kinds of instruments (cf. 2 Sam 6:3-5, condensed rendering in substance).

New cart: in the original, עֲגָלָה חֲדָשָׁה (ʿăgālâ ḥădāšâ); cart is H5699. See BDB, s.v. עגלה. [3] The problem hides inside this brand-new cart. Hauling the ark by ox cart — whose invention was it? The Philistines': stricken by the ark and anxious to send it away, after divination they used a new cart and two milch cows that had never borne a yoke to send the ark back to Israel (cf. 1 Sam 6:7-12). That was a foreign people's one-time expedient in ignorance; God had mercy on them, and the cows walked on their own to Beth-shemesh. But David was not ignorant: Israel had the law. When the ark came out of Egypt, God Himself designed rings and poles (cf. Ex 25:12-15) and commanded the Kohathites among the Levites to carry it on their shoulders, expressly saying they must not touch the holy things lest they die, nor even look at them for a moment (cf. Num 4:15, 18-20); when the tabernacle's furnishings were transported, the carts God assigned through Moses went to the sons of Gershon and Merari — the Kohathites' portion was shoulders (cf. Num 7:6-9). "New" is no virtue in worship: the newer the cart, the clearer it is that this is not the old way God appointed. Drive this lesson home to every servant greedy for efficiency: David's method was more effortless, more splendid, more festive; its only problem was — God had not said it. Every shortcut in worship and service that bypasses God's command is a new cart. However loud the singing, it cannot cover the fact that the wheels are not running on that road.

### 3. Uzzah Stretches Out His Hand (2 Sam 6:6-8)

When they came to the threshing floor [19] of Nacon, the oxen stumbled, and Uzzah put out his hand and held the ark of God; the LORD's anger was kindled against Uzzah and He struck him there for his error, and he died beside the ark of God (cf. 2 Sam 6:6-7, condensed rendering in substance).

Held: in the original, אָחַז (ʾāḥaz, H270), to grasp, hold fast. See BDB, s.v. אחז. [4] Error: the original underlying the Chinese Union Version, הַשַּׁל (haššal, H7944), occurring only this once in the whole Old Testament — negligence, fault. See BDB, s.v. שַׁל. [5] The place of the striking David called Perez-uzzah: in the original, פֶּרֶץ עֻזָּא (Pereṣ ʿUzzāʾ, H6560); "Perez" is a breach, an outbreak (H6555). See BDB, s.v. פרץ. [6] This scene must be preached slowly. The oxen stumble, the ark sways, Uzzah stretches out his hand — his motive is beyond reproach: he cannot watch the ark fall into the mud. Many pulpits have cried injustice for this hand, saying God is too severe. But pull the camera back: Uzzah's death was already written the moment the new cart set out. Had the ark been carried on shoulders according to the law, there would have been no stumbling ox, no swaying ark, and no second in which a hand had to steady it. The new cart placed the ark in a position where it could sway at any time, and then, at the moment the sway came, forced the nearest person to patch this methodological hole with his hand. Uzzah died of his own transgression, and he also died of the whole team's decision to replace shoulders with a new cart — when a leader uses the wrong method, the cost is often paid by the front-line co-worker closest to the holy things. And as for God's anger: Scripture never apologizes for God's holiness. The cherubim beside the ark are no decoration; He is the God who says, "Be holy, for I am holy" (cf. Lev 11:44). Compared with the mud, which is more fit to hold up the ark — the mud, or that hand? The mud had never touched the holy things; that hand crossed knowingly. God struck Uzzah not because He despised human goodwill, but because on the very first day the new dynasty went out to welcome the ark He would carve a boundary into the memory of all Israel: His presence can be approached, but only in His way; anyone may come, and no one may come carelessly.

### 4. Trouble, Fear, and the Ark Unloaded Halfway (2 Sam 6:8-11)

David was troubled because the LORD had struck Uzzah, and he was afraid of the LORD, saying, "How can the ark of the LORD come to me?" And David would not bring the ark into the city of David, but took it aside to the house of Obed-edom the Gittite (cf. 2 Sam 6:8-10, condensed rendering in substance).

Troubled: in one strand of the original, חָרָה (ḥārâ, H2734), to burn, be angry, be in anguish. See BDB, s.v. חרה. [7] Afraid: in the original, יָרֵא (yārēʾ, H3372), to fear, to revere — the same word is both fear and reverence. See BDB, s.v. ירא. [8] David's reaction has three layers, and each must be told honestly. The first layer is trouble mixed with anger: his grand ceremony had a death in it, his plan collapsed in public; in the king's trouble there is something toward God and something toward the situation — Scripture does not whitewash it. The second layer is fear: "How can it come to me?" — on the surface retreat, at the root this chapter's healthiest theology: for the first time David seriously thought that the ark's coming to his home was not him doing God a favor, but a matter of whether God was willing to come. Many treat God's presence as one item on their own schedule; on the threshing floor David learned that presence is grace, not equipment. The third layer is unloading the ark: he neither forced the ark into the city with a stiff neck nor sent it back to the hill; he stopped it at the nearest household — the house of Obed-edom the Gittite. There is an irony here to be pointed out gently: the ark that Israel's king dared not receive was received by a household whose name carries the place-name Gath (as for Obed-edom's background, 1 Chr 26 records his sons as Levitical gatekeepers; how to read the designation "the Gittite" is disputed among commentators — registered, not adjudicated [9]). Fear made David stop; this stopping was not a failure of faith: in Scripture, those who know how to stop have the chance to learn the right way to walk; those who never stop only repeat the error with a bigger spectacle.

**Ignition point (the first):** Brothers and sisters, today I say to everyone who has been startled in service and has stopped: David stopped too. The king stopped for three months, the nation stopped for three months, the ark stopped in an ordinary household. Stopping is not the end of the story; stopping is the beginning of making up the lesson. Today — not tomorrow — take down the new cart, open the law, and relearn, by God's method, that thing you have long propped up by sheer zeal. God did not withdraw His presence because David stopped; He sat in Obed-edom's house for three months blessing day after day — which was as if He called to David daily: when the method is right, come and fetch Me again.

## Part Two: Three Months of Blessing, and a Sacrifice After Six Steps (2 Sam 6:11-15)

### 1. Where the Ark Stays, That House Is Blessed (2 Sam 6:11-12)

The ark remained in the house of Obed-edom three months, and the LORD blessed Obed-edom and all his household; and it was told David, "The LORD has blessed the house of Obed-edom and all that belongs to him, because of the ark of God" (cf. 2 Sam 6:11-12, condensed rendering in substance).

Blessed: in the original, בָּרַךְ (bārak, H1288), to bless, bestow blessing. See BDB, s.v. ברך. [10] Note the list of blessing: Obed-edom himself, his whole household, and all that belonged to him — the blessing of the presence spills over the threshold: first to one person, then to a family, then to everything in his hands. What had this household done right? The text records nothing he offered or vowed; it records only that he received the ark and peaceably served as its neighbor and landlord for three months. The most underestimated way of being blessed in Scripture is hospitality: when others dare not receive, open the door. His name appears again later: his sons afterward became gatekeepers of the temple (cf. 1 Chr 26:4-8) — to a house that had sheltered the ark, God left a family office connected with the presence for generations. Note also how the news was reported: the blessing was so great that someone ran specially to tell the king. True blessing cannot be hidden; and it is this chapter's second lesson for David: the same ark — death on the threshing floor, blessing in Obed-edom's house; God did not change; what changed was the posture with which people approached Him. Hearing it, David did not envy and did not snatch; he said: fetch it — this time, by the rules.

### 2. Six Steps, One Sacrifice: The Rhythm Reverence Walks Out (2 Sam 6:12-13)

So David went and brought up the ark of God with gladness from the house of Obed-edom to the city of David; when those who bore the ark had gone six steps, he sacrificed an ox and a fattened animal (cf. 2 Sam 6:12-13, condensed rendering in substance).

"Bore": this time the original uses a verb of the carrying-on-shoulders strand, and the parallel account states the method more plainly: David himself summed up the cause of the first failure — "because we did not seek Him according to the rule" (cf. 1 Chr 15:13) — and commanded the Levites to carry it on their shoulders, "as Moses had commanded, according to the word of the LORD" (cf. 1 Chr 15:15). Six steps: in the original, צְעָדִים (ṣəʿāḏîm); a step is צַעַד (ṣaʿaḏ, H6806). See BDB, s.v. צעד. [11] Set the second procession beside the first: the first was seizing attention from the start — thirty thousand men, a full band, singing from beginning to end; the second takes six steps first, then offers a sacrifice. Six steps, short to the point of seeming absurd: just shouldered, six steps out, stop, slaughter an ox and offer it. Why? Because this time David had to verify one thing with his own ears: walking by God's method, will anyone die in the first six steps? The shadow of the threshing floor was still there; he dared not walk it all in one breath; he walked a short stretch, then offered a sacrifice, caught his breath, and confirmed once: God has accepted it — walk on. This is the rhythm reverence walks out: slowness is not small faith; it is that the last lesson was too costly. Preach this section to everyone who has been hurt in drawing near to God and now comes back trembling: God does not disdain your six short steps. He accepted David's sacrifice after six steps, just as He accepted the whole journey's song and dance that followed; the recovery of faith is in fact measured out six steps at a time. When the six steps are steady and the sacrifice is offered, you will find that without noticing you have reached the city gate — reverence walks slowly, but it arrives.

### 3. The Man Who Took Off the Royal Robe (2 Sam 6:14-15)

David danced before the LORD with all his might, wearing a linen ephod; and so David and all the house of Israel brought up the ark of the LORD with shouting and with the sound of the trumpet (cf. 2 Sam 6:14-15, condensed rendering in substance).

Ephod: in the original, אֵפוֹד (ʾēpôḏ, H646), the ephod worn by priests. See BDB, s.v. אפוד. [12] Danced: in the original, מְכַרְכֵּר (məkarkēr, H3769), to whirl in dancing, found in the whole Old Testament only in the two places in this chapter. See BDB, s.v. כרכר. [13] A second picture detail: all the house of Israel shouting and blowing trumpets — in the first procession those making music were David and the whole house; this time those carrying the ark are the Levites' shoulders, the one sacrificing is the king, those shouting are the whole house — each in his place. Look first at the ephod: that is not a royal robe. The robe speaks of authority; the ephod speaks of ministry — as a child Samuel wore a linen ephod (cf. 1 Sam 2:18), the garment of one who stands and serves before God. Before the ark David took off his identity as king and kept only his identity as a servant: today I have not come to be worshiped; I have come to worship. Then look at dancing with all his might: the original word means whirling and leaping, and "with all his might" means the whole person thrown in. This was no ceremonial nod; this was a king of nearly forty, before thirty thousand of his people, forgetting himself as he whirled and leapt. Scripture is never ashamed to record the bodily side of worship: Miriam, Moses' sister, took a tambourine and the women danced (cf. Ex 15:20); the Psalms command praising Him with tambourine and dance (cf. Ps 150:4); Proverbs says a joyful heart is good medicine — body and heart were never two separate ledgers (cf. Prov 17:22). On the New Testament side, Paul says presenting our bodies as a living sacrifice is our reasonable service (cf. Rom 12:1); and the Lord Jesus, speaking of true worship, fixes it on spirit and truth, not on forbidding the body (cf. John 4:23-24). David's dancing is not the whole of worship, but it is one vital sign of its reality: a person who has never once forgotten himself before God has probably kept his worship at the level of performance. Dignified worship is not necessarily false, but worship that is only dignified is often worship that has not yet truly met the King who sits enthroned on the cherubim.

## Part Three: The Eyes at the Window, and Becoming Even More Lowly (2 Sam 6:16-23)

### 1. The Same Scene, Two Pairs of Eyes (2 Sam 6:16-17)

As the ark of the LORD entered the city of David, Michal the daughter of Saul looked out of the window and saw King David leaping and dancing before the LORD, and she despised him in her heart (cf. 2 Sam 6:16, condensed rendering in substance).

Leaping: in the original, מְפַזֵּז (məpazzēz, H6339), to leap, spring. See BDB, s.v. פזז. [14] Despised: in the original, בָּזָה (bāzâ, H959), to despise, hold in contempt. See BDB, s.v. בזה. [15] Set the two locations side by side: David is in the street; Michal is at the window. As the ark enters the city, everyone in the city is walking, singing and blowing trumpets in the street; only the queen is upstairs, behind a window. It is not that she did not see; she saw from a distance and saw with prejudice: she saw only the dancing, not the ark; only the king's loss of composure, not the king's worship. The window is this chapter's sharpest image: a person at a window will forever only comment, never participate; only measure dignity, never experience the presence. The church never lacks window positions — safe, high up, no sweat, no robe to take off, no blessing to receive and no risk to run, yet able to pass judgment on all real worship in the street. The narrative then records the ark's setting up [20], the sacrifices, the blessing and the distribution of cakes (cf. 2 Sam 6:17-19, condensed rendering in substance): David blessed the whole congregation and gave every person, man and woman, a cake — worship lands where the whole people are fed and everyone goes home. The king danced before God, then turned around and became the man who distributes bread; true worship always ends in blessing people and feeding people. If, after a worship service disperses, no one has been blessed and no one has eaten their fill, that worship was most likely danced only for itself.

### 2. The First Battle at Home (2 Sam 6:20-22)

David returned to bless his household; and Michal the daughter of Saul came out to meet him and said, "How the king of Israel honored himself today, uncovering himself before the eyes of his servants' maids, as one of the vulgar fellows shamelessly uncovers himself!" David said to Michal, "It was before the LORD, who chose me above your father and all his house, to appoint me as prince over His people Israel; and I will make merry before the LORD. I will make myself yet more contemptible than this, and I will be abased in my own eyes; but by the maids of whom you have spoken, by them I shall be held in honor" (cf. 2 Sam 6:20-22, condensed rendering in substance).

Make merry: in the original, שָׂחַק (śāḥaq, H7832), to laugh, play, rejoice. See BDB, s.v. שחק. [16] Become yet more contemptible: the root is קָלַל (qālal, H7043), to be light, insignificant; "abased" is שָׁפָל (šāpāl, H8217), lowly. See BDB, s.v. קלל, s.v. שפל. [17][18] This dialogue must be taken apart sentence by sentence. Michal's words open with sarcasm: "How honored!" — said in reverse. The scale she uses is the maids' eyes: a king has lost his dignity before a group of maids. In her world the greatest thing is whether a king looks like a king — and that is exactly the family style of Saul's house: what Saul cared about most all his life was his weight in the people's eyes (cf. 1 Sam 15:30 — he begged Samuel to honor him before the elders and the people). David's answer has three clear layers. First, the object: "before the LORD" — this dance of mine was not danced for the maids, nor for you; it was danced before Him; before whom one stands decides whose scale is used. Second, the foundation: "who chose me above your father" — a sharp sentence, almost cruel; the preacher must not learn its tone for use at home; but understand its theology: my throne was not Saul's house's charity, but God's choosing, so the object of my allegiance and the standard of dignity are not set by the old measure of Saul's house. Third, the declaration: "I will become yet more contemptible." This is the chapter's crowning sentence. David did not say, I am humble enough already; he said, yet more: in his dictionary, lowliness is not a one-time posture but an endless adding. Proverbs says, "A man's pride will bring him low, but a humble spirit will obtain honor" (cf. Prov 29:23); in the New Testament the Lord Jesus gave this sentence its deepest footnote: He, being in the form of God, emptied Himself (cf. Phil 2:6-8), and said, "Whoever humbles himself will be exalted" (cf. Matt 23:12); Peter instructs all to clothe themselves with humility, for God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble (cf. 1 Pet 5:5-6). What David said below the window is exactly this law running through both Testaments: honor is not in the hands of those who grab at dignity, but in the hands of those willing to add lowliness.

**Ignition point (the second):** Brothers and sisters, in this city today there are two positions: in the street, and at the window. Those in the street sweat, take off their robes, and are pointed at; those at the window are clean, clear-headed, and right in every sentence. In the Lord's name I call you: come down from the window. Today — not waiting for tomorrow — trade the time you spend commenting on worship, on the church, on other people, for once standing truly before Him: sing aloud, pray aloud, be lowly to the end before God. However many years you have sat at the window, that many years the blessing in the street has had nothing to do with you. The ark has already entered the city; do not let the whole city eat its fill and go home while you alone stand at the window — cold, hungry, and correct.

### 3. A Life Without Bearing Children (2 Sam 6:23)

And Michal the daughter of Saul had no child to the day of her death (cf. 2 Sam 6:23, condensed rendering in substance).

The chapter's last sentence is austere in style, without a trace of warmth. It does not explain, does not try the case; it only registers a fact, and calls her one last time "the daughter of Saul" — not David's wife. The narrator closes with this title to leave Michal forever in Saul's household register: where her heart stood, there her name belongs. Commentators' readings of causation in this verse have been registered at the head of this manuscript, not adjudicated; but the narrative's juxtaposition itself preaches: a person who kept contempt in her heart has no share from then on in the future of David's house — David's genealogy runs on to Solomon, and to that King in the first chapter of Matthew's Gospel (cf. Matt 1:1, 6); Michal's name is not on that line. Here the preacher must slow his voice: this is not material for gloating; it is the period at the end of a tragedy. She was originally the one who loved David most — Scripture expressly says Michal loved David (cf. 1 Sam 18:20), and she once let David down from a window and saved his life (cf. 1 Sam 19:11-17); note — again a window: in those days she saved a man at the window; now she despises a man at the window. The same window, two endings; the only difference is who is in the heart: then, love was inside the window; now, bitterness is inside the window. Bitterness is never grown in a day: used as a pawn by her father, demanded as a condition by her husband, moved back and forth by two dynasties — her bitterness has an origin. So this verse is preached not to scold her, but to pity her, and to turn the mirror toward the hearers: what is your heart's window looking out at?

## Application: Six Kinds of People

First, to servants whose zeal abounds but whose methods are self-invented. The new cart is your mirror. Concrete assignment: write down the most bustling ministry in your hands, check it item by item against Scripture's express words and the church's established order, and wherever the method was changed, for ease or for spectacle, away from God's command, stop it this week, correct it first, then restart; zeal undiminished, method wholly set right.

Second, to those who have stalled their faith out of fear. David's three months are both your assignment and your comfort. Concrete assignment: admit the thing you stopped; find a spiritual elder to work through the cause of the failure with you — was the method wrong, or the people? Once it is clear, set a six-steps-style restart: take only a small step, offer the sacrifice and confirm first; do not rush back to full speed in one breath, and do not stay stopped forever.

Third, to those who have received God and whose homes are presently blessed. Obed-edom is your example. Concrete assignment: open your home once this week — receive one person who does not dare enter a church, one meal, one listening ear; and tell one concrete way your home has been blessed to someone still living in fear, learning from Obed-edom's house to let blessing become news reported to others.

Fourth, to believers who only comment from the window. Michal's window is your warning. Concrete assignment: within this month stop all remote commentary on worship and co-workers, and instead do three things in the street: arrive early once to prepare, actively bless one person after a service once, and once intercede aloud in prayer for the co-worker you most want to comment on. For every comment, one intercession — repay the debt of commentary with intercession.

Fifth, to those despised at home for their faith. David's first battle at home is your situation. Concrete assignment: do not imitate the sharp tone of David's answer; imitate only his firm stand — neither give up worship for dignity, nor wound a spouse for a stand; this week arrange one time together that does not touch the faith dispute, to nurse the relationship back first; write the disputed matters down and hand them to a pastor to mediate — do not confront in the heat of anger.

Sixth, to those who hold dignity and status and cannot lower themselves. "Become yet more lowly" is your training. Concrete assignment: choose a maid's task — sweeping, washing dishes, keeping the door, driving and picking people up — and do it with your own hands four times this month, without leaving your name, without photos, without posting. When done, ask yourself only one question: before Him, where does my honor actually come from?

## Closing Prayer of Response

Lord, we gather up 2 Samuel 6 into response. Take down our new carts and give us the Levites' shoulders; do not leave us in our three months of stalling, and bless, after the pattern of Obed-edom's house, those willing to receive You; call us down from the window, take off the royal robes we cannot bear to take off, and teach us before You to be wholehearted, joyful, and ever more lowly; stop the contempt in our hearts and heal the wounds faith has raised in our homes. May Your ark be settled among us; may our worship in the end all become blessing and the distribution of bread, so that men and women, old and young, all eat their fill and go home. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotion, 300-500 words in the original)

In 2 Samuel 6, David first welcomed the ark using a new cart drawn by oxen; at the threshing floor of Nacon the oxen stumbled, Uzzah stretched out his hand to hold the ark, and died beside it (cf. 2 Sam 6:3-7, condensed rendering in substance). David was afraid and left the ark at Obed-edom's house for three months, and that household was greatly blessed because of the ark (cf. 2 Sam 6:10-12, condensed rendering in substance). The second time, David carried it on shoulders according to the law, and offered a sacrifice after only six steps; wearing a linen ephod, he danced before the LORD with all his might (cf. 2 Sam 6:13-14, condensed rendering in substance). Michal despised him at the window, and he answered: I will become even more lowly (cf. 2 Sam 6:16, 21-22, condensed rendering in substance). Three questions for today's meditation: Is there a new cart in my service — the zeal real, but the method not God's? After my fear, did I make up the lesson and start again, or stop for good? When I worship, am I standing in the street or sitting at the window? Prayer: Lord, take down my new cart and give me shoulders; call me down from the window and teach me to become ever more lowly before You.

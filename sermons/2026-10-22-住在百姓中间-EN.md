## Summary (about 100 words)

Chapter 25 gives the furniture; chapter 26 gives the dwelling itself. Ten curtains of fine linen are coupled with clasps of gold into one tabernacle; the goats' hair tent covering and the leather covers lie layer upon layer above; boards of shittim wood stand in sockets of silver. Deepest within, a veil embroidered with cherubim divides the holy place from the most holy: the ark within, the table and the candlestick without. This chapter has no story, only a blueprint — yet it gives a shape to a promise: I will dwell among them.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the last chapter we stopped within the blueprint on the mountain. God told Moses to take from the people a willing offering — gold, silver, and brass; blue, purple, and scarlet; fine linen and goats' hair; rams' skins dyed red and badgers' skins; shittim wood — every item named. Then, piece by piece, He gave the dimensions and the making of the ark and the mercy seat, the table of shewbread, and the golden candlestick. In verse 8 of chapter 25, God states the purpose of it all as clearly as it can be stated: "And let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them." Chapter 26, which we read today, contains not a single story and not a single conversation; from beginning to end there are only instructions for making: how long and how wide this curtain, how that board is to be tenoned, on which pillars the veil is to hang. A first-time reader easily grows drowsy here, feeling that the numbers are many, the workmanship over-detailed, and the whole chapter unrelated to daily life. But first see this chapter's position clearly: chapter 25 is the furniture; chapter 26 is the dwelling itself. First the ark, the table, and the lamp — then the tent is pitched to house them. This order is marked in this manuscript as a homiletical observation, not as the words of the text: God's promised presence comes before the building, and the furniture before the shell; the tabernacle is not a house into which God is invited to move, but a dwelling that men pitched according to a promise God had already given. Reverse the order and men will think a building can purchase presence; set the order right and men learn that the building is only the shape of the promise, and presence itself is the tabernacle's very substance.

Let us first see the structure of the whole chapter clearly. Its thirty-seven verses fall roughly into five parts. The first is the ten curtains, verses 1 to 6: fine linen with thread of three colours, cherubim embroidered upon the curtains, five curtains coupled into one set and the two sets joined by clasps of gold, becoming one tabernacle. The second is the tent covering, verses 7 to 13: eleven curtains of goats' hair as a tent over the tabernacle, somewhat larger than the inner curtains, hanging over at front and sides to cover them. The third is the cover, verse 14 alone: rams' skins dyed red as a covering for the tent, and above them a covering of badgers' skins. The fourth is the boards and sockets, verses 15 to 29: boards of shittim wood standing upright, twenty boards on the north side and twenty on the south, six boards for the west side with two corner boards, sockets of silver bearing them, and bars passed through rings locking them together, raising the tabernacle into a frame that can be taken apart. The fifth is the veil and the hanging for the door, verses 31 to 37: the veil embroidered with cherubim hangs on four pillars overlaid with gold, dividing the holy place from the most holy; at the door hangs another curtain, standing on five pillars overlaid with gold and sockets of brass. When the five parts have been read, a whole tabernacle stands before us from inside to outside, from roof to walls, from its deepest chamber to its doorway: innermost, the splendid fine linen; outermost, the rough leather cover; deepest, the most holy place; then the holy place; then the court — the ordering of nearness is preached by this chapter once more, this time in architecture.

In preaching this chapter, this manuscript still keeps the rules of this series, stated honestly at the outset. First, dimensions and materials are taken up only where they relate to the main line, never spread out cubit by cubit: of the cubits, panels, and clasps the text gives, this manuscript treats only those that carry the chapter's main line — for example, the ten curtains coupled into one, the tent covering's extra breadth hanging over, and the complete frame formed by the number of the boards; the remaining details stay in the text, to be read verse by verse in study, not piled up here into a bill of materials. Second, every symbolic use is marked as such: to say that the innermost is the most splendid and the outermost the plainest is a structural observation drawn from this chapter's order of materials from inside out; to say that the glory is within and that from outside one sees only a tent is a homiletical use, not something the text says directly. Third, New Testament readings are marked as New Testament readings: that the veil was rent in twain from the top to the bottom when the Lord Jesus breathed His last is Matthew's record and the New Testament authors' retrospect; when this manuscript reaches the veil it will cite it and mark its source, and will never pour that later tearing back into what this chapter's veil meant at the time — in chapter 26 the veil's plain sense is division, a division God Himself commanded, and that must first be preached fully and rightly.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them. According to all that I shew thee, after the pattern of the tabernacle, and the pattern of all the instruments thereof, even so shall ye make it." (Exodus 25:8-9, KJV, excerpt)

"Moreover thou shalt make the tabernacle with ten curtains of fine twined linen, and blue, and purple, and scarlet: with cherubims of cunning work shalt thou make them. … The five curtains shall be coupled together one to another; and other five curtains shall be coupled one to another. … And thou shalt make fifty taches of gold, and couple the curtains together with the taches: and it shall be one tabernacle." (Exodus 26:1, 3, 6, KJV, excerpt)

"And thou shalt hang up the vail under the taches, that thou mayest bring in thither within the vail the ark of the testimony: and the vail shall divide unto you between the holy place and the most holy." (Exodus 26:33, KJV, excerpt)

"And thou shalt make an hanging for the door of the tent, of blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine twined linen, wrought with needlework." (Exodus 26:36, KJV, excerpt)

Let us pray: Lord, You said that You would dwell in the midst of Your people, and You showed us the pattern of Your dwelling inch by inch. Open our eyes today to see Your heart for presence in the curtains, the boards, and the sockets of silver; open our hearts to understand why Your holiness must have a dividing, and why Your nearness must have an order; and teach us to long for the presence within the veil, learning to walk toward You step by step along the way You have appointed. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Ten Curtains Become One Tabernacle — The Fine Linen Curtains, the Golden Coupling, and the Cherubim on the Curtains (26:1-6)

The chapter's opening sentence names the whole tabernacle at once: "Moreover thou shalt make the tabernacle with ten curtains." The word rendered "tabernacle" is, in the original, mishkan (מִשְׁכָּן, H4908) [1], whose plain sense is a dwelling, an abode, from the root meaning to settle down and abide. In Exodus 25:8 God says that He will dwell among the people, and that word "dwell" comes from the same root as mishkan — the tabernacle is not, in the first instance, the proper name of a religious building; the word literally means the dwelling of the God who will settle down there. The text first names the dwelling, then gives the instructions for making it: God's presence still walks ahead of the building; even the name carries the meaning of presence first. The ten curtains are this dwelling's first layer, and its innermost, lying closest to the holy things. Their materials are listed in verse 1 with great refinement: fine twined linen, and blue, and purple, and scarlet. The fine linen is a clean white; the three colours are a king's colours — blue as the sky, purple for honour, scarlet deep and grave; woven together, these four make the most splendid layer of cloth in the whole tabernacle. One clause is added about the making: "with cherubims of cunning work shalt thou make them." Embroidered — not printed, not painted, but worked stitch by stitch by the cunning hand of the craftsman. Here this manuscript offers an exposition and marks it as such: the innermost layer of God's dwelling must not be shoddily made; the finest workmanship is required — not because God needs display, but because the people offer their most painstaking skill in the place nearest to God.

Notice first where the cherubim stand. The cherubim are embroidered on the curtains; whoever is inside and lifts his eyes sees them upon the curtains overhead. Here an intertext must be cited, and this manuscript marks it as an intertextual observation: in Genesis 3:24, after man was driven out of Eden, God placed at the east of the garden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life. The same kind of angel, the same office: guarding the way that leads inward. In Eden, the cherubim guarded a way that had been closed, and man was kept outside the garden; at Sinai, the cherubim embroidered on the curtains guard a way that is being opened, yet still in order, and man is led inward step by step. Read side by side, the two passages change neither text's original meaning, yet together they let us hear this chapter's weight: the tabernacle does not fling Eden's gate wide open again for men to wander in and out; the tabernacle is another way of approach that God opens in the wilderness — a guarded, ordered way. The gate is opening, and the guard is still there; not a fraction of the holiness is discounted.

Consider next the curtains' measure and joining. Each curtain is twenty-eight cubits long and four cubits wide, and all ten are of one measure. This manuscript does not spread out the measurements item by item; it takes only one point that belongs to the main line: all ten curtains are of exactly one measure, and not one of them alters its own dimensions on its own initiative — the oneness that follows is hidden first in this uniformity. The joining is done in two steps: first five curtains are coupled into one set, and another five into a second set, forming two great sheets; on the edge of each sheet where they meet, fifty loops of blue are made, loop answering to loop; then fifty taches of gold are made, and the two sheets are hooked together with them. Verse 6 closes the section with very few words of very great weight: "and it shall be one tabernacle." These ten curtains are called, in the original, yeri'ot (יְרִיעֹת, H3407) [2], meaning lengths of cloth, curtain panels; a single yeri'ah by itself is only a piece of cloth — ten coupled and hooked together become the mishkan. This is exactly this section's main line, and this manuscript states the text's structure directly: oneness is not natural; it is made — made by one common measure, prepared by loops set evenly in place, and finally clasped, clasp by clasp, with fifty taches of gold. The place where the gold taches join is precisely the place where the two sheets meet, and also the place under the greatest strain in the whole layer of curtains; at the seam, of all places, the text uses gold. As a homiletical exposition, marked as such: God's dwelling becomes one whole not by melting ten lengths of cloth into a single seamless robe, but by each curtain keeping its own measure and being fastened to the others with gold at the joints. The people later become one worshipping community in the same way: not by everyone ceasing to be himself, but by each one keeping his appointed place and all being clasped to one another at the joints, becoming one tabernacle.

Note also that these ten curtains of fine linen could not normally be seen in full by the people. They lie within the boards and beneath the tent covering; a man standing in the court and looking up sees only the tent covering and the leather covers above; only the priests serving inside the holy place, in the light of the candlestick, could see the fine linen and the cherubim on the curtains. This manuscript marks this as an observation drawn from this chapter's layering: the tabernacle's most splendid layer is for those inside to see, not for those outside. God's dwelling does not draw men by its exterior; it hides its finest colours and workmanship near to His presence. This stands in exact contrast with the tent covering and covers of the next section: the further out, the plainer; the further in, the more splendid. From its very first section, the blueprint of this chapter fixes this direction.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Layer upon Layer Outward, and the Frame Stood Upright — The Goats' Hair Tent, the Leather Covers, the Boards and the Silver Sockets (26:7-29)

The second part moves one layer outward from the fine linen. Verse 7: "And thou shalt make curtains of goats' hair to be a covering upon the tabernacle." Goats' hair is coarse, dark in colour, and hard-wearing — exactly suited to a tent's outer layer against wind and rain. The eleven tent curtains are each thirty cubits long and four cubits wide, each two cubits longer than the inner linen curtains; their joining resembles the inner curtains — five coupled into one set and six into another, with fifty loops on each edge — except that the clasps change material: gold taches for the inner curtains, taches of brass for the tent. Verses 9 and 12–13 explain the extra measure: the remnant of the tent curtains hangs over, a cubit on this side and a cubit on that side, covering the tabernacle on both sides; and the extra sixth curtain is doubled over at the forefront of the tent. Hear clearly the intention behind these numbers; this manuscript states the structure directly: the tent covering must be larger than the inner curtains because its office is to shelter — without that extra cubit, the fine linen at the edges would be exposed to wind and rain; without that doubled layer, there would be a gap above the doorway. Protection must always be a size larger, and a layer thicker, than what it protects. Above the tent covering there are two further covers. Verse 14 stacks two layers within a single verse: "And thou shalt make a covering for the tent of rams' skins dyed red, and a covering above of badgers' skins." Badgers' skins, in the original tachash (תַּחַשׁ, H8476) [3]: the exact referent of this word is still discussed among scholars to this day. One view takes it as the skin of a marine animal — a seal or dolphin of some kind — hence the rendering "badgers' skins" in some versions and "sea cow" or seal in others, and the Chinese Union Version's rendering as seal skin; another view takes it as some kind of finely processed leather; yet another, on the basis of an Egyptian loanword, holds it to be a kind of tanned leather material. This manuscript sets the views side by side and issues no dogmatic ruling, taking only the one point the text can state directly: it is the outermost, most weather-resisting layer of leather covering.

Now the tabernacle's four layers, from inside to outside, stand complete: innermost, the fine linen with cherubim in blue, purple, and scarlet; outside it, the dark goats' hair tent; outside that, the rams' skins dyed red; outermost, the plain, hard-wearing leather cover. Layer upon layer outward, the plainer; layer upon layer inward, the more splendid — this is a structural observation this manuscript draws from the order of materials, marked here as such. A man in the wilderness looking at this tabernacle from afar would see only a weather-beaten tent, barely different from the tents of the wandering people themselves; the glory is not on the surface; the glory is within. As a homiletical use, marked as such: God's presence is often like this — He does not paint His glory on the outer wall for passers-by to gawk at; He sets His glory in the depths of the dwelling, and only the man who walks in along His appointed way will see it. This use does not alter this chapter's plain sense: the chapter commands only the order of the materials; it is the preacher who reads the character of presence out of that order, and the hearer must be able to tell which sentences are the text and which are the use made of it.

The tent covering and covers concern the roof; the boards and sockets concern the walls and the foundation. Verse 15 turns to the frame: "And thou shalt make boards for the tabernacle of shittim wood standing up." Shittim wood is acacia wood, hard in grain and resistant to decay — the finest timber the wilderness could supply; later verses command that the boards be overlaid with gold: wood within, gold without, standing as a wall of gold. Boards, in the original qerashim (קְרָשִׁים, H7175) [4], singular qeresh, denoting an upright board, a frame board. Of the specifications, only the main line is taken: each board is ten cubits long and a cubit and a half wide, with two tenons at its foot that fit together; each board is matched with two sockets of silver, one tenon entering one socket. The numbers must be fixed in mind, because the numbers form the frame: twenty boards for the north side of the tabernacle and twenty for the south side, with forty sockets of silver beneath each side; for the west side — the tabernacle's rear wall — six boards, with two more boards made for the two corners of the rear wall, eight boards in all and sixteen sockets of silver; the east side is the doorway, where no boards are set, but the hanging for the door. Every board stands by its tenons in sockets of silver; not one board is nailed fast into the ground. And board is locked to board by bars: five bars to a side, the middle bar running from end to end of the boards, the other four distributed above and below; the bars are also of shittim wood overlaid with gold, passing through rings of gold on the boards. Tenons, sockets, bars, and rings — four things together — set forty-eight loose boards upright as a dwelling whose walls are complete, yet which can be taken apart everywhere.

That it can be taken apart and carried — this is this section's most important line, and this manuscript marks it as exposition drawn from the structure: this dwelling has no foundation trench and no masonry wall; every board can be lifted out of its silver socket, every bar drawn out of its golden ring, every curtain unhooked and rolled up — because the God of the covenant does not intend to settle Himself in one place and wait for men to come and pay Him court; He will walk with His people through the wilderness. When the people break camp, the tabernacle is taken down and carried by the Levites in the midst of the host; when the people encamp, the tabernacle is pitched again at the centre of the camp. Yesterday's fine linen, gold taches, and silver sockets are all packed up for the road today, and tomorrow are set up again one by one. Brothers and sisters, what did this mean for Israel? It meant that their God was not the god of one locality, not the god of one temple, but a God who travels on the road with them: where they go, He goes; where they stop, He stops. Later the tabernacle would enter Canaan and stand at Shiloh, and later still Solomon would build a fixed temple — that is a later story; in this chapter's blueprint, the first dwelling God makes for Himself is a tent — and the first lesson of tent theology is companionship on the journey.

One further item must not be missed: where the silver of the sockets came from, this chapter does not say; this manuscript cites the same book and marks the source: Exodus chapters 30 and 38 record that at the numbering of the people, every man gave half a shekel of silver as the ransom money, and that the tabernacle's sockets of silver were cast precisely from this ransom silver (Exodus 38:25-28, cited here as an intra-book intertext). If this intertext holds, the picture is complete in the extreme: the whole dwelling stands upon the price of redemption; every gold-overlaid board has its feet planted in the record of the people's ransom. This manuscript marks the source clearly, so that hearers may compare the two passages for themselves; even holding to this chapter alone, the conclusion does not move: the boards stand firm not because the wood is hard, but because every board is borne up by a socket, and socket and tenon fit each other exactly — God's dwelling stands in the wilderness never because its surroundings are stable, but because of the manner of joining that He Himself appointed.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Veil Dividing the Holy Place from the Most Holy — The Veil, the Ark Within It, and the Hanging for the Door (26:31-37)

The third part comes to the eye of the whole chapter: the veil. Verse 31: "And thou shalt make a vail of blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine twined linen of cunning work: with cherubims shall it be made." This veil, in the original parokhet (פָּרֹכֶת, H6532) [5], is the term used in the tabernacle and temple literature specifically for that veil which divides off the most holy place; it is not a curtain in the general sense. Its materials are exactly the same as the ten curtains — the same fine linen, the same thread of three colours, the same cherubim worked by the cunning craftsman; the difference lies not in material but in position and office: the curtains are spread overhead, the veil stands upright before one's face; the curtains cover the dwelling into one whole, the veil divides the dwelling into two spaces. Its hanging is given in verse 32: the veil is to hang upon four pillars of shittim wood overlaid with gold, whose hooks are of gold, standing upon four sockets of silver — the same foundation as the boards. Verse 33 gives the veil's defining sentence, the weightiest sentence in the whole chapter: the veil is hung under the taches, and the ark of the testimony is brought in within the veil; "and the vail shall divide unto you between the holy place and the most holy." Divide, in the original hivdil (הִבְדִּיל, H914) [6], the causative form of the verb "to separate, to distinguish." This word appears again and again in Genesis chapter 1: God divided the light from the darkness, divided the waters from the waters, divided the day from the night — dividing is God's old way of working; at Sinai He uses a veil to divide the inside of the dwelling from its outside.

The furnishings on the veil's two sides are stated with complete clarity in verses 34 and 35. Within the veil is the most holy place: only the ark of the testimony, with the mercy seat upon it. Outside the veil is the holy place: the table of shewbread set on the north side, and the candlestick on the south side, over against the table. The holy place's order is thereby fixed — and the order is fixed from inside outward: first the innermost ark is settled, then the table and the lamp outside; first the most holy within the veil, then the daily service outside it. This manuscript offers a structural observation and marks it as such: nearness in the tabernacle is layered — the further in one goes, the fewer the furnishings and the fewer the people, until at the very inside there remain only the ark and the mercy seat, which even the high priest could enter only once a year, and then with blood (Leviticus chapter 16, cited here as a legal intertext). This layering is not God putting on airs; it is God teaching a nation fresh out of Egypt: nearness to Him is real, and His holiness is real; real nearness must walk within real division. The veil is not a wall; it is cloth — it reminds men that the division is real, yet is not an eternal refusal; it hangs on hooks of gold, a cloth that can be lifted — only the time and manner of its lifting are decided not by man, but by God.

It is precisely for this reason that the New Testament, when it comes to this veil, writes with such weight. By its rule, this manuscript marks what follows as the New Testament's reading, not this chapter's plain sense: Matthew 27:51 records that at the moment the Lord Jesus gave up the ghost on the cross, the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom; and the author of Hebrews speaks of the veil in saying that Christ has consecrated for us a new and living way through the veil (Hebrews 10:20, the New Testament's exposition). The New Testament authors plainly look back on the veil of this chapter and of the later temple, and in Christ read out of it the new sense of a division removed. This manuscript, in citing this, draws the line clearly: the veil in Exodus chapter 26, in its plain sense, divides by God's command; that division was in its day part of the order of worship — not a malfunction, still less a mistake; the tearing happened centuries later, at the moment of Christ's suffering; it is the New Testament's fulfilment, and cannot be read backward as though Moses, in hanging the veil, were already rehearsing its tearing. With the line drawn, the echo stands and is precious, and the sequence is exactly reversed: in this chapter, the veil is hung up, and only then do men learn what is holy and what is common; in the Gospels, the veil is rent open, and only then do men understand that the division had all along been preparing for the day when, in Christ, they would be brought into a deeper nearness. Hanging and rending — both acts are from God's hand, and between them lie all the years of the old covenant's service.

The chapter's last two verses give the hanging for the door. Verse 36: "And thou shalt make an hanging for the door of the tent, of blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine twined linen, wrought with needlework." Note two differences from the veil. First, the making: the veil is made with cunning work, embroidered with cherubim; the hanging for the door is said only to be wrought with needlework, and the text mentions cherubim no more — this panel at the doorway is plainer in pattern. Second, the pillars and sockets: the veil uses four pillars and four sockets of silver; the hanging for the door uses five pillars overlaid with gold, whose hooks are of gold, but whose sockets are of brass. The road from outside inward stands complete at this chapter's end: the people come in through the court gate and stand in the court; the priest lifts the hanging for the door and enters the holy place, serving between the table and the candlestick; the high priest passes through the veil and enters the most holy place, standing before the mercy seat. Court, holy place, most holy place — three spaces, three degrees of nearness — one line with the boundaries below Sinai in chapter 19 and the three levels of ascent in chapter 24: the people below the mountain, the elders worshipping on the mountain afar off, Moses entering the cloud alone (chapters 19 and 24 cited here as intra-book intertexts). God has not changed His character: He will dwell in the midst of His people, and He will also have His people learn, level by level, to draw near to Him; and this chapter gives that nearness a roof, raises it walls, divides its inside from its outside, and leaves it a doorway — presence has from now on a shape, and nearness a road.

Look once more at this door alone, for it is the last picture this chapter leaves us. There is only one hanging for the door, and only one road in and out: the priests, entering the holy place each day to trim the lamps, to burn incense, and to set out the shewbread, all pass through this hanging; the people bring their offerings to the gate of the court, yet may not enter this door. This manuscript offers an observation and marks it as such: this chapter leaves presence a door, and sets the door a boundary — the door stands open for those who serve to walk through; it is not torn down for everyone to stare through. Compare again the number and material of pillars and sockets: five pillars and five sockets of brass for the hanging of the door; four pillars and four sockets of silver for the veil — the further out, the more common the material, yet the wider the road of entry; the further in, the more precious the material, yet the fewer who may walk it. This contrast of width and preciousness is the last lesson this chapter sets out with its own hands: God's dwelling in the midst of His people was never a vague nearness, but a dwelling together that has a door, a road, an order, and a division. If a man is willing to walk inward from the doorway, level by level, he will discover that every layer of cloth, every board, and every veil is on its surface a division, but in fact a leading: what blocks a man was never these cloths and boards, but his own rushing and breaking in; and what leads a man was never his own zeal and ability, but the road God Himself appointed. At the chapter's end the whole tabernacle stands complete: four layers for its roof, walls of gold-overlaid boards, a veil within, and a hanging at its front — God's promise to dwell in their midst has here received its complete shape, waiting only for Moses to come down the mountain and pitch it according to the pattern.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 26. Remember first its order: the furniture first, the dwelling after; the promise of presence first, the blueprint of the building after. Remember next the coupling of the ten curtains: five coupled to five, fifty taches of gold clasping them into one tabernacle — oneness is made, not merely proclaimed. Remember also the direction of the four layers: the further out, the plainer; the further in, the more splendid; from outside, only a tent is seen — the glory is within. Remember again the silver sockets beneath the boards: forty-eight boards, every one with its tenons, every one with its socket, and the whole dwelling able to be taken down, lifted up, and carried with the people through the wilderness — our God is a God who goes with us. Remember last that veil: by command it divides the holy place from the most holy, the ark within, the table and the candlestick without; nearness is layered, and the division has meaning. In the New Testament's reading, it will one day be rent from the top to the bottom when Christ breathes His last — but in this chapter it must first be honestly hung in place. May we learn the blueprint's lessons today as well: to offer our finest workmanship in the place nearest to Him, to draw near to Him level by level in His appointed order — never rushing, never breaking in, and never stopping outside the door either.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You that You are willing to dwell in our midst; thank You for showing us the pattern of Your dwelling in such detail, and for making that dwelling able to be taken down and carried, accompanying Your people on the wilderness road. Forgive us for so often looking only at the outer leather cover and thinking that we know You; lead us inward along Your appointed road — through the court, into the holy place — longing for the presence within the veil. Teach us to keep our own appointed place, and at the joints to be clasped to our brothers and sisters, becoming one dwelling for You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] mishkan: Hebrew מִשְׁכָּן (H4908), tabernacle, dwelling, abode, from the verb to settle down and abide (shakan, H7931); Exodus 26:1 — "thou shalt make the tabernacle with ten curtains" — uses this word, the general name for the whole tabernacle section of the book. The sanctuary and the pattern shown in Exodus 25:8-9 answer to it; John 1:14 — "the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us" — uses a Greek verb meaning to pitch a tent, often regarded as a New Testament echo of this presence theme; the text of this manuscript does not develop it, and it is noted here only. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[2] yeri'ot: Hebrew יְרִיעֹת (singular yeri'ah, H3407), lengths of cloth, curtains, tent panels; Exodus 26:1-6 denotes the ten curtains of fine linen of which the tabernacle is made, and 26:7-13 turns to the eleven tent curtains of goats' hair — the same word covering the inner and outer layers of cloth alike; the text of this manuscript calls them the fine linen curtains and the goats' hair tent covering respectively, to avoid confusion. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[3] tachash: Hebrew תַּחַשׁ (H8476); Exodus 26:14 denotes the outermost leather covering of the tent. The Chinese Union Version renders it as seal skin (the KJV has "badgers' skins"). Its exact referent is discussed among scholars: one view takes it as the skin of a marine animal such as a seal or dolphin; another as a finely tanned leather; another connects it with an Egyptian loanword denoting a kind of processed leather material. The ancient versions likewise differ (the LXX renders it with a term for coloured leather goods). This manuscript sets the views side by side and issues no dogmatic ruling; its text takes only the sense of the outermost weather-resisting leather covering. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT, with reference to the versions' treatments.

[4] qerashim: Hebrew קְרָשִׁים (singular qeresh, H7175), boards, frame boards; Exodus 26:15-29 denotes the boards of shittim wood overlaid with gold, made with two tenons and standing in sockets of silver: twenty on the north side and twenty on the south, six for the west side with two corner boards — forty-eight boards in all — each locked to the next by bars of wood passing through rings of gold. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[5] parokhet: Hebrew פָּרֹכֶת (H6532), the veil, denoting specifically the veil that divides the holy place from the most holy; Exodus 26:31-35 is its locus: made of fine linen with blue, purple, and scarlet, with cherubim worked by the cunning craftsman, hung on the golden hooks of four pillars overlaid with gold — within it, the most holy place and the ark of the testimony; outside it, the holy place with the table and the candlestick. Leviticus chapter 16, where the high priest enters once a year within the veil, uses the same word. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[6] hivdil: Hebrew הִבְדִּיל (H914, the causative form of the verb badal), to cause to divide, to distinguish, to separate; Exodus 26:33 — "the vail shall divide unto you between the holy place and the most holy" — uses this word. Genesis 1:4, 6, 7, 14, and 18, God's dividing work in creation, uses the same word — a same-word intertext, read alongside in the text of this manuscript. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[7] The New Testament's reading of the veil: Matthew 27:51 records that when the Lord Jesus gave up the ghost, the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom; Hebrews 10:19-20 teaches that believers enter the holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way which He has consecrated for us, through the veil. This is the New Testament authors' retrospect and exposition, in Christ, upon the tabernacle's veil, not the plain sense of Exodus chapter 26 in its original setting; where the text of this manuscript cites it, the source and the line have been marked. See also Hebrews 9:3 on the most holy place within the second veil.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And it shall be one tabernacle." (Exodus 26:6, KJV) "And the vail shall divide unto you between the holy place and the most holy." (Exodus 26:33, KJV)

Meditate today on three things about the tabernacle. The first is joining: the ten curtains were ten separate pieces of cloth, and only when fifty taches of gold fastened them at the seams did they become one dwelling. It is the same with us and our brothers and sisters: oneness does not mean the end of all distinction, but each one keeping his place and all being fastened to one another at the joints. The second is foundation: the forty-eight boards all stand by their tenons in sockets of silver; not one of them stands by itself. Our steadiness is the same — not resting on our own stubborn strength, but on the bearing He Himself has prepared. The third is dividing: the veil reminds us that there is a way of drawing near that God Himself has appointed — no rushing, no breaking in, but going in step by step. From outside, the tabernacle looked like nothing more than a weather-beaten tent, yet the glory was within. Ask the Lord today for a heart that does not judge by outward appearance, and for the obedience that is willing to be joined together and to draw near in His appointed order, so that our homes and our days may become a place where He is willing to dwell, resting securely in His dwelling. He who is willing to dwell in the midst of His people will not disdain the dust of the wilderness or the jolting of the road; we have only to prepare ourselves according to His pattern, and His presence will turn the plainest tent into a sanctuary.

## Summary (about 100 words)

As the Book of the Covenant draws to a close, it first sets the rules of the judgment seat: spread no rumour, bear no false witness, follow no crowd in wrongdoing, take no bribe — and even an enemy's straying livestock must be brought back. Then God lays His hand on time itself: the land must rest, servants and livestock must breathe, and three times a year the feasts are kept in His presence. Finally an angel walks before the people and leads them into Canaan; idols are to be destroyed, no covenant made, and the enemies driven out little by little, year by year.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

We have read through the Book of the Covenant step by step — first the people in the home, then the oxen and pits in the courtyard, then lending and restitution. God's justice has walked step by step, and today it arrives at a place full of people and noise: the judgment seat. What the judgment seat never lacks is people. Where there are many people, voices grow loud; where voices grow loud, the truth is easily drowned out. One unverified word spreads, the anger of a crowd is lit, and if the judge follows the sound of the crowd, a wrongful case is made just like that. The second sentence of this chapter, only a few words long, lights up the eye of the whole chapter: Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil. This sentence is the eye of the chapter, and this manuscript's title comes from it. That everyone is saying something does not mean it is so; that the crowd is falling to one side does not mean that a person standing among God's people should fall with it — he must stand firm, must verify, must judge a case by the facts and not by the noise.

Hold the structure of this chapter in hand first; it is neater than the previous chapter. The first nine verses deal with judgment: on the bench, in the witness box, and between neighbours, how does justice stand firm? Verses 10 to 13 deal with rest: the land is tilled for six years and left in the seventh, and even oxen and asses, and servants, must breathe on the Sabbath. Verses 14 to 19 deal with the feasts: three times a year the males appear before the Lord, bringing the firstfruits, not empty-handed. Finally, from verse 20 to the end of the chapter, comes a word that is both promise and warning: God sends an angel before the people to lead them into the land of Canaan; if the people obey, God will be an adversary to their adversaries, but if they turn to worship the gods of Canaan and make a covenant with its inhabitants, that land will become a snare to them. In preaching this chapter, this manuscript still keeps the three rules laid down in the previous two chapters. First, grasp the structure and the order; do not spread out every verse. Second, distinguish facts from exposition and identify them as such: what the text plainly says is preached as the text says it; where the sermon extends it to today, that is identified as exposition, not passed off as the text's original meaning. Third, disputed points are presented together with no conclusion drawn: this chapter has at least two — the original meaning of the sentence about seething a kid, and the identity of the angel — on which scholars through the ages have held different readings; we set out the common views and do not rush to a verdict. Holding these three rules, we read honestly and hear steadily.

Let us read several key passages of today's text together:

"Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil; neither shalt thou speak in a cause to decline after many to wrest judgment." (Exodus 23:2, excerpt, KJV)

"Thou shalt not wrest the judgment of thy poor in his cause. Keep thee far from a false matter; and the innocent and righteous slay thou not: for I will not justify the wicked." (Exodus 23:6-7, excerpt, KJV)

"But the seventh year thou shalt let it rest and lie still; that the poor of thy people may eat: and what they leave the beasts of the field shall eat. In like manner thou shalt deal with thy vineyard, and with thy oliveyard." (Exodus 23:11, excerpt, KJV)

"Behold, I send an Angel before thee, to keep thee in the way, and to bring thee into the place which I have prepared. Beware of him, and obey his voice, provoke him not." (Exodus 23:20-21, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You are the Lord who judges all the earth in righteousness. Cleanse our mouths today, that we spread no unverified word; cleanse our ears, that we do not shout along with the crowd; and cleanse our hands, that no unrighteous gain sticks to them. Teach us also to rest, to remember, and to follow Your leading that goes before us, and little by little, year by year, to take back the inheritance You would have us gain. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Justice at the Judgment Seat — Do Not Follow the Crowd, Do Not Show Partiality, Take No Bribe (23:1-9)

The first nine verses are a set of commandments for the judgment seat, and its first sentence is nailed to the mouth and the ear: Thou shalt not raise a false report. "False report" — in the original, shema shav (שֵׁמַע שָׁוְא), compounded of the word for a hearing or report (the root concept of H8085) and shav (H7723), false, empty, groundless — a false report, a rumour without foundation [1]. Note that the text rebukes not only the one who invents the rumour but also the one who spreads it: you did not fabricate it, you only passed it on, only said it in passing, only heard others say so — but once the word leaves your mouth, it grows legs in the crowd. What judgment fears most is exactly such a word with legs: before the witness box has opened, the verdict in the street has already been pronounced; before the facts are checked, a reputation has already been executed. Immediately after, the text says: put not thine hand with the wicked to be an unrighteous witness. "Unrighteous witness," in the original 'ed chamas (עֵד חָמָס), compounded of 'ed (H5707), a witness, and chamas (H2555), violence, wrong — a witness who wounds by violence, testimony that commits assault by words [2]. The ninth commandment said, Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour; here false witness is named outright as violence, because one false word sent into the judgment seat can take away a person's field, freedom, or even life. Words from the mouth, in God's account, are never light things.

The second sentence is the eye of the chapter: Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil. The text goes on more concretely: neither shalt thou speak in a cause to decline after many to wrest judgment. The evil here is often not some monstrous, great evil, but the commonest spinelessness at the judgment seat: everyone says so, why should I be the one who sings against them; the rich and powerful side has the numbers, and if I go along with the majority I offend no one. It is exactly here that God forbids His people to drift with the current. Truth is not on the side with more people; truth is on the side of the facts. The duty of judge and witness is not to take the direction of the wind, but to establish what really happened. If this sentence is read only within a courtroom, it is also too narrow; this manuscript offers a brief, identified piece of exposition here: today the place where rumour and crowd-shouting surge most fiercely is no longer only the courtroom, but also the screen in everyone's hand. One word spread through a whole city unverified, a crowd rushing in together before seeing the whole picture — this is following a multitude to do evil in new clothes. The text's setting on the spot is lawsuits and bearing witness, and this manuscript does not pass the modern scene off as the original meaning; but the principle is the same: neither the mouth nor the finger of God's people may bear witness for an unverified matter.

The next two sentences are a pair of warnings in opposite directions, equally weighty, and neither can be missing. Verse 3 says: neither shalt thou countenance a poor man in his cause. Verse 6 says again: thou shalt not wrest the judgment of thy poor in his cause. (Leviticus 19:15 adds the companion warning against respecting the person of the mighty; that is a cross-reference, not part of this chapter's text, and is identified as such here.) Read together, the proportion comes out: that a poor man is pitiable does not mean the poor man is in the right in every lawsuit; that a great man has power does not mean his word deserves to be valued more highly. On the scales of justice, no finger may be added to either side: neither favouritism out of sympathy, nor submission out of regard for power. In the previous chapter we read that God especially protects the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless, and that whoever oppresses them, God Himself hears their cry; that is protecting the weak. But protecting the weak is not shielding their faults: protecting the weak from oppression, and standing on the weak person's side in a judgment regardless of right and wrong, are two different things. If a man wins his case merely because he is poor, that is not justice; that is the scales tilted the other way, and what is lost is still uprightness. This manuscript holds that this pairing is the most skilful stroke in this chapter: it blocks at once the two commonest forms of self-interest, one wearing the coat of kindness, the other wearing the coat of knowing the times. This observation is exegetical explanation made from the juxtaposition of the two verses, and is identified here as such.

In this section justice also has an unexpected scene, pushing it from the courtroom onto the road. The text says: If thou meet thine enemy's ox or his ass going astray, thou shalt surely bring it back to him again. If thou see the ass of him that hateth thee lying under his burden, and wouldest forbear to help him, thou shalt surely help with him. When an enemy's livestock has strayed, the easiest and most satisfying course is to pretend not to see it, to let it wander off, even to hope it wanders off; when the ass lies under its burden, the most natural reaction is to walk around it, thinking, he has it coming today. The ordinances forbid exactly this: at the moment when the beast suffers and its owner is helpless, the grudge is set down first — first bring the animal back, first put a hand to lifting the burden off. Deuteronomy chapter 22 has almost the same words, on not hiding oneself from a brother's straying ox or sheep and on helping up a fallen beast (Deuteronomy 22:1-4, cited in sense); the two passages answer each other, and this manuscript notes them together: the object in Exodus is written more piercingly — your enemy, the one who hates you; in Deuteronomy it is your brother. One passage speaks of the boundary of grudges, the other of the duty toward a countryman; together they form one whole line: justice crosses even the line of enmity, and help comes before the settling of scores. This reading-together is intertextual explanation made from the two texts. Extended in preaching to today, it is this: that you have a grievance with a man does not mean you may stand aside when he is in trouble; if a Christian's justice holds only toward friends, that is still mere human nature and does not count as obedience.

The last two warnings uphold together the hand at the judgment seat and the person of the sojourner. First: thou shalt take no gift. "Gift," here a bribe — in the original shochad (שֹׁחַד, H7810), a gift given to sway a judgment [3]. The reason the text gives is vivid: for the gift blindeth the wise, and perverteth the words of the righteous. The most insidious thing about a bribe is not that it openly tells a man to do evil, but that it blinds his eyes: having taken a man's benefit, the eyes tilt unawares — this eye open, that eye shut in looking at the evidence — and the words of the judgment are perverted. Many wrongful cases do not come from a judge bent on harming a man; his eyes were first covered by a gift. God forbids this blindfolding trade at His people's judgment seat, because once the judgment seat can be bought, the poor have nowhere left to plead. Second: thou shalt not oppress a stranger. This sentence has already sounded more than once in the Book of the Covenant, and here it sounds again, and its reason sentence sounds with it: for ye know the heart of a stranger, seeing ye were strangers in the land of Egypt. What is the heart of a stranger? The heart of one in an unfamiliar place; of one whose language fails and who has no door at which to plead; of one who is looked at once more and given a portion less. God does not ask His people to sympathize with the stranger by imagination; He asks them to do so by memory: you have tasted that flavour yourself, and you know it best. In the previous chapter this manuscript said that a person who has left Egypt must not bring Egypt back home; in this chapter the same sentence is said another way: a person who knows the heart of a stranger must not make another taste that flavour again. This is exposition, but its root is in this chapter's reason sentence, plainly stated.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Rest for Land and People, and the Three Feasts of the Year (23:10-19)

When the words about judgment are finished, the text suddenly grows quiet and turns from the courtroom to the fields: six years thou shalt sow thy land, and shalt gather in the fruits thereof; but the seventh year thou shalt let it rest and lie still. "Let it rest and lie still" — in the original two verbs: shamat (שָׁמַט, H8058), to let rest, to release; and natash (נָטַשׁ, H5203), to leave, to let lie, to leave a thing be [4]. (Note: 'azav, H5800, occurs in this chapter at verse 5, not in verse 11; an earlier draft misplaced it here.) The land in the seventh year is not abandoned; it is deliberately released: no sowing, no planting, no harvesting, but left to rest, and what grows of itself is left for the poor to eat, and what the poor leave, for the beasts of the field. The vineyard and the oliveyard are to be dealt with in the same way. Look carefully at the order of those who benefit: first the land gets its rest, then the poor of thy people may eat, then the beasts eat what is left. The Sabbath year here is not agricultural technique; it is a discipline of faith joined with provision for the neighbour: the owner of the land must learn that in the seventh year, if I do not reach out my hand, the land will not starve me; and the poor need not stretch out a hand to beg, but may walk into the field with dignity and gather. This arrangement runs in one line with the Sabbath, and the text next indeed speaks of the Sabbath: Six days thou shalt do thy work, and on the seventh day thou shalt rest: that thine ox and thine ass may rest, and the son of thy handmaid, and the stranger, may be refreshed. This word "refreshed" is worth pausing over: rest is not only stopping work; it is catching one's breath and recovering one's strength. The list of those who benefit is also worth pausing over: the ox and the ass, the son of the handmaid, and the stranger. Rest is not a privilege, not the master's favour, and not a taste in living for those who have property; rest is first of all a breathing space for those with the least security. Livestock will not claim rest for themselves, the son of a handmaid dare not ask leave for himself, and the stranger has no right to rest from work for himself — so God personally claims this day back for them in the law. Here this manuscript adds an identified piece of exposition: if, in a community, rest reaches only the strong, that is merely welfare; only when the weakest members and the voiceless livestock also receive it can it be said that God's order has entered life.

At the end of the section on rest there is one more sentence easily slid past: and make no mention of the name of other gods, neither let it be heard out of thy mouth. Read after the Sabbath, its meaning is deep: on the seventh day the people stop their hands, not only to let the body rest but also to let the mouth rest — not naming other gods, and not letting a pagan invocation occupy this day. Keeping the Sabbath and keeping exclusive worship are, here, two sides of the same matter. This manuscript reads it so, as exegetical explanation made from the order of the context, and identifies it here as such.

After rest come the feasts. "Feast," in the original chag (חַג, H2282), a festival, a pilgrim feast [5]. The text says: Three times thou shalt keep a feast unto me in the year. Which three? The first is the feast of unleavened bread: in the time appointed of the month Abib, unleavened bread is eaten for seven days, as God commanded, for in that month the people came out of Egypt; keeping this feast is remembrance — remembering that hurried night of departure, leaving on the road before the dough could rise, and remembering how God brought the people out with a mighty hand. The second is the feast of harvest: what is gathered is the firstfruits of thy labours, which thou hast sown in the field; this feast was later connected with the feast of weeks and Pentecost, but here this chapter names only the firstfruits, and this manuscript follows this chapter's terms and does not read the later completed institution back into it. The third is the feast of ingathering, at the end of the year, when thou hast gathered in thy labours out of the field; all the produce of the land is in the storehouse, and the year is closed with thanksgiving. The three feasts, at head and tail, nail the Israelites' calendar to two things: the redemption of the exodus, and the produce of the land. At bottom this is the calendar of a farming people, and also the calendar of a people who have received grace: in spring they remember being saved, in early summer they offer the firstfruits, and at autumn's end they gather in the whole harvest. The same command carries the rule of appearing: three times in the year all thy males shall appear before the Lord GOD; and when they appear, none shall appear before me empty. "Not empty" does not mean that God lacks that little gift of the people's; it means the people may not take grace for nothing and pretend gratitude with two empty hands; with firstfruits in the hand, the heart remembers from whom the produce came. The text also says: The first of the firstfruits of thy land thou shalt bring into the house of the LORD thy God. The first and the best are brought first into God's house, and what remains is one's own — this is the order the firstfruits teach the people.

Among the details of the feasts there are also a few rules about blood and leaven, and about the sacrifice, which must not be passed over: Thou shalt not offer the blood of my sacrifice with leavened bread; neither shall the fat of my sacrifice remain until the morning. Blood and leaven are not to be offered together, and the sacrifice is not to be kept overnight. Scholars classify the grounds of these rules differently; this manuscript speaks only of what the text states as fact: the offering is to be clean and without delay, and what is to be settled on the day is not to be left until the next day to spoil. Worship is not a makeshift; the people's care in matters of time and cleanness is itself part of reverence.

The last sentence of this section is the one that has troubled interpreters most in the whole chapter: Thou shalt not seethe a kid in his mother's milk. This sentence appears twice more, in Exodus and Deuteronomy — three times in all (Exodus 23:19; 34:26; Deuteronomy 14:21, the same sentence excerpted). What its original meaning refers to, scholarship still discusses; this manuscript presents the common views together and draws no firm conclusion. One view connects it with a Canaanite sacrificial custom: in ancient Canaan there was a custom of seething a kid in its mother's milk as an offering or as a rite for the harvest, and God forbade His people to imitate it; those who support this view often cite a related record in the texts unearthed at Ugarit in modern times, but the interpretation of that record is itself disputed, and this is honestly noted. Another is the view of compassion: to cook a young life in the very milk that nourished it is cruel and contrary to reason, and the law thereby teaches the people to keep mercy, and not to be cold-hearted even toward livestock; this reading circulated widely among later rabbis and not a few commentators. A third view holds that it belongs with the rules of the festival table — a boundary, after the firstfruits are offered, against unrestrained indulgence at the feast. Each of the three views has its grounds, and each has its unsettled points. As preachers, this manuscript's attitude is: let the investigation of the original meaning remain for scholarship to continue discussing, while the sentence's place in the context is clear — it stands immediately after the firstfruits are brought into God's house, a rule for the altar and for the festival table. One later development must also be honestly noted: the later Jewish dietary law of separating meat and milk — eating meat and milk separately, with separate vessels, that whole set of rules — has one of its textual sources precisely in this sentence; that is a derivative development unfolded by later rabbinic law from this sentence, not a whole dietary code written out on the spot in this chapter, and this manuscript identifies the levels separately, lest the later tradition be counted back into this chapter as its original meaning.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: An Angel Before You — Promise, Warning, and Gaining the Land Little by Little (23:20-33)

From verse 20 the tone changes sharply: the ordinances before were rules; this section is a road. God says: Behold, I send an Angel before thee, to keep thee in the way, and to bring thee into the place which I have prepared. "Angel," in the original mal'akh (מַלְאָךְ, H4397), a messenger, one who is sent; the word for angel in the Old Testament is mostly this word [6]. Who this angel who walks before them is, scholarship discusses; this manuscript presents the views together and draws no conclusion. One view holds that this angel is the embodiment of God's presence: the text below says, my name is in him, and says that he will not pardon the people's transgressions, since only God can forgive sins; the angel speaks and acts with God's own authority, and the dialogue in Exodus chapters 32 to 33, where Moses asks God to go in person and God promises that His presence shall go with him, is often cited alongside this angel. Another view holds that this angel is an angel sent by God, God's representative: a representative speaks for the one who sent him, so his words are God's words, and to provoke him is to provoke God, yet he remains distinct from God in station. Both views have textual grounds, and this manuscript does not decide for either; but one thing both views agree on, and which the text states on the spot, is that the people were to obey this angel as they would obey God. The text says: he is sent in my name — Beware of him, and obey his voice, provoke him not; for he will not pardon your transgressions: for my name is in him. "My name is in him" — this sentence lifts the angel's weight to the very top: the name represents the person himself, and with God's name in him, to meet him is like meeting God's own outstretched hand.

The promise for obedience unfolds at once, sentence by heavy sentence. The text says: But if thou shalt indeed obey his voice, and do all that I speak; then I will be an enemy unto thine enemies, and an adversary unto thine adversaries. "I will be an enemy unto thine enemies" — this does not rest on the people's own greatness, but on the covenant God personally standing on the people's side. The angel is to go before the Amorites, the Hittites, the Perizzites, the Canaanites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites, and God will cut them off. The list is set out to tell the people: the land ahead is not empty land; it is land full of inhabitants, and the road of gaining it will not be smooth sailing. Then the warning follows at once, without mercy: Thou shalt not bow down to their gods, nor serve them, nor do after their works: but thou shalt utterly overthrow them, and quite break down their images. The most dangerous moment for a people gaining land is precisely not the moment of losing a battle, but the moment of standing firm, settling down, and beginning to copy the neighbours: their altars look effective too, their ways look prosperous too — what harm in learning a little? Here God speaks absolutely: idols and images are to be overthrown and broken down, not kept as souvenirs, and still less kept as alternatives. If the people serve God with an undivided heart, the promise follows: And ye shall serve the LORD your God, and he shall bless thy bread, and thy water; and I will take sickness away from the midst of thee. There shall nothing cast their young, nor be barren, in thy land: the number of thy days I will fulfil. Bread, water, sickness, childbearing, and the number of days — these five are what a people wandering in the wilderness care about most, and God takes them up one by one. This manuscript must identify the proportion here: these are promises at the level of the covenant community, God's collective blessing on a covenant-keeping people; they must not be broken into single sentences and taken as a warrant that an individual gets whatever he asks. When a collective promise is applied to an individual, this proportion must be kept. After this, God will send fear and the hornet among the Canaanites, discomfiting them so that they flee before the people; whether the hornet is literally insects, or a figure for the panic God sends so that enemy armies lose heart, commentators read it differently, and this manuscript honestly notes both without fixing it here, holding only the layer the text states plainly: the initiative in the war is in God's hand, and the enemy is thrown into confusion before the battle.

The borders are also drawn: And I will set thy bounds from the Red sea even unto the sea of the Philistines, and from the desert unto the river: for I will deliver the inhabitants of the land into your hand; and thou shalt drive them out before thee. The Red Sea, the sea of the Philistines — that is, the region of the Mediterranean — the desert, and the river, that is, the Euphrates: these four limits draw the widest outline of the promised land. This manuscript identifies this: it is the outline of the promised borders, not the extent the people actually governed on the spot when they entered Canaan; the actual gaining of the land was a process, and the text immediately speaks of the rhythm of that process.

At the very end of the chapter there are two warnings and one arrangement of great weight, and none may be missing. The warnings are: Thou shalt make no covenant with them, nor with their gods. They shall not dwell in thy land, lest they make thee sin against me: for if thou serve their gods, it will surely be a snare unto thee. A snare is a hunter's trap for beasts, invisible in ordinary times, and tightening the moment a foot steps in; to make a covenant with the gods of Canaan and to let idol worship remain in the land looks at first like tolerance and peace, but when it later tightens, what it chokes is the people's worship of the one true God. The arrangement is this sentence — read it slowly: I will not drive them out from before thee in one year; lest the land become desolate, and the beast of the field multiply against thee. By little and little I will drive them out from before thee, until thou be increased, and inherit the land. This is the arrangement of verses 29 to 30, driving them out little by little. God did not promise to clear the field in one night, and did not promise that the people would wake to a wholly peaceful land; He set the rhythm of gaining the land as an advance year by year: drive them out too fast, and the empty land left behind, tilled by no one, breeds wild beasts and becomes a harm instead; where the people's numbers and their capacity to govern have grown, there the border of gaining the land is to be pushed. This manuscript holds that this is the most rewarding theological sentence this chapter leaves us, and to state honestly this arrangement of slowness is an indispensable lesson in preaching this section: God's omnipotence never equals God's haste, and the pace He sets for His people is often synchronized with the people's growth. This is exegetical explanation made from the text of verses 29 to 30 themselves; its root is in the text, not a sentiment added from outside. Many people in the life of faith resent God's slowness — slow victory, slow change, slow answers to prayer; if this verse is read into the heart, one must admit: there is a slowness that is God's protection. The land is gained step by step, people grow year by year, snares are dismantled one by one, and idols are destroyed one seat at a time — this is what the road into Canaan actually looks like.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today, from the judgment seat to rest and the feasts, and then to the angel who walks before, we have learned three things. First, at the judgment seat one must stand straight: raise no false report, bear no false witness, follow no multitude to do evil, countenance no poor man, and take no gift that blinds the eyes; even an enemy's straying livestock must be brought back — justice must cross the line of enmity. Second, time must be kept: in the seventh year the land is to lie still; ox and ass, the son of the handmaid, and the stranger are to be refreshed on the Sabbath; three times a year the feasts are kept, appearing with the firstfruits, and not empty-handed. Third, the road must be followed steadily: the angel walks before, and my name is in him; we need only obey — not bowing to the gods of Canaan, making no covenant with them, and keeping no snare beside us; and the land is gained little by little, advanced year by year — neither restless, nor standing still. May there be no rumour on our lips, no bribe in our hands, rest in our homes, and a leading before our feet, as we walk our generation's road into Canaan.

Let us pray together: Lord, Lord who judges in righteousness, we thank You. Your ordinances reach down to the truth or falsehood of a single sentence, a single straying ass, a single resting field, a single basket of firstfruits, because in Your eyes no matter is too small to be blurred, and no weak person may be passed over. Forgive our sin of shouting with the crowd, of passing on unverified words, and of refusing help within a grudge. Teach us to rest, and teach us to follow Your angel and gain the land slowly — not greedy for speed, making no covenant, and keeping no idol. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[2] 'ed chamas: Hebrew עֵד חָמָס ('ed, H5707, a witness, testimony; chamas, H2555, violence, wrong, injustice), a witness of violence, false witness that commits assault by words; Exodus 23:1b, "put not thine hand with the wicked, to be an unrighteous witness"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Exodus 20:16, the ninth commandment, "Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour"; this manuscript's "false witness is named as violence" is exegetical explanation made from the meaning of the word and its context.

[3] shochad: Hebrew שֹׁחַד (H7810), a bribe, a present given to sway a judgment; Exodus 23:8, "And thou shalt take no gift: for the gift blindeth the wise, and perverteth the words of the righteous"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Deuteronomy 16:19, the same word and the same command, as intertextual basis for this manuscript's "the bribe blinds the eyes."

[4] shamat and natash: shamat, Hebrew שָׁמַט (H8058), to let rest, to release, to remit; natash, Hebrew נָטַשׁ (H5203), to leave, to let lie, to abandon to rest; Exodus 23:11, "But the seventh year thou shalt let it rest and lie still," so that the poor may eat what grows of itself in the field and the beasts eat what is left; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Note: 'azav (עָזַב, H5800) occurs at Exodus 23:5, not in 23:11; an earlier draft misattributed it here. See Leviticus chapter 25, the completed institution of the Sabbath year, as the basis for this manuscript's note that "this chapter gives the concise ordinance; the completed institution comes later."

[5] chag: Hebrew חַג (H2282), a feast, a festival, a pilgrim feast; Exodus 23:14, "Three times thou shalt keep a feast unto me in the year," and Exodus 23:15-19 below lists the feast of unleavened bread, the feast of harvest, and the feast of ingathering, together with the rules of the firstfruits and the sacrifice; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Exodus 34:18-26, the feasts restated, and Deuteronomy chapter 16, the completed institution of the feasts; this manuscript notes them together to show that this chapter is the early form of the feast ordinances.

[6] mal'akh: Hebrew מַלְאָךְ (H4397), a messenger, one who is sent; the angels of the Old Testament are mostly called by this word; Exodus 23:20, "Behold, I send an Angel before thee, to keep thee in the way," and 23:21, "for my name is in him"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The two views of the angel's identity (the embodiment of God's presence, and a sent angel) stand side by side in scholarship, and this manuscript presents both in the text and draws no conclusion; see Exodus chapters 32-33, the dialogue of God's going with the people, and Deuteronomy chapter 7, the command concerning gaining Canaan, as intertextual basis for this manuscript's section on the angel.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil; neither shalt thou speak in a cause to decline after many to wrest judgment." (Exodus 23:2, excerpt, KJV) "I will not drive them out from before thee in one year... By little and little I will drive them out from before thee, until thou be increased, and inherit the land." (Exodus 23:29-30, excerpt, KJV)

When many voices are raised, the first thing easily lost is one's own judgment. Everyone passes a saying along; everyone joins in condemning a matter; if you do not speak with them, you seem not to belong. Into that crowd noise Exodus 23 sets one sentence: Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil. God has not called us away from people; He has called us to stand firm among them — no unverified word out of my mouth, no side taken by my feet before the truth is known. In the noise of today, ask yourself: of every word you pass on, could you say before God that it is true? And here is a slower word for the anxious heart. God did not drive out the enemies of Canaan in one year, but little by little, because land must be tilled and kept, and land taken too fast turns to waste. The ground you are to gain in your own life — a bad habit, an old temper, a tangle of many years — if God has not cleared it overnight, He has not forgotten; He is waiting until you have grown enough to hold it. Push this year's boundary this year, and next year's next year; make no covenant with the snare, and do not turn back to the idol. To stand straight and to walk steadily — that is this chapter's lesson for ordinary days.

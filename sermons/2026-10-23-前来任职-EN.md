# Coming Forward to Serve — A Sermon on Numbers 4

- Text: Numbers 4:1-49
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending cross-review (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Numbers 4; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations; verses not checked word for word are given in summary only, never as quotations.
- Note on the title: The tentative Chinese title, "serving and holding office," was checked against the Chinese base text of Numbers 4 and rejected on two grounds: the four characters do not occur there as one unbroken phrase, and the word rendered "hold office / serve in a post" in some other Chinese versions does not occur in the New Punctuation text of this chapter at all. The base text's own recurring unbroken wording is "come forward to take up the service" — at Numbers 4:3, 4:23, and 4:47, each checked verse by verse — and the Chinese title was corrected to those four characters. The KJV at those verses speaks of men who "enter into the host, to do the work" (4:3), who "enter in to perform the service" (4:23), and who "came to do the service of the ministry, and the service of the burden" (4:47). The English title renders the corrected Chinese title: "Coming Forward to Serve." See Endnote 2.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Chapter 3 numbers the Levite males from a month old and upward, for the sake of belonging — who stands beside the sanctuary in the stead of Israel's firstborn. Chapter 4 numbers the same men a second time, on an entirely different standard: this time only those from thirty years old until fifty, who can come forward to serve, are numbered, for the sake of office — who is fit, strong, and measured enough to do the work of the tabernacle and bear its burdens. In the chapter's forty-nine verses, the first part assigns the charges house by house: the sons of Kohath bear the most holy things, but only after Aaron and his sons have first covered the holy things piece by piece; they must not touch any holy thing, nor go in to see while the holy things are covered, even for a moment. The sons of Gershon bear all the curtains and fabrics, doing all their service at the appointment of Aaron and his sons, under Ithamar the son of Aaron the priest. The sons of Merari bear the boards, bars, pillars, and sockets of the tabernacle and the pillars, sockets, pins, and cords of the court, their instruments reckoned by name, likewise under Ithamar. The last part reports the numbering house by house: Kohath two thousand seven hundred and fifty, Gershon two thousand six hundred and thirty, Merari three thousand and two hundred — eight thousand five hundred and eighty in all, in exact agreement with the chapter's own stated total. This sermon reads the chapter in the text's own order: first, the threshold of thirty to fifty years and its difference from chapter 3's starting point of one month; then, house by house, the three houses' objects, manner of bearing, and boundaries; and finally the three houses' figures added up and verified item by item, closing with the words, "every one according to his service, and according to his burden." The thread the sermon traces is this: to be numbered is not yet to enter service — the register of belonging counts heads, but the register of office counts men on duty; and every man numbered here is assigned one concrete service and one concrete burden, down to instruments reckoned by name. No one in this chapter has a number without a charge.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 From one month to thirty years: this numbering is not of belonging, but of office

(Fact) Chapter 4 opens with the LORD speaking to Moses and Aaron, commanding the sum of the sons of Kohath to be taken from among the sons of Levi, after their families and by the house of their fathers; the age standard stands in the KJV: "From thirty years old and upward even until fifty years old, all that enter into the host, to do the work in the tabernacle of the congregation." (Num 4:3, KJV) Set this sentence beside the standard of chapter 3. There the Levites were numbered from a month old and upward (Num 3:15, Summary) — that numbering asked about belonging, and so even the child in arms was in the register. This time those in service are numbered, and the lower bound rises at a stroke to thirty years, with the upper bound drawn at fifty — the question is no longer whose a man is, but whether he can take up the office. Chapter 1 numbered the people's soldiers from twenty years old and upward (Numbers 1, Summary); the threshold for the tabernacle's service stands ten years higher than for war. A lower bound of thirty and an upper bound of fifty enclose exactly twenty years — the twenty years of a man's life when strength is fullest and judgment steadiest; and the text lays the tabernacle's heaviest bearing precisely on the shoulders of those twenty years. Too young, and a man is not assigned; past the age, and he is assigned no longer — service has a term, and the term is drawn clearly at this chapter's very opening.

(Fact) This standard of thirty to fifty years is not spoken to the house of Kohath alone. When the turn comes to the house of Gershon, the command stands in the KJV: "From thirty years old and upward until fifty years old shalt thou number them; all that enter in to perform the service, to do the work in the tabernacle of the congregation." (Num 4:23, KJV) Read against verse 3, the wording of age and of entering the service recurs unchanged. When the turn comes to the house of Merari, the standard is the same — from thirty years old until fifty, all who enter the service to do the work of the tabernacle are to be numbered (v. 30, Summary); and at the chapter's end, reporting the total, the text states the standard once more, of men who came "to do the service of the ministry, and the service of the burden in the tabernacle of the congregation" (v. 47, KJV; vv. 47-48 read together, see section IV and Endnote 1). The three houses are measured with one rule: their objects differ in weight and their manner of bearing differs, but the age threshold of office is identical for all three. Charges may differ from house to house; the qualification for office does not differ by house — a man enters at thirty and leaves at fifty, Kohathite and Merarite alike.

(Fact) The chapter's keywords must likewise be set out in this passage, because the three houses' charges all grow out of them. First, the pair "service" and "burden" is the chapter's skeleton: verse 49, gathering the whole chapter, says that they were numbered "every one according to his service, and according to his burden" (v. 49, KJV, quoted in section IV). The pair recurs house by house: for Kohath, "This shall be the service of the sons of Kohath" (v. 4, KJV); for Gershon, "This is the service of the families of the Gershonites, to serve, and for burdens" (v. 24, KJV); for Merari, the instruments of the charge of their burden are reckoned "by name" (v. 32). Second, the "service" is ʿăḇōḏāh (Strong's H5656), labour, service, the work of an office; in preparing this sermon its occurrences in the chapter were checked verse by verse in the Hebrew text as carried on wd.bible (BHS): the construct form at verse 4 for the Kohathites' service; the verb and noun forms together at verse 23; the construct form at verse 24 for the Gershonites; the parallel phrases "the service of the ministry" and "the service of the burden" at verse 47; and the construct form at verse 49, "according to his service." The verses listed here are a selection actually checked, not an exhaustive count of the word's occurrences in the chapter. Third, the "burden" is massāʾ (Strong's H4853), a load, what is lifted and carried. This word does not occur in chapter 3 — the sermon on that chapter checked it and set it aside, noting that it belongs to the carrying charges of chapter 4 — and here it is at home: its occurrences checked verse by verse for this sermon include verse 15, at the close of the Kohath passage, where the KJV reads "These things are the burden of the sons of Kohath"; verse 19, "to his burden"; verse 24, "for burdens"; verse 27, the Gershonites' burdens; verse 31, the Merarites' burden in its opening charge-phrase; verse 32, "the charge of their burden"; verse 47, "the service of the burden"; and verse 49, "according to his burden." Again, the list is a checked selection, not an exhaustive claim. "Service" names the office; "burden" names the load; and in this chapter the two are always assigned together — no service is given without a burden, and no burden without a service.

(Application) From one month old to thirty years there lie twenty-nine years, and they are this chapter's first lesson to everyone who serves. Chapter 3 says: belonging asks no age — a month old, and the name is already in the register. Chapter 4 says: office does ask age — a name in the register must still wait in the register, until at thirty years it is a man's turn to lay hands on the bearing of the most holy things. To be received by God is one thing; to be used by God is another. The first was already accomplished before this chapter; the second must pass through a long preparation. The children of Levi grew up beside the tabernacle, watching their fathers and brothers bear curtains and boards for nearly thirty years, and could not yet take the work into their own hands — and the text does not call those thirty years a waste. Precisely because what a man bears is the most holy things, the court pillars and sockets, the very frame of all the congregation's worship, this work cannot be given to the hot-blooded and the unpractised. Thirty years is the age when strength suffices and character suffices with it: strength in the hands, shoulders fit for boards and bars of real weight; and measure in the heart, knowing what may be touched and what may not even be seen. The years of preparation for service are never idle waiting; they are years of watching from alongside, learning in small things, standing steady outside the boundary — and when those years are full, the command comes to a man and says: now you are numbered; come forward to serve.

(Application) The upper bound of fifty must likewise be faced squarely, not slipped past. The text does not say that a man of fifty is useless; it says that the work of bearing has its term: at fifty, this load comes off a man's shoulders and passes to the next man of thirty. The man in office is most tempted to treat the office as his own property — pressing forward to take it while young, and refusing to lay it down when old; at both ends he sets himself above the office. This chapter's order blocks both ends: before thirty, zeal may not promote a man past his grade into the work; at fifty, seniority may not keep him lingering past his term. An office is given, and an office is taken back; glad to bear it when it is given, glad to unload it when it is taken back — that is the Levite's duty. A man's twenty strongest years are given to the tabernacle, and the remaining years belong to himself and his house — the text arranges even this fairly for the man in service: he is not spent to death in the work; he retires at the end of his term, with name and measure intact.

(Fact) One further discrepancy between text and text must be set out exactly as it stands, and not covered over. Numbers 8, treating the Levites' taking up of service, sets the starting age not at thirty but at twenty-five, in the KJV: "This is it that belongeth unto the Levites: from twenty and five years old and upward they shall go in to wait upon the service of the tabernacle of the congregation:" (Num 8:24, KJV) And the age of ceasing stands there: "And from the age of fifty years they shall cease waiting upon the service thereof, and shall serve no more:" (Num 8:25, KJV) Set the two passages side by side: this chapter (vv. 3, 23, 30, Summary) begins at thirty and ends at fifty; Numbers 8 (8:24-25) begins at twenty-five and ends at fifty. The upper bound agrees; the lower bound differs by five years. This sermon simply sets the two texts out, each in its own words, each with its own figure, altering neither — it does not change twenty-five into thirty, nor thirty into twenty-five, to manufacture agreement. A difference the text itself has written down must first be honestly owned by the preacher, before he has any right to preach application from beside it.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) This difference of five years between thirty and twenty-five has long been noticed by commentators, who have offered more than one reading: some hold that twenty-five was a year of apprenticeship and preparation — from twenty-five a man entered the tabernacle to assist and learn under his brethren (compare the word at 8:26 that those who have ceased may still assist their brethren in keeping the charge, Summary), and only at thirty took up independent office and bearing; some, that the two rules belong to different periods, the starting age being later relaxed; some, that Numbers 8 treats the Levites' tabernacle service in general, while this chapter treats the special charge of bearing the most holy things and the heavy frame on the wilderness march, and so sets the threshold higher. This sermon sets out the existence and drift of these discussions only, and decides for none of them; nor does its argument rest on the five years. This chapter's own standard is clear verse by verse — from thirty years old until fifty — and this sermon preaches this chapter from this chapter's own words, a standard unmoved by the other reckoning in chapter 8.

### II. 08:00-24:00 The house of Kohath: the most holy things — covered first, borne afterwards; not to be touched, not to be seen

(Fact) Of the three houses, Kohath is assigned first. The charter of their charge stands in the KJV: "This shall be the service of the sons of Kohath in the tabernacle of the congregation, about the most holy things:" (Num 4:4, KJV) The words "the most holy things" fix the weight of the Kohathites' whole life: all three houses bear the tabernacle's things, but the Kohathites alone bear the most holy grade of them — the ark, the table of shewbread, the candlestick, the golden altar, the altar of burnt offering, and all the vessels of the sanctuary (vv. 5-14 as listed, Summary). But mark the text's order: after the charter, the text does not at once call the Kohathites in to bear. It first gives ten whole verses (vv. 5-14) to how Aaron and his sons cover the things. When the camp sets forward, Aaron and his sons go in first, take down the covering veil and cover the ark of testimony with it, then put on the covering of badgers' skins, spread over it a cloth wholly of blue, and put in the staves (vv. 5-6, Summary). On the table of shewbread they spread a cloth of blue, and set on it the dishes, the spoons, the bowls, and the covers, with the continual bread still on it; then a cloth of scarlet, a covering of badgers' skins, and the staves (vv. 7-8, Summary). The candlestick of the light, with its lamps, tongs, snuffdishes, and all its oil vessels, is covered with a cloth of blue, put with all its vessels within a covering of badgers' skins, and set upon a bar (vv. 9-10, Summary). On the golden altar a cloth of blue, a covering of badgers' skins, and the staves (v. 11, Summary). All the vessels of ministry of the sanctuary are taken in a cloth of blue, covered with badgers' skins, and set on a bar (v. 12, Summary). The altar of burnt offering is emptied of its ashes, spread with a cloth of purple, laden with all its vessels — firepans, fleshhooks, shovels, basins — covered with badgers' skins, and given its staves (vv. 13-14, Summary). In ten verses the Kohathites never once appear; only the priest and his sons appear, and all their work is covering. Until the covering is finished, the bearing does not begin; that is this chapter's first rule for the house of Kohath.

(Fact) The covering finished, the Kohathites are at last called in; verse 15 stands in the KJV: "And when Aaron and his sons have made an end of covering the sanctuary, and all the vessels of the sanctuary, as the camp is to set forward; after that, the sons of Kohath shall come to bear it: but they shall not touch any holy thing, lest they die. These things are the burden of the sons of Kohath in the tabernacle of the congregation." (Num 4:15, KJV) The verse carries three layers, to be set out one by one. First, sequence: the covering is ended, and after that they come to bear — between "have made an end" and "shall come" there is no room for negotiation; the order itself is law. Second, boundary: the bearers must not touch the thing borne itself — they bear the staves, the bar, the cloths and skin coverings as wrapped; between their hands and the holy thing there always lies the layer the priests have wrapped beforehand; to touch is to die. Third, definition: at this verse's close the Kohathites' load is formally named their "burden" — the Hebrew massāʾ (H4853) stands at this verse's end, checked for this sermon verse by verse (see section I). What the Kohathites bear is not a thing at their own disposal; it is a burden assigned to them. The burden is theirs; the holy thing is not theirs; and in that distinction lies their life.

(Fact) One officer is named singly over the Kohath passage: Eleazar the son of Aaron the priest, to whose office belong the oil for the light, the sweet incense, the daily meat offering, and the anointing oil, with the oversight of all the tabernacle and all that is in it, in the sanctuary and in its vessels (v. 16, Summary; the verse is quoted in the KJV in Endnote 6). The "office" and "oversight" of this verse are, in the Hebrew, a noun form of the same root as pāqad (H6485) — Eleazar's post stands, in the original, under the same word-family as "numbering" and "appointing": he is the man who stands at the receiving and checking end of the numbering system. The Kohathites bear the most holy things, and over them stands Eleazar with oversight of the whole; the bearers have their assignment, the overseer has his total charge, and in the whole march the most holy things are never for one moment ownerless.

(Fact) At the passage's end, verses 17-20, the LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron a word of unusual severity: the tribe of the families of the Kohathites is not to be cut off from among the Levites (vv. 17-18, Summary) — and how they are to be kept from being cut off, and kept alive when they approach the most holy things, stands in the KJV: "But thus do unto them, that they may live, and not die, when they approach unto the most holy things: Aaron and his sons shall go in, and appoint them every one to his service and to his burden:" (Num 4:19, KJV) Verse 20 draws the boundary to its last inch, in the KJV: "But they shall not go in to see when the holy things are covered, lest they die." (Num 4:20, KJV) Mark the KJV's wording exactly: they must not go in to see at the very time of the covering — the moment when the veil is being handled and the cloths laid on, the bearers are not witnesses but absentees. The priests cover within; the Kohathites wait without; the wrapped thing is brought out, and only then do their hands take it. Seeing and touching are both sealed off; bearing alone is left to them — and it is precisely this sealed measure that keeps them living, and not dying.

(Application) The Kohathites' charge is the most glorious in the chapter, and the most dangerous, and it deserves to be preached slowly. Its glory: no second work in the tabernacle comes nearer the centre — the ark is on their staves; candlestick, table, and the two altars are on their shoulders and bars; the very centre of all Israel's worship walks through the wilderness carried by this one family. Its danger is the same fact from the other side: the man nearest the centre pays the highest price for crossing the line. Another house mishandles an object, and it is a failure of duty; a Kohathite touches a holy thing, and it is death. The protection laid around them is therefore the most thorough in the chapter: that the priests cover first is protection; that each man is appointed to his service and his burden is protection; that they may not even see is protection. Read these rules as mere restriction and you have read them backwards — verse 19 says it in so many words: deal thus with them, "that they may live, and not die." A boundary in Scripture is very often not an obstruction but a railing for life: the railing stands at the cliff's edge not to spite the traveller, but to keep him from falling. The Kohathites' lifelong lesson is to learn the strictest measure at the nearest distance: their hands bear the most holy things, while their hearts know clearly — this is not mine; I am only the shoulders under the burden.

(Application) There is also a beauty of order here, to be preached to everyone who "does great things" in the church. The Kohathites bear the most holy things, but the preparation is the priests' work: who takes down the veil, who spreads the cloths, who puts in the staves — the text writes it all down, and it is all Aaron and his sons. The bearers do not prepare; the preparers do not bear; the two companies each keep their own stretch, and neither may snatch the other's. If a Kohathite, trusting his strength and seniority, went in first and wrapped the ark himself — however carefully he wrapped it, it would be a death-bearing sin, because that stretch was not assigned to him. Many of the calamities of service come not from refusing to work, but from snatching work — taking up the stretch that was not assigned to us as well as our own, looking like zeal and in fact dismantling the order God has set. This chapter's order is cold and clear: do your own stretch to the uttermost, and do not even go in to watch the other man's stretch. That is not coldness; it is each in his place — and the tabernacle could be taken down, carried, and pitched in perfect order in the wilderness not because any one company could do everything, but because no company crossed its boundary.

(Fact) As for the Kohathites' manner of bearing, this chapter's own wording must be kept distinct from what follows later, and the later text must not be read back. What this chapter itself states is that the holy things, once covered, have their staves put in and are set on a bar, and the sons of Kohath come to bear them (vv. 6, 8, 10-14, 15, Summary). The express statement of bearing on the shoulder, and the assignment of carts, stand in chapter 7: there Moses gives the carts and oxen to the houses of Gershon and Merari, but gives none to the sons of Kohath, because their service was of the holy things, which they bore on their shoulders (Num 7:6-9, Summary) — that is chapter 7's word, and this sermon marks its level here and does not read chapter 7's cart figures or shoulder wording back as chapter 4's record. Within chapter 4, carts are never mentioned at all: this chapter's world has no carts, only staves, a bar, and men's shoulders — all three houses bear in this chapter, and they differ only in what they bear and under what boundary they bear it.

### III. 24:00-42:00 Gershon bears the cloth, Merari the frame: at appointment, and reckoned by name

(Fact) The second house assigned is Gershon (in the text, vv. 21-33 treat Gershon and Merari within one stretch; this section preaches Gershon first, then Merari). Gershon's numbering standard has already been quoted in section I (v. 23: from thirty years old until fifty, all who enter in to perform the service are numbered). The charter of their charge stands in the KJV: "This is the service of the families of the Gershonites, to serve, and for burdens:" (Num 4:24, KJV) The objects follow (vv. 25-26, Summary): "And they shall bear the curtains of the tabernacle, and the tabernacle of the congregation, his covering, and the covering of the badgers' skins that is above upon it, and the hanging for the door of the tabernacle of the congregation," (Num 4:25, KJV) and with these, the hangings of the court and the hanging for the door of the court — the court that is round about the tabernacle and the altar — and the cords of it, and all the instruments of its service; whatever is to be done with them, the Gershonites are to attend to it. Run an eye over the list: curtains, the tent itself with its coverings, the badgers' skin covering, the door hanging, the court's hangings and door hanging, cords — one whole line of fabrics and ropes, answering exactly to the line of objects committed to the Gershonites' keeping in chapter 3 (Num 3:25-26, Summary): in chapter 3 they are assigned to keep the cloth; in chapter 4 they are assigned to bear the cloth; keeping and bearing are two stretches of work on the same batch of objects. The character of the fabric line is this: the lightest in weight, and the bulkiest in size; and the most easily torn, soiled, and lost. The man who bears cloth does not need the greatest strength; he needs the most careful heart — a curtain rolled slack will trail, foul, and tear on the march, and at the journey's end the tabernacle will be pitched full of holes.

(Fact) The Gershonites' work has one governing rule, in the KJV: "At the appointment of Aaron and his sons shall be all the service of the sons of the Gershonites, in all their burdens, and in all their service: and ye shall appoint unto them in charge all their burdens." (Num 4:27, KJV) In this verse's Hebrew, "service" (ʿăḇōḏāh, H5656) and "burdens" (massāʾ, H4853) stand side by side twice, and the assigning is gathered up in the word "charge" (mišmereṯ, Strong's H4931) — checked for this sermon verse by verse in the Hebrew text: all the Gershonites' burdens are appointed to them as their charge to keep. The other occurrences of mišmereṯ in the chapter checked for this sermon are verse 31, at the head of the Merari burden passage, and verse 32, in the phrase "the charge of their burden" (see below and Endnote 9; this list, too, is a checked selection, not an exhaustive distribution). Verse 28 then sums up the house of Gershon: this is the service of the families of the sons of Gershon in the tabernacle; their charge is under the hand of Ithamar, the son of Aaron the priest (v. 28, Summary). Over the Gershonites stands not Eleazar but Ithamar — Aaron's youngest son. Kohath under Eleazar, Gershon and Merari under Ithamar: the line of oversight is drawn unmistakably in this chapter, and this sermon will meet it again with Merari.

(Application) Two words govern the Gershonites' whole life: "at the appointment." To bear cloth looks, on its surface, the least conspicuous portion of the three: no glory of the most holy things, no weight of the frame — only the rolling of curtains, the coiling of cords, the carrying of coverings. Yet the text lays the densest net of appointment over precisely this work: all their service, in all their burdens, is at the appointment of Aaron and his sons — when to roll, which curtain first, who bears which roll, none of it is for the Gershonites to settle among themselves; a word of appointment from above, and an answer from below. Do not read this as the Gershonites having no standing. Exactly the opposite: the more specific the appointment, the more seriously the office is taken. A post that no one ever asks about, appoints to, or receives back is the post that does not matter. Every curtain of the Gershonites stands on appointment's ledger; every roll has its appointed man and its charge to keep — they work under Ithamar's hand and at Aaron and his sons' appointment, and not one piece of cloth lies outside the system. The dignity of service never hangs on whether the thing borne is precious, but on whether the work comes by appointment and stands by assignment: a curtain borne at appointment, and the ark borne at appointment, are entered in the same column — the column of faithfulness.

(Fact) The third house: Merari (vv. 29-33, their standard in Summary, their objects in quotation). The numbering command: the sons of Merari are to be numbered after their families and by the house of their fathers, from thirty years old until fifty years old, everyone who enters the service to do the work of the tabernacle (vv. 29-30, Summary) — the standard differing not a hair from the other two houses. Their burden stands in the KJV: "And this is the charge of their burden, according to all their service in the tabernacle of the congregation; the boards of the tabernacle, and the bars thereof, and the pillars thereof, and sockets thereof," (Num 4:31, KJV) and continues: "And the pillars of the court round about, and their sockets, and their pins, and their cords, with all their instruments, and with all their service: and by name ye shall reckon the instruments of the charge of their burden." (Num 4:32, KJV) Put the two verses together and the Merarites bear the tabernacle's whole frame: boards, bars, pillars, and sockets are the structure of the tabernacle itself; the court's pillars round about, their sockets, pins, and cords are the structure of the court's circuit. This line's character is Gershon's exact opposite: Gershon's objects are light and soft; Merari's are heavy and hard — boards, pillars with their sockets, pins driven into the ground, every piece of it dead weight. Whether the tabernacle stands at all depends on this line: curtains, however fine, make no house without boards and pillars; vessels, however holy, make no sanctuary without a frame around them. The Merarites bear the least conspicuous part of the whole tabernacle, and the part that can least be spared.

(Fact) The last clause of verse 32 — "by name ye shall reckon" — is the crowning stroke of the Merari passage, and it must be set steady in the original. At this verse's close in the Hebrew, the verb of reckoning by name is a form of pāqad (H6485), and the phrase for the instruments borne joins mišmereṯ (H4931) and massāʾ (H4853) together — checked for this sermon verse by verse (see Endnotes 9 and 10). The chapter's three keywords meet in one sentence: numbering (pāqad), charge (mišmereṯ), burden (massāʾ). To reckon by name is to bring the numbering down onto the instruments one by one: which pillar is this man's to bear, which board that man's to carry, is appointed by name, with no room for vagueness. Verse 33 sums up the house of Merari: this is the service of the families of the sons of Merari in the tabernacle, all under the hand of Ithamar the son of Aaron the priest (v. 33, Summary) — under the same Ithamar as Gershon, so that the chapter's line of oversight is now complete: Eleazar with oversight of the whole tabernacle and of the Kohath line (v. 16, Summary), Ithamar over the two houses of Gershon and Merari (vv. 28, 33, Summary).

(Application) The Merarites' work is the one at which today's hearers should stop longest. Nothing this family bears can "show its face" in the worship: the congregation sees the curtains' colours, the ark's glory, the candlestick's light; the boards and bars hide behind the curtains, the pillars stand at the court's edge, and the pins are driven into the ground outright — the better they are driven, the less anyone sees them. Yet the rule laid on this house is not one degree looser than Kohath's: "by name ye shall reckon" — every instrument is appointed by name to a particular man's head. In God's ledger, hidden work and conspicuous work are kept in the same book: the ark is assigned by its staves, and the pins are assigned by name; no object is waved away with a "see to it yourselves" because it is small or out of sight. Much of the grievance of those who serve is grievance at "no one sees what I do." The house of Merari shows us another reckoning: the One who does see is precisely the One who appointed by name. If the name has been called and the instrument assigned, how can the work count as unseen? To be reckoned by name is itself the evidence of being seen. Gladly bearing boards and pillars, driving pins and hauling cords, letting the tabernacle stand steady first in the places no one looks — such a servant is the tabernacle's bone: the bone never shows its face, and the whole body stands by it.

(Application) Set the three houses side by side and one more lesson gathers them all. The houses do not differ in their age threshold — thirty to fifty for all; nor in the strictness of their oversight — a priest stands over each; nor in the fineness of their assignment — each is appointed passage by passage, and reckoned by name. They differ only in what they bear: the most holy vessels, the fabrics, the frame. And the text never permits the three houses to measure themselves against one another: the Kohathite must not laugh at the Merarite for bearing wood, exactly as the Merarite must not envy the Kohathite for bearing the ark — because the three houses' registers together make one whole tabernacle. Without Kohath, the holy things have no bearer; without Gershon, no covering and no curtain; without Merari, the whole tabernacle does not stand. One tabernacle's march is one company made of three pairs of shoulders; and one man's office, however regular, is only one pair of shoulders. Maturity in service is first to bear one's own line steadily, rightly, and to the end of one's term — and then to learn gratitude for the line beside one's own: the carefulness that bears the cloth, the reverence that bears the vessels, the strength that bears the frame are one and the same faithfulness, measured by the same Lord onto different shoulders.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The numbering rendered: two thousand seven hundred and fifty, two thousand six hundred and thirty, three thousand and two hundred — eight thousand and five hundred and fourscore in all

(Fact) The charges assigned, the chapter's last passage (vv. 34-49) reports the numbering's results house by house; the three houses' figures are stated here from the text verse by verse, each item copied out and the sum added by hand in preparing this sermon. The first house, Kohath (vv. 34-37, Summary): Moses, Aaron, and the chiefs of the congregation numbered the sons of Kohath after their families and by the house of their fathers, from thirty years old until fifty, all who entered the service to do the work of the tabernacle; the number stands in the KJV: "And those that were numbered of them by their families were two thousand seven hundred and fifty." (Num 4:36, KJV) — Kohath in service: two thousand seven hundred and fifty. The second house, Gershon (vv. 38-41, Summary): the sons of Gershon were numbered after their families and by the house of their fathers, from thirty years old until fifty, all who entered the service to do the work of the tabernacle, and the number stands in the KJV: "Even those that were numbered of them, throughout their families, by the house of their fathers, were two thousand and six hundred and thirty." (Num 4:40, KJV) — Gershon in service: two thousand six hundred and thirty. The third house, Merari (vv. 42-45, Summary): the families of the sons of Merari were numbered after their families, from thirty years old until fifty, all who entered the service to do the work of the tabernacle, and the number stands in the KJV: "Even those that were numbered of them after their families, were three thousand and two hundred." (Num 4:44, KJV) — Merari in service: three thousand and two hundred. In preparing the English manuscript, the three figures were independently re-checked against the KJV verses cited, not carried over from the Chinese manuscript's statements.

(Fact) Now let the addition be verified in the open; in preparing this sermon every item was added by hand, and the working is set down here: Kohath two thousand seven hundred and fifty, plus Gershon two thousand six hundred and thirty, makes five thousand three hundred and eighty; five thousand three hundred and eighty, plus Merari three thousand and two hundred, makes eight thousand five hundred and eighty. Now look at the total the chapter itself states: the closing summary (vv. 46-48, Summary) records all the Levites numbered by Moses, Aaron, and the chiefs of Israel, after their families and by the house of their fathers, from thirty years old until fifty, everyone who came to do the service of the ministry and the service of the burden in the tabernacle — and the total stands in the KJV: "Even those that were numbered of them, were eight thousand and five hundred and fourscore." (Num 4:48, KJV) The items added together make eight thousand five hundred and eighty; the chapter's own stated total is eight thousand five hundred and eighty — the two agree exactly, with no difference. This passage stands otherwise than chapter 3: there, the three houses' items added to twenty-two thousand three hundred, against a stated total of twenty-two thousand — a difference of three hundred, which the companion sermon sets out side by side exactly as it stands, with no harmonisation. Here, items and total fit without a seam. This sermon states each chapter's case as that chapter gives it: in chapter 3 it owns the tension of the three hundred; in chapter 4 it owns the agreement of the eight thousand five hundred and eighty. It does not alter a figure in one chapter for neatness' sake, nor doubt a figure in the other for tension's sake.

(Application) Inside these figures there hides one quiet fact, worth pointing out. Look back at chapter 3's totals of males from a month old: Kohath eight thousand and six hundred, Gershon seven thousand and five hundred, Merari six thousand and two hundred (Numbers 3, Summary) — there Kohath was the most, and Merari the fewest. In this chapter's men in service, from thirty to fifty, the order is wholly reversed: Kohath two thousand seven hundred and fifty, Gershon two thousand six hundred and thirty, Merari three thousand and two hundred — the house fewest in total numbers has the most men in service, and the house most in total numbers stands only second in service. This sermon states the comparison of the two sets of figures only (both sets follow their chapters' own texts; see Endnote 4), and invents no reason for it. But the comparison preaches by itself: the number of men fit for office is never dealt out automatically by a house's size — the house that bears the frame, smallest in population, stands fullest on duty. What God measures to each house is never an average: how many men of serviceable age each house has, and how many can stand on duty, stand exact to a man in His numbering. The small house need not despise itself — Merari's three thousand and two hundred stand on duty lacking nothing beside any house; and the large house must not boast — however many its infants on the register, those who can bear the holy things still have to be numbered one by one up to thirty years before they count.

(Fact) The last verse of the passage gathers the whole chapter's numbering into one sentence, in the KJV: "According to the commandment of the Lord they were numbered by the hand of Moses, every one according to his service, and according to his burden: thus were they numbered of him, as the Lord commanded Moses." (Num 4:49, KJV) Mark how this numbering proceeds: it is not that heads are first counted and work afterwards distributed; they are numbered "according to his service, and according to his burden" — numbering and assignment are one and the same procedure: to number a man is at the same moment to fix what service he does and what burden he bears. In this verse's Hebrew, the chapter's three keywords stand together in one sentence — "his service" is a form of ʿăḇōḏāh (H5656), "his burden" a form of massāʾ (H4853), and "numbered" is pāqad (H6485) — checked verse by verse against the Hebrew text (see Endnotes 8-10). Of the eight thousand five hundred and eighty men in service, not one is an empty number: under every number lies one concrete service and one concrete burden. That is the fundamental difference between this chapter's numbering and every census of population: a census counts how many people there are; this chapter counts how many people there are, and what charge each one bears.

(Application) Set the two numberings of the Levites in chapters 3 and 4 side by side, and a complete picture emerges. Chapter 3's numbering answers the question, whose are they? — twenty-two thousand Levites taken out whole, in the stead of all the firstborn of Israel: that is the register of grace, and everyone from a month old is in it. Chapter 4's numbering answers the question, who is on duty? — eight thousand five hundred and eighty men from thirty to fifty, assigned their charges: that is the register of office, and only men of the right age, with a burden assigned, are in it. The two registers must not be confused: one is wide, the other strict; one treats of belonging, the other of office. Apply office's strictness to belonging, and a man will think he must qualify before he can be graced; apply belonging's wideness to office, and a man will think that because he is graced he may lay hands on the work at any age and without any assignment. Much of the church's disorder comes from exactly this confusion of the two registers: some frighten new believers with the standards of office, as if a man not yet mature to thirty years' measure were not God's child at all; others plead the grace of belonging as a covering for their own boundary-crossing, as if being God's children meant the holy things could be touched at will. The text writes the two chapters separately to teach us to speak the two things separately: the door of belonging Christ has opened wide enough, and the infant is in the register; the door of office is narrow, and narrow with reason — age, appointment, boundary, reckoning by name, not one of them can be spared. Between the wideness and the strictness stand two kinds of one God's love toward the same people: in receiving, He asks no qualification; in sending, He always asks preparation.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: those numbered are those assigned a burden

(Fact) The chapter runs from the age command for the house of Kohath to the words "as the Lord commanded Moses": at its head, a standard; in its middle, three houses' objects and boundaries; at its end, the account of eight thousand five hundred and eighty rendered. Its three lines can now be gathered. First, office has its years: from thirty years old until fifty (vv. 3, 23, 47) — belonging numbers men from a month old; office numbers only the men within the twenty years of the term; being received and being sent are two registers. Second, charges are assigned by house and reckoned by name: Kohath bears the most holy things — covered first, borne afterwards, not to be touched, and not to be seen even for a moment while they are covered (vv. 4-20); Gershon bears the line of fabrics, all at the appointment of Aaron and his sons (vv. 21-28); Merari bears the line of boards, bars, pillars, and sockets, its instruments reckoned by name (vv. 29-33); two houses stand under Ithamar, and over the Kohath line and the whole tabernacle stands Eleazar with oversight (vv. 16, 28, 33, Summary). Third, the figures are exactly kept: two thousand seven hundred and fifty, plus two thousand six hundred and thirty, plus three thousand and two hundred, make eight thousand and five hundred and fourscore, in exact agreement with the chapter's own stated total (vv. 36, 40, 44, 48), and every man is numbered according to his service and according to his burden (v. 49) — no empty numbers, and no nominal offices.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, to come forward to serve, a man must first be able to wait: from being numbered at a month old to being assigned at thirty, nearly thirty years of preparation are no blank — the servant of God waits quietly in the register, until the command comes, the years are full, and the assignment is given. Second, to come forward to serve, a man must keep the boundary: the men nearest the holy things live under the strictest rules — not to touch, not to see, not to cross into the stretch the priests do first; the boundary is no obstruction, but the keeping that is "that they may live." Third, to come forward to serve, a man must bear something concrete: no Levite has a title without a burden — what service you do, what burden you bear, whose appointment you serve under, and in whose oversight you stand, must all be stateable, item by item. May what we covet be not the conspicuous place, but this: to be numbered, assigned, and reckoned by name — and then, for decades as one day, to bear our own portion steady, until at the term's end the portion is laid down and handed back, whole and undamaged, into the hands of the One who takes the number.

(New Testament reading) Two New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. The first is Hebrews 10: by the blood of Jesus, believers have boldness to enter into the holiest, by a new and living way which He has consecrated for us, through the veil (Heb 10:19-20, Summary) — the sanctuary into which the Kohathites might not go to see even for a moment, and the veil the priests took down to cover the ark, answer in the New Testament reading to that way "through the veil": on one side, the bearers of the holy things halt outside the veil; on the other, all the children are welcomed within it; and between the two stands the One who Himself became the way. The second is Luke 3: Jesus, when He began His ministry, was about thirty years of age (Luke 3:23, Summary) — this chapter's starting age for office is thirty years, and the Gospel records that the One who came forth to serve likewise began at about thirty. This sermon states the two figures as each text records them, and their correspondence, and does not read the sense of one back into the other. (Application) But the layers must be kept straight: Numbers 4 is first of all itself — a threshold of thirty to fifty years, an order of covering and bearing, a verified account of eight thousand five hundred and eighty. The New Testament reading is the light in which the church, in Christ, looks back on it; it must not be used to dissolve this chapter's rules. The more solid this chapter's rules — the ages checkable verse by verse, the objects countable house by house, the boundaries quotable word by word — the more weight the reading layer's correspondence carries: the place where the bearers of the holy things once halted is precisely the place where the way was afterwards opened; and the more clearly the halting's rule is preached, the greater the opened grace appears.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's own hands. You may have stood for years in the register of belonging, and still never have made clear what burden of office is yours; or you may be inside those nearly thirty years of waiting, watching others take up the work, with an impatient heart — this chapter has a word for both. To the waiting it says: if your name is in the register, do not jump the start; the years of preparation are not shelving, they are the first stretch of service measured out to you — watching from alongside, learning in small things, standing steady outside the boundary. To those already on duty it says: state your portion clearly, and bear it steadily — what service you do, what burden you bear, whose appointment you serve under, in whose oversight you stand, and at what year your term ends; let everything be clear, and everything gladly borne. May each of us, after being numbered, also find the portion assigned to him; and at the day when the burden is laid down at the term's end, looking back on the journey, may the objects be undamaged, the boundaries uncrossed, and the name still standing fairly in the register of the One who takes the number.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 4 first showed me only three lists of men in service: of Kohath, two thousand seven hundred and fifty; of Gershon, two thousand and six hundred and thirty; of Merari, three thousand and two hundred. I added the three numbers for myself: two thousand seven hundred and fifty and two thousand and six hundred and thirty make five thousand three hundred and eighty, and with three thousand and two hundred more, eight thousand and five hundred and fourscore — exactly the number the text itself records, not one different. What touches my heart more is how the numbering was done: they were numbered by the hand of Moses, every one according to his service, and according to his burden. Of the eight thousand and five hundred and fourscore, not one is a bare number; under every name lies one particular service and one particular burden, and even the instruments the sons of Merari were to bear were reckoned by name. I often envy the Kohathites who bore the ark, and cannot see the worth of the portion in my own hands, as if bearing curtains or carrying boards were a lesser thing. But the text tells me the three houses were all measured by one measure: all entered at thirty years old and ceased at fifty, all served at the appointment of Aaron and his sons, and all were assigned by name. However unnoticed my portion is, it was appointed to me by name; and however strict the boundary I keep, it is there that I may live, and not die. So I pray today: Lord, I thank Thee that I do not only have my name in the register; I have also a burden Thou hast appointed me. Make me able to wait through the years of preparation, to keep the boundary I must keep, and on every day of my service to bear steadily the one service and the one burden appointed me — until the day I lay the burden down and give it back into Thy hand, whole and undamaged.

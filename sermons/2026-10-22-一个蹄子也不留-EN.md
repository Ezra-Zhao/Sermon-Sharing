## Summary (about 100 words)

As the locust plague approached, Pharaoh's servants cried out first that Egypt was already destroyed; Pharaoh made offers twice — only the men may go, then the people may go but the livestock must stay — yet Moses answered: there shall not a hoof be left behind. Then three days of darkness that could be felt came upon the whole land, yet in the homes of Israel there was light. Worship allows no mortgage; compromise always bids "only a little short," but God, across a long process, produces testimony for generations to come.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The previous chapter stopped after the plague of hail. The hail ruined the flax and the barley, the fields were a scorched expanse, and Pharaoh confessed his sin in Moses' presence, saying, I have sinned; the LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked. It sounded very fine, but as soon as the rain stopped and the hail ceased, his heart hardened again. At the opening of today's chapter 10, God first lays the matter bare: why has this confrontation dragged on so long, why, plague after plague and Pharaoh reversing himself again and again, does God still not finish the matter in a single night? God Himself gives the answer: in order to show these signs among them, and in order for Israel to pass this matter on to their children and grandchildren, telling in their ears what God has done, so that the people may know that He is the LORD.

Please remember this order: the plagues were not yet finished, but the textbook was established first. God was not only delivering one generation out of Egypt; He was at the same time preparing lessons for the next generation. And the text of today's lesson cuts deep: on one side a Pharaoh who keeps getting better at making offers, on the other side a Moses who keeps refusing to accept a discount. Pharaoh says, you may go, but only part of you may go; then he says, you may all go, but the livestock must stay. Moses' answer becomes the anchor of the whole chapter and the title of today's sermon: our cattle also shall go with us; there shall not a hoof be left behind.

Let us first read together several key passages:

"And the LORD said unto Moses, Go in unto Pharaoh: for I have hardened his heart, and the heart of his servants, that I might shew these my signs before him: and that thou mayest tell in the ears of thy son, and of thy son's son, what things I have wrought in Egypt, and my signs which I have done among them; that ye may know how that I am the LORD." (Exodus 10:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And Pharaoh's servants said unto him, How long shall this man be a snare unto us? let the men go, that they may serve the LORD their God: knowest thou not yet that Egypt is destroyed?" (Exodus 10:7, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses said, Thou must give us also sacrifices and burnt offerings, that we may sacrifice unto the LORD our God. Our cattle also shall go with us; there shall not an hoof be left behind." (Exodus 10:25-26, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses said, Thou hast spoken well, I will see thy face again no more." (Exodus 10:29, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, speak to us today through the locusts and the darkness, we ask You. You know that in every one of our hearts there is a negotiating table, and someone making us an offer, asking us to discount our worship a little. Make us hear Moses' sentence clearly, and in days of darkness make us recognize the light You have placed in our homes. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: God States the Purpose First, and the Servants See Reality First (10:1-11)

First consider the weight of these two opening verses of God's speech. Many people reading the ten plagues have a question in their hearts: since God is almighty, why did He not settle it once for all? Why let Moses enter the palace again and again, be refused again and again, and let the people be disappointed again and again? The first two verses of chapter 10 are the explanation God volunteered. He hardened Pharaoh's heart for two purposes: first, to show His signs among them, so that Egypt would see with its own eyes who the LORD is; second, for Israel to pass this matter down generation after generation, so that later people would know, I am the LORD. Please note, among the audience of the signs was a group of people not yet born at the time. In what God does, He never reckons only the account of today's generation. He allowed the process to stretch long because He was producing testimony: some lessons cannot be learned by hearing a doctrine once; one must see them with one's own eyes and endure them in one's own body before they are carved into the bone and can be told to one's children. The exodus was not a quick decisive war; it was a school. The ten plagues were not God handling things slowly; they were God compiling a textbook. Establish this point first, and we can understand all the tug-of-war that follows: the confrontation was long not because God was powerless to end it, but because what God wanted to end was not only slavery, but also the people's illusions about Egypt in their hearts, and later generations' ignorance of God.

Immediately after, Moses delivered the word: Thus saith the LORD — how long wilt thou refuse to humble thyself before me? This sentence was asked of Pharaoh, and it also measured Pharaoh. If you refuse to let my people go, tomorrow I will bring locusts into your coast; they shall cover the face of the earth, so that one cannot see the earth; they shall eat the residue of what the hail left, and every tree that grows in the field, and they shall fill your houses and the houses of your servants. This was the announcement of the eighth plague, and unlike the earlier plagues, this time the first to collapse was not Pharaoh, but Pharaoh's servants.

The servants said to Pharaoh a sentence that was almost a plea and almost a protest: How long shall this man be a snare unto us? Let the men go, that they may serve the LORD their God. Knowest thou not yet that Egypt is destroyed? Brothers and sisters, please stop at this sentence: knowest thou not yet that Egypt is destroyed. The ones who said this were not Moses, not Aaron, but Pharaoh's own people. They were in the palace every day; they had watched with their own eyes the Nile turn to blood, and the frogs, lice, flies, pestilence, boils, and hail pass plague after plague; they could calculate the losses; they knew the treasury was empty, the fields ruined, and the hearts of the people scattered. Bystanders see reality before the king does — this is true in every age. The person seated in the highest place is often the last to admit failure, because for others it is only a loss, but for him it is face — the whole mythology of his self-deification. To admit destruction is to admit he is not a god. The servants carried no such burden, so they woke first; Pharaoh had this idol, so he pretended to sleep to the end.

There is a heavy word here: "destroyed." In the original it is a verb form, from the root 'avad (אָבַד, H6), to perish, be lost, be destroyed [2]. The servants said 'avadah Mitsrayim — Egypt has perished, Egypt is finished. This was not saying Egypt had suffered a small loss; this was a verdict that the nation was about to perish, spoken from the mouths of its own servants. The most dangerous moment for a regime is not when the enemy has fought its way in, but when its own people have already concluded it is finished, and only the king alone still refuses to admit it. Did Pharaoh hear it? He heard it, but he took in only half of it. He summoned Moses and Aaron again and began his first offer: go, serve the LORD; only, who exactly shall go must be stated first. Moses answered: we will go, men and women, old and young, our sons and our daughters, and our flocks and our herds shall go with us, for we must hold a feast unto the LORD. This answer forced Pharaoh's real hand into the open. He said: if I let you go and your little ones go too, that would be no good intent; you may go — only let the men go and serve the LORD.

Please see clearly the structure of this offer: the people may go — in part; the women and children stay. On the surface a concession; in the bones, hostage-taking. The grown men go to worship, while wives and children are pledged in Egypt — would you dare not return? If you return, you must go on being slaves; your worship becomes a leave of absence with a deadline. Pharaoh's calculation was never to oppose worship — he was too shrewd for that; what he opposed was complete worship, free worship, worship in which the whole household leaves. A little religious freedom for you is acceptable, but the root must stay in Egypt. This is the first kind of compromise this chapter teaches us to see through: "only a little short." It does not tell you to worship idols; it only tells you to leave a little behind: leave the family, leave the property, leave one road of retreat. The little bit left behind is the rope that ties you down.

Moses did not accept this price. Pharaoh drove them out from his presence. The first round of negotiation broke down. The announcement of the locust plague had been issued, and the east wind was about to rise. Brothers and sisters, keep this passage in your hearts first: God produces testimony across a long process, the servants wake first before reality, and what Pharaoh learned first before destruction was not repentance, but making offers. Next we shall see how his price rose step by step to his bottom line, and how Moses' answer hardened step by step until not a hoof was left.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Locusts Eat What Remained, and Moses Says Not a Hoof (10:12-26)

Moses stretched out his rod, and an east wind blew on the land all that day and all that night. In the morning, the east wind brought the locusts. The Scripture records the weight of the locusts, each sentence heavier than the one before: the locusts went up and rested in all the coasts of Egypt, covering the whole land; never before had there been such a plague in Egypt, nor would there be again; they covered the face of the whole earth, so that the land was darkened; they ate every herb of the land and all the fruit of the trees which the hail had left, all that remained in the field; and there remained not any green thing in the trees or in the herbs of the field throughout all the land of Egypt. First the hail had beaten down all but a little hope; now the locusts came to finish it off. God's judgment sometimes does not shatter in a single blow, but strikes first and then eats clean: it takes away even the leftover grain by which a person thought he could rise again. Here we must get to know this word: locust, in the original 'arbeh (אַרְבֶּה, H697) [1]. Whenever this word appears in the Old Testament, the picture is a swarm blotting out the sky, a disaster like an army. The prophet Joel later wrote of a locust plague using the palmerworm, the locust, the cankerworm, and the devouring host, and connected that disaster to the day of the LORD; the Psalms, recalling the plagues of Egypt, twice name the locusts eating up all the increase of the land; and in Revelation the image of locusts appears again as part of the picture of judgment. The Bible uses locusts to say one thing: when God lets disaster advance, the little store of security a person has saved up by himself will be eaten away bite by bite.

Pharaoh's reaction this time was abnormally fast. He called for Moses and Aaron in haste and said: I have sinned against the LORD your God, and against you; forgive, I pray thee, my sin only this once, and intreat the LORD your God, that he may take away from me this death only. He spoke even more humbly than in the previous chapter. Moses went out and prayed for him, and the LORD turned the wind into a mighty strong west wind, which took away the locusts and cast them into the Red Sea; there remained not one locust in all the coasts of Egypt. The prayer was real, and the mercy was real, but as soon as the plague stopped, Pharaoh's heart hardened again, and he still would not let the children of Israel go. Brothers and sisters, this is the most sigh-worthy cycle in the Book of Exodus: when the plague comes, he confesses sin; when the plague goes, he reverses himself. Pharaoh's repentance was barometric: as great as the pressure of the plague, so great the repentance; once the pressure was withdrawn, the original form stood exposed. It was not that God could not see through it; it was that God let this cycle repeat enough times that everyone who reads the Scripture later would understand: confession forced out by circumstances, if it never touches the idol in the heart, is forever only asking the plague to leave, not asking God to enter.

Right in this cycle, the ninth plague came: darkness that could be felt fell upon the whole land of Egypt. In the midst of the darkness, Pharaoh called Moses and held the most crucial negotiation, making his final offer: go ye, serve the LORD; only let your flocks and your herds be stayed; let your little ones also go with you. Compared with the first offer, this price had risen a big step: last time only the men were allowed; this time the whole family could go, even the children were released. Only one condition remained: the livestock stays. Does that not sound like only a little short? The people have all gone — why haggle over cattle and sheep? Yet Moses would not yield an inch on precisely this point. He first returned a sentence: Thou must give us also sacrifices and burnt offerings, that we may sacrifice unto the LORD our God. Then he spoke the peak of the whole chapter: Our cattle also shall go with us; there shall not an hoof be left behind; for thereof must we take to serve the LORD our God; and we know not with what we must serve the LORD, until we come thither.

Please read those last sentences slowly. "Hoof," in the original parsah (פַּרְסָה, H6541), the cloven hoof of cattle and sheep [3]. A hoof is the smallest, least valuable part of an animal, the part almost no one would notice. Moses meant: not even the smallest, most inconspicuous portion may be pledged in Egypt. Why? The surface reason is practical: sacrifices require cattle and sheep; without livestock, worship has no offering. The deeper reason Moses stated himself: we know not with what we must serve the LORD, until we come thither. This sentence is the complete definition of faith. The list of sacrifices is not in Moses' hand; it is in God's hand. God had not yet said which ox or which sheep He would use — how would Moses dare to leave part of them in Egypt first? What if the one left behind is the very one God names? So the way of faith is: take everything and go; only when you arrive will you know. It is not first calculating clearly how much God needs and taking only just enough; it is first handing over ownership completely, and then, before God, listening to His arrangement. Many people's faith today stalls right here: we want first to know exactly how much God wants me to offer, how much to change, how much to lay down, and only move when the calculation is clear and the deal looks worthwhile. Moses says, no — the order is reversed: take everything first; only when you arrive will you know. If worship sets a ceiling in advance, it is not worship; it is a transaction.

We must also see the real danger of leaving the livestock behind. If all the cattle and sheep were pledged in Egypt, then even if Israel went out of the territory, they would still have to come back: if your assets are in someone's hand, your heart can come back, and your feet will come back too. Pharaoh releasing the people but holding the property, and last time releasing the men but holding the women and children, is the same technique, only more hidden and more respectable: he even appears reasonable — I let all the people go, didn't I? When compromise is upgraded to its final form, it always wears reasonable clothing: only the livestock stays, only a little short, only this once. But the completeness of worship dies precisely at "only a little short." Moses' "there shall not an hoof be left behind" is a declaration: we are not going to take a leave of absence; we are migrating. We are not going to perform a ceremony; we are changing masters. Egypt may no longer hold any mortgage over us — not even one hoof.

Look one layer deeper: what the locusts ate was not only grain. After the hail, the Egyptians actually still kept one thought in their hearts: the flax and the barley were ruined, but the wheat and the spelt had not been beaten down, because they had not yet grown up. That is to say, there was still a reserve in the fields and still hope in the storehouses, and a person could still say to himself, once this spell passes, we can plant again next year. On the morning the locusts came, what they ate was precisely this reserve. The text deliberately says there remained not any green thing; green is the color of survival, the sign that the land has not yet died through and through. When the green was gone, hope was cut off at the root. When God's discipline comes upon a stiff-necked person, it is often like this: first it takes away his harvest, then it takes away his hope, so that he can no longer fool himself with the sentence that next year will be better. The servants had long understood this layer, which is why they said Egypt is destroyed. Destruction is not only a poor harvest this year; destruction is a nation that no longer even has a next year. Only Pharaoh kept using negotiation to buy time, as if, so long as Moses left one day later, the throne could be sat on one day longer.

Please also note Moses' identity in this negotiation. He was not contending for anything for himself; he had no private list in his hand. Everything he contended for was necessary to worship: the people must all go, because worship is a matter for the whole household — the old must be present, and the children must be present too; the children must see with their own eyes, in the ranks of the feast, how God leads them out, so that in the future they can tell this history; the livestock must all be taken, because the offerings must be taken from among them, and worship is not going empty-handed to say a few fine words — worship must carry a real cost, carrying the cattle and sheep taken from one's own flock. This explains why Moses never haggled over trivial matters, yet on this point would not yield an inch. What does not matter can be yielded; the constitution of worship cannot be yielded. One person missing, and worship lacks a generation; one portion of property missing, and the altar may lack the very offering God names. Pharaoh thought he was bargaining; Moses knew he was guarding a boundary line: on this side of the line is serving the LORD, on that side is serving Pharaoh, and one cannot stand with one foot on each side.

There is another layer, spoken to those who are parents and leaders today. Pharaoh's first offer tried to hold the women and children; his second offer switched to holding the livestock. It looked like a concession, but in the bones it was the same prescription with a different label: some portion of Israel's substance must always stay in Egypt, so that Egypt would always have something to say over Israel. The world reaches toward our children in the same order: first it says, going to church is fine, just do not spend too much time; then it says, believing is fine too, just keep a little something else in your heart to rely on. It does not fear your worship; it fears your worshipping with your whole family, your whole heart, and your whole estate. So Moses' answer was not spoken only to Pharaoh; it was spoken to parents of every generation: our children shall go with us, our possessions shall go with us; in the ranks of our feast, not one person may be held back, and not even one hoof of the livestock may be left to Egypt as a souvenir. Once we understand this passage, and then enter the plague of darkness, we will know why, when the whole land was dark, God especially recorded one stroke: that in the dwellings of the Israelites there was light — because that light was preserved for those who refused to pledge their children and their possessions to the darkness.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Darkness That Could Be Felt, Light in the Homes, and the Final Break (10:21-29)

At this point God no longer had Moses enter the palace to deliver a message; the ninth plague came directly: And the LORD said unto Moses, Stretch out thine hand toward heaven, that there may be darkness over the land of Egypt, even darkness which may be felt. Moses stretched forth his hand toward heaven, and there was a thick darkness in all the land of Egypt three days. For three days they saw not one another, neither rose any from his place. At the end the text adds the warmest sentence in the whole chapter: but all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings.

First, this word "darkness." In the original choshek (חֹשֶׁךְ, H2822), darkness [4]; verse 22 also uses 'aphelah (אֲפֵלָה), gloom, thick darkness [5]. Even more special is that word "felt." The text says this darkness may be felt; the verb is veyamesh (וְיָמֵשׁ, H4184), meaning to feel, to grope, to touch [6], and the KJV renders it as "darkness which may be felt". What kind of darkness was this? Not the black of a night with the lights turned off, but a blackness thick as if it were a substance pressing down, so that an outstretched hand could feel resistance. For three days people could not see one another, and no one dared rise from his place. All Egypt stopped dead. Many commentators point out that the Egyptians worshipped Ra, the sun god, as a supreme deity, and sunrise and sunset were the foundation of their faith; three days of darkness over the whole land was a frontal demolition of that religion. It must be explained that this correspondence is a reading common among scholars and commentaries; the text itself does not expressly state Ra's name, and this manuscript uses it accordingly as background explanation, not as the statement of the text [7]. By contrast, at the opening of Genesis, the first thing God did on the first day was to say, Let there be light, and to divide the light from the darkness. The same God who divided light and darkness at creation also divided light and darkness in Egypt: the whole land of Egypt was dark, yet in the homes of His people there was light. This light was not a skill Israel lit by itself; it was the distinction God preserved for His people in the land of judgment.

Now bite into one more word: "dwellings." In the original moshvot (מוֹשְׁבֹת, H4186), dwellings, habitations [8]; verse 23 says that in the dwellings, the habitations, of the Israelites there was light. The light was not in the wilderness, not on the road, but in the dwellings, in the places of daily living. Brothers and sisters, this is extremely important for us today. The light God gives His people is often not the removal of the darkness of the whole land at once, but first the lighting of a lamp in your home. The whole land is still dark, the judgment is not yet over, the day of the exodus has not yet arrived, yet God's people are not people groping in darkness: they have light, they can see one another, they know who they are and where they are going. Light still in one's dwelling in the midst of darkness — this is the most solid comfort chapter 10 gives to those who suffer: God may not immediately end your night, but He will never let you endure the night without light.

It was after the three days of darkness, and against this background, that Pharaoh summoned Moses and made the offer that the livestock stay; and when Moses refused it with "not a hoof," Pharaoh finally tore off the mask. He said to Moses: Get thee from me, take heed to thyself, see my face no more; for in that day thou seest my face thou shalt die. This was the final ultimatum: from the negotiating table to the line of life and death. Moses' answer is calm in a way that commands reverence: And Moses said, Thou hast spoken well, I will see thy face again no more. No pleading, no bargaining, no lingering attachment. The messenger's words had been fully delivered, the king's offers had been fully exhausted, the signs had been shown enough, the textbook had been fully prepared, and at this point the relationship broke. This break was not Moses acting on impulse; it was the conclusion of a stage: the words of exhortation had been spoken to the end, and what remained was the great event of the Passover night. Brothers and sisters, life also has such points of rupture: some negotiations cannot be settled by one more round of talks; when the other side's offer is always that you mortgage your worship, mortgage your children, mortgage your conscience, that table must be left. Leaving is not failure; leaving is handing what comes after over to God. Moses turned and went out of the palace; of Egypt's ten plagues only the last remained, and the door of Israel's exodus was already letting light through in the darkness.

Apply this chapter to ourselves, and I will speak of three things. First, count the things in your worship that are pledged in Egypt. Pharaoh does not want all of you; he only wants you to leave a little: leave a little time to the calculations of the world, leave a little of your children's education to Egypt's values, leave a little of the decision over money un-surrendered, leave one road by which you can return at any time. Each item looks small, small as a hoof. But Moses says: there shall not a hoof be left behind. Today lay the pledge list out and ask God to shine light on it: what exactly do I still have pledged in the hand of the old master? Second, learn Moses' order: take everything and go; only when you arrive will you know. You do not need to settle the accounts of your whole life clearly before you are willing to follow; you cannot settle them clearly, and God has not asked you to. What He wants is for ownership to come over first, and the arrangements to follow afterward. In marriage, in matters concerning your children, in choices about staying or leaving a job, first place everything in His hand, then listen to Him say which portion to use and which portion to keep. Third, in the darkness, keep the light in your home. Your situation may see no improvement for three days, three months, or three years, and the people around you may not be able to see one another, but you must be the person whose home has light: pray as usual, read the Scripture as usual, see one another and speak to one another as usual. The light is not large, but it is enough for a family to see one another's faces. Keep this lamp, and the night cannot last forever; the day of the exodus will not be absent.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In today's chapter we have seen three things: a group of servants who woke first, a king who made offers to the very end, and a Moses who turned and left in the darkness. Egypt was already destroyed; only Pharaoh refused to admit it. The people were all released; only the livestock was still to be held — and Moses said: there shall not a hoof be left behind. Compromise always bids "only a little short," yet the completeness of worship allows no mortgage. God allowed this confrontation to stretch so long not because He delayed, but because He was producing testimony for children and grandchildren, so that every generation would know: He is the LORD. And when the whole land was dark enough to be felt, in the homes of His people there was light all along. This light is not a reward for the strong; it is a mark for those who, in the long night, still refuse to mortgage their worship away. They waited long, but they did not wait wrongly, because the One who leads them out has not changed His intent from the beginning to the end, nor taken back a single word He has spoken.

Let us pray together: LORD our God, thank You that You do not forsake us across a long process, and that You store up Your works for our children and grandchildren. Today, expose every "only a little short" transaction in our hearts, and forgive us our sin of discounting worship and pledging our children and our possessions in Egypt. Give us Moses' clarity: everything of ours must go with us; there shall not a hoof be left behind. And in our days that are still in darkness, preserve the light in our dwellings, so that we can see one another, and so that we know the day of the exodus is near. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[7] The correspondence between Ra, the sun god, and the plague of darkness: in Egyptian religion Ra, the sun god, was one of the supreme deities, and the three days of darkness are read by many commentators as a frontal demolition of Egyptian sun worship; this is a correspondence reading common among scholars and commentaries, and the text of Exodus chapter 10 does not expressly state Ra's name, so in the body of this manuscript it has already been noted as background explanation rather than the statement of the text. Facts and exposition are marked separately.

[8] moshvot: Hebrew מוֹשְׁבֹת (H4186), the plural form of moshav, dwelling, habitation, place of sitting; Exodus 10:23, "all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings," in the original referring to all the dwellings of the Israelites; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "light in the places of daily living" is a homiletical exposition based on this word.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Our cattle also shall go with us; there shall not an hoof be left behind; for thereof must we take to serve the LORD our God; and we know not with what we must serve the LORD, until we come thither." (Exodus 10:26) "But all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings." (Exodus 10:23)

Pharaoh's final offer sounds most like the voice in our own hearts: let the people go, only leave the livestock behind. Only a little short — why haggle over it? But Moses knew that the portion pledged in Egypt is the very portion that will one day drag a person back. Worship cannot be mortgaged, and faith cannot leave home carrying only half. We do not yet know with what God will have us serve Him, so we must take everything and go, and only when we arrive will we know. Today, hand over your pledge list: that little bit of time you refuse to yield, that little habit you refuse to change, that little escape route you keep for yourself. The darkness may not yet have lifted, but when the whole land is dark, God first lights a lamp in the homes of His people. Take everything, keep that light, and the day of exodus is just ahead. If one hoof still stays in Egypt today, tomorrow there will still be a rope tied around the heart; if you are willing to hand everything over today, the person walking in the wilderness tomorrow will be a free person. May our generation learn this sentence first, and then tell it to our children exactly as it is, so that from childhood they know: our God does not accept discounted worship, and He never lets His people grope alone in the darkness.

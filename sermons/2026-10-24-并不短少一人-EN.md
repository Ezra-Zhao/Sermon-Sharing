## Summary

The ordinances of vows have just been laid down (chapter 30, preceding-text layer) when, the words hardly fallen, the drum of war sounds. The LORD charges Moses: "Avenge the children of Israel of the Midianites: afterward shalt thou be gathered unto thy people." (31:2) This is the last war Moses is commanded to fight before his death. A thousand from every tribe, twelve thousand in all, are armed for war; Phinehas the son of Eleazar the priest goes with them, carrying in his hand the holy instruments and the trumpets to blow (31:5-6). They war against the Midianites as the LORD commanded, slay all the males, and slay also the five kings of Midian — Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba — and Balaam the son of Beor with the sword; the women and the little ones are taken captive, the cattle, the flocks, and the goods are taken as spoil, the cities and castles are burnt with fire, and all the spoil is brought back to the camp on the plains of Moab (31:7-12). But Moses meets them outside the camp in anger: the victorious army has kept the women alive. His ground is one only, nailed to the matter of Peor: "Behold, these caused the children of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to commit trespass against the LORD in the matter of Peor, and there was a plague among the congregation of the LORD." (31:16) There follow, outside the camp, both a command of disposal (31:17-18) and an ordinance of purification: the whole army abides without the camp seven days and purifies itself on the third day and on the seventh; gold, silver, brass, iron, tin, and lead are to go through the fire and then be purified with the water of separation, and what cannot abide the fire is to go through the water; on the seventh day they wash their clothes, are clean, and may come into the camp (31:19-24). Then comes the longest stretch of account in the whole chapter: the LORD commands that the prey be divided into two parts, one half for the men who went out to battle and one half for all the congregation; from the men's half, one of every five hundred is levied for Eleazar the priest, as an heave offering to the LORD; from the congregation's half, one of every fifty is taken and given to the Levites, who keep the charge of the tabernacle of the LORD (31:25-30). The account is opened item by item: sheep 675,000, beeves 72,000, asses 61,000, women 32,000; each half receives its number, each levy follows its rate, and item by item they agree (31:32-47). At the end, the captains over thousands and over hundreds come before Moses and report: "Thy servants have taken the sum of the men of war which are under our charge, and there lacketh not one man of us." (31:49) Because not one is lacking, they bring the jewels of gold each man had gotten and offer them, to make an atonement for their souls; and Moses and Eleazar bring the gold into the tabernacle of the congregation, for a memorial for the children of Israel before the LORD (31:50-54). One half of this chapter is the sword, and the other half is the account: the sword's part takes only twelve verses, and the account's part takes more than thirty. What it would have us see is not only what war was fought, but that this war, from the receiving of the command to the rendering of the account to the memorial, is at every step set within ordinance — even the victor's hands must first be purified before they may enter the camp; even the spoil must first be divided before it may become anyone's own.

## Main Text

### Part One: The Last Commanded War — the Charge, the Going Out, and the Bringing Back (Numbers 31:1-12) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) In verse 1 the scene of transmission is registered from the base text: the LORD speaks unto Moses; this is the source of every action in the chapter. In verse 2 the command is quoted verbatim in full: "Avenge the children of Israel of the Midianites: afterward shalt thou be gathered unto thy people." (31:2) Registered item by item: first, the command's verb is "avenge," its object "the Midianites," and its ground is not unfolded in this verse in the base text, being gathered only in the phrase "the children of Israel['s vengeance]"; what it concretely denotes appears in Moses' own statement in verse 16 (the matter of Peor) — the two are recorded in their separate places and are not read together in advance. Second, the second half of the verse nails Moses' death to this war: when the war is done, he will be gathered unto his people; this is the last military command Moses is charged to execute, and his death itself has its own chapter later (Deuteronomy 34, following-text layer), whose existence only is registered here. In verse 3 Moses relays the command to the people, and the wording has changed; from the base text: "Arm some of yourselves unto the war, and let them go against the Midianites, and avenge the LORD of Midian." (31:3) Registered: God's words read "Avenge the children of Israel," and Moses' words read "avenge the LORD" — between the two sentences the belonging of the vengeance moves from the people toward the LORD; this sermon registers the difference of wording only and offers no harmonization of the two sentences. Verse 4 gives the ordinance of the levy: a thousand from every tribe. Verse 5 records the execution, quoted verbatim in full: "So there were delivered out of the thousands of Israel, a thousand of every tribe, twelve thousand armed for war." (31:5) Registered: one thousand multiplied by twelve tribes equals twelve thousand; the arithmetic agrees with the text. Those who go out are less than a tenth of the whole host drawn out from it, and the rest remain in the camp; the chapter's later twofold division between "them that went out to battle" and "all the congregation" begins here. Verse 6 records who goes with the army, quoted verbatim in full: "And Moses sent them to the war, a thousand of every tribe, them and Phinehas the son of Eleazar the priest, to the war, with the holy instruments, and the trumpets to blow in his hand." (31:6) Registered: the one who goes with the army is not an officer but the priest Phinehas; what is in his hand is not a weapon but the holy instruments and the trumpets — in this chapter's telling, this war carries from the start the mark of the sanctuary's presence. Phinehas' role in the matter of Peor stands in chapter 25 (preceding-text layer); this verse calls him only "the son of Eleazar the priest," recorded as it stands.

(Fact) Verse 7 passes over the battle itself in a single sentence, whose wording is registered: they warred against the Midianites, as the LORD commanded Moses, and slew all the males — of the fighting itself this chapter records no exchanges, no place-names, and no casualties, only the outcome. Verse 8 registers the list of the slain: the five kings of Midian, Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba, slain beside the rest of them that were slain; and Balaam the son of Beor also slain with the sword — Balaam's death has in this chapter this one sentence only, with no dying words and no narrative elaboration; recorded as it stands. Among them, the name Zur is the same name as the father of Cozbi, the Midianite woman slain in chapter 25 (25:15, preceding-text layer); whether it is the same man this chapter does not state plainly — the sameness of name only is registered, and no identification is made. Verse 9 records the taking of captives and spoil, quoted verbatim in full: "And the children of Israel took all the women of Midian captives, and their little ones, and took the spoil of all their cattle, and all their flocks, and all their goods." (31:9) Registered: the captives in this verse fall into the two classes "women" and "little ones"; this keeping alive is precisely the occasion of Moses' anger in verse 14 — the fact layer registers the sequence only and passes no prior judgment here on right or wrong. Verse 10 records the burning: all their cities wherein they dwelt, and all their goodly castles, are burnt with fire. Verse 11 sums up: all the spoil and all the prey, both of men and of beasts, are taken. Verse 12 registers the place of return and those who receive them: the captives, the prey, and the spoil are brought unto Moses, and Eleazar the priest, and unto the congregation of the children of Israel, unto the camp at the plains of Moab, which are by Jordan near Jericho — the camp is still east of the Jordan and has not crossed the river; recorded as it stands.

(Argument) The writing of this movement first gives us an unexpected quiet: twelve verses finish a war of extermination, and the drum of war is scarcely heard. There is no scene of two armies in array, no close-up of heroes in single combat, and even Balaam — the man who pours out words across three chapters — is given only the one sentence, slain with the sword. Scripture's restraint about slaughter lies exactly here: it does not render the flash of blades; what it renders is command and account. Why the war is fought, the text gives one chain only: the matter of Peor, the counsel of Balaam, and the plague of twenty-four thousand (chapter 25, preceding-text layer; stated by this chapter itself at 31:16). How the war is closed out, the text gives more than thirty verses: the meeting, the questioning, the purification, the division of the account, the memorial. Reading this chapter today, if we fix our eyes only on the sword of the first twelve verses we miss most of its disposition of space; by the text's own weighting, most of the ink is given to what comes after the sword — how, having won, one disposes, purifies, divides, and renders account to God. This corrects every cast of mind that thinks "winning is the end of it": on God's account, winning is only the beginning; rendering the account is the completion.

(Argument) Notice also Moses' position. When he is charged to fight this last war, his death has already been plainly spoken: "afterward shalt thou be gathered unto thy people." A man who knows his days are few, having fought the war, does not hurry to celebrate the victory; he first, outside the camp, inquires into the disposal, sets up the ordinances of purification, divides the spoil in the account down to single digits, and receives the officers' gold and sends it into the tabernacle for a memorial — everything he does is the setting of rules for the camp that will remain after his death. Moses has in this chapter not one sentence pleading his own case: he does not plead to enter Canaan, does not plead for a name after death, but spends his last breath on rendering account. This gives everyone counting their days in a post a pattern: before leaving office, what matters most is not how much war-merit is left behind, but that the account is rendered clear, the ordinances set in order, and the memorial sent into the tabernacle — so that whoever takes over finds every item agreeing with its number.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Avenge / vengeance": the noun neqāmâ (H5359) and the verb nāqam (H5358); the command of verse 2 and the relay of verse 3 use the same root. "War" is milḥāmâ (H4421). "Spoil" is šālal (H7998); the "prey that was taken" of the general account in verse 32 uses a different word, baz (H957) — the two words are recorded separately; see Endnote 5. The "be gathered" of "shalt thou be gathered unto thy people" is the passive use of ʾāsap (H622), in the same form as at Aaron's death in chapter 20 (preceding-text layer); the word-form only is registered, without expansion.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The relation of this chapter to the Peor incident of chapter 25 is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one reading takes this chapter as the direct sequel of chapter 25 — 25:16-18 has already plainly commanded that the Midianites be vexed, this chapter is the execution of that command, and the attribution to Balaam's counsel (31:16) nails the two chapters together; another reading takes the sequence and arrangement of the two chapters to have a further historical layering of their own, the command sentence of chapter 25 and the execution account of chapter 31 not necessarily belonging to the same narrative layer, Midian having another face elsewhere in this book (as in the dealings with the family of Moses' father-in-law in chapter 10, preceding-text layer). This sermon registers only the correspondences the two chapters state of themselves (the command of 25:17-18 and the command sentence of 31:2; the attribution sentence of 31:16) and the existence of the tension within the same book, and does not adjudicate arrangement or dating at the fact layer. The main text's argument uses only the command, the attribution, and the account this chapter states of itself.

(Fact) This movement closes by registering the first movement's account: the command, one verse (31:2); the relay, one verse (31:3); the ordinance of the levy, one verse (31:4); twelve thousand sent out (31:5); one priest going with the army (31:6); the battle and the slaying of the males (31:7); the five kings and Balaam slain (31:8); the women and little ones taken captive, with the cattle and goods (31:9); the cities and castles burnt (31:10); the spoil summed up (31:11); the bringing back before the camp on the plains of Moab (31:12). And one whole-chapter contrast is registered: from beginning to end of this chapter, Moses is not on the battlefield — he sends others out and himself remains in the camp awaiting the account; Moses' actions in the whole chapter are only charging, meeting, being wroth, teaching the disposal, dividing the account, and receiving the memorial; recorded and registered as they stand.


### Part Two: The Meeting Without the Camp — Wrath, Attribution, and the Ordinance of Purification (Numbers 31:13-24) (10:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 13 registers the meeting: Moses, and Eleazar the priest, and all the princes of the congregation, went forth to meet them without the camp — the place of the meeting is without the camp, and the questioning, the disposal, and the purification that follow all take place without the camp; entering the camp is the last step; recorded as it stands. Verse 14 registers the wrath: Moses was wroth with the officers of the host, with the captains over thousands and captains over hundreds, which came from the battle — the object of the wrath is the officers, not the men of war: in this chapter the responsibility for the disposal is recorded under the names of those who led the force. Verse 15 registers the substance of the question: "Have ye saved all the women alive?" (31:15) — the form of the question is recorded and registered; the wrath issues in a question. Verse 16 gives the attribution, quoted verbatim in full: "Behold, these caused the children of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to commit trespass against the LORD in the matter of Peor, and there was a plague among the congregation of the LORD." (31:16) Registered item by item: first, the chain of attribution has three links — Balaam's counsel first, the women's enticement in the middle, Israel's trespass against the LORD after, and the fruit a plague among the congregation. Second, this verse is the one place in the whole Bible that states plainly that Balaam had a counsel in the matter of Peor (the Peor incident itself stands in chapter 25, preceding-text layer, and that chapter does not mention Balaam's counsel); this sermon registers only the two chapters' division of record and does not supply what chapter 25 does not have. Third, Moses' words here are the ground of rebuke addressed to the officers, not a newly enacted ordinance; the disposal command that follows in the chapter (31:17-18) rests on this as its basis. Verses 17-18 give the disposal command, whose wording is registered: the male among the little ones and the women that have known man are within the command, and the women children that have not known a man are kept alive — the original wording of the command is registered from the base text in Endnote 3; the fact layer records only its scope and its objects and passes no ethical judgment here; the accounts of judgment are set down at this section's discussion layer. Verse 19 gives the first part of the purification ordinance, quoted verbatim in full: "And do ye abide without the camp seven days: whosoever hath killed any person, and whosoever hath touched any slain, purify both yourselves and your captives on the third day, and on the seventh day." (31:19) Registered: they abide without the camp seven days; the purification is on the third day and on the seventh day; its objects include the captives as well. Verse 20 gives the second part of the ordinance, registered: all raiment, all that is made of skins, all work of goats' hair, and all things made of wood are to be purified — the purification of things stands alongside the purification of persons. Verses 21-22 register the priest's teaching: Eleazar the priest declares to the men of war the ordinance of the law which the LORD commanded Moses, and lists the metals that may abide the fire — gold, silver, brass, iron, tin, and lead, six in all. Verse 23 gives the third part of the ordinance, quoted verbatim in full: "every thing that may abide the fire, ye shall make it go through the fire, and it shall be clean: nevertheless it shall be purified with the water of separation: and all that abideth not the fire ye shall make go through the water." (31:23) Registered: after going through the fire there must still be purification with water — a double purification; what cannot abide the fire goes through the water only. Verse 24 closes the ordinance, registered: on the seventh day they wash their clothes and are clean, and afterward they may come into the camp — entering the camp is the endpoint of the purification ordinance, not the starting point of a triumph.

(Argument) This movement speaks first of the sense of place in the two words "without the camp." A victorious army, bringing spoil, ought by rights to march triumphantly into the camp; Scripture instead keeps them outside the camp for seven days. Moses goes out to meet them — he does not welcome them through the gate; the questions are asked outside the camp, the wrath burns outside the camp, the washing is done outside the camp, and the account is reckoned outside the camp. Why? Because their hands have touched death — whoever has killed any person, and whoever has touched any slain, is unclean within this book's system of purification (the water of separation of chapter 19, preceding-text layer). The war fought was a commanded war, yet the death touched is still unclean death: obedience to the command does not amount to automatic cleanness, and victory does not amount to walking straight into the camp where God dwells. This touches us very nearly: we commonly suppose that if the thing was done right, the battle won, and the argument carried, we may come before God with our heads high to claim the credit. This chapter says: stop outside the camp first. What your hands have touched — even what they touched under orders — must be purified item by item: the clothes washed, the vessels put through fire and water, and the persons purifying themselves twice, on the third day and on the seventh. What God has always valued is not only what you have done, but in what state you come back before Him.

(Argument) Consider next Moses' wrath. It is not wrath at a lost battle — the battle was won; it is wrath at a battle "fought by halves": the males were slain to the last, yet the very line that had enticed the congregation to trespass against God was brought back into the camp intact. The officers' arithmetic is not hard to understand: women and children are spoil, part of the victor's takings, and keeping them alive was customary in the warfare of the time. What Moses interrupts is exactly this "custom": some things that count as custom by convention count as an unsettled account on God's ledger. The plague at Peor killed twenty-four thousand (25:9, preceding-text layer); that wound had not yet scabbed over, and the victors were leading the wound's source into the camp as spoil. Moses' wrath falls on the leaders' failure to see: the men of war are charged only with fighting, but the officers are charged with knowing what war this is and what account stands unsettled in it. Set upon everyone who leads a team today, this still holds: completing the task is not the same as settling the account; bringing back as results what ought not to have been brought back is the leader's charge — and it is the officers whom Moses questions.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Trespass" is maʿal (H4604), the sin of unfaithfulness against the LORD, used in verse 16; "plague" is maggēpâ (H4046); the verb "be clean / purify" is ṭāhēr (H2891), and "the water of separation" is mê niddâ (niddâ H5079); "ordinance" is again ḥuqqîm (the plural of ḥōq H2706), the same word as closes chapter 30 (preceding-text layer); see Endnote 5. The "go through" of "go through the fire / go through the water" is the causative stem of ʿābar (H5674); the root only is registered.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The ethical evaluation of the disposal command of verses 17-18 is set out side by side without adjudication — the evaluation of this chapter's warfare and of this command is where readings have always diverged most sharply, and this sermon, by its settled rule, states the accounts only and passes no verdict at the fact layer or the argument layer. Four readings are set side by side. First, the historical-setting reading: this command is understood within the war conventions of the ancient Near East and the narrative logic of the Peor incident — the enticers are the occasion of the plague, and the disposal aims at cutting off a recurrence; the reader should first restore it to its historical context before speaking of evaluation. Second, the canonical-tension reading: elsewhere in this book and in the whole canon there are other provisions for foreign women, captives, and those who surrender (as in the war ordinances and the case of marrying a captive in Deuteronomy 20-21, following-text layer), and read alongside this command there is tension; the existence of the tension is itself part of reading the text and need not be smoothed away. Third, the narrative-function reading: in the narrative this command completes the settling of the Peor account and opens the purification and the division; its weight rests on the camp's holiness and order, not on laying down a general rule of war for later ages; the thirty verses that follow in this chapter are all about purification and division, and the proportion of space supports this reading's judgment of where the weight lies. Fourth, the modern ethical-questioning reading: modern readers question the rightness of this command on humanitarian and war-ethical grounds; the questioning really exists and is an unavoidable part of reading this chapter, and this sermon registers its existence and its grounds as they are (such as the questions of collective punishment and the implication of the innocent) and does not adjudicate its right or wrong here. The four readings stand together; the main text's argument uses only the three things this chapter states of itself: the scope of the command, the form of the attribution sentence (31:16), and the fact of the chapter's disposition of space, in which purification and division follow immediately on the disposal. The adjudication of the ethics is left to the discussion layer of pulpit and fellowship and is not written into the fact layer.

(New Testament Reading) Revelation, speaking of the church in Pergamos, points its rebuke back to Balaam: he "taught Balac to cast a stumblingblock before the children of Israel, to eat things sacrificed unto idols, and to commit fornication" (Rev 2:14, cross-reference reading layer; quoted verbatim from the KJV at that place). One contrast is registered at the reading layer: the New Testament's looking back on the Peor-Balaam line takes the side of "teaching" — Balaam's counsel is raised again as the archetype of the stumblingblock, while this chapter's disposal command and division ordinances are nowhere cited by the New Testament as a pattern for the church's action; the mentions of Balaam in 2 Peter 2:15 and Jude 11 (cross-reference reading layer) follow the same line of teaching. The reading layer registers only how the New Testament looks back on this matter; it does not read the New Testament's teaching back in as the fact of this chapter's command, nor cover the New Testament's teaching with this chapter's command. The two layers are kept apart, and the main text's argument still rests on this chapter's own text.

(Fact) This movement closes by registering the second movement's account: the meeting without the camp (31:13); the wrath and the questioning of the officers (31:14-15); the one sentence of attribution (31:16); the disposal command, two verses (31:17-18); the purification ordinance, six verses (31:19-24) — the persons abide without the camp seven days and are purified on the third day and on the seventh; the things go through fire or water according as they can or cannot abide the fire; on the seventh day they wash their clothes and enter the camp. The distribution of speakers is also registered: the speakers in this movement are Moses (the questioning and the command) and Eleazar (the declaring of the ordinance), two men; the officers have no recorded word of reply in this movement — their reply and their offering will appear only in the fifth movement (31:48-50), recorded as it stands.

### Part Three: The Law of the Division — Two Halves, One of Five Hundred, and One of Fifty (Numbers 31:25-31) (24:00-34:00)

(Fact) Verse 25 registers the scene of transmission: the LORD speaks unto Moses and lays down the rule of the division — the division is not Moses' decision on the spot; it is a command; recorded as it stands. Verse 26 registers those charged and the objects: Moses, Eleazar the priest, and the chief fathers of the congregation together take the sum of the prey that was taken, both of man and of beast — first there is a total, and only then a division; the order is recorded as it stands. Verse 27 states the rule of the two halves, whose wording is registered: the prey is divided into two parts, one part between them that took the war upon them, who went out to battle, and one part between all the congregation — the division is an equal halving, not by heads and not by merit; recorded as it stands. Verses 28-29 register the levy from the men's half: from the portion of the men of war which went out to battle, "one soul of five hundred, both of the persons, and of the beeves, and of the asses, and of the sheep" (31:28), is levied and given unto Eleazar the priest, for an heave offering of the LORD — the rate of levy is one of five hundred, its destination the priest, and its name the LORD's heave offering. Verse 30 registers the levy from the congregation's half: from the children of Israel's half, "one portion of fifty, of the persons, of the beeves, of the asses, and of the flocks" (31:30), is taken and given unto the Levites, which keep the charge of the tabernacle of the LORD — the rate of levy is one of fifty, and its destination the Levites. Verse 31 registers the general statement of execution: Moses and Eleazar the priest did as the LORD commanded Moses — the law here enters into execution, and the next movement is the account item by item.

(Argument) The first striking thing about this movement's division is the "unfairness" of the equal halving. Those who went out to battle are twelve thousand men; those who remained in the camp are a congregation of several hundred thousand; yet the spoil is cut in two with one stroke — what falls to each fighting man is several dozen times what falls to each of those who stayed in camp. Scripture does not practise equality by heads here; it reckons another account: the war is the whole camp's war — the men were drawn from every tribe, the camp was kept by all, and the spoil, before it entered the camp, passed through the whole camp's ordinance of purification; at the division, what is acknowledged first is the large share, "one half for all the congregation," and only then, within the two halves, is a portion for God drawn out of each. Hidden in this method of division is an order: the fruit of victory is first not the fighters' private property but the whole camp's common property; and within the common property, God's portion is drawn out first, and only then does it come round to men dividing. Reverse the order — divide everything up first, and only then think of God — and however much is divided, the account is in disorder. In our handling today of everything "won" — an unexpected income, the fruit of a project, a lawsuit won — the order is this movement's examination question: is God's portion drawn out first, or is it left to see what remains at the end of the dividing?

(Argument) Consider next the asymmetry of the two rates of levy: from the men's half, one of five hundred; from the congregation's half, one of fifty — the congregation's rate of levy is ten times the men's. At first sight unfair; looked at closely, there is reason in it: the men's half has few people and heavy portions — they are the ones who risked their lives; the congregation's half has many people and light portions, and only a heavier rate makes the totals proportionate. What matters still more is the difference of destination: the levy from the men's portion goes to the priest and bears the name "an heave offering of the LORD"; the levy from the congregation's portion is given to the Levites — the Levites have no inheritance of land in this book (chapter 18, preceding-text layer), and their living is kept by precisely this one-of-fifty from the congregation. That is to say: those who fought declare, at a very low rate to God, that the fruit is His; those who stayed in camp sustain, at ten times the rate, the company who devote themselves to keeping the tabernacle. The rates are high and low; the principle is one and the same: within the portion that falls to every person, there is embedded a portion that is not his own. When a division is carried to this point, what is being divided is no longer only spoil; it is the whole camp's confession of "who sustains whom, and who belongs to whom."

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Heave offering" is terûmâ (H8641), the portion lifted up from the total and given to the LORD; the "take … and give" construction of the levy in verse 28 corresponds to this word's liturgical use. The "take the sum" of "take the sum" and the report of verse 49 use the same root, pāqad (H6485); the notion of the "sum" in verse 26 answers to the officers' use of the word in their report in verse 49; see Endnote 5. The original word-form behind the base text's rendering at verse 28 is marked pending verification in this sermon; see Endnote 5 — the base text's translated wording and the rate of levy themselves only are recorded.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The historical readings of the equal halving and the two-track levy are set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one reading takes as its background the spoil conventions of the ancient Near East — that king and temple took their portion of the spoil first was the common practice, and this chapter's levy can be understood within that convention, its singularity lying in how low the rate of levy is and how fine the division; another reading fixes its eye on this book's internal economic structure — the Levites have no land, and the priests live by the offerings (chapter 18, preceding-text layer), and this chapter's division is a further funding of that system of sustenance in an exceptional time, the two halves and the two rates both tailored to that structure. This sermon registers only the two readings' points of view and this chapter's numerical facts (the two halves, one of five hundred, one of fifty), and does not determine the law's origin or intent at the fact layer; the main text's argument uses only the proportions and destinations the law states of itself.

(Fact) This movement closes by registering the third movement's table of the law: the objects — the total of the prey that was taken, of persons and of beasts (31:26; the total is taken first); the method — an equal halving, one half for the men of war and one half for the congregation (31:27); the levy from the men's half — one of five hundred, to Eleazar the priest, as the LORD's heave offering (31:28-29); the levy from the congregation's half — one of fifty, to the Levites (31:30); those who execute — Moses and Eleazar (31:31). It is also registered: the law covers only the general account of "the prey that was taken"; what each man of war had gotten for himself on the battlefield is not within this rule (stated separately at 31:53; see Part Five) — the two accounts are kept apart, recorded as they stand.


### Part Four: The Account Itself — the Totals, the Two Halves, and the Levies Item by Item (Numbers 31:32-47) (34:00-48:00)

(Fact) Verse 32 registers the scope of the general account: what is listed below is the part of the spoil the men of war had caught that passed through the taking of the sum; what each man of war had gotten besides is not in this account (31:53) — recorded and kept apart. Verses 32-35 give the totals in four items, registered one by one; this sermon states them in Arabic numerals as its own accounting (not as quotation): sheep 675,000; beeves 72,000; asses 61,000; women 32,000 — these "women" are limited in verse 35 of the base text to women that had not known man, answering to the keeping alive commanded in verse 18; recorded and registered as they stand. Verses 36-40 give the account of the men's half, registered item by item: of the sheep the half is 337,500, of which the LORD's tribute is 675 (337,500 divided by 500, agreeing with one of five hundred); of the beeves the half is 36,000, and the tribute 72; of the asses the half is 30,500, and the tribute 61; of the persons the half is 16,000, of which the LORD's tribute is 32 persons — all four levies agree exactly with one of five hundred, with no remainder. Verse 41 registers the delivery: Moses gave the tribute, which was the LORD's heave offering, unto Eleazar the priest, as the LORD commanded. Verses 42-46 give the account of the congregation's half, registered item by item: of the sheep the half is 337,500, of which the portion taken for the Levites is 6,750 (337,500 divided by 50, agreeing with one of fifty); of the beeves 36,000, and the portion taken 720; of the asses 30,500, and the portion taken 610; of the persons 16,000, and the portion taken 320 persons — all four items agree exactly with one of fifty. Verse 47 registers the delivery: Moses, as commanded, gave the one-portion-of-fifty unto the Levites, which kept the charge of the tabernacle of the LORD. This sermon has recalculated by its own hand: the sums of the two halves agree item by item with the four totals (675,000; 72,000; 61,000; 32,000), and the eight levies recalculate item by item in agreement with the rule; for the details see Endnote 4.

(Argument) That Scripture keeps accounts without tiring, down to single digits, is itself a thing worth pausing over. Of 675,000 sheep, the LORD's portion is 675 — from a literary point of view this string of numbers has no "elegance" at all, and the reader would not notice if half of it were deleted; yet it stands here without one item missing. Why? Because in Scripture's world, the account is part of worship. A number recorded wrong amounts to an offering falling short; a number left vague amounts to God's portion made vague. Our age loves to say "it is the intention that counts"; Scripture's attitude is: the intention must arrive, and the account must arrive too; the feeling may surge, but the numbers must be calm. Where churches and individuals handle money and goods, trouble almost never begins with the great principles; it begins with the account — who handled it, how much it was, what fraction was levied, and into whose hands it was delivered. These sixteen verses of account are a mirror set up for everyone who handles common goods: can your account be spread out item by item like this, for others to recalculate? Only what recalculates to agreement is called clean; what does not recalculate is emptiness, however much spiritual language is spoken over it.

(Argument) There is one further item easily read past: the two halves have the same number of sheep, yet the levies differ tenfold — from the men's half 675 are levied, and from the congregation's half 6,750. The congregation's half of the sheep never went onto a battlefield, yet for the Levites it yielded 6,750. This is not a fine; it is an acknowledgment of shares: those who remained behind acknowledge that the tabernacle needs people devoted to keeping it, and that those who keep it need people to sustain them steadily; sustenance is not charity — it is a portion reckoned into the division from the start. Looked at from the other side, at the Levites: they do not fight and they divide no land; their "inheritance" is precisely this one-of-fifty on the congregation's account (chapter 18, preceding-text layer). Whether a camp is healthy is seen exactly in whether these two ends acknowledge the account: those who labour acknowledge God's portion, those who stay acknowledge the portion of sustenance, and those who keep the tabernacle acknowledge that they live by these two portions and do not reach out to seize a portion of land. When all three acknowledgments are in place, the account balances; where one place refuses to acknowledge, the account begins to rot.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The numbers of this movement are written out in Hebrew number-words in the original; this sermon registers its account in Arabic numerals and does not treat them as quotation. For "the prey that was taken," baz (H957), see the Summary of Part One. The objects of the "giving" differ between the two halves: the tribute is given to Eleazar the priest (31:41), and the one-of-fifty to the Levites (31:47); this sermon makes no further numbered pronouncement on the verbs and the procedure.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The historical readings of this chapter's numbers are set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one reading takes the large numbers, such as 675,000 sheep, to reflect the real order of magnitude of Midian's livestock in a pastoral economy, and takes the numbers' exact divisibility (neither the halves nor the levies leave a remainder) to show that the account has been put in order — a feature of the genre of administrative record; another reading takes these numbers to carry the stylized colouring of ancient battle reports and sanctuary accounts, the exact divisibility and symmetry being the work of the genre, not to be read directly as modern statistics. This sermon registers only the numbers themselves, their relations of divisibility, and the two readings, and does not adjudicate the numbers' character as historical material at the fact layer; the main text's argument uses only the three facts the account states of itself — the totals, the proportions, and the deliveries — and these three stand under both readings. One further textual fact is registered alongside: in the general account of the spoil there is no total of gold and silver vessels — gold appears only in the next movement, in the form of the officers' offering (31:50-52); the two accounts are kept apart, recorded as they stand.

(Fact) This movement closes by registering the fourth movement's account: the totals in four items (sheep 675,000; beeves 72,000; asses 61,000; women 32,000); the men's half in four items, with the tribute in four items (337,500 / 675; 36,000 / 72; 30,500 / 61; 16,000 / 32 persons); the congregation's half in four items, with the Levites' levy in four items (337,500 / 6,750; 36,000 / 720; 30,500 / 610; 16,000 / 320 persons); two deliveries (the tribute to Eleazar, 31:41; the one-of-fifty to the Levites, 31:47). It is also registered: from the law of verse 25 to the end of this movement, command and execution correspond in wording item by item — the dividing into two halves, the one of five hundred, the one of fifty, the giving to the priest, the giving to the Levites — not one falls empty; recorded as it stands.

### Part Five: Not One Man Lacking — the Officers' Gold and the Tabernacle's Memorial (Numbers 31:48-54) (48:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 48 registers who comes: the officers which were over thousands of the host, the captains of thousands and captains of hundreds, came near unto Moses — the very company of officers who bore the wrath in verse 14; this time they come of themselves. Verse 49 gives the report, quoted verbatim in full: "Thy servants have taken the sum of the men of war which are under our charge, and there lacketh not one man of us." (31:49) Registered item by item: first, the officers call themselves "thy servants" as they speak to Moses. Second, the first thing in their report is a counting — "have taken the sum": twelve thousand went out, and twelve thousand have come back; the zero loss is something counted out, not something felt. Third, this number agrees, beginning to end, with the number sent out in verse 5 (12,000 out, 12,000 back); this sermon registers it on recalculation. Verse 50 registers the motive and the objects of the offering: the officers state of themselves that each man has brought what he had gotten, jewels of gold, as an oblation for the LORD, for the purpose stated in their own words, "to make an atonement for our souls before the LORD" (31:50); the objects are listed as one class of gold articles — chains, and bracelets, rings, earrings, and tablets (31:50, for the base text's item list see Endnote 3) — gold each man had gotten besides his divided portion. Verse 51 registers the receiving: Moses and Eleazar the priest took the gold of them, even all wrought jewels. Verse 52 registers the total weight: all the gold of the offering that the captains of thousands and of hundreds offered up to the LORD was sixteen thousand seven hundred and fifty shekels — this number is a weighed total, and this sermon registers it as 16,750 shekels. Verse 53 registers a side note: the men of war had taken spoil, every man for himself — what each man had gotten is not within the general account, and this verse makes that boundary plain, answering at beginning and end to the scope of the general account in verse 32. Verse 54 closes the chapter, registered: Moses and Eleazar the priest took the gold and brought it into the tabernacle of the congregation, for a memorial for the children of Israel before the LORD — the chapter's last entry in the account falls on the word "memorial": the gold is not in the treasury; it is in the tabernacle; it is not sealed up on men's account; it is recorded before the LORD.

(Argument) The eye of this movement is the officers' own initiative. By the law, they owe nothing: the tribute has been levied, the account rendered clear, the battle won, and the men brought back. It is they themselves who, having finished the count, cannot stop — not one lacking: the thing is too disproportionate. Twelve thousand men went onto a battlefield, where sword and spear have no eyes, and came back without a single man lost. The law has no provision prepared for such a case, so they prepare an offering themselves: a portion of gold from each man, to make an atonement for their souls before the LORD. This is not the payment of a vow; it is an awakening: it turns out that our coming back alive is not all ability; this life is something extra, freely given. At such a moment, what men most easily do is hold a feast of celebration; what the officers did was send gold into the tabernacle. We too meet with unexpected preservation today — a sickness passed, a danger escaped, a stretch of days in which the whole household is safe and unharmed — and no article of Moses' law says what you must offer; yet the account in your own heart is clear: this safety is disproportionate. The officers' road is the one that gives this disproportion a place to go: take it out, offer it up, and keep it as a memorial; do not let it rot inside the three words "good luck."

(Argument) Consider next where Moses sends this offering: into the tabernacle, "for a memorial for the children of Israel" — note, not for a memorial for the officers themselves, but for the children of Israel. What the officers offered was their own gold; what is recorded is the whole people's name. This is the last thing this chapter teaches us: a memorial of grace received personally must be stored within the common worship if it is to be stored long. Private gratitude keeps its heat for three days; gold sent into the tabernacle is seen year after year, and year after year reminds those who come after: this camp's people once came back with not one lacking. The "not one lacking" moments in our lives — a family reunited with not one lacking, a roll called to the end with not one lacking — deserve more than the tears of that evening; they deserve a memorial that can be kept: write it down, offer it up, set it within worship, so that on the days of your greatest forgetfulness it is still there before God, remembering for you.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The "taken the sum" rests on pāqad (H6485), and the "lacketh" of "there lacketh not one man" is likewise the passive of this word — at the taking of the sum, one absent is said to be lacking; here not one man is lacking; the same word binds the taking of the sum in verse 26 and the report in verse 49 together at beginning and end. "To make an atonement" is kāpar (H3722); "memorial" is zikkārôn (H2146); "shekels" is šeqel (H8255); "gold" is zāhāb (H2091). Of the base text's item words for the gold articles the officers offered, this sermon records the general sense of the classes only; individual word-forms are marked pending verification; see Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The historical readings of the zero-loss report are set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one reading takes "there lacketh not one man of us" as record — this expedition overwhelmed by numbers and fell on its enemy unawares, and zero loss is by no means unheard of in ancient battle reports; the text records it in the form of a report by count, an administrative report rather than a hymn. Another reading takes the zero loss to belong to the stylized closing of this chapter's account-genre — of a piece with the numbers' exact divisibility (see the discussion layer of Part Four) — its weight lying in declaring this expedition's completeness and its being kept, not to be read directly as a modern battle report. This sermon registers only the report's own words, the textual fact that the outgoing and returning numbers agree, and the two readings, and does not adjudicate at the fact layer; the main text's argument uses only the motive the officers state of themselves (31:50, to make an atonement for their souls) and the gold's destination (31:54, brought into the tabernacle for a memorial) — both of them stated by the text itself.

(New Testament Reading) The Lord Jesus, speaking of keeping, says: "Of them which thou gavest me have I lost none" (John 18:9, cross-reference reading layer; quoted verbatim from the KJV at that place). One contrast is registered at the reading layer: this chapter's "not one lacking" is a completeness counted out by the officers — the men returned to the camp, the gold entered the tabernacle; the word in John's Gospel is the completeness of the Lord's rendering of account to the Father — the men are kept, and not one is lost. In both places keeping is spoken of in the manner of a counting: in Scripture, keeping is never a matter of feeling; it is something that can be counted, one by one. The reading layer registers only the contrast between the two texts; it does not read John's word back in as the fact of this chapter's report. This chapter's report still rests on its own text, and its meaning stops at the atonement and the memorial the chapter states of itself.

(Fact) The whole chapter's data are gathered in closing, every item already counted (for the details see Endnote 4): the whole chapter has 54 verses; the five parts have, in order, 12 verses (31:1-12), 12 verses (31:13-24), 7 verses (31:25-31), 16 verses (31:32-47), and 7 verses (31:48-54) — 54 in all. Twelve thousand sent out (a thousand from every tribe, twelve tribes); the list of the slain: all the males, the five kings of Midian (Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba), and Balaam; the totals of the spoil in four items (sheep 675,000; beeves 72,000; asses 61,000; women 32,000); the two halves each a half, and the eight levies all in agreement with the rule (from the men's half, one of five hundred: 675, 72, 61, 32 persons; from the congregation's half, one of fifty: 6,750, 720, 610, 320 persons); the purification ordinance: seven days without the camp, the third day and the seventh day, the six metals through the fire and then water, the rest through water, and on the seventh day the washing of clothes and the entering into the camp; the report: there lacketh not one man of us; the total of the gold offered, sixteen thousand seven hundred and fifty shekels, brought into the tabernacle for a memorial. The chapter's joins with the chapters before and after: behind it stands the matter of Peor in chapter 25 and the command of 25:16-18 to vex the Midianites (preceding-text layer) — this chapter is that command's execution and settling of account; ahead of it opens the request for an inheritance by the two tribes and a half in chapter 32 (following-text layer) — whose existence only is registered: the war fought and the account divided, the division of the land follows upon it, and it is not read back into this chapter.


## Devotional Excerpt

The war was over, and the officers took the sum, and counted out a sentence that makes one stop: "Thy servants have taken the sum of the men of war which are under our charge, and there lacketh not one man of us." (31:49) Twelve thousand went out, and twelve thousand came back. The law did not require them to offer anything further for this; yet of themselves they brought out the gold, to make an atonement for their souls before the LORD, and brought the gold into the tabernacle for a memorial. They understood one thing: a disproportionate safety cannot be dismissed with the word "relief" alone. Count your own numbers in your heart today as well: this year, this household — father and mother, sons and daughters, brothers and sisters — is there still not one lacking? After the most dangerous stretch of days had passed, did you ever take the sum? If, having counted, you find not one lacking, do not let gratitude stop at a prayer at the table: learn from the officers, and give this keeping a place where it can be kept — something offered, something recorded, a testimony spoken before others. Safety that can be counted out in numbers needs a memorial that can be kept; gratitude stored within worship will not be washed thin by the next busy season. Be honest, too: if, taking the sum, you find someone lacking, do not cover grief over with busyness — bring the lost and the not-yet-returned, one by one, before the Lord; weep for them, and intercede for them. Only what is counted clearly can be prayed for pointedly; a long memory makes for a deep love. May the people of our camp, too, on the Lord's account, be not one man lacking.

English (KJV-based):

"And they said unto Moses, Thy servants have taken the sum of the men of war which are under our charge, and there lacketh not one man of us." (Num 31:49, KJV) Twelve thousand went out; twelve thousand came back. No law required another offering, yet the officers brought gold of their own, to make an atonement for their souls, and laid it in the tabernacle for a memorial. Count your own household today. If not one is missing, do not let gratitude evaporate into a sigh of relief — give it a lasting place: offer it, record it, and store it in worship as a memorial before the Lord.

8. Blanks of this chapter, registered as blanks: The battlefield's concrete place and the course of the fighting this chapter does not record; the total number of the Midianite males slain this chapter gives no figure (only the names of the five kings and of Balaam); the officers' motive in keeping the women alive this chapter does not state (31:14-15 record only Moses' wrath and his question); the outcome of the execution of Moses' disposal command this chapter keeps no separate receipt for (the text passes directly to the purification and the division); a total of the gold and silver vessels in the general account of the spoil this chapter does not list (gold appears only in the officers' offering movement); the kinds and quantities of what each man of war had taken for himself (31:53) this chapter does not list; the manner in which the 16,750 shekels of gold were kept in the tabernacle this chapter states only as "for a memorial," without the details of keeping. The above are registered as blanks only, and are not filled by extrapolation.

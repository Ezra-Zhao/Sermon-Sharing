## Summary (about 100 words)

God commanded Israel to turn back and encamp by the sea, between Migdol and Pi-hahiroth. Pharaoh thought they were entangled in the land, and pursued with six hundred chosen chariots. By the sea the people cried out and complained against Moses, and Moses said: Fear ye not, stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD. That night a strong east wind divided the sea, the people crossed on dry ground, Egypt's whole army was overthrown, and the people feared the LORD and believed Him and His servant Moses.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The end of the previous chapter was a beautiful picture: the pillar of cloud did not depart by day, and the pillar of fire gave light by night; God Himself went before them, the people had just come out of Egypt, the blood of the Passover lamb was still in their steps, they held the gold and silver they had asked of the Egyptians, and they carried Joseph's bones in their arms. They had come out of the house of bondage — what a night that was. Today we open chapter fourteen, and its first sentence is God telling them to turn back. Not forward, but back; not a shortcut straight toward Canaan, but a retreat to a place by the sea with no road on three sides, to encamp there. Brothers and sisters, have you ever had such an experience: you prayed for a long time, God finally opened a door, you had just stepped through it, and the road bent back — even narrower and more desperate than before you set out? Work that had just settled changes again, an illness that had just improved relapses, a family that had just settled down must move again. In your heart you ask: did I take the wrong road, or did God lead the wrong way? This chapter is spoken to people standing on the road that turns back.

This chapter is also the best known chapter in the whole Bible. Even people who have never read the Bible have heard of Moses dividing the sea. But we are so familiar with the picture that we easily miss the people in the picture: how Pharaoh's heart moved from regret to pursuit, how the people's mouths moved from singing to complaining, and why the few sentences Moses spoke at that critical moment still comfort people more than two thousand years later. And there is the sentence most easily misused of all, today's title: "Stand still." Some people make this sentence into a slogan for comforting others, as if faith were doing nothing at all and sitting and waiting for God. Is that what the text itself means? Today we will honestly read the whole chapter, including verse fifteen, where God corrects Moses, and skip not a single verse.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Speak unto the children of Israel, that they turn and encamp before Pi-hahiroth, between Migdol and the sea, over against Baal-zephon: before it shall ye encamp by the sea. For Pharaoh will say of the children of Israel, They are entangled in the land, the wilderness hath shut them in. And I will harden Pharaoh's heart, that he shall follow after them; and I will be honoured upon Pharaoh, and upon all his host; that the Egyptians may know that I am the LORD. And they did so." (Exodus 14:1-4, excerpt, KJV)

"And when Pharaoh drew nigh, the children of Israel lifted up their eyes, and, behold, the Egyptians marched after them; and they were sore afraid: and the children of Israel cried out unto the LORD. And they said unto Moses, Because there were no graves in Egypt, hast thou taken us away to die in the wilderness? wherefore hast thou dealt thus with us, to carry us forth out of Egypt?" (Exodus 14:10-11, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses said unto the people, Fear ye not, stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD, which he will shew to you to day: for the Egyptians whom ye have seen to day, ye shall see them again no more for ever. The LORD shall fight for you, and ye shall hold your peace." (Exodus 14:13-14, KJV)

"And the LORD said unto Moses, Wherefore criest thou unto me? speak unto the children of Israel, that they go forward: But lift thou up thy rod, and stretch out thine hand over the sea, and divide it: and the children of Israel shall go on dry ground through the midst of the sea." (Exodus 14:15-16, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses stretched forth his hand over the sea; and the sea returned to his strength when the morning appeared; and the Egyptians fled against it; and the LORD overthrew the Egyptians in the midst of the sea... Thus the LORD saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians; and Israel saw the Egyptians dead upon the sea shore. And Israel saw that great work which the LORD did upon the Egyptians: and the people feared the LORD, and believed the LORD, and his servant Moses." (Exodus 14:27, 30-31, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today we stand by the sea and read this chapter with You. Let those on the road that turns back know that turning may also be Your design; let the afraid hear that word, fear not; and teach us both to stand still and, when You command, to go forward. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Turning Back to Encamp — The Road Back Is God's Design (14:1-9)

First look at God's command; its weight is heavy. God did not vaguely say, do as you see fit; He named the place: "Turn and encamp before Pi-hahiroth, between Migdol and the sea, over against Baal-zephon: before it shall ye encamp by the sea." To turn is to reverse direction, to walk the road back. The people had just come out of Egypt and their spirits were high; who is willing to reverse? In human eyes reversing has only one explanation: the leader took the wrong road, or the leader regretted it. But the text writes it plainly: the one who commanded the turning was the LORD Himself. God not only commanded it, He also declared Pharaoh's thinking in advance: "For Pharaoh will say of the children of Israel, They are entangled in the land, the wilderness hath shut them in." In Pharaoh's eyes this people had lost their way in panic and drilled themselves into a dead end: the sea in front, the wilderness beside, and once the army blocked the rear, they would be caught like a turtle in a jar. God said, this is exactly the view I want. "And I will harden Pharaoh's heart, that he shall follow after them; and I will be honoured upon Pharaoh, and upon all his host; that the Egyptians may know that I am the LORD."

Brothers and sisters, these verses make clear a matter we easily misread: the people walked the road back not because Moses misjudged, and not because the people disobeyed, but precisely because the people obeyed. The text says, "And they did so" — they turned and encamped according to God's word. This was a deliberate turning, a design set by God: the camp pitched by the sea looked like trapping themselves in a desperate place, but in fact it drew Pharaoh and all his host onto a stage where God's judgment and salvation would be manifested in the same scene. By this point in the Exodus, what God meant to settle was no longer only getting slaves out of Egypt, but making the Egyptians — and Israel itself — thoroughly know who the LORD is. The ten plagues had already manifested it, yet Pharaoh could push them aside as coincidence; after this night by the sea, there would be no room left for excuses.

There is a very practical lesson here. Some turnings in life truly are our own wrong turns; those require admitting fault and repenting, and today's text must not be used to cover our own errors — this is a fact, and it must be distinguished clearly. But there is another kind of turning: you have done nothing wrong, the direction was prayed over, the steps were obedient, and yet the road bends back, and the situation is tighter and harder to explain than before. Bystanders, like Pharaoh, conclude for you: he is lost, he is trapped. Brothers and sisters, do not hurry to define your road by the terrain in front of you. Everyone can see the terrain; only God knows the design. The seashore where Israel encamped was, militarily, dead ground; in God's plan it was a stage. The same ground, two views — the difference is not in the ground, but in whether you believe that the God who commanded the encampment is still reigning. The hardest part of walking the road back is not the extra miles, but the loss of face, the comments of others, and that sentence in your own heart: did I believe wrongly? Today's text presses that sentence down first: an obedient turning is not evidence of failure.

Now look at Pharaoh's side. Someone reported to Pharaoh that the people had fled, and the Bible says Pharaoh and his servants changed their hearts and said: "Why have we done this, that we have let Israel go from serving us?" Note the direction of this regret: the people regretted coming out of Egypt; Pharaoh regretted letting the people go. The slaves had gone overnight, Egypt's construction sites lacked hands, the brick kilns stopped work, and Pharaoh's accounts no longer balanced. So he made ready his chariot and took his people with him, and he took six hundred chosen chariots and all the chariots of Egypt, and captains over every one of them. What were six hundred chosen chariots? They were the fastest heavy weapons on the battlefields of that day, the equivalent of pressing all the finest armor into service. Pharaoh was not pursuing a disorderly escape of a mob; he meant to use one swift victory to recover, with principal and interest, the humiliation of the ten plagues — and he truly caught up with them: beside Pi-hahiroth, before Baal-zephon, by the sea, he overtook Israel encamped there.

Here the text adds another sentence: "And the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh king of Egypt." Reading forward from the earlier chapters of Exodus we know that concerning Pharaoh's hardened heart, sometimes the text says he hardened it himself, and sometimes it says God hardened it; both sides are written, with no partiality. Human stubbornness is real, and God's sovereignty is also real; a person must bear responsibility for his own hard heart, yet God is not bound hand and foot by human hardness — He can even take human stubbornness into His process of manifesting glory. This does not excuse the evildoer: Pharaoh pursued slaves to drag them back to the brick kilns, and his sin was his own. This is to reassure the pursued: the hard heart pursuing you is not greater than the hand of the God who reigns. Pharaoh came out in full force, thinking victory was assured, and he did not know that where the camp was pitched, when the pursuers would arrive, and where the water would flow were none of them in his abacus.

Note also the people's journey up to this point: they were not a routed mob driven out by Pharaoh; the previous chapter says they came out of Egypt by their armies, with high hand, in ranks, in order. God leading an army on the road back is itself a silent declaration: an army's advance and retreat are decided not by the enemy's situation, but by its commander. The place of encampment, the direction it faced, and the length of the stay were all written in the command; the first lesson the people had to learn was not how to fight, but how to take orders. Many people have believed for years, yet their prayers still ask God to endorse their own route, and rarely ask God to give the order directly, because an order means even the turning must be obeyed. If God's people today would hand the road map back, the seashore would no longer be evidence of being lost, but proof of having taken orders. From far away Pharaoh saw a trap; from on high God saw a chessboard, and every move on the board, including this move back, had been calculated in advance.

There is one more detail not to miss: the people encamped facing the sea. They could see the sea's vastness, and they must have heard the sea's sound. In the next few hours, on that same shore, they would hear the sound of chariots behind them coming from far to near. Water on one side, soldiers on the other: God's people stood in such a pinch for the first time. The first station after being saved was not a garden in Canaan, but a pinch by the sea. That itself is a lesson: salvation is never the period at the end of the story; the Exodus was only the first half. God saved us out, and He also means, in the pinch, to teach us to know who He is, and to know who we ourselves are. Who are the people themselves? In the next part, the answer comes quickly, and it does not sound pleasant.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Complaints by the Sea and That Word, Fear Not (14:10-16)

Pharaoh drew near, the people lifted up their eyes, and saw the Egyptians marching after them. The Bible uses three movements to write their response: they were sore afraid, they cried out unto the LORD, and they complained against Moses. The order is worth savoring: the fear was real, and the crying out was also real, but after crying out, they turned around and their words went straight at Moses: "Because there were no graves in Egypt, hast thou taken us away to die in the wilderness? wherefore hast thou dealt thus with us, to carry us forth out of Egypt? Is not this the word that we did tell thee in Egypt, saying, Let us alone, that we may serve the Egyptians? For it had been better for us to serve the Egyptians, than that we should die in the wilderness."

Brothers and sisters, this is the first time the people complained against Moses after being saved, and we must be honest and not whitewash them. A few days earlier their bricks were not yet dry and the whips were still on their backs; a few days later, as soon as the cost of freedom appeared, they said that being slaves was better than dying in the wilderness. Their words were cutting; they taunted Moses with graves, as if Moses had led them out solely to find a well-situated place to die. Were these words fair? They were not fair. Moses had no selfish motive; he had given up his own settled days in Midian and returned to Egypt to lead this people out. But when people speak in extreme fear, what comes out first is often not reason, but fear itself. The sea blocked the front, soldiers pursued from behind, and in between were pressed the old, the children, and the livestock, without a single proper weapon in hand. Fear was a real response to a real situation; the Bible does not scold them for cowardice; it simply records fear and complaint in the same frame, so that we may see clearly: a saved person is not the same as a grown person, and coming out of Egypt is not the same as Egypt coming out of the heart. Salvation is accomplished by God in a single night; learning to trust takes a whole lifetime of wilderness to practice, and this lesson by the sea is only the first lesson.

This passage is a mirror for us today. When we give thanks, we all speak well; when trouble comes, what is our first sentence? Is it crying out to God, or first finding someone to blame? Blaming the leader, blaming co-workers, blaming a spouse, blaming the person who made the decision back then — even the sentence blaming ourselves often bends at the end into blaming God for allowing it. The people's crying out and their complaint were separated by only one verse; our prayer and our complaint are often separated by only a sheet of paper. This chapter does not evade the ugliness, yet it does not stop at the ugliness either: God did not abandon the people by the sea because they complained, and Moses did not throw up his hands and quit because he was taunted. The complained-against leader would next speak one of the most famous words of comfort in the whole Bible. The leader's lesson and the people's lesson opened in the same session by the sea.

Let us read Moses' answer sentence by sentence. "Fear ye not." This sentence appears many times in the Bible, mostly at the moments when people have the most reason to fear. It is not a courtesy; it does not mean "do not think too much"; it means: fear is not permitted to be in charge. "Stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD, which he will shew to you to day." "Stand still," in the original hityatsevu (הִתְיַצְּבוּ, H3320), to stand firm, to take one's stand, to be positioned and stand unmoving [1]. This is not collapsing to sit, nor fleeing; it is standing firm as an army stands in formation: the feet must not scatter, the heart must not scatter, but the eyes must open and see. See what? See the salvation of the LORD. yeshu'at YHWH (יְשׁוּעַת יְהוָה, H3444 plus H3068), the salvation of the LORD, the victory the LORD accomplishes [2]. Note whose the noun is: the salvation is the LORD's, not planned by Moses and not earned by the people. The protagonist tonight is not human courage, but God's own showing. "For the Egyptians whom ye have seen to day, ye shall see them again no more for ever." At the time, this sounded almost unbelievable: the chariots were already in sight — how could they never be seen again? What Moses spoke was not empty comfort, but a prophetic declaration: what God meant to end was not one pursuit, but the whole old order of slavery. "The LORD shall fight for you, and ye shall hold your peace." The one who fights is the LORD; the people's duty is to hold their peace. This "hold your peace," spoken to people who had just finished complaining, could not be more apt: first stop the words of complaint, stop the disorderly commands of self-rescue, be quiet, and watch how God works.

But the sermon must be honest; this is the place in the chapter where ambiguity is least permitted. "Stand still" is not a sentence that can be clipped from its context and made into a slogan of spiritual posture, because the text immediately, in the very next verse, gives God's correction of Moses: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Wherefore criest thou unto me? speak unto the children of Israel, that they go forward: But lift thou up thy rod, and stretch out thine hand over the sea, and divide it." Standing still and going forward are separated by only a few verses, on the same page. How are they to be understood? Standing still speaks to the people's state of heart: in panic, do not flee, do not scatter, do not complain; stand firm and watch God. Going forward speaks to the people's feet: when the command that opens the road arrives, the people who have stood still must step forward and walk into the sea. Silence and advance are on the same page; neither can be missing. To speak only of silence and not of advance turns faith into an excuse for passivity: precisely when one should step out, take responsibility, and act, one hides behind "waiting on God" and does not move — that is not standing still, that is being stuck. To speak only of advance and not of standing still makes people rush blindly and replace God's work with their own disorderly movement. Moses' words treat panic; God's words treat stagnation: first stand firm, then go forward, and the order is right. That night Israel did both: they stopped their complaint, and they truly walked, household by household, into the divided sea. Brothers and sisters, examine which one you are stuck on: are you panicking so much you cannot stand, or have you stood so long you refuse to go? In this chapter, God speaks to both sides.

Think again of that sentence in the people's complaint, "Let us alone, that we may serve the Egyptians." It tells the horror of slavishness thoroughly: people enslaved long enough mistake chains for security and the brick kiln for a rice bowl, and at the first wind and waves of freedom they want to go back. They did not truly miss Egypt; they missed the days of no responsibility, no risk, and no need to trust. People today are the same: leaving a situation that has bound them for long, at first all is thanksgiving, but after some days, when wind and waves come, nostalgia comes: old people, old places, and old habits are all remembered as good. The text records this sentence as it was, not to humiliate the people, but to remind us: coming out of Egypt is God's grace, but not turning back after coming out must be kept by daily trust. If the complaint by the sea is not dealt with, it will be uttered again in the wilderness; a lesson not finished here will have to be retaken on the road ahead.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: An East Wind All Night, the Sea as a Wall, and the Salvation of That Day (14:17-31)

God commanded Moses to lift up his rod and stretch out his hand over the sea, and Moses obeyed. Then the Bible writes the work of that night: "And Moses stretched out his hand over the sea; and the LORD caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind all that night, and made the sea dry land, and the waters were divided." [4] East wind, in the original ruach qadim (רוּחַ קָדִים, H7307 plus H6921), wind of the east, a wind blowing from the east [3]. Please note the manner of God's working: He could have split the sea in an instant with one sentence, yet He used a wind that blew all night. The people stood on the shore, listening to the wind blow all night, the water retreating little by little, the dry ground appearing little by little. God's salvation is often like this: not an instantaneous performance, but the work of a whole night, gradual, with the sound of the wind audible. The hardest part for those standing still is precisely this night: the command has been heard, the sea has not yet fully opened, and the pursuers are still behind. Half the lesson of standing still is learned in the hours of waiting for the wind to finish blowing.

The children of Israel went into the midst of the sea upon dry ground, and the waters were a wall unto them on their right hand and on their left. Wall, in the original chomah (חוֹמָה, H2346), a city wall, a rampart [5]. The sea water, which by nature swallows people, was now restrained by God on both sides and became a wall that protected people. What the people walked on was not muddy seabed; the Bible repeatedly stresses that it was dry ground: the aged could walk, the children could walk, the livestock could walk. The multitude crossing that night was vast and the journey urgent, yet the text's tone is steady, as if to say: the road God opens, God takes responsibility for making it passable.

There are two more repositionings that night, often missed by readers. The angel of God, which went before the camp, removed and went behind them, and the pillar of cloud also moved from before them and stood behind them, coming between the camp of the Egyptians and the camp of Israel. Thus the cloud was darkness to one side and gave light by night to the other: on Egypt's side was night, on Israel's side was light, and the one came not near the other all the night. The God who led the way turned to be the God of the rear guard; on the side the people could not see, God Himself stood between them and the pursuers. Brothers and sisters, behind you, where you fear most, God is standing in the middle. Often we only stare at the sea in front and forget to look back once: the pursuers did not come near all night, not because their hands were merciful, but because One stood in between. Let thanksgiving be specific: give thanks not only that the sea opened, but also that the soldiers did not come near; not only for the road out, but also for the calamities that were held back.

But Pharaoh's whole host followed them down. The horses, the chariots, and the horsemen all followed into the midst of the sea. In the morning watch, the LORD looked unto the host of the Egyptians through the pillar of fire and of cloud, and troubled their host, and took off their chariot wheels, that they drave them heavily. Only then did the Egyptians say: "Let us flee from the face of Israel; for the LORD fighteth for them against the Egyptians." This sentence of surrender came from the mouth of the hardest side: even the pursuers had to admit that the one arrayed against them that night was not Moses, but the LORD. Sadly, they recognized it too late. At God's command Moses stretched out his hand over the sea again, and the sea returned to its strength, covering the chariots, the horsemen, and all the host of Pharaoh that came into the sea after them; there remained not so much as one of them.

The narrative's landing point is only one sentence, yet it weighs a thousand pounds: "Thus the LORD saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians." "The LORD saved that day," in the original vayo'sha' (וַיּוֹשַׁע, H3467), He saved, He brought victory [6]. The subject is the LORD, and the object is Israel. The whole chapter begins with God commanding the encampment and ends with God accomplishing the salvation; the people truly did only three things: they stopped their complaint, they stood firm and watched, and they stepped forward. Everything else — the wind God blew, the sea God divided, the soldiers God held back, the enemy God overthrew. This is the theological skeleton of the Exodus: salvation is God's work, and the human duty is the response of trust and obedience. The people's final response is recorded in two things: "the people feared the LORD, and believed the LORD, and his servant Moses." Fear first, belief after: first, before a great work, treat God as God, and then entrust trust to God and to the one God has sent. Moses, who a few days earlier had been taunted about whether there were graves, was today believed by the people; not because Moses won the argument, but because God accomplished the work. In the previous chapter the people carried Joseph's bones on the road, symbolizing that a promise of four hundred years had not been broken; in this chapter the people saw with their own eyes the great waters close, symbolizing that the old world which enslaved them was shut behind them. From now on, Egypt was permitted to exist only in memory and in song, never again to ride its chariots into their future.

Nor should we miss the weight this crossing left in Israel's faith. Later poets sang this night again and again: the Psalms say He made the sea dry land and the people passed through on foot; the prophet Isaiah, when the people were again in a desperate place, prayed that God would awake as in the days of old; in the New Testament, Paul read the fathers' passing through the sea as being baptized unto Moses, Hebrews recorded the crossing in the register of faith, and in Revelation the victorious stand on a sea of glass singing the song of Moses. Generation after generation, whenever there was no road to walk, God's people returned to this chapter to take courage. Why? Because this night established a fact that can be drawn on again and again: God once opened a road in a desperate place, and the One who opened it has not changed. Your sea today may not be water; it may be a bill, a diagnosis, a relationship that can neither be returned to nor crossed — but the One who governs wind and water is still the same. Those who stand still in this chapter are not reciting an ancient saying; they are joining a company that from ancient times has been led across the sea in just this way.

Applied to us, this part can be gathered into three sentences. First, if the wind must blow all night, do not draw conclusions at midnight: God is already working, only the dry ground has not yet fully appeared; those who stand still wait with grounds, because the God who commanded them to stand has never stopped His hand. Second, read the wall rightly: the sea that in your eyes would swallow you can, in God's hand, stand as a wall that protects you; the nature of a situation is decided by who reigns, not by the situation itself. Third, after that day, keep reverence, not only excitement: the people's belief was not shouted out, it was seen out; once every week let us look back on what God has done and store up reverence, so that the next time we stand by the sea, our first sentence will not again be about graves. One last word for those now by the sea: the pursuers you see today may not be pursuers you will see forever; if God says you will see them no more forever, He takes responsibility for making it forever.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through chapter fourteen: God commanded the turning, and the people encamped by the sea; Pharaoh pursued with six hundred chosen chariots, and the people feared, cried out, and complained; Moses said, Fear ye not, stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD, yet God immediately said, speak unto the children of Israel, that they go forward; that night the east wind blew all night, the sea was divided, the waters were a wall, the people crossed on dry ground, and Egypt's whole host was overthrown in the sea. That day, the LORD saved Israel. Standing still is not not moving, and holding one's peace is not doing nothing: standing still is a heart not disordered, holding one's peace is a mouth stopped from complaining, and then, when God commands, the whole camp stepping forward. Those on the road that turns back, those standing by the sea, and those quick of heart and quick of mouth all have a sentence to take away today: The LORD shall fight for you.

Let us pray together: LORD, who fights for us, thank You that when deep water was ahead of us and pursuers behind us, You did not abandon us. Forgive our complaints in fear, stop our disorderly movements in panic, and also push open our being stuck in hesitation. Teach us to stand firm, teach us to hold our peace, and teach us even more, when You command us to go forward, to walk into the water by faith. May we, like the people of that day, see Your great work, and fear You and believe You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who fights and prevails for us, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] hityatsevu: Hebrew הִתְיַצְּבוּ (H3320, Hitpael imperative plural of yatsav), to stand firm, to take one's stand in position, to set oneself in array; Exodus 14:13, Moses' word to the people "stand still," uses this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "standing firm as an army in formation" is a homiletical exposition based on this word's context of array, and is marked separately: the word meaning itself is to stand firm in position; the exposition is a heart not disordered and feet not fleeing.

[2] yeshu'at YHWH: Hebrew יְשׁוּעַת יְהוָה, yeshu'ah (H3444, salvation, deliverance, victory) plus the proper name of the LORD (H3068); Exodus 14:13, "see the salvation of the LORD, which he will shew to you to day"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the salvation is the LORD's, and the one who shows it is God" comprises a textual fact and an exposition, marked separately: the fact is that the original assigns the salvation to the LORD; the exposition is that tonight's protagonist is not human courage.

[3] ruach qadim: Hebrew רוּחַ קָדִים, ruach (H7307, wind, spirit, breath) plus qadim (H6921, east, east wind); Exodus 14:21, "the LORD caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind all that night"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "God worked gradually with a wind that blew all night" is an exposition based on the original fact "all that night," not the word meaning itself.

[4] yam suph: Hebrew יַם־סוּף, yam (H3220, sea) plus suph (H5488, reeds, seaweed); the sea of the crossing is named yam suph in Exodus 13:18 and 15:4, 22 (Exodus 14 itself speaks only of "the sea," hayyam), which the Chinese Union Version mostly renders "Red Sea"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Geographical note (fact and scholarly discussion): the Hebrew original literally means "Sea of Reeds / Sea of Weeds," and suph is the same word as the "flags" (bulrushes/reeds) in Exodus 2:3, 5, where the infant Moses was hidden, forming a textual echo — the infant preserved by God among the reeds, and grown, leading the people to salvation through the Sea of Reeds; this is an observation at the level of the text. Traditionally called the Red Sea, its specific body of water remains debated in scholarship to this day: there are various lake theories (referring to the lakes along the Isthmus of Suez, such as the Bitter Lakes and Lake Timsah) and a Gulf of Suez theory, among others, each with supporters and difficulties; this manuscript presents the views side by side honestly, makes no assertion, and notes: the sermon's message does not take any geographical conclusion as its premise, and whichever body of water the exact site was, the narrative points recorded in the text — God leading the people out of danger and the overthrow of Egypt's host — do not depend on that research conclusion.

[5] chomah: Hebrew חוֹמָה (H2346), a city wall, a rampart, an enclosure wall; Exodus 14:22, "the waters were a wall unto them," and likewise 14:29; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the man-swallowing sea restrained to stand as a protecting wall" is a homiletical exposition; the word fact is only "wall / rampart."

[6] vayo'sha': Hebrew וַיּוֹשַׁע (H3467, Hiphil form of yasha'), He saved, He brought deliverance and victory; Exodus 14:30, "Thus the LORD saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians," with the LORD as subject; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the subject of the whole chapter is God, and the human duty is response" is an exposition based on the subject structure of this verb.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Moses said unto the people, Fear ye not, stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD, which he will shew to you to day... The LORD shall fight for you, and ye shall hold your peace." (Exodus 14:13-14, KJV) "And the LORD said unto Moses... speak unto the children of Israel, that they go forward." (Exodus 14:15, KJV)

Fresh out of Egypt, the people were led back to the shore: deep water ahead, chariots behind. Their first response was fear, and right after it, complaint — they even mentioned graves in Egypt. This is familiar: when we are frightened, words rush out first too. Moses' answer came in two halves: one half stilled their hearts — fear not, stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD; the other half God Himself supplied — stop crying out, and go forward. Standing still was never the destination; it was how a panicked people stood steady enough to hear the next step, and then take it. If you are at the shore tonight, do not draw conclusions at midnight: the east wind blew all night, and the dry ground appeared little by little. Stop the complaint first, plant your feet, and when God says go, go. The pursuers you see today, if God says you will see them no more forever, He Himself will see to it to the end. Think back on the people's road: the turning was not failure, the encampment was not abandonment, and only when the waters closed did they understand that every step had been within the design. Tonight, hand your turning back to God as well: do not hurry to defend yourself, do not hurry to flee; stand a while in reverence, and hear the next step clearly. Blessed are those who stand still, for they shall see with their own eyes how the LORD fights for them to the end, until the final victory.

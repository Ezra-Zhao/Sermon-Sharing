> Reading reminders: Five things must be nailed down before teaching this chapter. First, Saul is the LORD's anointed; his death is a lament, not a joke. The narrator records his death in extremely brief, restrained words, without one syllable of gloating; David opens the next book with a lament for him, weeping and fasting (cf. 2 Samuel 1, condensed rendering). The preacher must not stand on Mount Gilboa applauding, still less use Saul as a comic negative example — judgment belongs to God; mourning is what is left to us. Second, Saul's falling on his sword is recorded, not commended and not sentenced: Scripture narrates it without praising it and without pronouncing a moral verdict on it at this point. If anyone in today's audience is surrounded by despair, the preacher must make this clear first — this passage never tells anyone to imitate how Saul died. A person in despair should first find someone to be with them, seek help, and stay alive: say it out loud, get through tonight. The pulpit's first word to the despairing is always: stay, be accompanied, seek help — not "follow the ancients." Third, Jonathan dies in this chapter inside his father's defeat, not for his own sin: he is one of the brightest figures in the whole book (cf. 1 Samuel 14 and 20). Do not preach father and son dying together as one blanket retribution — Scripture records a loyal man accompanying a ruined house to the end; the tone is grief, not disparagement. Fourth, the Philistines fastening the body to the wall of Beth-shan is ancient warfare's art of humiliation, not the chapter's endpoint: the real ending is at Jabesh — a night's journey, a burial, seven days of fasting. Do not leave the message on the city wall; bring the hearers to rest under the tamarisk tree at Jabesh. Fifth, the burning of the bodies at Jabesh is rare in the Old Testament; commentators mostly explain it by the bodies already being damaged and hard to transport intact (details registered only; final verification done in cross-review). Do not build a doctrine of cremation or burial on this chapter alone: what it establishes is not a burial method but gratitude and the decency of recovering the dead.

## Summary

First Samuel 31 is the closing chapter of the book and the end of Israel's first king. The Philistines routed Israel on Mount Gilboa; Saul's three sons — Jonathan, Abinadab and Malki-Shua — fell before him; Saul, severely wounded by archers, told his armor-bearer to run him through so the uncircumcised would not abuse him; the man would not, and Saul fell on his own sword and died. Thus Saul, his three sons, his armor-bearer and all his men died together that day. The next day the Philistines cut off Saul's head, sent messengers throughout their land with the news, put his armor in the temple of the Ashtaroth, and fastened his body to the wall of Beth-shan for public display. But the chapter does not stop at the wall: the inhabitants of Jabesh-gilead heard — the city Saul had personally rescued at the beginning of his reign (cf. 1 Samuel 11) — and their valiant men arose, walked all night, took the bodies of the king and the princes down from the wall, brought them back to Jabesh for burial, and fasted seven days. This sermon treats three things: how a defeat walked step by step to Gilboa — God speaks not one direct word in this chapter, and the silence itself is the echo of judgment; a king's final fear and misplaced dignity — to his death he still feared human abuse; and how a group of people who remembered a kindness used one night's journey to preserve the last decency of a failed king. The book closes with a lament about to rise: the king is dead, but kindness has not died, and remembrance has not died.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, today we read one of the heaviest chapters in Scripture: an anointed king fallen on a mountain, the brightest of princes dying with him, a body fastened to an enemy's wall. Restrain our gloating hearts and our curious eyes; teach us to read this chapter with the narrator's restrained pen and mourning heart. Speak to those among us whose hearts are hardening, walking step by step toward their own Gilboa — while it is still called today, let them turn back, listen to counsel, and ask of You. For those among us surrounded by despair, keep them here first; send someone to be with them and teach them to say it out loud. For those among us who have received kindness and gradually forgotten it, set before our eyes that night's road to Jabesh; teach us to remember kindness, to recover the fallen, and to preserve decency for those who have fallen. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: The Fire at Ziklag and the Mountain of Gilboa

Read the previous chapter and this one side by side. In those same days (Scripture does not state that the two events fell on the same day; reading them together is a narrative contrast, not a chronological claim), David in the south faced the ashes of Ziklag, and Saul in the north faced defeat at Gilboa. In chapter 30 David wept until he had no strength left, and then he did one thing: he inquired — "Shall I pursue? Will I overtake them?" God answered him: you will overtake them and surely rescue all. In chapter 31 Saul says not one word to God at the end: he commands his armor-bearer, he fears the uncircumcised, and he disposes of his own life. Read together, the hinge of the whole book is clear: it is not that David's sword was faster than Saul's; it is that in extremity one man turned to God and the other turned only to himself. The book begins with Hannah's prayer (cf. 1 Samuel 1-2) and ends with Saul's silence; it opens with a childless woman praying softly in the temple and closes with a king falling on his sword without a word on a battlefield — prayer and silence are the first and last pages of this book.

The chapter has three movements. First, defeat at Gilboa (vv. 1-7): Israel flees and falls, the three princes die in battle, Saul is wounded and falls on his sword, the armor-bearer dies with him, and the inhabitants of the valley and west of the Jordan abandon their cities and flee. Second, humiliation on the wall (vv. 8-10): the Philistines strip the corpses, cut off the head, send the news around to their temples, and fasten the body to the wall of Beth-shan. Third, the night at Jabesh (vv. 11-13): the valiant men of Jabesh travel by night to recover the bodies, bring them back for burial, and fast seven days.

Three inner threads run through the chapter. The thread of silence: God says not one word in this chapter — in chapter 30 God spoke to David; in chapter 28 what Saul heard at Endor was also a word of judgment; by chapter 31 heaven has closed its mouth. The silence of the text is not the author's oversight but narrative theology: a man who repeatedly refused to listen (cf. 1 Samuel 15 and 28) in the end hears only the sound of bowstrings. The thread of fear: Saul is led by fear all his life — fear of the people (cf. 1 Samuel 13 and 15), fear of David, fear of death, and at the end fear of abuse; whatever you fear, you surrender your life into its hands, and he surrendered his life to his own sword. The thread of remembered kindness: the chapter's only bright action comes from the people of Jabesh — not from the palace, not from the army, but from a small city rescued decades earlier; the places where kindness is kept longest are often not at the center but at the margins.

Before reading, set three boundaries. First, do not read Saul's end as a simple formula of cause and effect to frighten people: Scripture gives the conclusion of a long process — offering sacrifice presumptuously in chapter 13, disobeying and sparing spoil in chapter 15, hunting David from chapter 18, killing the priests in chapter 22, consulting a medium in chapter 28. Gilboa is not one day's accident but the settled account of many years of a hardened heart; the preacher should draw this long line, lest hearers imagine God as one who lies in wait on a mountain to trap people — He is the God who waited decades and sent Samuel to speak again and again. Second, do not make a spectacle of Jonathan: his death is carried in half a verse, and Scripture gives him no dying words; the preacher should learn this restraint — some loyal people die in disasters others caused, and the pulpit must preserve their dignity, not use them as supporting jokes. Third, do not miss the contrast between the people who abandon their cities in verse 7 and the valiant men who arise in verse 11: at the same defeat, some abandon their cities and flee, and some set out through the night; faced with defeat, people take two roads, and the chapter's final question is not which side you stand on watching, but which way you are willing to walk tonight.

## Part One: Defeat at Gilboa (1 Samuel 31:1-7)

### 1. The men of Israel fled before the Philistines (1 Samuel 31:1-2)

The Philistines fought against Israel; the men of Israel fled before the Philistines and fell slain on Mount Gilboa. The Philistines pursued Saul and his sons and killed Saul's sons Jonathan, Abinadab and Malki-Shua (cf. 1 Sam 31:1-2, condensed rendering of the sense).

Gilboa: גִּלְבֹּעַ (Gilbōaʿ, H1533), a mountain name, located southeast of the Jezreel plain; see BDB, s.v. גלבע. [1] Philistines: פְּלִשְׁתִּים (Pəlištîm, H6430), Israel's hereditary enemy, dwelling on the coastal plain; see BDB, s.v. פלשתי. [2] The opening has no buildup and no battle commentary, only two actions: flee, fall. As Samuel's narrative reaches its close, the pen grows ever more sparing, like a bearer of bad news who states only the result at the door. Note the battlefield: Mount Gilboa is where the Philistines, advancing from Aphek into the Jezreel area (cf. 1 Samuel 29, the very army from which David had been dismissed), had pushed into the hills; Israel placed the decisive battle on its own mountain and still could not hold it — favorable terrain cannot save a regime whose heart has already departed. Note also the order of the casualty list: the first named are not Saul but his three sons. That Jonathan's name appears in this chapter is the most painful stroke in the whole book: in chapter 14 he climbed a cliff alone and broke the enemy (cf. 1 Samuel 14); in chapter 20 he made a covenant with David in the wilderness and willingly yielded his place (cf. 1 Samuel 20 and 23); now his name stands first in the list of the fallen. The narrator gives him no epitaph, and this restraint is itself mourning — for some deaths, Scripture pays tribute with silence. Preach this verse first to every innocent person caught in a defeat: Jonathan made none of the decisions that caused this war, yet he died inside his father's dynasty. Scripture never promises that good people will not be swept up in the defeats of their age; it records their names and, in the next book, lets David sing for him one of the most beautiful laments in all Scripture (cf. 2 Samuel 1). The accounts of this life sometimes cannot be balanced; Scripture's way is not to whitewash them, but to write the name down and leave it to the lament and to the coming judgment together.

### 2. Draw your sword and run me through (1 Samuel 31:3-5)

The battle grew fierce against Saul; the archers overtook him and he was severely wounded. He said to his armor-bearer, "Draw your sword and run me through, lest these uncircumcised come and run me through and abuse me." But his armor-bearer was terrified and would not, so Saul took his own sword and fell on it. When the armor-bearer saw that Saul was dead, he too fell on his sword and died (cf. 1 Sam 31:3-5, condensed rendering of the sense).

Archers: מוֹרִים (môrîm, H3384), shooters, from the root for shooting arrows; see BDB, s.v. ירה. [3] Severely wounded / writhing in anguish: חוּל (ḥûl, H2342), to writhe in pain, tremble; see BDB, s.v. חול. [4] The uncircumcised: עֲרֵלִים (ʿărēlîm, H6189), uncircumcised men, the contemptuous term Saul and his ancestors used for the Philistines (cf. the usage registered in 1 Samuel 14 and 17); see BDB, s.v. ערל. [5] Abuse: הִתְעַלֵּל (hitʿallēl, H5953), to treat arbitrarily, mock and abuse; see BDB, s.v. עלל. [6] Run through: דָּקַר (dāqar, H1856), to pierce through; see BDB, s.v. דקר. [7] Sword: חֶרֶב (ḥereb, H2719), sword; see BDB, s.v. חרב. [8] This passage must be read very slowly. First, Saul's dying words: he opens his mouth not in prayer, not in entrusting himself, not in confession, but with a command and a fear — commanding the armor-bearer to pierce him, fearing abuse by the uncircumcised. To his last breath his mind still turns on outward dignity: he must not fall into enemy hands to be shamed. Preach this point thoroughly, and with compassion: Saul's lifelong tragedy, in one sentence, is that he valued dignity above the word of God. In chapter 13 he offered the sacrifice presumptuously because he feared the people scattering and the Philistines coming down (cf. 1 Samuel 13); in chapter 15 he spared the best of the sheep and cattle and set up a monument for himself (cf. 1 Samuel 15); his years of hunting David grew from the people's song "David ten thousands" wounding his dignity (cf. 1 Samuel 18). A man who worships his own face as a god ends with his face as his only last words. Second, the armor-bearer: he was terrified and would not — he dared not pierce his own king, and in that reluctance lies the Old Testament reverence for the anointed (David twice refusing to stretch out his hand against Saul is the same line; cf. 1 Samuel 24 and 26); yet after the king died, he too fell on his sword — his loyalty had no exit and could only be buried with his master. Leave a word for such people: loyalty bound to the wrong person and locked inside a corrupt system often ends by falling together with it; loyalty is a virtue, but loyalty must have a higher object of allegiance, or it turns from a virtue into a rope. Finally, repeat Reading Reminder Two: Scripture records Saul falling on his sword without praise and without any summons to imitate it; commentators' moral evaluations of this verse are registered only and are not expanded into a verdict in this sermon (verified in cross-review, 2026-11-03). If anyone in today's audience stands at a similar edge of despair, the preacher must put the pulpit's word first: do not, like Saul, dispose of yourself alone — let one person know first, let those who will accompany you arrive first, speak it out to God and to people — only by staying alive is there a next chapter. Saul left his story no next chapter; you must leave one for yours.

### 3. They all died together that day (1 Samuel 31:6-7)

So Saul, his three sons, his armor-bearer and all his men died together on the same day. When the men of Israel across the valley and west of the Jordan saw that Israel's army had fled and that Saul and his sons were dead, they abandoned their cities and fled, and the Philistines came and lived in them (cf. 1 Sam 31:6-7, condensed rendering of the sense).

To die: מוּת (mût, H4191), to die; see BDB, s.v. מות. [9] The list in verse 6 grows longer and longer: the king, three sons, the armor-bearer, all his men — a dynasty uprooted in one afternoon. Read this beside chapter 28: the night before, what Saul heard at Endor was, "Tomorrow you and your sons will be with me" (cf. 1 Sam 28:19, condensed rendering); in less than twenty-four hours it was fulfilled, not one word more or less. Let the hearers be quiet a moment in this paired reading: God's word, whether a word of promise or a word of judgment, never falls empty; the promise David heard in chapter 30 was fulfilled word for word, and the warning Saul heard in chapter 28 was fulfilled word for word. On both sides God keeps His word; the only difference is which side the hearer stands on. The abandoning of cities in verse 7 is the ripple of defeat: the front line falls and the rear flees first; the Philistines need not storm the cities — the cities empty themselves. The reminder for today is very practical: a nation, a church, or a family that in ordinary times does not build, store up, or prepare successors cannot withstand one leader's fall. At this moment the only successor Israel could count on, David, is still in exile at Ziklag fighting for his own household — the result of Saul's years of hunting him is that when the king died, the nation could find no successor in place. To persecute your successor is to book verse 7 in advance for your own dynasty.

### Ignition Point One: Do Not Postpone Turning Back Until Mount Gilboa

Here I stop and speak to one kind of heart: it is not that you do not know. You have heard the reminders too — not once, but many times; you know that thing is wrong, that the habit is dragging you up the mountain, that the relationship, the account, the hardened heart will sooner or later be settled. Saul's problem was never that he did not hear — Samuel spoke plainly, and he went in person to Endor to hear it once more. He heard, and he would not turn. He postponed turning back past Mount Gilboa. Tonight, in the Lord's name, I call you directly: do not postpone any longer. Today, not tomorrow; when this chapter is finished, not after you have propped up the situation a while longer. Turning back is never something done when the mood improves; it is done while the word can still be heard, the door is still open, and people are still waiting for you. That God still speaks to you today is itself grace — the most terrifying thing in chapter 31 is not the arrows and not the sword, but God's silence; when a person can no longer hear even a word of judgment, that is the true extremity. Today you can still hear words that sting, still hear counsel, still hear this sermon — which means your chapter 31 has not yet arrived. Stop, turn, and return before the God you have known for years. Mount Gilboa is not yet under your feet — do not walk it into your mountain with your own feet.

## Part Two: Humiliation on the Wall (1 Samuel 31:8-10)

### 1. The next day, they came to strip the slain (1 Samuel 31:8)

The next day, when the Philistines came to strip the slain, they found Saul and his three sons fallen on Mount Gilboa (cf. 1 Sam 31:8, condensed rendering of the sense).

To strip: פָּשַׁט (pāšaṭ, H6584), to strip off, take off; see BDB, s.v. פשט. [10] The work of the second day on a battlefield is clearing and plundering: the victors returned to strip clothing, armor and valuables, and in the heap of the dead they recognized the king and the princes. Note the narrative order: the thing Saul feared most — falling into the hands of the uncircumcised — came upon his dead body anyway. The abuse he dodged by falling on his sword, the Philistines inflicted on him in full; only he could no longer feel it. This is a quiet reminder to everyone who treats dignity as life itself: you cannot protect dignity by arranging and ending things yourself; dignity is given by God and kept for you by others — in the end Saul's dignity was kept not by his sword but by the night journey of the men of Jabesh (see Part Three). The preacher should also pause here for all the war dead: on Mount Gilboa there fell not only royalty but countless unnamed soldiers; in this chapter Scripture names only the royal house, yet David's lament in the next book begins with the beauty of all Israel (cf. 2 Samuel 1). In remembering the fallen, the pulpit should have this breadth: remember not only generals but nameless soldiers; not only honorable deaths but every person fallen on the mountain.

### 2. They cut off his head and sent messengers throughout the land (1 Samuel 31:9)

They cut off his head, stripped off his armor, and sent messengers throughout the land of the Philistines to proclaim the news to their idols in their temples and to the people (cf. 1 Sam 31:9, condensed rendering of the sense).

Head: רֹאשׁ (rōʾš, H7218), head; see BDB, s.v. ראש. [11] To proclaim news: בָּשַׂר (bāśar, H1319), to report news, usually good news; see BDB, s.v. בשר. [12] This verse hides a stinging irony: the Philistines treated the news of Saul's death as "good news," sending it into their temples and reporting it to their idols — as they saw it, Dagon and the Ashtaroth had defeated the LORD. War in the ancient Near East was always a war of god against god, and a king's corpse was the war god's trophy; an Israelite reading only this verse might think the story's conclusion was that the enemy's god had won. But the whole book of Samuel wrote the answer earlier: Dagon once fell face down before the ark, his head and hands broken off (cf. 1 Samuel 5; preached in this series' earlier sermon "Dagon Fell Before the Ark"). When the Philistines set the trophy in their temple, the god in that temple had already lost a round in this very book. Preach this layer to those who cannot lift their heads amid public opinion and shame: that the enemy circulates your failure throughout the land, hangs it on a wall, and reports it in his temple does not mean heaven's verdict has been written; in the moment when news flies everywhere, God has not yet spoken, and when God speaks is often in the next book. It is the same today: a person's defeat is broadcast, watched, and made a topic of talk — Scripture does not deny that the scene is truly humiliating, but it insists that the onlookers' reporting is not the final judgment.

### 3. His body fastened to the wall of Beth-shan (1 Samuel 31:10)

They put Saul's armor in the temple of the Ashtaroth and fastened his body to the wall of Beth-shan (cf. 1 Sam 31:10, condensed rendering of the sense).

Ashtaroth: עַשְׁתָּרוֹת (ʿAštārôt, H6252), the plural form of the name of the Canaanite goddess Ashtoreth; see BDB, s.v. עשתרת. [13] Beth-shan: בֵּית־שָׁן (Bêt-Šān, H1052), a city name, east of Gilboa, commanding the road from Jezreel to the Jordan Valley; see BDB, s.v. בית שן. [14] The armor placed in a temple, the body fastened to a wall: one as a trophy for a god, one as a warning to passersby. This was the ancient world's most thorough disposal of an enemy king: to let him have no rest even after death, and to let every passerby see, "This is what comes of opposing the Philistines." At this point the narrative should logically be at its darkest page — the king dead, his sons dead, his head gone, his body displayed on a wall, Israel fled from its cities. Yet the author of Samuel precisely does not end the pen at this verse. When Scripture tells of judgment, it never ends with humiliation: it always writes one verse more, about a group of people rising in the night. Here the preacher should lower his voice and leave the pause long enough, then go on: a wall is not a tomb, and public display is not the ending; in this chapter — indeed in the whole Bible — God always leaves a company of night-walkers for those who have fallen.

## Part Three: The Night at Jabesh (1 Samuel 31:11-13)

### 1. The inhabitants of Jabesh-gilead heard (1 Samuel 31:11-12)

When the inhabitants of Jabesh-gilead heard what the Philistines had done to Saul, all their valiant men arose (cf. 1 Sam 31:11-12, condensed rendering of the sense).

Jabesh: יָבֵשׁ (Yāḇēš, H3003), Jabesh-gilead, a city east of the Jordan; see BDB, s.v. יבש. [15] Valour: חַיִל (ḥayil, H2428), strength, valour; the phrase "valiant men" denotes men able to fight; see BDB, s.v. חיל. [16] The chapter's turning point is one word: heard. People elsewhere in Israel heard the news of defeat and responded by abandoning their cities and fleeing (31:7); the people of Jabesh heard not only defeat but humiliation — the king fastened to a wall — and their response was to arise. Why Jabesh, of all places? Turn the clock back decades: Saul had just become king, and his first battle was fought for Jabesh — Nahash the Ammonite besieged Jabesh-gilead and would gouge out the right eyes of the whole city as a disgrace; Saul burned with anger, cut a yoke of oxen in pieces, sent them throughout Israel, 330,000 men assembled, and at dawn he broke the enemy and the whole city was saved (cf. 1 Samuel 11, condensed rendering). That was the day Saul was most like a king, and the highest day of his character. Decades passed; the king was long no longer the king of that day: he disobeyed, he envied, he killed priests, he visited a medium. But what the people of Jabesh remembered was not his later corruption but his earlier rescue. Preach this layer into people's hearts: those who remember kindness remember a person by the kindness, not by his later fortunes. Saul showed Jabesh kindness once, and Jabesh repaid it with a night's journey at the risk of renewed war. What today's churches and families lack most is exactly this long memory: if someone was good to me once, I remember it for life; if he later fell, went bad, and collapsed, that one kindness still counts. The men of Jabesh did not defend Saul's sins — in their seven days of fasting they understood everything; they simply would not allow their benefactor to lie exposed on a city wall. Remembering kindness and judging right and wrong are two ledgers, and Jabesh kept both of them clearly.

### 2. They walked all night and took the bodies down (1 Samuel 31:12)

They walked all night, took the bodies of Saul and his sons down from the wall of Beth-shan, brought them to Jabesh, and burned them there (cf. 1 Sam 31:12, condensed rendering of the sense).

From Jabesh to Beth-shan is a night's road: out of the Jordan Valley, up onto the wall of Beth-shan, under the eyes of the Philistine garrison taking down four bodies, and carrying them back the same way — this is a military operation, not a ceremonial stroll; had they been discovered, it would have been a second Gilboa. Read this "walked all night" against Saul's life: Saul walked many night roads — nights hunting David, the night to Endor (cf. 1 Samuel 28, when he went by night to the medium); this night of Jabesh is the only night journey in the whole book walked for gratitude and for recovering the dead. It is the same night, but Saul's nights grew darker as he walked, and Jabesh's night grew brighter. On the burning, per Reading Reminder Five, register only the action itself — the bodies had been taken down from the wall and exposed, and commentators explain the burning in differing ways (details verified in cross-review, 2026-11-03) — this sermon takes only one point for teaching: they would not let the king's remains suffer one more injury; rather than keep the usual burial custom, they first stopped the humiliation and preserved the remains for burial. Decency is sometimes not keeping the old rules; in an extraordinary situation it is first treating a person as a person. Note also that they took not only Saul: the bodies of his sons were taken down together — Jonathan among them. Jabesh's gratitude covered the king's sons; they did not distinguish which one deserved it and which was merely included. This absence of calculation is the foreshadowing of David's heart in later singing one lament for Saul and Jonathan together (cf. 2 Samuel 1).

### 3. Buried under the tamarisk tree, and they fasted seven days (1 Samuel 31:13)

They buried their bones under the tamarisk tree at Jabesh and fasted seven days (cf. 1 Sam 31:13, condensed rendering of the sense).

Tamarisk: אֵשֶׁל (ʾēšel, H815), tamarisk (the Chinese Union Version renders it as a "weeping willow-like" tree); see BDB, s.v. אשל. [17] To fast: צוּם (ṣûm, H6684), to fast; see BDB, s.v. צום. [18] The whole book rests under this tree. First Samuel begins with a woman praying in the temple (cf. 1 Samuel 1) and ends with a people fasting for a dead king; in between are the rise of a kingdom, wars, a court, and pursuits, and it lands at last in the mourning of ordinary people — Scripture hands the close of a whole book to a small city's seven days of fasting. These seven days answer across the distance to the one day on which David, hearing the news of death, wept and fasted for Saul and Jonathan (cf. 2 Sam 1:12, condensed rendering): the king's era is handed over in tears. One more stroke of the ending must be supplied for the hearers, lest they think the bones were lost from then on: years later, when David had become king over all Israel, he personally brought the bones of Saul and Jonathan up from Jabesh and reburied them with others in the land of Benjamin, in the tomb of Saul's father Kish (cf. 2 Sam 21:12-14, condensed rendering) — the burial at Jabesh that night was temporary; David's later reburial was formal. Those who remembered kindness first brought the man back; the king after God's own heart then settled him properly. One night's loyalty waited until it received the echo of a dynasty. Read this stroke aloud here for everyone who feels that their good deeds are seen by no one: the road you walked at night, the occasion when you cleared up after a fallen person — at the time there was no applause and no record, but Scripture recorded it for you. Not one name of the men of Jabesh is left in the text, yet the city's name remained; when God keeps accounts, He sometimes records the city and not the names — what you did, He counts it all in that city's ledger, nothing missing.

### Ignition Point Two: Tonight, Are You Willing to Walk This Night?

At this last place I speak to those standing under the wall watching the spectacle, and to those who have heard the news but have not yet set out. Saul has fallen and the news has spread: some abandoned their cities, some watched, some reported it in their temples as good news — only the men of Jabesh heard the news as a calling. They held no meeting to discuss whether it was worth it, waited neither for daylight nor for someone else to go first; they arose and walked all night. Tonight — not after the storm has passed and it is safe to declare yourself — is there a fallen person beside you, fastened to the wall of everyone's talk? Is there someone who once rescued you, helped you, pulled you up, who has now been defeated, has collapsed, whom no one will acknowledge? Tonight it is your turn to walk: go and take him down from the wall — a word that preserves his decency, an attitude that refuses to throw stones into the well, an actual act of taking him in and burying him. Do not let your gratitude stay in your heart; gratitude must walk night roads. Say it also to the church: our generation does not lack reporters; it lacks night-walkers. It does not lack people who send others' defeats into the temple as conversation; it lacks people who carry the person inside the defeat home, bury him under a tree, and fast seven days for him. Arise tonight: for the name that comes to your mind, walk one journey, do one thing, repay the kindness, bring the person back. God has remembered Jabesh to this day — and He will remember your night as well.

## Cross-Reading: Four Passages Read Together

The matter of this chapter has echoes elsewhere in Scripture; four cross-readings nail it down. Old Testament, first: the fulfillment of a prophecy — the night before, Saul heard at Endor, "Tomorrow you and your sons will be with me" (cf. 1 Sam 28:19, condensed rendering; the key sentence checked against the Chinese Union Version and verified word by word in cross-review, 2026-11-03); in verse 6 of this chapter, Saul and his three sons die on the same day — within one night, word and event meet. Read together, the two passages show the certainty of God's word and the shape of Saul's tragedy: to the end he could hear the truth; he simply never turned back according to the truth. Old Testament, second, a contrast: the origin of Jabesh — Saul's first achievement as king was rescuing Jabesh-gilead from Nahash's threat to gouge out eyes (cf. 1 Samuel 11, condensed rendering); his final journey was the men of Jabesh risking death to recover his body. The book is framed by this head and tail: the king's first good deed and the people's last repayment enclose decades of corruption between them, letting the reader weigh it — a person is remembered, often, not for the time of his greatest power, but for the time he saved others. Psalms, one passage: the lament over the rejected anointed — the psalmist asks, "But You have spurned and rejected Your anointed; You have renounced the covenant with Your servant" (cf. Ps 89:38-39, condensed rendering of the sense, checked against the Chinese Union Version and verified in cross-review, 2026-11-03). The first half of Psalm 89 praises the covenant God made with David's house, and the second half laments the anointed one's humiliation; it interweaves exactly with this chapter: the anointed one's humiliation is real grief, not material for celebration — even the Psalms weep over it, let alone the pulpit. Proverbs, one passage: "Whoever remains stiff-necked after many rebukes will suddenly be destroyed — without remedy" (Prov 29:1, Chinese Union Version) — Saul's life is precisely the long footnote to this proverb: rebukes were not lacking — Samuel confronted him twice (cf. 1 Samuel 13 and 15), and Jonathan, Michal and David all reminded him by word and deed; nor did the sudden destruction come as a bolt without warning — it was the total explosion of a stiffened neck accumulated to the final day. New Testament, one passage: in Pisidian Antioch Paul, reviewing Israel's history, said the people asked for a king, and God gave them Saul; he reigned forty years; then God removed Saul and raised up David as their king, testifying of him that he was a man after His own heart (cf. Acts 13:21-22, condensed rendering of the sense, checked against the Chinese Union Version and verified in cross-review, 2026-11-03) — the New Testament's final verdict on Saul is only three words' worth, "God removed him," and all its ink is given to David and to David's descendant, Jesus. Read in both Testaments together, Gilboa is not the final narrative: God removed one king in order to give the kingdom to the man after His own heart, and then to that eternal King.

## Application: Six Groups of People

First, to those repeatedly reminded who still stiffen their necks. You have a list in your heart: the people who reminded you, you have crossed them off one by one — first saying he does not understand, then that he is targeting you, and finally simply not seeing him. Saul walked exactly this road: he heard Samuel's words as opposition, Jonathan's counsel as betrayal, and attributed everything unpleasant to others. The hand this chapter reaches to you is concrete: today, restore one severed channel of counsel — go yourself to the person who once spoke frankly to you, sit down, listen to the end, do not defend yourself; and before God set one act of turning back for that long-postponed matter — write it down, tell one person, and ask him to check on you. Do not wait for the sudden moment, and do not wait for the wall.

Second, to those in positions of authority and leadership. Saul's lesson is not "do not be king"; it is that being anointed does not mean exemption from inspection. Position magnifies a person's old faults: a heart that fears the people becomes, on a throne, the knife that kills priests; a heart that loves dignity becomes, on a throne, decades of pursuit. Those who lead today should set for themselves the rules Saul never set: regularly let trustworthy people examine your decisions and motives; leave a rhythm of inquiring and waiting for major decisions, and do not imitate chapter 13's rushing ahead; keep beside you one or two Nathan-like figures who dare to say, "King, you are wrong" (cf. 2 Samuel 12, registered), and protect them by structure, never settling accounts with them later yourself. How you treat those who remind you today is how you book verse 6 — or a succession — for your kingdom.

Third, to Jonathan-like people — loyal and upright, yet trapped in a declining house or a declining system. This chapter understands your grievance fully: you held no authority to make the wrong decisions, yet you must share the consequences of the defeat. Scripture's comfort for you has three layers: your name is recorded and not mixed with the corruption; someone has seen your loyalty — David's lament sings of you first (cf. 2 Samuel 1); your worth is not defined by this system's outcome. Hear also a word of proportion: loyalty does not mean being bound without limit — Jonathan once saw God's will in David and willingly yielded his place (cf. 1 Samuel 23); you today must also discern in prayer when it is accompaniment, and when you should seek, for yourself and your children, another way out that accords with God's heart — bring the judgment before God and trustworthy people, and do not carry it alone all the way to Gilboa.

Fourth, to those surrounded by despair. There is one verse in this chapter you are most likely to misread: Saul falling on his sword. Read Reading Reminder Two again: it is a record, not a summons. If today you feel you cannot go on living, feel you are a burden to everyone, feel that ending it is the dignified way — these feelings are genuinely painful, but they are not the whole of the facts, and still less are they what God says to you. Today do only one thing: tell this to one person — family, friend, pastor, doctor, any living person — do not stay alone tonight; let the person who will accompany you arrive. One part of Saul's tragedy is that the person closest to him was only an armor-bearer who dared not speak; do not let only silence remain beside you. Give others a chance to hold you up, and leave God room to write a next chapter for you.

Fifth, to the church and to those beside the fallen. After Gilboa, the two things the church most easily does are both judged "not this" by this chapter: abandoning the city and fleeing, and watching and reporting. The third road the chapter gives is Jabesh's road: hear, arise, walk the night road, recover the body, bury it, fast. In today's terms: when a coworker or a family falls, the church first sends people, first protects their decency, first settles their family, and only then slowly discusses right and wrong and restoration; let talk be the least, presence the earliest, and tears the truest. Set also a household rule: do not forward other people's defeats in group chats, and do not use another's wall as your own conversation material — as you treat the one fastened to the wall today, so others will treat you tomorrow; Jabesh's ledger God has kept for millennia, and that ledger is still being kept today.

Sixth, to those who have received kindness, and to parents. Jabesh's gratitude spanned decades: the benefactor had long changed, fallen, and provoked public anger, yet they still honored the account. Today you can check your own list of benefactors: who lent you money, took you in, spoke up for you, pulled you up in your hardest time — and who among them is now in a low place? Do not wait to add flowers to someone's brocade in his glory; gratitude should deliver coal in the snow when he is fastened to a wall — that is Jabesh-style remembering. Parents should especially tell this chapter to their children: not to frighten them with how Saul died, but to tell them of Jabesh's night — let children know from small that our family remembers kindness, not grudges; whoever showed us kindness, when he falls on hard times we go back. The way of gratitude children hear at the table today is the strength in their feet for walking night roads decades later.

## Closing Prayer of Response

Lord, we gather 1 Samuel 31 into a response. We pray for those stiffening their necks, walking toward their own Mount Gilboa: while Your word can still be heard, let them turn back tonight. We pray for the Jonathans trapped in corrupt systems: remember their names, discern their loyalty, and open for them a way out that accords with Your heart. We pray for those surrounded by despair: send someone to accompany them, keep them here first, and do not let them dispose of themselves alone. We pray for those standing under the wall: restrain our mouths from watching and passing on talk, and raise up our feet to walk night roads; teach us to learn from Jabesh — to hear and arise, to take the fallen down, bury them under the tree, and fast for them. May our generation not be people who abandon cities, nor people who report defeats as if preaching good news, but people who walked all night. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Extract (for daily devotion, 300-500 words in the original)

In 1 Samuel 31, Saul and his three sons died on Mount Gilboa, and the Philistines fastened his body to the wall of Beth-shan for display. When the news reached Jabesh-gilead — the city Saul had personally rescued at the start of his reign — the valiant men among its inhabitants arose, walked all night, took the bodies of the king and the princes down, brought them back to Jabesh for burial, and fasted seven days (cf. 1 Sam 31:11-13, condensed rendering). Saul had many corrupt years afterward; what the people of Jabesh did not forget was that one rescue long ago. Those who remember kindness remember a person by the kindness, not by his later fortunes. On the day the bad news spread, some abandoned their cities and fled, some watched and talked; the people of Jabesh were the company who heard and set out — the same news, falling into different hearts, grows completely different feet. Three questions for meditation today: From whom have I received kindness, and in what condition is he now? When I hear of another's defeat, do I flee, watch, or arise and walk? Is there a severed channel of counsel that I should restore today? Prayer: Lord, teach me to remember kindness and not grudges, to hear and arise; and teach me, while it is still today, to be willing to listen to counsel and to turn back.

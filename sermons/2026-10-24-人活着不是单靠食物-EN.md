# Not by Bread Only — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 8

> First translation, pending proofreading （初译待校）. English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV); the Chinese source manuscript's base text is the CUNPSS (New Punctuation Chinese Union Version, simplified-character "God" edition), and every statement about that base text refers to it as such.

> Title verification: Five candidates were considered — (1) "Not by Bread Only," from verse 3, "man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the Lord doth man live"; (2) "It Is He That Giveth Thee Power to Get Wealth," from the corrective sentence of verse 18, which covers only the closing section; (3) "Remember These Forty Years," from the command to remember and the number of years in verse 2, which covers only the wilderness section; (4) "Lest Thou Say in Thine Heart," from the inner monologue of verse 17, which covers only the warning section; (5) "Who Brought Thee Forth Water Out of the Rock of Flint," from the retrospective sentence of verse 15, which covers only the retrospective section. Candidate (1) is adopted: the chapter's twenty verses form a single arc of memory — "remember" (v. 2) and "remember" (v. 18) answer each other at head and tail, with the warnings against forgetting (vv. 11, 14, 19) in between; and the sentence in verse 3 is the theological eye of the whole chapter: hunger in the wilderness is the classroom, fullness in the good land is the examination hall, and what man lives by, from first to last, is the same speaking God. When Jesus was tempted, the first sentence of His answer to the devil quotes precisely this verse (Matt 4:4). English title: Not by Bread Only (from KJV 8:3, "man doth not live by bread only").

(11) Verse 17: the base text joins two items with an enumeration comma, "this wealth was gotten by my power and the might of mine hand"; the 2010 edition reads, in Chinese, "this wealth was gotten by my power and by the might of my hand"; entered. The KJV reads "and serve them, and worship them," with the object present.

## Main Text

Introduction (00:00–05:00). [Fact Layer] The last sentence of Deuteronomy 7 falls inside one's own house (the abominable thing must not be brought into the house); chapter 8 turns, in one move, into memory: in all twenty verses Moses speaks of one thing only — remembering. The structure is registered first: The Wilderness Classroom, vv. 1–5 (5 verses); The Good Land and Blessing, vv. 6–10 (5 verses); The Warning Against Fullness, vv. 11–16 (6 verses); The Words of the Heart and Their Correction, vv. 17–18 (2 verses); The Warning and the Outcome, vv. 19–20 (2 verses) — twenty verses in total (note 1). The verbal skeleton is also registered first: "remember" occurs twice (vv. 2, 18), "forget" three times (vv. 11, 14, 19) — remembering at both ends, forgetting in the middle; the whole chapter is a tug-of-war between remembering and forgetting, and the battlefield of that tug-of-war is not the wilderness but the dinner table. The chapter's place in the book is also registered: it belongs to the opening group (chs. 6–11) of Moses' second great discourse (chs. 5–26); before it stands chapter 7's commands of separation, and after it chapter 9's "Speak not thou in thine heart… saying, For my righteousness the Lord hath brought me in to possess this land" (Deut 9:4, checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 8:17) — the "lest thou say in thine heart" of chapter 8 (v. 17) and the "speak not" of chapter 9 form a pair: one guards against boasting of wealth gotten, the other against boasting of land possessed. [Argument Layer] Chapter 7 speaks of identity: a peculiar people, His own; chapter 8 speaks of how that identity is kept: by remembering. Election is freely given (7:7–8), but remembering must be practised — both occurrences of "remember" in this chapter are imperatives. This is also the most "everyday" chapter in Deuteronomy: no war, no lists; it speaks of eating, clothing, housing, and money — in this chapter the decay of faith does not begin with betrayal; it begins with fullness. [Summary Layer] The chapter's governing sentence can be put this way: Moses stands east of the Jordan and gives a lesson, in advance, to people who have not yet entered the land: the forty years in the wilderness were not a waiting room of punishment but a father's classroom; and once you have entered the good land, do not graduate — because the examination questions have changed: when you were hungry, you lived by the word of God's mouth; when you are full, do you still remember?

### I. Remember the Forty Years: The Wilderness Classroom (8:1–5; 05:00–16:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 1, quoted in full from the base text (here, as throughout, in the KJV wording): "All the commandments which I command thee this day shall ye observe to do, that ye may live, and multiply, and go in and possess the land which the Lord sware unto your fathers." Items registered: (1) The chapter's opening follows the same pattern as chapter 6 ("which I command thee this day"); the original's "all the commandments" is a singular-form collective (kol-hammiṣwâ) — the commandment is spoken of here as one whole, not as an itemized list. (2) The purpose clause strings three items in a row: live, multiply, go in and possess the land — the fruit of obedience is first living, then multiplying, and last entering the land; the order is registered. (3) "Multiply" (rāḇâ) will reappear in verse 13 in its negative aspect (the danger of increase); here its positive aspect is set down first. [Fact Layer] Verse 2, quoted in full: "And thou shalt remember all the way which the Lord thy God led thee these forty years in the wilderness, to humble thee, and to prove thee, to know what was in thine heart, whether thou wouldest keep his commandments, or no." Items registered: (1) The chapter's first "remember" stands here, and it is a command; the object of remembering is not a list of events but "all the way which… led thee" — the forty years are characterized as a leading, not a losing of the way. (2) Three purpose items: to humble (ʿānâ), to prove (nāsâ), and to know what was in thine heart — the purpose clause of the wilderness is written out plainly here: to make the heart manifest. (3) "His commandments" in this verse has the ketiv/qere variant reading in the BHS (see headnote, item 2); registered. [Fact Layer] Verse 3, quoted in full: "And he humbled thee, and suffered thee to hunger, and fed thee with manna, which thou knewest not, neither did thy fathers know; that he might make thee know that man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the Lord doth man live." Items registered: (1) The concrete form of the humbling is "suffered thee to hunger" together with "fed thee with manna" — first the natural bread is cut off, then a bread never seen before is given; the manna is defined in this verse as what "thou knewest not, neither did thy fathers know": a food outside the experience of two generations. (2) The conclusion is cast as a definition: man lives (the ground of his living) not by bread only — the original leḥem is bread, the same word rendered the same way in verse 9. (3) "Every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the Lord" is an expression parallel to "his commandments": verse 2 asks "whether thou wouldest keep his commandments, or no," and verse 3 answers — man lives by everything that proceeds out of the mouth of Israel's God, and the commandments are within it. [Fact Layer] Verse 4, quoted in full: "Thy raiment waxed not old upon thee, neither did thy foot swell, these forty years." Items registered: (1) This verse is the evidential sentence for the "led thee" of verse 2: the forty years' provision was not only in eating (manna) but also in clothing (raiment waxed not old) and in walking (thy foot did not swell). (2) This verse, the restatement in Neh 9:21, and the clothes-and-shoes version in Deut 29:5 (registered as reported paraphrase) form a three-place chain of the same account; registered. (3) The verse's negations are all negative-form testimony: did not wax old, did not swell — provision in the wilderness is remembered in the form of "no lack," answering from afar to the good land's "thou shalt not lack any thing" in verse 9. [Fact Layer] Verse 5, quoted in full: "Thou shalt also consider in thine heart, that, as a man chasteneth his son, so the Lord thy God chasteneth thee." Items registered: (1) The humbling of the preceding four verses receives its name here: chastening (yāsar) — and it is expressly compared to a father chastening his son. (2) "Consider in thine heart" is an inward command: the wilderness lesson must be gone over in the heart before it counts as learned. (3) The father-son comparison connects with the citation chain of Prov 3:11–12 (registered in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for v. 5) and Heb 12:5–11 (registered as reported paraphrase); registered. [Fact Layer] The chain of commands in this section, recounted by hand: observe to do (v. 1, outward), remember (v. 2, backward), consider in thine heart (v. 5, inward) — three commands for one stretch of wilderness: Moses wants the people to walk by obedience, to look back by remembering, and to digest by considering. Further, Job 23:12, "I have esteemed the words of his mouth more than my necessary food." (checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for Prov 30:8), strikes the same note as verse 3: the words of the mouth outweigh the food that is needed — the Job tradition here speaks with one voice with the law's discourse; registered. [Argument Layer] Inferences of this section: (1) The wilderness's characterization in this chapter is double: it is a proving ground (v. 2) and a father's classroom (v. 5) — the proving manifests the heart, the chastening shapes the son; Moses does not permit the people to read the forty years merely as "delay." (2) The order in verse 3 is worth recording on its own: first hunger, then the manna, then the conclusion — "man doth not live by bread only" is not a proverb; it is a definition only the hungry can understand; in this chapter theological conclusions always grow out of experience. (3) Remembering here is a hermeneutical act: the same forty years, unremembered, are material for complaint (as with the generation of Num 14); remembered, they are a textbook of faith. [New Testament Reading Layer] This section carries the chapter's firmest New Testament citation: in Matthew 4:4, Jesus, tempted in the wilderness, answers, "It is written, Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God." (checked directly on the per-verse page for Matt 4:4) — what Jesus quotes is precisely verse 3 of this chapter, and the scene is likewise wilderness, hunger, and temptation; registered: the New Testament uses this verse as the answer under temptation. Luke 4:4 quotes it likewise (registered as reported paraphrase). Further: all three of Jesus' answers under temptation come from Deuteronomy 6–8 (8:3; 6:16; 6:13; registered as reported paraphrase); this sermon registers only this fact of citation and does not develop its theology. The manna discourse of John 6 (registered as reported paraphrase) connects with this section's manna motif; registration only. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three matters presented side by side. (1) The readings of verse 2's "to know what was in thine heart" are presented side by side: (a) the manifestation reading — the purpose of the proving is not that God should learn (God knows all things) but that the heart be made manifest to man himself, and the text's arrangement of its subject ("to know") is anthropopathic expression; (b) the covenant-evidence reading — the proving supplies public evidence for the covenant relationship, in the same pattern as Gen 22:12, "now I know" (registered as reported paraphrase); (c) the literal reading — God's knowing, taken literally, is also unfolded within the narrative. The three readings are presented side by side. (2) The etymology of manna is registered: in Exod 16:15 the people ask, in the Chinese base text's wording, "What is this?" (mān hûʾ; the verse's text checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 8:16 — the KJV reads, "they said one to another, It is manna: for they wist not what it was") — the connection between the name "manna" and this question is the standard scholarly etymological registration; what it refers to historically (the various natural-substance proposals) belongs to another level of question and is registered only, not asserted. (3) The readings of verse 4's "thy raiment waxed not old" are presented side by side: (a) the literal-miracle reading — clothing unspoiled for forty years is part of the miracle of provision; (b) the poetic-hyperbole reading — a hyperbolic sentence summarizing provision without lack; (c) the trade-and-supply reading — during the wilderness period there were still sources of clothing (registered as a proposal). The three readings are presented side by side; the only thing that can be fixed is the textual fact: this verse appears under verse 2's proposition of the leading, as an evidential sentence. [Summary Layer] The section's governing sentence: the forty years in the wilderness — Moses gives them only two names: proving, and chastening. Hunger was no accident and the manna no snack — "man doth not live by bread only" is a sentence understood only by those who have graduated from hunger.

### II. The Good Land, and Blessing in the Midst of Fullness (8:6–10; 16:00–27:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 6, quoted in full: "Therefore thou shalt keep the commandments of the Lord thy God, to walk in his ways, and to fear him." Items registered: (1) This verse is verse 1's echo, concentrated: keep the commandments (v. 1), walk in his ways, fear him — three verbs gathering up the preceding section. (2) Structurally this verse serves as a gate: the section before speaks of the wilderness, the section after of the good land, and the requirement for passing the gate is this verse. [Fact Layer] Verse 7, quoted in full: "For the Lord thy God bringeth thee into a good land, a land of brooks of water, of fountains and depths that spring out of valleys and hills;" Items registered: (1) "A good land" (ʾereṣ ṭôḇâ, a good land) occurs twice in this chapter (vv. 7, 10); it is the frame of verses 7–10. (2) Three items of water source: brooks (naḥal, streams), fountains (ʿăyānôt, springs), depths (təhômôt, subterranean deep water) — standing in exact contrast to the wilderness's "drought, where there was no water" (v. 15): the good land's first blessing is water, and water that springs out of itself (springing out of valleys and hills), not drawn by man. (3) The verb of this verse is in the present-progressive form ("bringeth thee into"): the land has not yet been entered, and the blessing is already spoken in advance — the whole passage vv. 7–10 is in the tense of advance description of an unseen land; registered. (4) The hydrology of this passage, in its comparison with 11:10–12, is registered (registered as reported paraphrase): that land is not like the land of Egypt from which they came out (where seed was sown and watered with the foot), but a land of hills and valleys, watered by the rain of heaven, a land which the Lord cares for, on which His eyes rest from the beginning of the year to its end — the good land's water comes from heaven and from beneath together, and the people's way of life must change from "watering with the foot" to "looking to His care," the same conversion of a way of life as verse 3's "living by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth"; registered. [Fact Layer] Verse 8, quoted in full: "A land of wheat, and barley, and vines, and fig trees, and pomegranates; a land of oil olive, and honey;" Items registered: (1) The produce list, recounted by hand, has seven items: wheat, barley, vines, fig trees, pomegranates, olive (in the KJV's phrasing, "oil olive"), and honey — seven items in full (note 2). (2) The list's structure: two grains (wheat, barley), four fruiting plants (vine, fig, pomegranate, olive), one sweet thing (honey). (3) In the original the olive item is phrased "a land of oil olive" (see headnote, item 7) — the list's final stretch is a processed product (oil) and a gathered product (honey), not all of them field crops; registered. [Fact Layer] Verse 9, quoted in full: "A land wherein thou shalt eat bread without scarceness, thou shalt not lack any thing in it; a land whose stones are iron, and out of whose hills thou mayest dig brass." Items registered: (1) The "bread" of "thou shalt eat bread without scarceness" is still leḥem (the same word as in v. 3) — verse 3 says man does not live by it only; verse 9 says that in the good land he will not lack it: read together, the two sentences are the chapter's complete teaching on bread. (2) "Thou shalt not lack any thing" (ḥāsar) is the summarizing sentence. (3) The blessing list turns from above ground to below: stones are iron, and out of the hills brass may be dug (ḥāṣaḇ) — two mineral items (iron and brass; the original nəḥōšet is copper/bronze), the only two items in the blessing list that must be "dug"; registered. [Fact Layer] The mineral sentence of verse 9 has a registered echo within the canon: Job 28:2, "Iron is taken out of the earth, and brass is molten out of the stone." (checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 8:9) — the pairing of iron and brass reappears in wisdom literature, written likewise with the labour of digging and smelting; this chapter's blessing list goes as far as underground, and stops there; registered. [Fact Layer] Verse 10, quoted in full: "When thou hast eaten and art full, then thou shalt bless the Lord thy God for the good land which he hath given thee." Items registered: (1) "Art full" (sāḇaʿ) occurs twice in this chapter (vv. 10, 12) — the same word, its first occurrence joined to blessing, its second to warning; the chapter's pivot word is registered here. (2) The reason clause for the blessing (bārak) is "for the good land which he hath given thee": the object of the blessing is the land, and still more the Giver of the land. (3) In the base text this verse is the last verse of the first quoted block (vv. 1–10; see headnote, item 8) — the first discourse closes with blessing, and the second discourse (from v. 11) opens with "beware": the contrast between close and opening is registered. [Argument Layer] Inferences of this section: (1) This section's blessing list is one of "gift in advance": the land not yet entered, the grain not yet eaten, and Moses has already spoken the blessing in full and already stated the duty of blessing plainly — in this chapter thanksgiving is not an emotional reaction but a rule laid down in advance: when thou hast eaten and art full, "then thou shalt bless." (2) The blessing list's range deserves notice: water, grain, fruit, oil, honey, iron, brass — all things that can be eaten or used, not one of them a luxury; this chapter's view of abundance is "not lack any thing," not "have everything." (3) The water of verse 7 and the waterlessness of verse 15 mirror each other: the same God gives water from the rock in the wilderness and lets water spring out of itself in the good land — the difference of environment does not change the identity of the Giver. [New Testament Reading Layer] This section's New Testament echoes are registered at the reading layer only: 1 Timothy 4:4–5, on food — "nothing to be refused, if it be received with thanksgiving" (its verse text checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 8:10 — KJV 4:4: "For every creature of God is good, and nothing to be refused, if it be received with thanksgiving") — belongs, with this section's "fullness — blessing" structure, to a motif echo; and the Lord's Prayer's "Give us this day our daily bread." (Matt 6:11, checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for Prov 30:8) stands in the same line as this chapter's leḥem motif. Both are registered only, without reading back. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three matters presented side by side. (1) The seven items of verse 8 are commonly called in Jewish tradition "the seven species" of produce (registered from scholarship and tradition); the later liturgical lists for offering firstfruits are drawn up on this basis (registered as reported paraphrase) — whether the list's "seven" is a number of completeness or a number of record is a matter of readings presented side by side, not adjudicated. (2) The geographical readings of verse 9's iron and brass are presented side by side: (a) the record reading — copper mining is attested since antiquity on both banks of the Jordan and in the Arabah valley (registered from scholarship), and iron deposits are also distributed there; this verse is a record-style summary of the land's resources; (b) the rhetoric-of-plenty reading — "stones are iron" is a hyperbolic sentence of plenty, meaning mineral wealth in abundance, ready to be taken. The two readings are presented side by side; the only thing that can be fixed is the textual fact: this verse closes the blessing list with minerals, and its verb is "dig" — part of the blessing must be taken by hand. [Summary Layer] The section's governing sentence: of the good land's blessings, Moses speaks first of water, then of grain, and last of the iron in the stones and the brass in the hills; and the whole passage's conclusion is one sentence only: when thou hast eaten and art full, then thou shalt bless — fullness is not the destination; blessing is.

### III. Lest Thou Forget: The Danger of Fullness (8:11–16; 27:00–39:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 11, quoted in full: "Beware that thou forget not the Lord thy God, in not keeping his commandments, and his judgments, and his statutes, which I command thee this day:" Items registered: (1) The second discourse (vv. 11–20) opens with "Beware" (hiššāmer, of the same root as šāmar, "to keep"). ### IV. It Is He That Giveth Thee Power to Get Wealth (8:17–18; 39:00–49:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 17, quoted in full: "And thou say in thine heart, My power and the might of mine hand hath gotten me this wealth." Items registered: (1) This is the chapter's second rehearsal of the audience's inner monologue (the first, in 7:17, registered the words "these nations are greater than I"; the monologue's content has changed here: not fear of a strong enemy, but the claiming of one's own merit). (2) The original of the monologue has a parallel pair: kōḥî ("my power") and ʿōṣem yāḏî ("the might of mine hand") — power and might, both words carrying "my." (3) "This wealth" (haḥayil hazzeh) — the original ḥayil can also mean power or ability, and it is the same word as in verse 18; the pairing of the two verses is shown below. [Fact Layer] Verse 18, quoted in full: "But thou shalt remember the Lord thy God: for it is he that giveth thee power to get wealth, that he may establish his covenant which he sware unto thy fathers, as it is this day." Items registered: (1) The chapter's second "remember" stands here, answering verse 2 at head and tail — what verse 2 remembers is the leading (the past); what verse 18 remembers is the giving of power (the present). (2) The correction's sentence form is one of substitution: not "thy power hath gotten wealth," but "it is he that giveth thee power to get wealth" — the word for man's power (kōaḥ) is not denied; what is denied is the attribution of its source: the power is real, and its source is given. (3) The word "covenant" occurs only here in the whole chapter (note 5): the purpose clause of getting wealth is "that he may establish his covenant which he sware unto thy fathers" — in this chapter's theology, wealth is evidence of the covenant, not a medal of success. (4) "As it is this day" draws the fathers' covenant back to the scene of the discourse: the covenant is no antique; it is a thing still being established today. [Fact Layer] This section's two canonical echoes are registered, both checked directly on per-verse pages: Daniel 4:30, Nebuchadnezzar's words — "Is not this great Babylon, that I have built for the house of the kingdom by the might of my power, and for the honour of my majesty?" [Fact Layer] The Psalms' balancing sentence is also registered: Psalm 62:10, "if riches increase, set not your heart upon them." [Argument Layer] Inferences of this section: (1) Verses 17–18 are the chapter's theologically sharpest two verses: Moses does not deny man's power, might, or hand — he asks only after their source. The line between faith and self-deception lies not in whether you have ability, but in whose account you enter the ability. (2) Verse 18's remembering and verse 2's remembering form the chapter's theological closed loop: remembering the leading (He brought you through) and remembering the giving of power (He gives you the power to go on) — past and present are both entered under His name; what remains on man's side is one thing only: remembering, and the keeping that is born of it. [New Testament Reading Layer] This section's New Testament echoes are registered at the reading layer only, at two places checked directly: 1 Corinthians 4:7, "For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it?" [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two matters presented side by side. (1) The wordplay of kōaḥ in verses 17–18 is registered: both verses use kōaḥ and ḥayil alike — in verse 17 man boasts in his kōaḥ and calls what he has gotten ḥayil; in verse 18 God is acknowledged as the giver of kōaḥ, and the purpose is "to get ḥayil": the same pair of words, with the subject exchanged — scholarship generally registers this as a deliberate verbal counter-positioning (registered as the outline of a consensus reading); this sermon registers only the textual fact: each of the two words occurs once in each of the two verses, with the subjects exchanged. (2) The modern readings of verse 18's "to get wealth" are presented side by side: (a) the prosperity reading — this verse is cited as a promise of enrichment and blessing; the reading is registered, and the discussion layer sets beside it the questioning voices as to its fit with the context (this verse is tied to the establishing of the covenant, and the whole chapter is framed by warnings); (b) the covenant-theology reading — this verse's subject is God, its purpose the covenant, its object the community; it speaks of the source of the giving of power, not of a technique of enrichment; (c) the wisdom-balance reading — read together with Prov 30:8–9 and Prov 10:22 ("The blessing of the Lord, it maketh rich," its verse text checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 8:18), riches are a gift, and one to be received with remembering. The three readings are presented side by side, not adjudicated; the only thing that can be fixed is the textual fact: this verse ties the source of ability and the purpose of wealth alike to the covenant. [Summary Layer] The section's governing sentence: "My power and the might of mine hand hath gotten me this wealth" — Moses says this sentence is wrong in exactly one place: the power is yours; the source is not. It is He who gives you power to get wealth, in order to establish His covenant; enter the source correctly, and wealth is a blessing; enter it wrongly, and it is the doorway of verse 19.

### V. I Testify Against You This Day (8:19–20; 49:00–57:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 19, quoted in full: "And it shall be, if thou do at all forget the Lord thy God, and walk after other gods, and serve them, and worship them, I testify against you this day that ye shall surely perish." Conclusion (57:00–60:00). [Fact Layer] The chapter reviewed in headings: the wilderness is a classroom (vv. 1–5), the good land is a gift (vv. 6–10), fullness is an examination hall (vv. 11–16), power is borrowed (vv. 17–18), and the outcome is tied to obedience (vv. 19–20). [Argument Layer] If this chapter were allowed to leave only two sentences for next week's self, they would be the two "remembers": remember how He led you these forty years, and remember that it is He who gives you power to get wealth. [Summary Layer] Remember, and you will not come to forget; not forget, and you will not come to walk after other gods — the whole weight of Deuteronomy 8 is pressed onto this chain.

## Devotional Extract

Scripture (base text) — Deuteronomy 8:3, 18: "…man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the Lord doth man live." … "But thou shalt remember the Lord thy God: for it is he that giveth thee power to get wealth, that he may establish his covenant which he sware unto thy fathers, as it is this day."

Reflection: In the wilderness the Israelites learned one thing: bread runs out; the word does not. The manna was there every morning, but God's word stands forever. The moment when a person most easily keeps the wrong account is not in lack but in fullness — in lack we pray every day; in fullness we begin to say: this was gotten by my power and my might. Moses blocks that sentence in advance: it is He who gives you power to get wealth. Your ability is real, but its source is a gift; your effort counts, but His hand came first. If today you are anxious over lack, remember how He fed you in the wilderness; if you are complacent over what you have, remember from whom all this came. To remember is to keep the account straight.

Prayer: Lord, You fed Your people in the wilderness and warned them in the good land not to forget. Teach me to remember: in lack, to remember Your provision; in fullness, to remember Your giving; never to call Your grace my own ability, and to take Your word alone as the ground of my living. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

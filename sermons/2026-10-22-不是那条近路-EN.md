## Summary (about 100 words)

As soon as the people came out of Egypt, God declared that all the firstborn in Israel belong to Him, and He instituted the Feast of Unleavened Bread and the rite of explaining it to one's son, so that salvation would be a sign on the hand and a memorial on the forehead; Moses took the bones of Joseph along on the journey. At the fork in the road, God did not lead them by the short road through the land of the Philistines, lest they face war and repent and return to Egypt; instead He led them the long way around, with a pillar of cloud and a pillar of fire, day and night. The first thing after salvation is not freedom without restraint, but belonging and remembrance.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

During the night of the previous chapter, the Israelites were still slaves. By daybreak, they were already on the road out of Egypt. Four hundred and thirty years, turned over in a single night. By our way of thinking, once the story reaches this point, what should follow is a gallop straight for Canaan: the shorter the road the better, the faster the arrival the better. But the Bible's pen deliberately slows down right here. Exodus chapter 13 contains almost no description of hurrying along the road. The first thing God speaks of is not the route, but belonging; the first thing He institutes is not a marching plan, but a feast and its rites; and at the end of the chapter, when it is truly time to choose a road, God deliberately bypasses the near road and chooses the far one.

What this chapter places before us are four things: a declaration, "all the firstborn are mine"; a piece of unleavened bread, to be eaten for seven days, and while eating it, to tell the children why it is eaten; a set of bones, the faith of a man dead for centuries, carried by the living in the middle of the procession; and a pillar, cloud by day and fire by night, without ceasing, never leaving. Today we will follow these four things to see how God treats a people who have just been saved: He first receives them under His own name, then teaches them how to remember, how to pass it on to their children, and only then personally chooses their road — and the road He chooses is not the shortest one.

Let us read together several key passages of today's Scripture:

"And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Sanctify unto me all the firstborn, whatsoever openeth the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and of beast: it is mine." (Exodus 13:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And thou shalt shew thy son in that day, saying, This is done because of that which the LORD did unto me when I came forth out of Egypt." (Exodus 13:8, excerpt, KJV)

"God led them not through the way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near; for God said, Lest peradventure the people repent when they see war, and they return to Egypt." (Exodus 13:17, excerpt, KJV)

"And the LORD went before them by day in a pillar of a cloud, to lead them the way; and by night in a pillar of fire, to give them light; to go by day and night: He took not away the pillar of the cloud by day, nor the pillar of fire by night, from before the people." (Exodus 13:21-22, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, many of us, soon after being saved, want to run, want the shortcut, want to arrive in one step. Teach us through this chapter: first belonging, first remembrance, first learning to be led by You, even if the road is farther than we imagined. You would rather walk the long road than abandon us, and Your pillar never leaves. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: All the Firstborn Are Mine — The Ownership of the Saved Child (13:1-16)

At the very beginning of the text, God speaks to Moses and says a sentence of immense weight: "Sanctify unto me all the firstborn, whatsoever openeth the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and of beast: it is mine." Please note the tense and the tone of this sentence. It is not a suggestion, and it is not asking for opinions; it is a declaration: it is mine. The people of the Exodus have just come out from under Pharaoh's hand. Pharaoh ruled them for over four hundred years; what fed and watered them was the Nile, and what drove them to hard labor was the king of Egypt. Now God says: you have come out, but it is not that no one governs you now; you are free, but the first lesson of freedom is a change of masters — the firstborn belong to Me, and the belonging of the whole is declared from the firstborn.

Let us chew on the words first. "Sanctify unto me," in the original qiddesh li (קַדֶּשׁ־לִי, H6942), means to make holy, to set apart as holy unto Me [1]. "The firstborn," in the original peter rechem (פֶּטֶר רֶחֶם, H6363 and H7358), literally "that which first opens the womb," referring to the first to come out of the mother's womb, whether of man or of beast [2]. Why does God want precisely the firstborn? Because the tenth plague has just passed. In one night, all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, from Pharaoh's to the cattle's, were struck down; only in the houses of Israel, because there was the blood of the lamb on the doors, were the firstborn preserved alive. In other words, the firstborn of Israel owed a life: their lives had been bought back that night with the blood of the lamb. When God says "mine," it is not an unreasonable requisition; it is the return of ownership — the Savior takes back under His own name those whom He redeemed with blood. Brothers and sisters, this is exactly the portrait of every one of us who believe in the Lord. It is not that from now on no one governs us; we have changed our belonging: formerly we belonged to the world, to sin, to the one that held us in bondage; now we belong to the One who shed His blood for us. The first thing after salvation is not freedom without restraint, but acknowledging this new Master, and acknowledging Him gladly.

Next, God brings this principle down to the concrete. Of the firstborn of the livestock, the clean ones are to be offered to the LORD; the firstling of an ass, being unclean and unable to be offered directly, is to be redeemed with a lamb, bought out; if it is not redeemed, its neck must be broken. The firstborn of man, even more, must all be redeemed. The word "redeem" here, in the original padah (פָּדָה, H6299), means to buy back at a price, to ransom out [3]. Please read this rule slowly: even an ass cannot simply be kept in one's own hand for free; either a price is paid to redeem it, or it is lost. This rule is like a needle, pricking a delusion very familiar to us all: the idea that having received grace, we can pay no price at all and hand over nothing at all. God's grace is free, but God's people are not lax. If this is true of an ass, how much more of a person. Later on, Israel would redeem its firstborn sons, and the Levites would take the place of the firstborn in service before God; and this line runs all the way to the New Testament: after the Lord Jesus was born, Joseph and Mary, according to the law, presented the firstborn child before the Lord, as it is written in the law of the Lord, Every male that openeth the womb shall be called holy to the Lord; the pair of turtledoves they offered was the sacrifice for the mother's purification under Leviticus, and the redemption price of the firstborn was a separate matter paid according to the law — the Infant who would one day be the ransom for all mankind was first, according to this law, reverently presented by His parents before God. Belonging is not a slogan; belonging has a price, has rites, and has an account that can be checked.

There is another layer of meaning in this passage, hidden in the distinction between offering and redeeming. The clean firstborn of the livestock go directly to God; the unclean must be redeemed; the firstborn of man must be redeemed. Why must man, of all, be redeemed rather than simply "offered" and be done with it? Because a person is not a sacrificial victim; a person is someone God wants to keep, and to use in history. What God wants is not to place us on an altar and burn us up; what God wants is that we live and belong to Him: living to work, living to worship, living to tell this salvation to the next generation. Offering is one expression of belonging; redeeming is likewise an expression of belonging. Every time someone asks a redeemed child about the sign upon him, he must tell the story of the Passover night once more. In this way, belonging becomes the entrance to testimony.

There is one more detail worth pausing over: this ordinance was not spoken only to priests; it was spoken to all Israel, landing in every household's animal pen and beside every cradle. In the kingdom of God there is no such thing as "spiritual belonging being the business of a few": a household that raises asses must understand redemption, a household that bears children must understand redemption, and even the firstborn of the livestock must pass through the procedure of belonging. Faith here is not an abstract doctrine; it is the daily business every household must handle: with the first litter of livestock in spring, remember God first; with the first child in the home, remember God first. The ordinance of the firstborn is like a sign nailed at the door of every Israelite household: the first portion of this house is His. Later, when they entered Canaan, the first sheaf of the field, the first fruit of the trees, the first yield of the vineyard — all of it followed the same logic: the firstfruits go to God first, and only then does the rest come to oneself. This is a trained sense of order: God first, self afterward; belonging first, enjoyment afterward. When the order is right, enjoyment is clean; when the order is reversed, even abundant produce becomes a seed of strife and corruption.

We must also honestly ask ourselves a question: after believing in the Lord, is there any area where our mouths say "it belongs to the Lord" while our hands clutch it and will not let go? Does our time belong to the Lord? Does the first portion of our money belong to the Lord? Have we truly handed over our children's futures, or do we hand them over in prayer and take them back when the prayer is over? The theology of chapter 13 is simple, and it stings: God saved you, and God has the right to ask for your "firstborn" — to ask for your best portion, your first portion, the portion you are most reluctant to give up. Not because He lacks it, but because that portion, left in your hand, will become your idol, while placed in His hand it becomes your blessing. The first lesson of the Exodus is not how to walk fast, but who the Master is. If this lesson is not learned, however far you walk afterward it will be wandering; if this lesson is learned, however winding the road afterward, it will be a going home.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: You Shall Tell Your Son — Remembrance, Telling, and the Bones That Travel Along (13:3-19)

Once belonging is settled, God immediately institutes a second thing: remembrance. And it is not enough to remember it in the heart; it must be eaten out, spoken out, and worn out.

First, look at the eating. Moses said to the people: remember this day, and keep the Feast of Unleavened Bread — for seven days eat unleavened bread, and on the seventh day keep a feast to the LORD; no leavened bread shall be seen within all your borders, and no leaven at all shall remain. Why so strict? Because on the night of the Exodus they left in such haste that the dough had no time to ferment and was carried away in their clothing. Unleavened bread is the bread of flight, the bread of a grace that came too quickly to prepare for. God wanted them to eat it for seven days, year after year, once a year, with the mouth, with the stomach, with a whole week of the dinner table, reliving that night. In the Bible, remembrance is never merely an emotion; it is a discipline: emotions recede, but discipline remains. Many people's faith is defeated precisely here — they have emotion but no discipline: moved to tears in the moment of feeling, and once life gets busy, everything is forgotten. God does not trust our fickle emotions, so He built containers for remembrance — feasts, foods, days, once a year, immovable.

The removal of leaven has another meaning that must not be missed. In the Bible, leaven is often a symbol of corruption: a little leaven leavens the whole lump. In having them clear the leaven out of their borders completely during the feast, God was not only re-enacting the haste of that year; He was also teaching them: a redeemed household must regularly clean house. Some things, kept around, look insignificant, but given time they ferment through the whole house: stored-up resentment, hidden greed, old relationships one cannot bear to sever, old habits brought out of Egypt. The people had come out of Egypt, but Egypt often still lived inside the people; the household labor of the Feast of Unleavened Bread was an annual reminder to them: the door has been exited — has the leaven in the heart been cleared? We today also need this sense of the feast: fixed days, fixed disciplines, turning out the leaven stored up in home and heart and throwing it away, so that a house bought back with blood remains clean enough to be worthy of that blood.

Next, look at the telling. There is a sentence in the text that is the key to this chapter: "And thou shalt shew thy son in that day, saying, This is done because of that which the LORD did unto me when I came forth out of Egypt." Note this: God did not hand the right to explain salvation over to a monopoly of priests, nor outsource it to schools; God handed it to fathers and mothers, to the dinner table. The child will ask: why does our family eat this bread this week? Why are these things marked on the door? God says: this question is yours to answer, and the answer is already written: this is because the LORD brought us out of the house of bondage in Egypt with a strong hand. The phrase "with a strong hand" appears again and again in this chapter, like a nail driven into the child's ears. The essence of remembrance is telling between parent and child: the previous generation tells the salvation it personally experienced to the next generation in the first person — not "they were brought out back then," but "we were brought out of the house of bondage." A child who never served as a slave in Egypt, listening at the dinner table, comes to recognize himself into this story. This is the transmission of faith: not moving knowledge across, but recognizing identity across. At many families' dinner tables today, phones outnumber people and news outnumbers Scripture, and when a child asks about faith, the parents brush it off with "go to church and listen to the teacher." Chapter 13 sets the order straight: the first teacher of a child's faith is the parent who opens his mouth at home; the church helps the parents, it does not replace the parents.

Next, look at the wearing. Twice the text says: this shall be "for a sign unto thee upon thine hand," and it shall be "for a memorial between thine eyes," for frontlets between the eyes [4][5]. The hand is the place of work; the forehead is the place of thought and identity. God wants salvation not merely to stay in the heart, but to be carried out the door and be visible in daily life: when you stretch out your hand to work, you remember it; when you lift your head to meet people, you wear it. Later Jewish generations, on this basis, developed the tradition of wearing phylacteries (tefillin), binding small boxes containing Scripture texts on the arm and the forehead — that is a later traditional practice; what the text itself defines is the principle of the sign and the memorial, not the form of the boxes [5]. The principle itself is enough to benefit us for a lifetime: a faith that is not carried out the door will soon not be able to remain even at home; and a person who, at his work station, at the negotiating table, before the account book, still remembers "I am one who was brought out by a strong hand" — his hand cannot do shoddy work, and his forehead cannot bow through the door of idolatry. The sign is not for God to see; God does not need signs. The sign is for ourselves to see, and for our children and neighbors to see: this person has a belonging.

Finally, look at a set of bones. As the procession set out, Moses did something easily overlooked: he took the bones of Joseph along with them. Before Joseph died, he had said: "I die: and God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land unto the land which he sware to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob" (Genesis 50:24, KJV). And Joseph took an oath of the children of Israel, saying: "God will surely visit you, and ye shall carry up my bones from hence" (Genesis 50:25, KJV). These words had been spoken centuries earlier, with the whole era of slavery lying in between. Where Joseph's bones rested during those centuries, the Bible does not detail; but on this day, Moses personally took them along. The faith of a dead man walked in the procession of the living. A man dead for centuries, by virtue of a single promise, was still participating in this exodus: his bones in the middle of the procession were a reminder to every living person along the way — someone believed this centuries ago; God has not forgotten, and we must not forget either. Later these bones were carried all the way into Canaan and buried at Shechem, which is the entry recorded at the end of the Book of Joshua [6]. When the Epistle to the Hebrews speaks of the heroes of faith, it picks out this very matter, saying that Joseph, by faith, when he died, made mention of the departing of the children of Israel and gave commandment concerning his bones. In the eyes of the New Testament author, the arrangement of a set of bones was a sermon of faith.

Brothers and sisters, our generation today is also leaving "bones" for those who come after — not bones, but examples and words. Whether or not you speak at the dinner table today determines whether or not your children will speak at theirs tomorrow; whether or not you wear salvation on your hand and remember it on your forehead today determines whether your grandchildren will know this faith is real. In the matter of transmission there is no neutrality: if you do not transmit it, the world will gladly give your children its lessons instead. The exodus procession was vast and mighty, yet what was carried in its middle was an old set of bones — God seems deliberately to let us see this: in His kingdom, those who believed yesterday and those who walk today are one and the same procession.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Not the Short Road — The Tenderness of the Detour and the Pillar That Never Leaves (13:17-22)

Now we come to the climax of the whole chapter, which is also the eye of this sermon. The people had come out of Egypt and Pharaoh had let them go, and before them lay two roads. One was the near road along the coast, leading to the land of the Philistines — short, level, with sources of water, the road anyone using common sense would choose. The other was the road of the wilderness — a long way around, arid, with an uncertain future. God chose the latter. The text writes out the reason plainly, with no concealment at all: "for God said, Lest peradventure the people repent when they see war, and they return to Egypt."

Please pause on this word "repent." In the original nacham (נָחַם, H5162), it carries the meanings of changing one's mind, regretting, turning back [7]; and the verb for His "leading" them along the road is nachah (נָחָה, H5148), to guide, to lead [7]. Placed in one chapter in contrast with each other, the two words are deeply meaningful: God personally leads the way (nachah) precisely because He knows so well that people repent (nacham). What the people were made of, God knew better than the people themselves: they had only just been slaves; their backs had not yet straightened, they had no weapons in their hands, and they had never fought a war in their hearts. At the end of the short road were the garrisons of the Philistines, and a single encounter in battle would have been enough to collapse this procession's faith entirely: they would have said, had we known it would be like this, we might as well have stayed in Egypt — at least there were pots and meat, and a way to stay alive. A person's heart for returning to Egypt does not begin with unbelief; it begins with "had we known." God did not lead them by the short road not because the short road was wrong, but because the people walking it were not yet right — their faith had not yet grown up, and could not walk that road. The detour is not punishment; it is protection. The distance is not God delaying; it is God sparing the weakness of new believers. He would rather accompany them several hundred miles farther than let them die in regret on the short road.

This theology matters enormously to us today. In our prayers, we often ask God only for the short road: heal faster, turn things around faster, let the child succeed faster, reverse the situation faster. If God does not give the short road, we think God is not listening, God is late, God has forgotten us. Chapter 13 lifts the curtain and shows us the reason behind the scenes: there are many short roads that God does not refuse to give — He knows that if He gave them, we would die on them. The opportunity that promotes you instantly but requires stepping over your conscience to take it; the relationship that ends your singleness instantly but requires setting your faith aside; the sum of money that recovers your losses instantly but requires gambling away the whole family's livelihood — all of these are the short road through the land of the Philistines. God leads us away from them, around a great bend, and at the time we feel stifled and humiliated; years later, looking back, we learn that the bend skirted a battle we could not possibly have won. God measured our faith first, and only then measured our road. He does not assign roads according to our impatience; He assigns roads according to our capacity to bear them. This is not a lack of love; this is exactly love: a father will not hand the steering wheel to a child just because the child is crying for it.

There is another layer, hidden in the direction of the detour. God led them around through the wilderness, toward the Red Sea. In the people's eyes, this was going further and further astray; in God's eyes, this was so that they would once more see the strong hand with their own eyes — the Red Sea parted, the pursuers drowned, and the threat of Egypt settled once and for all. Had they taken the short road, with Pharaoh's chariots pursuing behind them, even the thought of turning back could have come true; God deliberately led them onto a road that "looked like no way through," so that they would know: the God who led them out will be responsible for leading them all the way through. The detour was God's second lesson prepared in advance: the first lesson was on Passover night, about the blood of the lamb; the second lesson was at the shore of the Red Sea, about how His hand is never shortened.

Immediately after, the text gives this chapter its most tender stroke: the pillar of cloud and the pillar of fire. "And the LORD went before them by day in a pillar of a cloud, to lead them the way; and by night in a pillar of fire, to give them light." The pillar that leads the way, in the original 'ammud (עַמּוּד, H5982), is simply a pillar or column; the pillar of cloud is called 'ammud 'anan, and the pillar of fire 'ammud 'esh [8]. In the desert the heat is fierce by day, and the pillar of cloud is both a guidepost and a shade; by night it is cold and pitch dark, and the pillar of fire is both illumination and warmth. What God gave this band of new believers was not a map and not a doctrine, but a Presence walking ahead of them: they did not need to understand the whole journey first; they only needed to follow the pillar for that day's stretch of road. Most moving of all is the last sentence: "He took not away the pillar of the cloud by day, nor the pillar of fire by night, from before the people." "Took not away" — the pillar does not rest, and God takes no leave. The people's faith rose and fell, and afterward there was murmuring and even the making of a golden calf; yet according to this chapter's record, the pillar was already there before the people's faith had matured: His presence precedes the maturing of the people's faith. It is not that the pillar stayed because the people performed well; it is that the pillar was there all along, and so the people slowly learned to follow. It is the same with us today: in the years since you believed in the Lord, how many stretches of road were walked amid tears and complaints — yet count backward: on which day did He ever leave? Presence is not a prize; presence is the starting point. God first gives His presence, and only then slowly teaches us to grow up.

This pillar later accompanied the Israelites through all the years of the wilderness. When the pillar rose, they broke camp; when the pillar stayed, they camped. Sometimes the cloud stayed one day, sometimes a month, sometimes a year, and the people journeyed or stayed according to the pillar's movement. In other words, God did not only give direction on the first day of the exodus; He trained "following" into their way of life for decades: not understanding a whole lifetime's road in one hearing, but lifting the head every day to see where the pillar is today. We often resent such days as too slow and too unplanned, yet God deliberately used this way to raise their faith: yesterday's cloud cannot serve for today, and yesterday's fire cannot light tonight; grace is distributed daily, and faith is exercised daily. This is also why the pillar must "never leave" — if it left for even one day, this procession could not survive in the wilderness. Our spiritual life is the same: it does not run on a single revival lasting a lifetime, but on a presence that can be followed day by day.

Let me apply this chapter at this point in several ways. First, for those who have just believed, or whose faith is still weak: do not envy another's short road, and do not be ashamed because your own road is the long one. If God leads you around, it means He is carefully protecting you. What you must do is not to copy a shortcut, but to follow the pillar closely: today's cloud by day, tonight's fire by night — keeping up is walking on the right road. Second, for co-workers and parents who are leading new believers and leading children: learn God's way of leading. Do not press new believers with the standards of the mature, and do not push people directly onto battlefields they cannot win. First give presence, first give companionship, first give signs and feasts, so that faith grows slowly in discipline rather than dying in fright. Third, for everyone who is complaining about the detour: please recalculate the account. The years you thought were wasted, God may have been using to skirt a battle for you; the opportunities you thought you missed, God may have been using to spare you a regretful return to Egypt. Do not turn back in the middle of the detour — turning back is what is truly far. For the one who follows the pillar, what winds is the road, not the life.

One more word for the young people anxious about their future. Your generation is urged by the whole age to take short roads: instant courses, instant money, instant fame; everyone fears going around, fears falling behind, fears that peers will arrive first. But look at God's rhythm in this chapter: He never saves people in an instant-made fashion, and in leading the road He first looks at what a person can bear. Going far around is not frightening; what is frightening is losing your life, your faith, and yourself on the short road. If you find your road longer than that of your classmates and colleagues, do not curse the road first; ask first: is God skirting for me a battle I cannot win? Move your eyes off other people's route maps and fix them on your own pillar. When the pillar moves, you move; when the pillar stops, you stop. Those who reach the end are never those who copied shortcuts; they are those who followed to the very last.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today, we have seen four things: the belonging of the firstborn, telling us that the first thing after salvation is a change of masters — handing back the first and most reluctant portion into the Savior's hand; the unleavened bread and the words spoken to one's son, telling us that salvation must be eaten into our days, spoken at our dinner tables, worn on our hands and foreheads, carried out the door, and made visible in daily life; the bones of Joseph, telling us that the faith of the dead can walk in the procession of the living, and that how we transmit today decides how those after us will believe tomorrow; and that bypassed short road and the pillar of cloud and fire that never leaves, telling us: God would rather lead you the long way than let you die in regret on the short road; His presence precedes your maturity, and He measures your road according to your capacity to bear it.

Let us pray together: Lord who led us out of Egypt, thank You for first receiving us under Your own name, and for placing Your presence before us. Forgive our hearts that covet the short road, and forgive our weakness of complaining and turning back in the detour. Teach us to hand the firstborn over to You, to speak of salvation to our children, and, as Moses took up the bones of Joseph, to carry the faith of those before us as we walk forward. Let Your pillar of cloud shelter us by day and Your pillar of fire light us by night, not leaving for a single day, until we reach the land You have promised. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who bought us back with His own blood and who Himself walks before us, Amen.

See Leviticus chapter 27 and Numbers 18:15-17, the ordinances for redeeming the firstborn, as the basis for this manuscript's "redemption has a price and an account that can be checked."

[4] 'ot 'al-yad: Hebrew אוֹת (H226 'ot, sign, mark) עַל־יָדְךָ (upon thy hand), Exodus 13:9 "it shall be for a sign unto thee upon thine hand," and likewise 13:16; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Deuteronomy 6:4-9, "bind them for a sign upon thine hand, and they shall be as frontlets between thine eyes," as the scriptural comparison for this manuscript's "the hand is the place of work, and the sign is carried out the door."

[5] totafot: Hebrew טוֹטָפֹת (H2903), a memorial or band worn on the forehead, Exodus 13:16 "for frontlets between thine eyes"; scholars still discuss what exactly this word denotes; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Later Jewish tradition, on the basis of this verse together with Deuteronomy 6:8 and 11:18 and similar texts, developed the practice of wearing phylacteries (tefillin): leather boxes containing Scripture texts bound on the arm and the forehead — this is a later Jewish traditional practice, not a form defined by the text of Exodus chapter 13 itself, and the body of this manuscript has noted this distinction.

[6] The bones of Joseph: Exodus 13:19, Moses took the bones of Joseph along, fulfilling Joseph's dying charge and the Israelites' oath in Genesis 50:24-26; later, Joshua 24:32 records that the bones were buried at Shechem; Hebrews 11:22 cites this as evidence of Joseph's faith. The second part of this manuscript, "the faith of a dead man walked in the procession of the living," is a homiletical exposition based on these three texts.

[7] nacham and nachah: nacham, Hebrew נָחַם (H5162), to repent, to change one's mind, to be comforted; Exodus 13:17 "lest the people repent when they see war" uses this word; nachah, Hebrew נָחָה (H5148), to guide, to lead, the concept of leading in the narrative of Exodus 13:17 and 13:21 "to lead them the way"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The contrast between the two words in the third part of this manuscript is a homiletical exposition. See Exodus 23:20-23, the promise that God would send an angel ahead to guide and keep them, as the comparison of the promise of guidance, "God personally leads the way."

[8] 'ammud: Hebrew עַמּוּד (H5982), pillar, column; Exodus 13:21-22, the "pillar of a cloud" is עַמּוּד עָנָן ('ammud 'anan) and the "pillar of fire" is עַמּוּד אֵשׁ ('ammud 'esh); "He took not away ... from before the people" is the scriptural basis for this manuscript's "presence precedes the maturing of faith"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "God did not lead them on the road through the Philistine country, though that was shorter. For God said: If they face war, they might change their minds and return to Egypt. ... By day the LORD went ahead of them in a pillar of cloud to guide them on their way and by night in a pillar of fire to give them light, so that they could travel by day or night. Neither the pillar of cloud by day nor the pillar of fire by night left its place in front of the people." (Exodus 13:17, 21-22, NIV)

In our prayers we always ask God for the shortcut: faster, nearer, ideally in a single step. But Exodus chapter 13 tells us that God Himself deliberately bypassed the short road. It was not that He did not know the shorter way; it was that He knew us too well: hearts just freed from slavery cannot yet withstand a hard battle, and at the first difficulty they would start longing for the pots of meat in Egypt. The detour is not punishment but protection; the longer way is not delay but tenderness. What comforts us even more is that the detour is not blind wandering: He set a pillar of cloud and a pillar of fire ahead of us, shielding by day and lighting by night, never leaving. His presence precedes the maturing of our faith; He does not wait until we have performed well before He accompanies us. If today you are walking a road longer than you imagined, do not be quick to turn back, and do not envy another's shortcut. There is only one thing for you to do: follow today's pillar closely and walk today's stretch of road. The God who led you out will see to it that He leads you all the way.

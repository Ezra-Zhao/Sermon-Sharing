## Summary (about 100 words)

The fifth plague, pestilence, fell on Egypt's livestock, and not one of Israel's livestock died; the sixth plague, boils, made the magicians unable to stand before Moses; before the seventh plague, the hail, God first gave a warning and preserved Pharaoh, to show His power and make His name known through all the earth. Pharaoh confessed for the first time, saying the LORD is righteous, yet when the hail stopped he sinned again. If confession is only fear of disaster, it is not a turning of the heart.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

By Exodus chapter 9, the plagues have come down to the seventh. Let us first count the road so far: the water of the Nile turned to blood, frogs covered the land, lice came out of the dust, and swarms of flies entered the palace. And Pharaoh? His reaction runs like a line slowly descending: first he paid no attention, then he asked Moses to intercede, and after the prayer he went back on his word; later the magicians themselves could not hold out first and confessed that this was the finger of God, and Pharaoh still would not listen; by the plague of flies, he began to set conditions — you may go and serve God, but do not go very far. A person can learn many verbal skills inside a disaster, and yet be unable to learn one single thing: surrender.

In today's chapter, God speaks more heavily, and also more thoroughly. Three plagues arrive in succession: first the livestock in the field, then the human body, and finally hail falling from the sky. Each plague comes closer than the one before: first it touches Egypt's property, then the flesh of the Egyptians, and at last even the sky seems to collapse. And precisely in this heaviest plague, Pharaoh for the first time opens his mouth and confesses sin, and his confession sounds quite proper: the LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked. Brothers and sisters, are these words true? They are, every word of them. Yet the Bible deliberately writes out in full what follows these words: as soon as the hail stopped, he sinned yet more. So what we must ask today is not whether a person can say the words of confession, but whether a person's confession has actually reached the heart.

This chapter also contains an extremely weighty sentence, spoken by God Himself to Pharaoh: I have raised you up, to show My power in you, and that My name may be declared throughout all the earth. These words were later quoted verbatim by the apostle Paul in Romans chapter 9, and have become the passage most often raised in the whole Bible when God's sovereignty is discussed. Today we will not go around it, nor will we state it more absolutely than the Scripture itself does; we will simply read it honestly, see what the text itself says, see how Paul quotes it, and then see what it means for us today.

Let us read together several key passages of today's Scripture:

"And the LORD shall sever between the cattle of Israel and the cattle of Egypt: and there shall nothing die of all that is the children's of Israel." (Exodus 9:4, excerpt, KJV)

"And the magicians could not stand before Moses because of the boils; for the boil was upon the magicians, and upon all the Egyptians." (Exodus 9:11, excerpt, KJV)

"And in very deed for this cause have I raised thee up, for to shew in thee my power; and that my name may be declared throughout all the earth." (Exodus 9:16, excerpt, KJV)

"And Pharaoh sent, and called for Moses and Aaron, and said unto them, I have sinned this time: the LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked." (Exodus 9:27, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You are righteous; these words once came out of Pharaoh's mouth, and we ask You today to let them come out of our hearts. Do not let us confess only when we are suffering, and return to our old ways as soon as the disaster stops; give us a heart that truly turns to You. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Plague of Pestilence on the Livestock — The Distinction Appears Again, and Not One Dies (9:1-7)

The opening words of the fifth plague have already been repeated for the fifth time: "Let my people go, that they may serve me." Every time, God first states the demand clearly, then states the consequence clearly; He never strikes by ambush. Moses said to Pharaoh: if you refuse to let them go and still hold them, the hand of the LORD will fall heavily upon your livestock in the field. Please note this order: first the word, then the plague; first the warning, then the judgment. The plagues of Exodus are never an outburst of a God whose temper has run out of control; they are judgments executed only after letter upon letter has been delivered. Every time, Pharaoh first heard, first refused, and only then bore the consequence.

What did this plague strike? It struck Egypt's livestock: horses, donkeys, camels, cattle, and sheep. The original word is dever (דֶּבֶר, H1698), pestilence, plague [1]. In the Old Testament this word often stands alongside sword and famine as one of the common instruments of God's judgment; here it falls concretely upon livestock — a nationwide murrain. For an empire built on farming and herding, livestock were not pets but the whole estate: oxen for plowing, donkeys and camels for transport, horses for the cavalry, flocks for meat and wool. For them to fall dead in masses overnight was to pull a main beam out of Egypt's economy, its grain routes, and its military force at the same time. The plagues God strikes never strike the margins; He lets the Egyptians touch with their own hands the fact that the things they rely on cannot stand before a word of God.

But the most important thing about this plague is not how much died, but how clearly it was divided. "And the LORD shall sever between the cattle of Israel and the cattle of Egypt: and there shall nothing die of all that is the children's of Israel." Brothers and sisters, this act of "severing" is not appearing for the first time in the ten plagues. At the plague of flies, God severed the land of Goshen so that there were no swarms there; today, at the murrain, the distinction appears again, and it is drawn down to counting them one by one: of Israel's livestock, not one thing shall die. This is not luck, not terrain, and not that Egypt's veterinarians were more skillful. It is a boundary line drawn by God's own hand: on this side of the line, the plague falls in full; on that side, not one is missing. Pestilence has no eyes; it cannot recognize doorplates by itself. What makes it recognize doorplates is the sovereignty of God. The same fields, the same plague, and God's people and the Egyptians are divided with perfect clarity — this phrase "set apart as holy" is never a religious slogan; here it is a fact that can be seen and counted.

On the next day, the LORD did this thing. The record of the Scripture is calm to the point of cruelty: "all the cattle of Egypt died," yet on the side of Goshen, "but of the cattle of the children of Israel died not one." One total, one nothing — the contrast is carried to the extreme.

Pharaoh's reaction deserves a pause from us. He sent men to inquire. Brothers and sisters, Pharaoh was not a man without a spirit of verification; he sent people to check on the spot, and the report they brought back was accurate: of the Israelites' livestock, indeed not one had died. The evidence lay on the table, beyond rebuttal. And the result? "And the heart of Pharaoh was hardened, and he did not let the people go." The original here says his heart was heavy and dull, like a stone of ballast. This scene must be engraved on our hearts: to what degree can a person's heart be hardened? Hardened to the point that, having personally verified a miracle, he still refuses to move. Pharaoh's problem was never insufficient evidence. From the water turned to blood until today, in which plague was the evidence insufficient? His problem was this: however much evidence there is, it cannot command his heart to bow. If anyone among us says, "When I am a little more certain, then I will believe, then I will change" — Pharaoh is the mirror. Verification cannot save a person who refuses to surrender, because what he lacks is not information; it is surrender.

Look one layer deeper: when this plague struck, not one of the animal gods the Egyptians worshiped came out to protect its own sacrificial animals. The Egyptians held cattle sacred and sheep as deities, and Pharaoh himself claimed to be a son of god; yet when the pestilence passed through, the idols could not even protect the livestock under their own names. From beginning to end, the ten plagues are the LORD exposing one by one the impotence of Egypt's gods: the river god could not protect the river, the frog goddess could not stop the frogs, and today the gods of livestock could not protect the livestock. God let the people see with their own eyes: what you have bowed to all your life cannot save you. Brothers and sisters, the bankruptcy of an idol is never announced when people do not need it, but at the very moment people need it most, when it does not utter a sound. This murrain was a day when all the gods of Egypt fell silent together.

One detail should not be missed: the Egyptians' livestock died all over the ground, yet in the following plagues Egyptian livestock keeps appearing — the boils fall on livestock, and before the hail people are told to bring their livestock home. So "all died" is the strong way of speaking of the batch struck in the field at that time, or Egypt later bought replacements from elsewhere. The biblical narrative does not haggle with us over statistical scope; what it wants us to see is that boundary line: when God says He will sever, He severs; when God says not one shall die, not one dies. And this point Pharaoh's own investigators have already verified for us.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Boils and the Hail — The Collapse of the Counterfeit and the Purpose of Preservation (9:8-21)

Between the fifth plague and the sixth, the Scripture records no further visit by Moses to warn Pharaoh. When the sixth plague came, even negotiation was omitted: the LORD commanded Moses and Aaron to take handfuls of ashes from the furnace, and Moses sprinkled them toward heaven in Pharaoh's sight. The ashes went out, settled over all the land of Egypt, and became boils breaking forth with blains upon man and beast. The original word is shechin (שְׁחִין, H7822), a boil, an inflamed ulcerating sore [2]. The sore that broke out on Job's body was this same word; and Deuteronomy warns Israel that if they break the covenant, they too will suffer the boils of Egypt. This time the plague walked in from the field into the body: the livestock had fallen, and men could still stand and talk terms; now even their own flesh broke open, and the pain was in their own bodies.

In this plague the Bible gives a landmark picture of the first half of Exodus: the magicians could not stand before Moses. Brothers and sisters, please recall the record of this company of magicians: they once turned rods into serpents, once turned water into blood, once produced frogs — that counterfeiting system of Egyptian wonders could at first learn from Moses item by item, although everything it learned only made more plague, and it never once relieved a single plague. At the lice, for the first time they could not reproduce it, and with their own mouths confessed that this was the finger of God. Today they could not even stand: their own bodies were covered with boils, they were in too much pain to remain upright, and they withdrew from the scene before Moses. After this verse, the magicians never appear again in Exodus. A religious system that lived by imitation, by secret arts, and by holding up Pharaoh's stage thus reached its end amid the boils: it can imitate the beginning of a sign, but it cannot imitate the ending of a sign; it could keep Pharaoh propped up through the fifth plague, but not through the sixth. Human-made faith and counterfeit power all have a point of collapse; the question is never whether they will collapse, only on which day. Egypt's servants too would soon face the same question: when the thing that props you up falls, where will you stand?

Before the seventh plague took the stage, God first sent Moses early in the morning to stand before Pharaoh and speak the longest and heaviest word in the ten plagues. This word has three layers. The first layer is a declaration: "For I will at this time send all my plagues upon thine heart, and upon thy servants, and upon thy people; that thou mayest know that there is none like me in all the earth." God Himself states the purpose of the plagues: not to vent anger, but to make people know — know that there is none like the LORD in all the earth. Every plague is a lesson in knowing God; Pharaoh simply played truant from every lesson.

The second layer is the most shaking hypothetical sentence of the whole chapter: "For now I will stretch out my hand, that I may smite thee and thy people with pestilence; and thou shalt be cut off from the earth." Please hear this clearly: God says that, by His power, He could have finished it in one stroke. The word dever appears here again [1]: had He stretched out His hand, Pharaoh and his people would long ago have been cut off clean, and would not even have had the chance to stand before God and discuss terms. That the preceding six plagues came one by one, slowly, was not because God could only do that much, but because God did not do it all at once to the utmost. The slow tempo of judgment is itself mercy: every pause between plagues was a door left open for Pharaoh to turn back. Brothers and sisters, that we can still hear this sermon today and still have the chance to repent rests precisely on this tempo — if God settled accounts with us in one stroke according to what we deserve, not one person could stand.

The third layer: God states plainly the purpose of preserving Pharaoh: "And in very deed for this cause have I raised thee up, for to shew in thee my power; and that my name may be declared throughout all the earth." The original is he'emadtikha (הֶעֱמַדְתִּיךָ, H5975), I have made you stand, I have caused you to remain [4]. Pharaoh thought he sat on his throne by his own armies and statecraft; God says: that you can still stand is because I have set you standing. That you remain until today is not because you won; it is because I have kept you, to show My power in you, and through this confrontation to make My name declared throughout all the earth. And later the facts were exactly so: this confrontation was written into the Bible, handed down for more than three thousand years, down to today, into this very room — Pharaoh's name became a byword for hardness, while the name of the LORD is recited and praised. The purpose that His name be declared throughout all the earth has not fallen short by one bit.

This verse was later quoted verbatim by Paul in Romans 9:17: "For the scripture saith unto Pharaoh, Even for this same purpose have I raised thee up, that I might shew my power in thee, and that my name might be declared throughout all the earth." There Paul is speaking of the sovereignty of God's election and mercy, and the context continues into the next verse: God has mercy on whom He will have mercy, and whom He will He hardens. How do we face this passage today? First, honestly acknowledge what the Scripture says: the text of Exodus itself stresses the showing of God's power and the declaring of His name, while Paul uses it within his argument on sovereignty; neither place should be misread. Second, keep in mind Exodus's own narrative order: as to Pharaoh's hardened heart, the text first has the side of his own hardening of his heart and refusal to listen, and God lets that hardness stand and uses it, accomplishing His purpose of declaring His name; the two statements coexist in the text, and this manuscript does not, from the pulpit, settle centuries of theological controversy with a single sentence. Third, as to the homiletical application stance — and this is this manuscript's exposition, not a lexical fact: no hearer may use God's sovereignty as an excuse for his own hardness. Paul himself, immediately in Romans, warns people not to reply against God and not to exalt themselves. The Scripture speaks sovereignty to the full, and it also speaks responsibility to the full; when we receive it, we delete neither side. That is what it means to preach according to the Scripture.

With the declaration finished, the timetable of the hail was announced: "Behold, to morrow about this time I will cause it to rain a very grievous hail, such as hath not been in Egypt since the foundation thereof even until now." The original word is barad (בָּרָד, H1259), hail [3]. Please note God's way of doing things: He announced the day and the hour of the judgment a day in advance, and even stated its severity beforehand. This is not a God in a hurry to punish; this is a God who draws the escape route out for you to see. For immediately afterward, He gave a warning full of mercy: send now, and gather your livestock and all that you have in the field; every man and beast found in the field and not brought home, the hail shall come down upon them, and they shall die. The judgment had not yet fallen, and the warning arrived first; the plague was scheduled, and the door was open.

So Egypt's servants were divided into two kinds before the hail fell. The Scripture records it clearly: "He that feared the word of the LORD among the servants of Pharaoh made his servants and his cattle flee into the houses: and he that regarded not the word of the LORD left his servants and his cattle in the field." Brothers and sisters, this is the sorting within judgment: the same warning, two responses, one life and one death. What separated these two kinds of people was not intelligence, not status, not luck, but one thing only — whether or not one regarded the word of the LORD in one's heart. As for the servants who feared the word of the LORD, their fear was not superstition: the word they heard was one that had already been fulfilled six times — God said blood, and the water became blood; God said He would sever, and not one of the livestock died. This warning stood on the shoulders of six fulfillments. Those who heed the warning have a road to walk in the midst of judgment; those who do not regard the warning were not unbelieving but contemptuous — the livestock and servants left in the field would pay the price the next day. The warning itself is grace: God could perfectly well have said nothing; that He spoke was to leave people a way to live.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Hail and the Confession — As Deep as One Plague (9:22-35)

Moses stretched forth his rod toward heaven, and the LORD sent thunder and hail, and fire ran along upon the ground. The scale of this hail is measured by the Scripture with several "alls": the hail struck throughout all the land of Egypt, all that was in the field, both man and beast, all the herbs of the field, and every tree of the field it broke. Since the foundation of Egypt there had been no such hail in all the land. Thunder, hail, and fire fell mingled together; the sky seemed to have opened its mouth. This is the summit-preview of the book's plague scenes: the ten plagues are not yet finished — locusts, darkness, and the firstborn are still to come — but in sheer violence of the heavens and breadth across the land, the hail is the peak of the first seven plagues. Only in the land of Goshen, where the children of Israel were, was there no hail — the distinction appeared a third time, drawn so finely that even the hail from the sky recognized the boundary. When God says He will sever, even the weather obeys.

It was in this hail that Pharaoh said the words that had been waited for through seven plagues. He sent and called for Moses and Aaron, and began: "I have sinned this time." The original is chatati happa'am (חָטָאתִי הַפָּעַם, H2398), this time, this once, I have sinned, I have missed the mark [5]. This is the first time in the text that Pharaoh admits he has sinned. Before this he had said "entreat for me," he had said "you may go," he had stated conditions and made promises — everything except "I have sinned." The words of confession finally came out of the mouth of this king who regarded himself as a son of god. And he went on: "The LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked." The original is tsaddiq YHWH (צַדִּיק יְהוָה, H6662), the LORD is righteous, He is just [6]. This sentence is the title of today's sermon, and it is the most correct sentence Pharaoh ever spoke in his life: he ascribed all righteousness to God, and all wickedness to himself and his people; the verdict was accurate, with no evasion and no balancing of blame fifty-fifty. When a person can speak such words, we almost think the turning point has arrived.

He then made his request: this thunder and hail are enough; entreat the LORD for me, and I will let you go, and you shall stay no longer. Then look at how Moses responded. Moses said: as soon as I have gone out of the city, I will spread abroad my hands unto the LORD, and the thunder shall cease, and there shall be no more hail, that you may know that the earth is the LORD's. Brothers and sisters, please note two details. First, Moses did not first collect the decree of release and only then pray; he prayed first — the order of the intercessor is to discharge the duty of prayer first, and not to use prayer as a bargaining chip. Second, Moses in fact saw through it clearly: in the same conversation he said directly, but as for you and your servants, I know that you will not yet fear the LORD God. He knew this confession could not be trusted; he knew that as soon as the hail stopped, the other side would reverse itself. Yet he still went out of the city, still spread abroad his hands, still prayed in earnest for this enemy, and the prayer was answered at once: the thunder ceased, the hail ceased, and the rain was not poured upon the earth. The office of the intercessor is not discounted according to the genuineness of the other party's repentance. Moses took even an enemy's false repentance seriously in prayer — this is a mirror Exodus leaves for every person who prays: when we pray for family and for those who treat us badly, we often first judge in our hearts whether the other person is sincere enough; Moses's example is that, knowing full well the other may not be sincere, the prayer still leaves his mouth, and the judging is left to God.

In between there is also a calm record of the farming seasons: the flax and the barley were struck, for the barley was in the ear and the flax was in bloom; but the wheat and the rye were not struck, for they had not yet grown up. These seemingly idle sentences in fact show that the judgment, though severe, still had its limit: the late-growing crops were left, the Egyptians still had reserve grain, and within God's punishment there remained measure. The God who judges is not a God who is satisfied only by destroying everything; He struck what had come to ear, and left what had not yet grown.

Then, in the last two verses, the pen of the Scripture turns cold as iron: "And when Pharaoh saw that the rain and the hail and the thunders were ceased, he sinned yet more, and hardened his heart, he and his servants. And the heart of Pharaoh was hardened, neither would he let the children of Israel go; as the LORD had spoken by Moses." Please note the bluntness of the verb: it does not say "Pharaoh changed his mind again," nor "Pharaoh yielded to pressure from his servants"; the text writes it straight: "he sinned yet more." How deep was his confession? Only as deep as one plague. While the hail was striking, he said the LORD is righteous; as soon as the hail stopped and the sky cleared, that sentence scattered with the sound of the thunder. His confession was a conditioned reflex frightened out of him by the disaster: when it hurt, he confessed; when it stopped hurting, he hardened. This is not repentance. The mark of repentance has never been what was said during the disaster, but what is done after the disaster; not how many tears were shed, but whether the road has turned. Pharaoh's road never turned by one inch from beginning to end: God's people he did not let go for a single day.

Speaking this chapter's application to ourselves, there are five things.

The first: examine our confession. Do we have a Pharaoh-style confession? Vows made when gravely ill, resolutions made in hardship — and once the storm passes, everything as before. If confession is only a painkiller for the emotions, it stops the pain of the moment and cures nothing of the hardness of the heart. The shape of true confession, as the text gives it, is this: not only to say the LORD is righteous, but also to let the people go — confession must come with letting go, with an actual releasing of the grip. That matter, that person, that account you clutch so tightly — when you confessed, did you let it go? A confession without letting go — God has heard it seven times already, and He is still waiting for the eighth to be real.

The second: those who hear the warning should move early. The two kinds of servants before the hail are sitting among us today. God's word has been spoken and the examples laid out; do we regard His word in our hearts, or leave it in the field? The action of gathering in the livestock is not at all difficult; what is difficult is admitting that the warning was spoken to oneself. Do not wait until the hail falls to discover that you have been standing all along in the company of those who do not regard the word.

The third: pray for people who are not sincere. May God engrave the picture of Moses spreading abroad his hands outside the city on our hearts. Your family member does not believe, relapses, says one thing and means another — should you still pray for him? Yes. Prayer is not a reward for the other party's sincerity; prayer is your own office. And besides, who among us dares to say that his own confession has been wholehearted every time? If God listened to prayer according to the grade of our sincerity, our prayers would have been cut off long ago.

The fourth: for leaders and for parents — when Pharaoh hardened his heart, he was not the only one who paid the price. The Scripture says he and his servants hardened their hearts; and when the hail fell, it was the servants' servants and livestock that died in the field. When a person in a high place hardens his heart, those below suffer with him; when the head of a household leaves God's word out in the field, the whole family's livestock must stand in the hail. Conversely, the servants who heeded the warning saved their whole households and their full pens of livestock. The direction in which your heart hardens, and the direction in which it softens, is never a matter for you alone. Husbands, parents, and leaders: may God make us the kind of person who brings the livestock into the house, and not the kind who leaves his whole family out in the field.

The fifth: do not make light of the days in which you are preserved. God preserved Pharaoh to show His power and declare His name — that is the use the text gives for Pharaoh, not a promise given to us; using it this way is this manuscript's exposition: we who have received mercy, who are still alive and still have today, are preserved likewise for a use. That you are kept in your post, kept in your home, kept in the church, is not for you to harden your way through the days one by one, but for God's name to be declared through you. Pharaoh was preserved and became a specimen of hardness; we are preserved by mercy, to be vessels of witness. The same preservation, two ways of living it — the difference lies in whether the heart turns.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today we have seen three things: a murrain divided with perfect clarity, a counterfeit system that could not stand, and a confession that lived only as long as one hailstorm. God is righteous — Pharaoh said it, and it is true; the pity is that he took it back as soon as he had said it. But God has not taken back His own word: when He said He would sever, He severed to the end; when He said He would preserve Pharaoh so that His name might be declared, His name really was declared throughout all the earth, and has reached even us.

Let us pray together: Righteous Lord, every word of Yours counts, and all Your warnings are grace. Forgive us those confessions frightened out of us by disaster, and those resolutions forgotten as soon as they are spoken; give us a heart that truly turns to You: when we confess, we let go; when we turn back, we set out on the road. And make us like Moses, still spreading abroad our hands in earnest prayer for people who relapse and for people who are not sincere. May Your name continue to be declared through us who have been preserved. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

Chatati is the first-person perfect of the verb chata (חָטָא, H2398): I have sinned, I have missed the mark; happa'am means "this time, this once." The Chinese Union Version renders it "This time I have sinned." The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "a confession only as deep as one plague" is narrative exposition based on 9:34-35, where Pharaoh sinned yet more when the hail ceased.

[6] tsaddiq YHWH: Hebrew צַדִּיק יְהוָה, Exodus 9:27. Tsaddiq (צַדִּיק, H6662) is an adjective: righteous, just, in the right and vindicated; Pharaoh calls the LORD by this word, and calls himself and his people "wicked," forming the contrast. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's title is taken from this sentence.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Pharaoh sent, and called for Moses and Aaron, and said unto them, I have sinned this time: the LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked." (Exodus 9:27, KJV) "And when Pharaoh saw that the rain and the hail and the thunders were ceased, he sinned yet more, and hardened his heart, he and his servants." (Exodus 9:34, KJV)

Pharaoh's confession lived only as long as one hailstorm. While the hail was falling, he said the LORD is righteous; as soon as the sky cleared, he took the words back. We smile at him, but we often do the same: vows made in trouble are forgotten in comfort, and resolutions made in tears dissolve when life runs smooth again. God does not want a confession startled out of us by disaster; He wants the turning of the heart. Today's passage has another side that comforts us: before the hail fell, God first sent a warning, and the servants who heard it and brought their livestock home lost nothing. The warning itself was grace. If you have heard God's word, do not stand in the field watching. While it is still called today, move, gather in, and turn back. Confess early, and let repentance show itself in action — to still know, after the storm, that God is righteous: that is truly knowing it. Today, while your heart is still soft, hand over to the righteous and merciful God that one thing you have been clutching and refusing to release; He will surely give you a road to turn back on.

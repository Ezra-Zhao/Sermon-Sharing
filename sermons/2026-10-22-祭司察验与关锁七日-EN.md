# The Priest Shall Look: Examination and Seven Days Shut Up

Preacher: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries

Status: Draft, pending cross-review (Sermon mass-production batch 2, 22 October 2026, unpublished). English version: initial translation, pending review, translated from the Chinese original.
> 复审：2026-10-23 复审波（滚动新稿首审）——过·待补Gemini腿（记录见审稿记录/复审波-利12-13-记录.md）。

Scripture: Leviticus 13:1-59 (KJV)

Abstract: Leviticus 13 gives the priestly procedure for examining an affliction of the skin: the rising, the bright spot, the scab and the boil; the criteria of depth beneath the skin, hair turned white, raw flesh and spreading; two rounds of seven days shut up for re-examination; the paradox that a body turned wholly white is pronounced clean; and the affliction in garments, washed, torn out or burned. This sermon distinguishes fact from homiletical use, insists that the priest's office is diagnosis and pronouncement, not healing, and closes with a New Testament reading.

---

## I. The One Who Examines: The Priest's Office and the Place of This Chapter 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, after the distinctions of food in chapter 11 and the ordinance of childbirth in chapter 12, we come today to chapter 13. Its fifty-nine verses contain almost no story and no dialogue, only a procedure repeated again and again: a person shows the sore to the priest, the priest looks, and after looking he either pronounces the person unclean or shuts him up for seven days, looks again on the seventh day, and decides. It reads like a manual, dry yet solemn. God wrote such a manual into Scripture for a people encamped in the wilderness, and its weight deserves a pause: where does this chapter stand in the whole book, and what question is it actually addressing?

First, its structural position, which is a matter of textual fact. Leviticus 11-15 forms the purity corpus at the centre of the book: chapter 11 treats food and the uncleanness of animal carcasses, chapter 12 a woman's uncleanness and cleansing after childbirth, chapters 13-14 afflictions of the skin together with mould in garments and houses, and chapter 15 bodily discharges. Chapter 13 stands at the centre of this group. It receives the categories of clean and unclean established in chapters 11 and 12, and it opens the way to the rite of cleansing in chapter 14, with its two living birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn and hyssop, its washing and shaving, its seven days outside the tent and its offerings on the eighth day. Without the diagnosis and pronouncement of chapter 13, the rite of chapter 14 would have no object; without the restoration of chapter 14, the pronouncements of chapter 13 would be incomplete. The two chapters are a pair: chapter 13 determines the status, chapter 14 restores the person. Read chapter 13 with chapter 14 in mind, lest we read only separation and never return.

Second, the priest's office, the most important factual distinction in this chapter, to be stated strictly from the text. Throughout the chapter the priest performs one governing action: he looks, he examines. The text says the priest shall look on the plague, look on the rising, look on the sore; and after looking, the priest shall pronounce him unclean, or pronounce him clean. Nowhere does the text record the priest prescribing medicine, applying a dressing, performing surgery, or undertaking any treatment whatsoever. The priest is not a physician. His office is diagnosis and pronouncement: to examine according to the standards God has given, and then to declare whether this person is ritually clean or unclean, whether he may remain in the camp and worship with the people or must for a time dwell outside the camp. The pronouncement decides a place in the worshipping community, not the healing of the body itself. The rite of chapter 14, performed after cleansing, is likewise not a treatment but the confirmation and witness of a restored status. Keep this distinction clear, and chapter 13 will not be misread as an ancient medical textbook, nor as a record of priests practising medicine. It is a liturgical diagnostic ordinance, concerned with the purity order of sanctuary, camp and community. Every application later in this sermon proceeds from this distinction: the priest's authority is to pronounce according to the standard, not to judge by feeling; his examination must be careful, but his conclusion is bound by the criteria. That this also instructs the church today belongs to the level of homiletical use, to be marked as such below.

One word must also be clarified at the outset, or the whole chapter will be misread. The phenomenon rendered in Chinese versions by the traditional term for leprosy is, in the Hebrew, a word of far wider range than modern readers imagine. It is used not only of the skin of a person: the second half of this chapter uses it for mould spots in a garment, and chapter 14 uses it for mildew in the walls of a house. Something that grows on a woollen garment and on a wall plainly cannot be one bacterial disease of the human body in the modern medical sense. This sermon therefore fixes its reading from the start: what the text describes is a ritual category of afflicted state, judged by the priest according to visible signs, not a disease name that can be carried directly into a modern medical dictionary. The scholarly caution and debate over the old rendering are set out side by side in the notes; the body of the sermon holds only this safe position: read the signs the text records as the text records them, and do not pour a later disease name back into an ancient ordinance. With this settled, we will not spend the sermon practising medicine on the ancients, nor use this chapter to judge anyone who suffers from a skin disease today. The text treats the purity order of the camp, and as the purity order we shall read it.

The chapter opens thus: "And the LORD spake unto Moses and Aaron, saying, When a man shall have in the skin of his flesh a rising, a scab, or bright spot, and it be in the skin of his flesh like the plague of leprosy; then he shall be brought unto Aaron the priest, or unto one of his sons the priests" (Lev 13:1-2, KJV). Note the first step of the procedure: the person is brought to the priest. He does not hide and guess by himself, nor is the matter settled by neighbours' talk. The sore must be brought into the light and handed to one who holds the office and the standard. A hidden sore cannot be pronounced upon; examination must precede pronouncement. The procedure itself teaches: questions of purity in the community are to be handled openly, responsibly and in a way open to re-examination, not by rumour and not by private labelling. Let us now follow the priest's eyes, and learn step by step how he looks, how he waits, and how he pronounces.

## II. Deeper Than the Skin, Hair Turned White: The Criteria and the Seven Days of Waiting 08:00-24:00

What does the priest look for? The text gives a set of visible signs that can be tested on the spot. This sermon follows the main line and does not catalogue every sign. The main line has four strands, depth, hair colour, raw flesh, and spreading or not spreading, together with one procedural line: two rounds of seven days shut up for re-examination. Four signs and one procedure form the skeleton of diagnosis in this chapter.

The first is depth. The priest looks on the plague, and if the plague in sight be deeper than the skin, that is, if the sore appears sunken and penetrating, beyond the surface of the skin, the text takes this as a weighty ground for pronouncing uncleanness. The Hebrew says, literally, that the appearance of the plague is deeper than the skin. It is a visual criterion: the priest judges with his eyes whether the sore rests on the surface or has gone in. A surface matter and a deep matter carry entirely different weight in this chapter. A spot only on the surface may be a passing phenomenon; a sore deeper than the skin shows that the problem has entered within. The priest's looking is therefore not a glance at colour but a judgement of depth. This is crucial for the whole chapter: from beginning to end its concern is not whether there is a spot, but how far the spot has gone. In almost every case the priest must answer the same question: deep, or not deep?

The second is hair colour. If the hair in the sore has turned white, the text counts this among the signs that the affliction is established. Hair colour is a minute matter, yet the priest must bend down and look closely: has the hair kept its colour, or has it turned white? Hair turned white and depth beneath the skin often stand together, and where the two signs meet, the priest pronounces the person unclean. A single sign alone may still require waiting. Here the rigour of this chapter's diagnosis appears: the conclusion does not rest on one impression; the signs must confirm one another. The priest does not judge from a rough look; he examines depth and hair, the sore itself and the minute changes within it.

The third is raw flesh. If raw flesh appears in the rising, living and exposed new tissue within the sore, the text counts it a certain ground of uncleanness. Raw flesh shows that the sore is not an old, still trace but a lesion active and ulcerating. Where there is raw flesh, the priest need not shut the person up and wait; he may pronounce uncleanness at once. Keep this strand in hand, for it stands in exact contrast to the paradox we shall meet next: what is active, spreading and marked by raw flesh is unclean; what covers the whole body yet is no longer active receives the opposite judgement.

The fourth is spreading. If the affliction spreads abroad in the skin, if its area enlarges, the text takes spreading as plain evidence of uncleanness; if the affliction stays in its place and does not spread, and its colour fades, it is handled otherwise. Spreading is a sign in time: it cannot be fully determined in one examination. Only after days have passed and the priest looks again can he know whether the sore is stable or advancing. Because of this strand, the chapter has its most distinctive procedure: shutting up.

Shutting up is the disposition that recurs through the chapter. If the priest sees that the signs are not yet decisive, that the plague is not deeper than the skin and the hair has not turned white, he shuts up the afflicted person for seven days. On the seventh day he looks again; if the plague has stayed in its place and has not spread, he shuts him up for seven more days; after the second seven days he looks again, and only then decides. See this procedure clearly: it is not punishment and not expulsion, but diagnostic waiting. The Hebrew verb means, at root, to close in, to enclose; here it sets a person apart for a time, pending re-examination. During the seven days of shutting up there is no treatment and no pronouncement, only waiting and observation. The priest confesses that his first look could not decide, and so he does not hurry to a conclusion; he lets time bear witness. After seven days he looks once; if it is still unclear, he grants another seven. In the ancient world this is an exceptionally cautious procedure: rather two rounds of seven days than a lifelong label fixed by a single look. Once the pronouncement of uncleanness is spoken, the person must dwell outside the camp; precisely because the consequence is grave, the procedure is so restrained.

Under this main line of criteria, the chapter treats several kinds of case in turn, of which the sermon notes only the outline. The rising is a swelling, shining class of sore; the bright spot a shining white spot on the skin; the scab a crusted, spreading class; and the boil, a sore breaking out again upon the scar where a boil had healed. To recurrence on an old scar the chapter gives special attention: if on the scar of a boil or of a burn there rises a white or reddish-white spot, the priest examines whether it goes deep or rests on the surface, whether it is active or still; if it is only on the surface and its colour is dim, he pronounces it clean, a mere phenomenon of the scar. An old scar is not a new disease: a person who bears an old wound need not be condemned for a slight change in the scar, until it is seen whether the change truly goes deep and is truly active. In its demand for evidence, the ordinance protects those who bear old scars. All its proportion lies in this looking: it does not overlook what goes deep, and it does not wrong what rests on the surface.

Note, too, the chapter's sense of time. The only number that appears is seven: shut up seven days, and again seven days. Seven days is the constant of the purity procedures in Leviticus: after childbirth one waits, after cleansing one waits, at the consecration of priests one keeps seven days. Seven days is a complete cycle of observation. The priest's waiting is not delay but institutionalised caution: God left room in diagnosis for time, and left room for the examined person to change. Within seven days many sores declare themselves: they spread, or they recede. Time is the most honest re-examiner. At the level of fact, remember the procedure: signs, shutting up, re-examination, pronouncement, four distinct steps. Only at the level of application may we draw the lesson: if the church, in dealing with persons, learned the priest's caution, refusing to fix a person by one impression or to conclude from one spot, willing to wait and to look again, much injustice and hurt could be avoided. Application is application and fact is fact; this sermon keeps them marked apart.

## III. The Paradox of the Body Turned Wholly White: The Scall of the Head, Outside the Camp, and No Hasty Verdict 24:00-42:00

The most startling passage in the chapter stands at verses 12-13. The text says that if the affliction breaks out abroad in the skin and covers all the skin of the afflicted person, from his head even to his foot, then the priest shall consider: "and, behold, if the leprosy have covered all his flesh, he shall pronounce him clean that hath the plague" (Lev 13:13, KJV). The whole body turned white: while the whiteness was only local, it could lead to a verdict of uncleanness; now that it covers everything, the verdict is cleanness. The local is the problem; the universal is clean. This is no scribal slip but the plain text of the ordinance, and readers of every age have stopped here.

On this paradox, scholarship offers several explanations, set out side by side in this sermon without a final verdict. The first is the course-of-disease reading: when the affliction covers the whole body, its active lesions have passed; the uniform whiteness shows that there is no longer raw flesh and no ulceration still spreading, as though a condition of the skin had burned out its active phase and left only a still trace. Its textual support lies in the limitation that follows at once in verses 14-17: the one turned wholly white is pronounced clean, yet if raw flesh appears in him he is unclean again, and when the raw flesh turns white once more, he is clean again. The decisive factor throughout is activity: raw flesh means activity, and activity means uncleanness; wholly white with no raw flesh means activity has ceased, and so the verdict is clean. This reading coheres with the raw-flesh criterion earlier in the chapter, and its explanatory power is strong. The second is the symbolic reading: in the logic of ritual symbolism, a local spot signifies disorder and the breach of a boundary, whereas a uniform covering constitutes a complete and even state, a whiteness over all no longer constituting a local invasion of the whole. This reading appreciates the symbolic logic of the rite, but it is weaker in direct textual warrant, for the text itself supplies no symbolic explanation. The third is the procedural reading: the priest's pronouncement is a judgement of ritual status, not a medical prognosis; the verdict on the wholly white body states only that, by this chapter's standards, the person presents no ground for isolation, and guarantees nothing about his bodily condition. This reminder is itself a guardrail for reading the whole chapter. Set side by side, this sermon's judgement is that the first and third readings together, the course-of-disease and the procedural, lie closest to the text's own thread of raw flesh; the symbolic reading may serve as a reference, but is not announced as the settled answer. To preach any one of them here as final would pass beyond the text.

After the paradox, the text turns to the scall of the head and the beard, an affliction in the regions of hair. If a sore arises in the head or the beard, the priest looks: if it is in sight deeper than the skin, and the hair in it is yellow and thin, he pronounces him unclean; it is a dry scall, a leprosy of the head or beard. This class has its own sign: thin yellow hair is its mark, different from the white hair of sores on the body. The priest's handling is still the same procedure: if the signs are not decisive, he shuts up the afflicted person for seven days; on the seventh day he looks again, and if the scall has not spread, the hair has not turned yellow, and the scall is not deeper than the skin, the person shaves himself, but the scall he does not shave, and the priest shuts him up for seven more days; then he looks again, and if the scall has stayed in place and is not deeper than the skin, the priest pronounces him clean, and he washes his clothes and is clean. If afterwards the scall spreads abroad in the skin, the priest must look and judge again. The detail of shaving and not shaving shows the fineness of the procedure: the surrounding hair is shaved so that the sore can be seen clearly, while the hair of the sore itself is left as evidence for the re-examination. Diagnosis needs a chain of evidence; even a shaving must preserve a sample for the second look. Baldness is treated separately: a man whose hair has fallen off his head is bald, yet he is clean; but if in the bald head or forehead a white reddish sore arises, it is an outbreak, and the priest looks, and if its appearance is like the affliction on the skin of the flesh, he pronounces him unclean. Hair loss itself is not an affliction, and baldness is no offence; only an active sore newly arising on the bald place enters the judgement. Here again the ordinance protects the normal: natural change is not condemned; only the signs of the affliction are dealt with.

One short passage in the latter part of the chapter must also be stated truthfully, without embellishment and without sensationalism. Verses 45-46 say: "And the leper in whom the plague is, his clothes shall be rent, and his head bare, and he shall put a covering upon his upper lip, and shall cry, Unclean, unclean. All the days wherein the plague shall be in him he shall be defiled; he is unclean: he shall dwell alone; without the camp shall his habitation be" (Lev 13:45-46, KJV). That is the statement of the text, and this sermon states only the fact, with two necessary remarks. First, this disposition belongs to the ritual order of the chapter: the cry of "Unclean" is a warning sounded to others, so that they may know to keep distance; dwelling alone outside the camp is the condition that follows a verdict of uncleanness, and it differs in nature from the seven days of shutting up. Shutting up awaits examination; the camp's outside follows judgement, and it lasts until cleansing restores the person by the procedure of chapter 14. Second, a sermon must handle this passage with restraint: it is the arrangement of an ancient camp community within its particular liturgical framework. We state it and understand its place in the order of that time; we do not make entertainment of it, we do not trade in curiosity about it, and above all we do not apply it to any sick person today. Reading it today, we should read out the gravity of the pronouncing system and the loneliness of the one who dwelt outside the camp, the tension between the order of the community and the condition of the individual, and no more. Its echo belongs to the fifth section, where the New Testament reading looks back upon it.

To gather this third section: the judgements of the chapter turn throughout on the lines of active and still, deep and surface, spreading and stayed, and the priest's work is to check these lines repeatedly across time. The paradox of the wholly white body, the fine hair of the scall, the new sore on the bald head, the cry from outside the camp, differ widely on the surface yet share one underlying caution: look at the evidence, leave room, let time speak, do not seal a person's verdict lightly, and do not overlook what is truly deep and spreading. This caution is not sentimentality; it is even rather severe. But its severity aims at accuracy, not at exclusion.

## IV. The Plague in a Garment: Shutting Up, Washing, Tearing Out and Burning 42:00-56:00

In the last section of the chapter, from verse 47 to verse 59, the object changes suddenly from the skin of a person to a garment. "The garment also that the plague of leprosy is in, whether it be a woollen garment, or a linen garment; whether it be in the warp, or woof; of linen, or of woollen; whether in a skin, or in any thing made of skin" (Lev 13:47-48, KJV): if the spot is greenish or reddish, it is a plague, and it is shown to the priest. That an affliction can grow in a garment proves once more that the central word of this chapter is no name of a human disease in modern medicine, but a ritual category governing a class of visible, corrosive spotting on skin, fabric and house alike. Read the garment section with this recognition, and it will not read as absurdity.

The priest's handling of a garment follows the very pattern of his handling of a person: first shutting up, then re-examination. The priest looks on the plague and shuts up the afflicted thing for seven days. On the seventh day he looks again: if the plague has spread in the garment or the skin, it is a fretting, corroding plague, and the thing is unclean. The dispositions fall into grades, of which the sermon takes the main ones. The heaviest grade is burning: where the plague spreads in a garment or a skin, the priest burns the afflicted thing in fire, whether in warp or woof, in wool or in linen, wholly burned, without negotiation. The next grade is observation with washing: if the priest sees that the plague has not spread, he commands that the afflicted thing be washed, and shuts it up for seven more days; after washing he looks again, and if the plague has not changed its colour and has not spread, though it has not spread it is unclean and is burned in fire, for it is a fretting corrosion, on the inner side or the outer. The next grade is tearing out: if after washing the priest sees that the plague has faded and grown dim, he tears out the plagued part from the garment or the skin, and the rest of the thing may be kept in use. If after the tearing out the plague breaks out again in the garment, it must still be burned; but if after washing the plague has wholly departed, the thing is washed a second time, and it is clean. Verse 59 gathers the whole section: "This is the law of the plague of leprosy in a garment of woollen or linen, either in the warp, or woof, or any thing of skins, to pronounce it clean, or to pronounce it unclean" (Lev 13:59, KJV).

Three points in this procedure deserve notice. First, its object is a thing, not a person: there is no shaming and no pronouncement upon anyone's identity; only the corroded object itself is treated. The plague grows in the garment, and the garment is what is burned; the ordinance keeps person and thing clearly apart. Second, the dispositions are graded by degree: washing, tearing out, burning, each under its conditions, from the lighter to the heavier. What can be cleansed by washing is not burned; what can be saved by tearing out the afflicted part is not wholly destroyed. Burning is the last resort, for a corroding plague that spreads without ceasing and does not change after washing. The grading shows that the ordinance aims not at destruction but at stopping loss: preserve what can be preserved, remove what must be removed. Third, the procedure still leaves seven days and two lookings. Even for a garment, the priest does not order burning on a single look; he shuts it up, washes it, and looks again. If a thing is treated with such care, how much more a person. From the person to the garment, the consistency of the procedure in chapter 13 is itself a lesson: nothing concerning cleanness and uncleanness may be disposed of hastily, on a moment's impression.

Looked at across the whole chapter, chapter 13 presents only one executor, the priest; only one authority, the authority to pronounce according to the standard; and only one timeline, the seven days and seven days again of re-examination. Rising, bright spot, scab, boil, scall of the head, baldness, mould in a garment: the cases multiply, the procedure does not change. The repetition is not redundancy but the ordinance's way of teaching: it would have its readers memorise the procedure into a habit through repetition. The order of purity is not maintained by a single burst of zeal, but by generation after generation of priests keeping the same standards and the same patience. The people show their skin to the priest and their garments to the priest, and the order of the camp is kept, looking by looking and pronouncement by pronouncement. At this point the factual level of the chapter has been fully stated: its position, office, criteria, procedure, paradox, cases and garment section have all been set out from the text; where interpretations differ they have been placed side by side without a forced verdict; and not one word of medicine has been added, because this chapter is not a medical book and the priest is not a physician. That restraint is precisely the honesty that reading this chapter requires.

One seam remains to be pointed out before the close, as a bridge into the ending: the seam between chapters 13 and 14. Every pronouncement of uncleanness in chapter 13 stands open toward the rite of cleansing in chapter 14. When the afflicted person is cleansed of his affliction, he performs the rite of his cleansing, the priest performs the rite for him, and he is clean. Separation is not the last word of this chapter; return is the destination of this whole pair of ordinances. Outside the camp is not forever. The torn clothes, the uncovered head and the days of crying "Unclean" all have their appointed procedure of ending. By the priest God pronounces uncleanness, and by the priest God pronounces cleanness: the first pronouncement guards the boundary of the community, the second welcomes back the one who had been apart. To read chapter 13 and stop outside the camp is to read only half; to read through the doorway of chapter 14 is to read the heart of this pair of ordinances whole.

## V. A New Testament Reading and Our Waiting Today 58:00-60:00

Finally, by the rule of this sermon, the New Testament reading is marked at its own level. What follows belongs to New Testament reading and homiletical use, and is not read back into the original meaning of this chapter, whose meaning, within the Old Testament liturgical framework, is that the priest diagnoses by visible signs and pronounces cleanness or uncleanness. This sermon also states expressly: the isolation ordinances of the ancient camp are not directly equivalent to modern systems of public health; this sermon argues no modern arrangement of disease control from them, and offers no medical advice on their basis. For sickness of body, seek a physician; that is the business of medicine, and outside the scope of this sermon.

Two New Testament passages fit most closely. The first is Matthew chapter 8. As Jesus came down from the mountain, a man with the affliction came and worshipped him, saying, "Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean. And Jesus put forth his hand, and touched him, saying, I will; be thou clean. And immediately his leprosy was cleansed." Yet Jesus charged him: "See thou tell no man; but go thy way, shew thyself to the priest, and offer the gift that Moses commanded, for a testimony unto them" (Matt 8:2-4, KJV). Notice two details. First, Jesus put forth his hand and touched him: within the order of this chapter, contact with one pronounced unclean defiled; Jesus instead touches of his own accord, and the source of cleanness himself draws near to the unclean, the order reversed by grace. Second, Jesus still sends him to show himself to the priest: the procedure of examination and pronouncement in chapter 13 is not abolished by Jesus but fulfilled through it, as the cleansed man returns to complete it and the priest's pronouncement becomes a testimony to them all. The healing is Jesus' work; the pronouncement still follows the procedure. The second passage is the parallel account in Mark chapter 1, which adds one further word: Jesus, moved with compassion (Mark 1:40-45). Compassion is the warmth the New Testament adds to all this procedure: the priest of the old covenant examined by the standard; the Lord of the new reaches out his hand in person. In homiletical use, the church today may learn both: the priest's caution, judging by evidence and not by impression, willing to wait and to look again; and, still more, the Lord's compassion, a mercy that precedes the verdict and a warmth of the outstretched hand beyond the pronouncement. Caution guards against wronging a person; compassion guards against coldness. Lacking either, the church's way of treating people goes astray.

One further use concerns waiting. Shut up seven days, and again seven days: in two rounds of seven the priest handed judgement over to time and evidence. Our lives, too, have seasons of being shut up, when matters are not yet clear, no conclusion can be drawn, and we can only wait for the second look. Chapter 13 tells us that to withhold a verdict while things are unclear is not weakness but reverence: give a person seven days, give a matter seven days, give yourself seven days, and in the waiting let what is spreading declare itself and what is still grow quiet. The end of the priest's waiting was a pronouncement; the end of the believer's waiting is the Lord's own looking and judgement. He examines more deeply than the priest: the priest looked at depth in the flesh, He looks at depth in the heart; the priest looked whether hair had turned white, He looks whether motives are pure. May we all be willing to bring ourselves before Him, as the sick man showed his sore to the priest, without hiding and without disguise, and let Him look, let Him decide, let Him pronounce.

Let us bow our heads in prayer. Lord, You are the God who examines man; Your eyes run to and fro through the whole earth, and You look closely at every afflicted place in our bodies and our hearts. Give us the priest's caution, to treat people not by impression, to leave room in our judgements, to be willing to wait and to look again; and give us, still more, the compassion of the Lord Jesus, to reach out a hand, to draw near, to walk with people through the days of shutting up and of dwelling outside the camp. Examine us, heal us, separate us from every sin and wound that goes deep and spreads, pronounce us clean, and bring us back into the midst of Your people. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Notes

1. tzara'at: Hebrew צָרַעַת, transliteration tzara'at, noun, Strong's number H6883. Lexical basis: BDB, leprosy, including its use for persons, garments and houses; HALOT, disease of the skin, leprosy, noting the analogous use for fabrics and houses. Its semantic range spans three domains: afflictions of human skin (Lev 13-14), mould spots in fabrics (Lev 13:47-59), and mildew in houses (Lev 14:34-53). The prevailing caution in modern scholarship is that tzara'at is by no means an equivalent term for Hansen's disease in modern medicine, the disease caused by Mycobacterium leprae, for three reasons: first, its range includes inanimate garments and houses, to which no human pathogen corresponds; second, the signs recorded in the text, hair turned white, raw flesh, changes within seven days, do not match the chronic course of Hansen's disease; third, the Septuagint rendered it lepra, Latin and older English versions followed with leprosy, and the traditional Chinese rendering followed likewise, all historical inheritances of pre-modern translation. The debate over the rendering is set out side by side: those who retain the old rendering argue from tradition and continuity of reference; those who favour a rendering such as skin affliction or affliction argue from avoiding misunderstanding and stigma. Milgrom, Wenham and Hartley all warn the reader expressly against a direct equation. This sermon follows their caution and speaks in the body of the affliction.

2. nega': Hebrew נֶגַע, transliteration nega', noun, Strong's number H5061, at root a stroke or touch, and by extension an affliction, sore or spot. Lexical basis: BDB, stroke, plague, disease, mark, plague spot; HALOT, plague, affliction, lesion. In Leviticus 13 it is the general term: nega' ha-tzara'at is the plague of the affliction (13:3 and elsewhere). Its verbal root naga' (H5060) means to touch or strike and shares a root with the language of the plagues of Exodus, but its use in this chapter is that of the sore in liturgical diagnosis, not a theology of calamity.

3. se'et: Hebrew שְׂאֵת, transliteration se'et, noun, Strong's number H7613, literally a lifting up, a swelling. Lexical basis: BDB, an elevation, exaltation, and for skin a swelling; HALOT, swelling. Traditional versions render it at 13:2 as the rising; it recurs in the cases that begin with a rising (13:10, 19, 28, 43), standing with baheret and sappachat as the three presenting forms of the opening verse.

4. baheret: Hebrew בַּהֶרֶת, transliteration baheret, noun, Strong's number H934, a bright spot. Lexical basis: BDB, a bright spot (on the skin); HALOT, bright spot. At 13:23-28 the bright spot after a boil or a burn is judged: if its colour is dim, it is not deeper than the skin, and it has not spread, it is pronounced clean, showing that a bright spot in itself does not automatically constitute the affliction, but returns to the main criteria of depth and spreading.

5. sappachat: Hebrew סַפַּחַת, transliteration sappachat, noun, Strong's number H5597, a scab or crust, a thing attached. Lexical basis: BDB, scab; HALOT, scab, eruption. Its root carries the sense of attaching or joining. At 13:2 it stands as the third presenting form beside se'et and baheret; at 13:6-8 the scab is re-examined, and if after washing it spreads abroad in the skin it is pronounced unclean. The word occurs in the Old Testament only in Leviticus 13-14.

6. neteq: Hebrew נֶתֶק, transliteration neteq, noun, Strong's number H5424, the scall of the head or beard. Lexical basis: BDB, scurf, scall; HALOT, scab on the head or beard. Its root ntk carries the sense of tearing away or peeling, suiting its flaking appearance. Verses 13:29-37 treat this condition exclusively: its sign is depth beneath the skin with hair yellow and thin, differing from the white hair of sores on the body; its handling includes shaving while leaving the hair of the scall itself, and two rounds of seven days shut up for re-examination.

7. hesgir: Hebrew הִסְגִּיר, transliteration hesgir, the causative form of the verb sagar (סָגַר, H5462), literally to cause to be shut up, to deliver over to confinement. Lexical basis: BDB, shut up, confine; HALOT, to shut up, isolate. In Leviticus 13 the word denotes exclusively the priest's diagnostic isolation: shutting up the afflicted person or thing for seven days pending re-examination (13:4-5, 21, 26, 31, 33, 50, 54). It differs in nature from dwelling alone outside the camp after a verdict of uncleanness (13:45-46): hesgir is a procedure awaiting examination, not a condition after judgement. The sermon's account of shutting up as diagnostic waiting rests on this.

8. 'ammiq min-ha'or: Hebrew phrase עָמֹק מִן־הָעוֹר, transliteration 'ammiq min-ha'or, literally deeper than the skin. 'amoq, Hebrew עָמֹק, adjective, deep, Strong's number H6013; 'or, Hebrew עוֹר, noun, skin, Strong's number H5785. Lexical basis: BDB, 'amoq, deep; HALOT, deeper than the skin. The phrase recurs through chapter 13 (13:3, 20, 25, 30-32, 34) and is the key visual criterion of the diagnosis: the priest judges with his eyes whether the appearance of the sore is deeper than the surface of the skin. Deeper, and it falls under the judgement of the affliction; shallower, and it is either pronounced clean or held for further examination.

9. chai, read with tum'ah and tahor: raw flesh, Hebrew חַי, transliteration chai, adjective, living, Strong's number H2416; at 13:10, 14-17 and 24 the phrase בָּשָׂר חַי (basar chai, raw flesh) denotes the exposed living tissue of the sore and is certain evidence of an active affliction. The pair of ritual categories: tame', Hebrew טָמֵא, transliteration tame', unclean, Strong's number H2931; tahor, Hebrew טָהוֹר, transliteration tahor, clean, Strong's number H2889. Lexical basis: BDB and HALOT. The pronouncement formulae of Leviticus 13, the priest shall pronounce him clean or pronounce him unclean, use the causative declarative forms tiher (טִהֵר, H2891) and timme' (טִמֵּא, H2930), showing that the pronouncement is a speech act, not an act of treatment, on which the sermon's account of the priest's office rests.

10. The paradox of the body turned wholly white (13:12-13): the Hebrew text makes the affliction's covering of the skin, of the whole body from head to foot, its condition, and the priest's seeing that it has covered all the flesh its point of judgement, and pronounces cleanness; verses 14-17 then set reciprocal clauses for raw flesh appearing and turning white again. Milgrom favours the reading that the active course has passed, a uniform whiteness over all showing that the lesion is no longer locally active; Wenham observes that the passage shows the judgement to rest on ritual criteria rather than medical diagnosis, local ulceration and uniform coverage falling into two classes in ritual logic; Hartley cautions that the pronouncement is no guarantee of bodily healing. The three agree that the core of the judgement lies in the presence or absence of raw flesh and activity; the symbolic reading is one later line of interpretation among others. This sermon sets them side by side without a final verdict on this basis.

11. The vocabulary of the garment plague: at 13:47-59 the spot in a garment is called nega', and its colours are yeragrag (יְרַקְרַק, adjective of the H3422 family, yellowish-green, greenish) and adamdam (אֲדַמְדָּם, H125, reddish). Its corroding character is described by mam'eret (מַמְאֶרֶת, H3992, literally stinging, corroding), BDB, malignant; HALOT, malignant, corroding. The verbs of disposition are four grades: shutting up (hesgir), washing (kibbes, H3526), tearing out (qara', H7167, used of tearing away the plagued part), and burning (saraph, H8313), corresponding to the commands to burn at 13:52 and 55, on which the graded account in the body of the sermon rests.

12. Sources for the New Testament reading level: Matthew 8:2-4 and Mark 1:40-45 record Jesus cleansing a man with the affliction: Jesus puts forth his hand and touches him, says, I will, be thou clean, and charges him to show himself to the priest and offer the gift Moses commanded, for a testimony to them. Two points belong to the Gospel text itself: the touch, and the continued following of the examination and offering procedure of Leviticus 13-14 (compare the rite of cleansing at Lev 14:1-32). In homiletical use of this passage, it must be marked as a New Testament reading: it shows Jesus' authority and compassion transcending and fulfilling the old procedure, and does not rewrite the original meaning of Leviticus 13 within its Old Testament framework; nor do the ancient isolation ordinances thereby gain any direct application to modern public health or medical practice.

## Devotional Excerpt

Sometimes what I fear most is hiding myself. There is a place in my body or my heart that feels wrong, neither deep nor shallow, and I cannot name it, dare not show it, and only hope it will pass on its own. Leviticus 13 teaches me another way: bring the sore into the light, let the one appointed examine it, and if it is not yet clear, wait quietly for seven days, and then seven more. Waiting is not delay, and re-examination is not shame; to refuse both a hasty verdict and a pretence that nothing is wrong, that is honesty. The priest looked at depth; my Lord looks deeper still. He knows which place is only an old scar on the surface and which is spreading within. He does not decide my whole life by one appearance; He gives me time to be revealed, and grace to turn. And the paradox of the body turned wholly white reminds me not to fix my eyes on one white spot in self-pity: what matters is whether raw, restless flesh is still at work inside. Today I will bring out the hidden place and say to the Lord: examine me, and decide concerning me according to Your truth; wash what must be washed, let me wait for what must be waited for, and tear away what must be torn away. I am learning, too, to look at the brothers and sisters beside me with the same eyes: not to label a person for one failure, nor to push anyone outside the camp for one spot; to wait in love, to look again in truth, to walk with them through the days of shutting up, and to welcome with them the pronouncement of cleanness. And when You pronounce, I will return in peace among Your people, as one whom You have cleansed. Amen.

# He Beheld the Serpent of Brass, and Lived — A Sermon on Numbers 21

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Initial translation — pending proofreading (初译待校), unpublished
Text: Numbers chapter 21 (35 verses in full, in both the Chinese and the English numbering; the chapter and verse divisions of the Hebrew canon have been checked and agree with the Chinese and English in chapter and verse — see Endnote 1)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), taken from the anchor text of this chapter checked against two sources (sacred-texts and Bible Gateway); the companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (Simplified, "Shen" edition; hereafter "the Chinese base text"), checked verse by verse on 24 October 2026. The English text is not a back-translation of the Chinese quotations: every quotation below is KJV wording, and where the two base texts differ in wording, the difference is registered, not harmonized.
Title verification: The English title is taken from the very words of the accomplished event in verse 9 of the KJV: "And Moses made a serpent of brass, and put it upon a pole, and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived." The chapter has three movements — the victory at Hormah (21:1-3), the fiery serpents and the serpent of brass (21:4-9), and the fall of the two kings beyond the Jordan (21:21-35) — with the middle movement as its hinge: the first victory did not keep the people from speaking against God, and the second victory is marched out by a people who have been given life. The candidates were checked verse by verse: "shall live" (21:8) is the word spoken when God gave the command, when the thing made was still called a "fiery serpent" and the name "serpent of brass" had not yet appeared; it gathers the promise only, not the accomplishment. "Fear him not" (21:34) governs only the five verses of Og king of Bashan. "Utterly destroy / utterly destroyed" (21:2, 3) governs only the three verses of Hormah. "We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD, and against thee" (21:7) is only the people's one sentence of confession and cannot gather in the two victories. Verse 9 alone has the promise of 21:8 landing in it, with act and outcome both present, and it is also the scene the New Testament itself seizes when it reads this chapter back (John 3:14, New Testament reading layer). It is therefore the title. The Chinese title is taken from the same verse in its own base text; each manuscript follows its own base text.
Base-text notes (where the two base texts or common paraphrase differ, each base text is recorded as it stands): At 21:2 the KJV reads that Israel "vowed a vow unto the LORD"; the object destroyed is "their cities." At 21:4 the KJV reads "the soul of the people was much discouraged because of the way" — the discouragement is expressly tied to the way; the Chinese base text likewise makes the hardship of the way the stated cause. At 21:5 the KJV reads "the people spake against God, and against Moses," and the food is "this light bread." At 21:8 the thing God commands to be made is a "fiery serpent"; the name "serpent of brass" appears only at verse 9, when Moses makes it — the two verses have different work to do, and the name is not moved forward. At 21:14-15 the KJV's quotation from the book of the wars of the LORD runs "What he did in the Red sea, and in the brooks of Arnon, And at the stream of the brooks that goeth down to the dwelling of Ar, and lieth upon the border of Moab," where the Chinese base text transliterates the place-names in its own wording ("Vaheb in Suphah" and the slopes of the valleys of Arnon); each manuscript quotes its own text, and the divergence of these two verses is registered here, not harmonized. At 21:18 the KJV says the well was digged "by the direction of the lawgiver, with their staves," where the Chinese base text names the instruments themselves (the sceptre and the staves with which they dug); the fact layer of each manuscript follows its own wording. At 21:22 the messengers' words in the KJV are "we will go along by the king's high way" — the king's highway; the message to the king of Edom in chapter 20 uses the same phrase in the KJV, "we will go by the king's high way" (20:17), one phrase and one form in both chapters, and common paraphrase ("the main road") is not followed. At 21:28 the KJV reads "the lords of the high places of Arnon," where the Chinese base text reads "priests" and carries its own footnote, "the original reads 'lords'" — the KJV stands on the footnote's side of that register, and each manuscript keeps its own text. At 21:30 the KJV carries a further clause beyond the words quoted in the Chinese base text: "and we have laid them waste even unto Nophah, which reacheth unto Medeba." At 21:34 the KJV reads "Fear him not: for I have delivered him into thy hand." At 21:35 the KJV reads "until there was none left him alive" — none left him alive, in its own wording.
Form: The main text is divided into five parts, with verse ranges and time codes (00:00-08:00 / 08:00-24:00 / 24:00-42:00 / 42:00-56:00 / 56:00-60:00). Paragraphs are marked (Fact) / (Argument) / (Summary) / (New Testament Reading) / (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) to keep the layers distinct. Original-language words appear only in the (Summary) paragraphs as philological statements; material from other chapters enters only as cross-reference, with its layer marked, and is never read back into this chapter's fact layer.

## Summary

In chapter 20 Aaron dies on Mount Hor; in chapter 21 the company journeys on from Mount Hor. The chapter opens with a victory: the king of Arad comes out to fight and takes some prisoners; Israel vows a vow to the LORD, the LORD hearkens, and the place is called Hormah (21:1-3). The warmth of the victory does not carry far: on the way around Edom the people are much discouraged because of the way, they speak against God and against Moses, and they loathe the light bread; fiery serpents come among the people, and much people of Israel dies (21:4-6). The people come and confess their sin and ask Moses to pray; God does not take the serpents away, but commands Moses to set a fiery serpent upon a pole — and Moses makes a serpent of brass, and everyone who was bitten, when he beheld it, lived (21:7-9). After that the list of campings marches north (21:10-20); at Beer God gives water and the people sing. The last movement is two hard battles: Sihon refuses passage and comes out to fight, is defeated at Jahaz, and Israel possesses the land from Arnon to Jabbok and dwells in Heshbon (21:21-31); Og king of Bashan comes out to meet them at Edrei, God first says "Fear him not," Og is utterly defeated, and the land falls to Israel (21:32-35). The same company first dies by its own mouth, then lives by a look, and at last stands on land east of the Jordan.

## Main Text

### Part One: Hormah — the Vow and the First Victory (21:1-3) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) Verse 1, as the base text gives it: "And when king Arad the Canaanite, which dwelt in the south, heard tell that Israel came by the way of the spies; then he fought against Israel, and took some of them prisoners." (21:1) Three items registered: the enemy is the Canaanite king of Arad who dwelt in the south — this sermon registers the region as the base text gives it and fixes no site for the city; Israel is the party attacked, loses the first encounter, and has some of its people taken prisoner; the exact route of "the way of the spies" the text does not state — see Endnote 8. Verse 2 is the people's answer, quoted verbatim: "And Israel vowed a vow unto the LORD, and said, If thou wilt indeed deliver this people into my hand, then I will utterly destroy their cities." (21:2) The condition of the vow rests with God — "If thou wilt indeed deliver this people into my hand"; the people's pledge is to destroy the cities utterly. The speaker, as the base text gives it, is "Israel" as a whole, and the vow speaks in the singular, "my hand" — the whole people speaking as one body. Verse 3, as the base text gives it: "And the LORD hearkened to the voice of Israel, and delivered up the Canaanites; and they utterly destroyed them and their cities: and he called the name of the place Hormah." (21:3) The order is read as it stands: the LORD hearkens first, the delivering up follows, the destroying follows the delivering, and the place-name closes the account — Hormah, a name left as the memorial of this one battle.

(Argument) The first lesson of the chapter lies in the order of opening the mouth. After some of them had been taken prisoner, the people's first sentence is not a complaint but a vow — they open their mouth to God first, lay the condition before God, and only then set their hand to the work. In the wilderness the people's opening of the mouth to God is mostly an accusation that looks backward; this once, they open their mouth toward the battle ahead. The same mouth can vow and can speak against God: the vow is in this part, the speaking against God is in verse 5, one verse apart. After a victory, what a man must guard most is not his hand but his mouth — let the mouth slacken, and the next disaster enters by the mouth. That is the chapter's own arrangement.

(Summary) The words of this part are stated as philology only. The Hebrew of "Hormah" is ḥormâ, from the same root ḥ-r-m as the ḥērem behind "utterly destroy" in verses 2 and 3: the place-name and the battle share one root, and the place is named for the utter destruction. The numbers were supplied and checked in the review round: the verb ḥāram, "utterly destroy," is H2763, and the place-name Ḥormâ is H2767 (checked against BLB and the Xinyang Ai interlinear notes); the number of the noun ḥērem is still registered as pending verification — see Endnote 5. The Hebrew behind "vowed a vow" is nādar (H5087), to vow, to make a vow; it occurs in this chapter only here.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The geography of Arad and of the way of the spies has no settled conclusion in scholarship; it is set out here without adjudication. The chronological relation of verses 1-3 of this chapter to chapter 20 is also a matter of register: Aaron's death is recorded at Mount Hor at the end of chapter 20, and in verse 4 of this chapter the company journeys on from Mount Hor; one reading takes the events of these verses as contemporary with Aaron's death or prior to it, the material being arranged by subject; another reads in the literal order. The sermon text argues only from this chapter's own order; the chronological proposals are kept on record, not adjudicated.

(Fact) The place-name Hormah was already a scar in Israel's memory (cross-reference layer): in Numbers 14 the previous generation, refusing Moses' warning, went up presumptuously to the top of the mountain to fight, and were beaten back by the Amalekites and the Canaanites as far as Hormah (Num 14:45, key points). The same ground: the previous generation went up in its own heat of blood and was routed; this generation first vows and prays, and wins outright. Two battles lie thirty-eight years and one generation apart, and the text pins the place-name down in both places and lets the reader make the comparison himself: the ground did not change; what changed is the way men came before God. That the chapter opens at Hormah is no accident — the author first sets up a witness: the new generation is not condemned only to repeat the old failure; they have learned the thing the previous generation never learned, to pray before the sword is drawn. Register also the order of the opening battle: verse 1 first records prisoners taken — this victory does not begin with winning; it begins with losing an encounter and having men carried off; and the people neither scattered at the first defeat nor complained at it; in the losing position they vowed. The victory of Hormah is a victory obtained by crying out in defeat, not a victory of fair winds.

(Argument) This part gives today's hearers a second lesson: place-names speak. From now on Hormah stands on Israel's map: every time the march passes it, every time anyone speaks the name, what is remembered is two things — that the LORD hearkened to the people who vowed, and that the vow of utter destruction was aimed at the enemy, not at their own people. A man's life has place-names like that too: a place that makes you remember a rout, a house that makes you remember shame, a stretch of road you never want to travel again. This chapter's way of reading is: one and the same place can record defeat first and victory afterwards; God did not erase Hormah from the map — on the same ground He rewrote its meaning. You do not need to go around your life's Hormah; what you are to do is, on the same ground, change the way you come before God — vow first, cry out first, lay the condition before God first, and then watch how He turns the ground of defeat into the ground of victory. The place-name does not change; the memorial changes. That is the shape of redemption in geography.

### Part Two: Speaking Against God, the Fiery Serpents, and the Serpent of Brass — He Beheld, and Lived (21:4-9) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 4 is the turning point, as the base text gives it: "And they journeyed from mount Hor by the way of the Red sea, to compass the land of Edom: and the soul of the people was much discouraged because of the way." (21:4) The starting point, Mount Hor, is where Aaron died (20:28, cross-reference layer); Edom would not let them pass (20:18-21, cross-reference layer), and the company can only go the long way around. The base text names the cause of the discouragement expressly: because of the way. In verse 5 the discouragement becomes speech, quoted in full verbatim: "And the people spake against God, and against Moses, Wherefore have ye brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? for there is no bread, neither is there any water; and our soul loatheth this light bread." (21:5) Registered item by item: the object of the speaking is God and Moses — the mouth names Moses, but the account is charged to God; the accusation has three items: that the exodus was a bringing up to die, that there is no bread and no water, and that this light bread is loathed by their soul. What "this light bread" refers to — the manna — this verse itself does not name; it is identified by cross-reference with the narratives of Exodus 16 and Numbers 11 (cross-reference layer), and this sermon registers it so, without putting the word "manna" into the quotation of this verse. The bread is the same bread and the road the same road; the only thing that has changed is the hardship of the way — this time what they loathe is not absence, but the lightness of what they already have.

(Fact) Verse 6, as the base text gives it: "And the LORD sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people; and much people of Israel died." (21:6) The sequence nails the causality down: the serpents come after the speaking against God. Much people died — the number is not stated; see Endnote 8. In verse 7 the people come and confess, quoted in full verbatim: "Therefore the people came to Moses, and said, We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD, and against thee; pray unto the LORD, that he take away the serpents from us. And Moses prayed for the people." (21:7) What they confess is their speech: "We have sinned, for we have spoken against..." — they do not say the road was too hard or the bread too light; they say only that their own mouth sinned. Their request is also concrete: that he take away the serpents from us. Of Moses' prayer the base text records only that he "prayed for the people"; it does not record the words of the prayer. Verse 8 is God's answer, quoted in full verbatim: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live." (21:8) Registered item by item: God does not do as the people asked and take the serpents away — the serpents are still biting, and the healing opens another road; the thing to be made is called in the command a "fiery serpent," bearing the same name as the serpents that bite; the manner of setting it is upon a pole, lifted high, so that men can look upon it; the condition is "every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it," and the outcome is "shall live." Verse 9 is the accomplishment, quoted in full verbatim: "And Moses made a serpent of brass, and put it upon a pole, and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived." (21:9) The material is named here for the first time — brass — and the thing made is from now on called the serpent of brass; the promised "shall live" is now written as narrative, "he lived" — command and accomplishment differ by a word's turn, and this sermon records both as the base text gives them, without merging or rewriting either.

(Argument) The bone of this part is the way God answers prayer. What the people asked for was removal; what God gave was a lifting up. Removal clears the trouble away from before the eyes; lifting up sets the sign of deliverance in the midst of the trouble: the serpents still range the ground, the bites are still happening, and the bitten man lifts his head where he lies, and lives. Men at prayer mostly recognise only the first kind of answer — only when the surroundings have been cleared clean do they count God as having heard; this chapter sets up another kind. The shape of the way out also stings the eye: the thing that gives life is made, of all shapes, in the shape of the thing that bites — to live, a man must first lift his head and look straight at the thing that made him suffer. The confession "We have sinned" and the look upward are two faces of one act: no hiding, no going around — look straight at it.

(Summary) The words of this part are stated as philology only. The Hebrew for "serpent" is nāḥāš (H5175); for "brass" (copper/bronze) it is nəḥōšeṯ (H5178); the "serpent of brass" of verse 9 reads in sequence nəḥaš nəḥōšeṯ, the two words sharing the sound n-ḥ-š — a sound-correspondence present in the original itself, and this sermon registers the phonetic fact only. The śārāp̄ (H8314) behind "fiery serpent" is a word whose sense is bound up with burning; both the serpents that bite and the serpent that is made are called by this word. "Bit" is nāšak (H5391), close in sound to nāḥāš. The "pole" is nēs (H5251), in its base sense a standard-pole, a sign, so that the whole camp can see it. The "looketh upon it" of verse 8 rests on rāʾâ (H7200), to see in the general sense; verse 9 has hibbîṭ, from nābaṭ (H5027), to look intently, to fix the gaze — the verse of accomplishment changes to the heavier verb, and this sermon registers the original word of each verse separately. The Hebrew behind "spake against" is the intensive use of dābar (H1696), speaking words in attack; verses 5 and 7 both have this word, and in their confession the people use the very same word to point back at their own speech. "Sinned" rests on ḥāṭāʾ (H2398); "prayed" on pālal (H6419). Every number above was checked one by one before writing; the verb behind "live," ḥāyâ, is numbered H2421 (according to BLB; H2425 is a cognate entry under the same root, and this sermon follows H2421 — the difference is registered in Endnote 5).

(New Testament Reading) This chapter is read back in the New Testament at one place by the Lord Jesus Himself: in John 3 the Lord says to Nicodemus that as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life (John 3:14-15, key points) — the New Testament reads the "look" as "believe," and the object lifted up passes from the serpent of brass to the Son of man; at this chapter's fact layer only this chapter's own facts are registered: serpent, pole, a look, life. First Corinthians 10 sets the serpent plague down as a warning (1 Cor 10:9); the one reading gives warning, the other promise, and both are set out together in Endnote 6.

(Fact) The origin of the fiery serpents is registered one layer further (cross-reference layer): the wilderness had serpents all along. When Deuteronomy rehearses the forty years it says that God led Israel through that great and terrible wilderness, wherein were fiery serpents, and scorpions, and drought, where there was no water (Deut 8:15, key points) — the fiery serpents are not a new thing created for this chapter; they are a danger native to the wilderness; the punishment of this chapter is that this danger is sent "among the people." In other words, God's ordinary keeping had been holding the serpents outside the camp; after the people spoke against God, the serpents entered the camp. The text does not say that God dispatched new serpents; it says He "sent fiery serpents among the people": the shape of the punishment is the withdrawal of protection. This layer of the register matters: many punishments in Scripture are not hardships newly added by God, but the taking back of a protection that had been there all along — and a man does not feel the protection exists until the day it is withdrawn. The making of the serpent of brass is likewise registered as it stands: the maker is Moses himself (the subject of 21:9 expressly, "And Moses made..."), and of the source of the brass, the size of the serpent, and the form of the pole this chapter says nothing at all (for the blanks see Endnote 8); none of the keys of the remedy lies in the workmanship — they lie in the setting up and the looking up: high enough to be seen, visible, and that is enough.

(Fact) Set this chapter's speaking against God back into the genealogy of the whole book (cross-reference layer), and the inheritance of the syntax is plain at a glance: in Exodus 17 the previous generation strives for water at Rephidim, and the question is why they were brought up out of Egypt; in Numbers 14, after the spies return, the whole congregation says it wishes it had died in Egypt, or died in the wilderness; in Numbers 20 the new generation at Kadesh replays the same set of accusations; and at verse 5 of this chapter it sounds a fourth time — still the exodus, still dying in the wilderness, still no water. Four accusations across forty years and two generations, their wording almost unchanged — the only change is the one new item: formerly they complained of absence; in this chapter they complain of lightness. The genealogy is registered here to make one point: speaking against God is not one generation's patent, nor an improvisation forced out of men by circumstances; it is a syntax that gets handed down. The way parents explain suffering at the table is the way children will explain a hard road in the wilderness. The good news of the Hormah part is therefore the heavier for it: a syntax can be handed down, and the order of the vow can be handed down too — this chapter records both, and which of the two is handed down is each generation's own choice.

(Argument) This part must also preach Moses' intercession through to the bottom. Hidden inside the people's plea for prayer is one unspoken premise: they knew Moses would pray. Across forty years they had abused him, surrounded him, taken up stones against him — yet when disaster struck, the first thing they thought of was still "pray unto the LORD" through him: the man spoken against is, in the people's hearts, at the same time the intercessor, and that in itself is the hardest witness of Moses' whole life. Nor is there any condition attached to Moses' prayer: he does not require the apology to be finished first, does not require the words to be taken back first; the base text records only "And Moses prayed for the people" — the sequence is crisp: one sentence is the confession, the next sentence is the intercession, with no bargaining in between. For those who lead in church and family today, this part is a mirror: your first reaction after you have been hurt decides whether the people under you will dare to come to you in their trouble. If a man bears grudges, the people will not come to confess; if the people do not come to confess, the serpent of brass has no way to be lifted up. Because Moses was willing to pray, the remedy came to the whole camp — intercession is not a leader's spare-time programme; it is the channel by which the whole camp is saved.

(New Testament Reading) First Corinthians 10 reads this part back from the other end, its pivot sentence in key points: neither let us tempt Christ, as some of them also tempted, and were destroyed of serpents (1 Cor 10:9, key points). Paul sets the disaster of the serpents up as a warning: speaking against God is not a small temper; it is temptation. One and the same event — John's Gospel reads hope out of it, First Corinthians reads warning — one speaks of the look, the other of the sin, and the two readings set side by side are the whole of this chapter as the New Testament holds it. There is also a later historical echo (historical layer): in 2 Kings 18, at Hezekiah's reform, he brake in pieces the brasen serpent that Moses had made, because to those days the children of Israel burned incense to it, and he called it Nehushtan (2 Kings 18:4, key points). The instrument that had saved the whole camp had, some centuries later, become an idol, and was broken by a godly king — God never told men to worship the serpent of brass; He told men to look to the One to whom the serpent of brass pointed. An instrument may be remembered; it may not be worshipped. An experience may be told; it may not be enshrined. Every instrument and method in the church of which men say "how God used this in those days" must keep this boundary: what points to Him, keep; what replaces Him, break.

### Part Three: From Oboth to Beer — the List on the Road and the Song by the Well (21:10-20) (24:00-42:00)

(Fact) After the serpent of brass the narrative changes into an itinerary, registered station by station as the base text gives it: they set forward and pitched in Oboth (21:10); then to Ijeabarim, in the wilderness which is before Moab, toward the sunrising (21:11); they pitched in the valley of Zared (21:12); then to the other side of Arnon — and here the base text pauses to state the geography: "From thence they removed, and pitched on the other side of Arnon, which is in the wilderness that cometh out of the coasts of the Amorites: for Arnon is the border of Moab, between Moab and the Amorites." (21:13) The Arnon is the geographical axis of the second half of this chapter: beyond its north bank are the men the next part has to deal with. Verses 14-15 quote a piece of older writing, as the base text gives it: "Wherefore it is said in the book of the wars of the LORD, What he did in the Red sea, and in the brooks of Arnon, And at the stream of the brooks that goeth down to the dwelling of Ar, and lieth upon the border of Moab." (21:14-15) The book quoted is named "the book of the wars of the LORD"; apart from this quotation it is found nowhere else — see Endnote 8. In verse 16 they come to Beer, and the base text states plainly where this well comes from: "And from thence they went to Beer: that is the well whereof the LORD spake unto Moses, Gather the people together, and I will give them water." (21:16) Verses 17-18 are the song, its beginning and end as the base text gives them: "Then Israel sang this song, Spring up, O well; sing ye unto it: The princes digged the well, the nobles of the people digged it, by the direction of the lawgiver, with their staves." (21:17-18) There the song ends, and the itinerary runs on: from the wilderness to Mattanah, to Nahaliel, to Bamoth, into the valley in the country of Moab, to the top of Pisgah, which looketh toward Jeshimon (21:18-20).

(Argument) Set chapter 20 beside this part and read, and the quiet of this part sounds all the louder. That chapter too had no water; what came out of the mouth was strife, and the rod struck the rock. In the itinerary of this part not one sentence about water is recorded as spoken by the people — God speaks first, "I will give them water"; the water arrives, and the people sing. The same need, two kinds of sound: one time complaining first and drinking afterwards, one time receiving first and singing afterwards. The singers are the very people of verse 5: the sound of the speaking against God had scarcely fallen, a few stations on, when the singing rose. The text does not cover up the first sound for them, nor cancel the second because of the first — the song is not sung for the ears of the blameless; it is sung by men who have just been bitten and are still alive.

(Summary) The words of this part are stated as philology only. The Hebrew of "Beer" is bəʾēr, whose base sense is simply "well"; the place-name and the thing share one name, and this sermon attaches no number to it. The designations in the quoted poem ("Vaheb," "Suphah" in the Chinese base text's transliteration) have no settled identification; this sermon records only the base text's wording. The sceptre and the staves standing side by side are the marks of identity of the men who digged the well; this sermon registers only the base text's words.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The two quotations of this chapter — the book of the wars of the LORD here, and the song of "they that speak in proverbs" in the next part (21:27-30) — show the narrative taking up older materials of war and song; their sources and dates are much discussed in scholarship, set out here without adjudication. This sermon establishes only the name of the book and the source of the song as the base text expressly records them.

(Argument) Hidden in the song of the well is this chapter's tenderest contrast, and it is in an instrument: the staff. In chapter 20 the staff is the rod that smote the rock — one blow down, the water came out, and the judgment came down with it. In the song of the well in this chapter the staves are digging-staves — "The princes digged the well, the nobles of the people digged it, by the direction of the lawgiver, with their staves" (21:18): the princes take the marks of authority in hand and do not strike the rock but dig the soil of the well, one staff-stroke at a time bringing the water out, while the people stand around and sing. The same staff: once used in anger, once used in labour; once bringing out water with loss attached, once bringing out water with song attached. The instrument of authority itself has not changed; what has changed is the heart and the handling of the man who holds the staff. Men who hold authority today — a parent's authority, a manager's authority, a pastor's authority — choose between these two handlings every day: striking, or digging? Striking works fast: one blow and the water is out. Digging works slowly: it needs digging and waiting. But on which side does the text record the singing? On the digging side. The water that was struck out — those who drank it did not sing. The well that was digged out — those who digged it sang first. When was your staff last used: to strike, or to dig?

(Fact) One further layer is registered on the itinerary: the direction of the ten stations is from south to north, from the edge of the wilderness toward the eastern border of the promised land (for the route item by item see Endnote 7). In the list there is not one complaint and not one accident; the text reports station-names only — and that plainness is itself information: after the serpent of brass the company enters, for the first time, a stretch of road on which "nothing happens." In wilderness narrative the passages where nothing happens are the shortest and the most precious: they prove that this company is not capable of existing only in crisis; they can also walk quietly, pitch, and set forward. The spiritual record of a man's life is mostly the same: what gets written large is always crisis and victory, while the plain, uneventful days are carried past in a single stroke; yet what truly nourishes a man is exactly days like those from Oboth to Beer — setting out on time, pitching on time, and at the water's edge, singing a song.

(Argument) One layer more: the pairing of station-names and song. Of the ten station-names, Beer alone has left a song behind — not because that station's scenery was the finest, but because at that station the water came in the right order: God spoke first, and men sang afterwards; when the order is right, the song comes out by itself. It is the same in the life of the church: worship is not a product of emotion but a product of order — first hear what God says, first receive what God gives, and the singing will come of itself; reverse the order, sing first and hunt for feeling afterwards, and however loud it is, it is dry. The song of Beer is only a little over two lines, yet the text gathers the whole of it into the war-history: the songs God values are not measured by length, but by whether they were sung out of receiving.

### Part Four: Sihon Refuses the Way, and the Song of Heshbon (21:21-30) (42:00-56:00)

(Fact) In verses 21-22 Israel sends messengers to Sihon king of the Amorites, and the messengers' words are quoted verbatim as the base text gives them: "Let me pass through thy land: we will not turn into the fields, or into the vineyards; we will not drink of the waters of the well: but we will go along by the king's high way, until we be past thy borders." (21:22) The same form as the words sent to the king of Edom in chapter 20, and the same "king's high way" (20:17, cross-reference layer); the two letters are nearly identical — what differs is what follows: Edom refuses the way, and Israel goes around; Sihon refuses the way, and gathers all his people together and comes out to fight. In verse 23 Sihon's answer is not a sentence but a marching out: "And Sihon would not suffer Israel to pass through his border: but Sihon gathered all his people together, and went out against Israel into the wilderness: and he came to Jahaz, and fought against Israel." (21:23) Verse 24 is the outcome of the battle, quoted verbatim: "And Israel smote him with the edge of the sword, and possessed his land from Arnon unto Jabbok, even unto the children of Ammon: for the border of the children of Ammon was strong." (21:24) The boundaries of the land possessed, registered item by item: from Arnon in the south, to Jabbok in the north, eastward as far as the border of the children of Ammon — and the reason for stopping is stated by the base text itself: that border was strong; they did not push on with the momentum. In verse 25 they dwell in the cities they have taken: "in Heshbon, and in all the villages thereof" (21:25, key points). Verse 26 supplies the old account, as the base text gives it: "For Heshbon was the city of Sihon the king of the Amorites, who had fought against the former king of Moab, and taken all his land out of his hand, even unto Arnon." (21:26) Heshbon was originally the land of the former king of Moab; Sihon took it from him, and Israel now takes it from Sihon's hand — the two layers of its history set side by side, registered as the base text gives them.

(Fact) Verses 27-30 are the second song, its source given by the base text as "they that speak in proverbs say" (21:27). The first half of the song celebrates Sihon's old victory, excerpted as the base text gives it: "For there is a fire gone out of Heshbon, a flame from the city of Sihon: it hath consumed Ar of Moab, and the lords of the high places of Arnon. Woe to thee, Moab! thou art undone, O people of Chemosh: he hath given his sons that escaped, and his daughters, into captivity unto Sihon king of the Amorites." (21:28-29) This fire once burned Moab; now Sihon, who set it, has himself fallen. In verse 30 the voice turns sharply, as the base text gives it: "We have shot at them; Heshbon is perished even unto Dibon." (21:30) Who this "we" is, the base text does not name — see Endnote 8. A song celebrating Sihon's triumph, gathered into Israel's war-history, has become the footnote to Sihon's fall — the song is still there; the man it pointed to is no longer there.

(Argument) This part first establishes the messengers' measure, then the sword's measure. The messengers' words concede everything that can be conceded: no turning aside, no drinking of water, only along the king's highway, straight through and past the borders — an asking both firm in its right and clean in its conduct; the battle is one the other side starts, gathering all his people and coming out into the wilderness to fight. After the land is possessed, the measure stands at the boundary: they stop at the border of the children of Ammon, because that border was strong — the text does not write this stopping as cowardice, nor as a new command; it simply records border and strength side by side. At the height of momentum the hand is hardest to stop: not every piece of ground that can be pushed is to be pushed; a visible boundary is sometimes the measure itself.

(Summary) The words of this part are stated as philology only. The original forms of the kings and places are recorded as they stand: Sihon sîḥôn, Heshbon ḥešbôn, Arnon ʾarnôn, Jabbok yabbōq, Jahaz yahṣâ, Chemosh kəmôš (the name of Moab's god); this sermon records the forms only and attaches no numbers. The original of "the king's high way" reads in sequence derek hammelek, whose base sense is precisely "the king's way"; chapter 20 has the same word in the same rendering, "the king's high way" (20:17) — the two chapters agree, registered as the base text gives them.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The original author of the song of Heshbon and the referent of the "we" of verse 30 (the speakers in proverbs, Israel, or Sihon's old company) are read differently in scholarship, set out here without adjudication; the sermon text establishes only the base text's source-sentence, "they that speak in proverbs say," and the text of the song, and does not claim an author for the song. The boundaries of Sihon's kingdom and the identification of the site of Jahaz likewise belong to the discussion layer.

(Argument) The boundary of the land possessed, preached one layer deeper. The border of the children of Ammon was strong, and Israel stopped — and of this stopping the text records no new command from God and no murmuring from the people; it records only border and strength set side by side. Deuteronomy later supplies what lies behind it: God had commanded that they were not to meddle with the children of Ammon nor contend with them (Deut 2:19, cross-reference layer): this chapter's stopping accords exactly with that command; this chapter simply does not say so. Stopping in the full tide of victory is the hardest lesson to learn: the sword is sharpest, the ground most open, the men most together, and the reason for stopping often lies not before the eyes but in God's timing. Men today, expanding a business, advancing a plan, managing a relationship, need this same sense of an Ammonite boundary: that a thing can be pushed does not mean it should be pushed; that a thing can be seen does not mean it is to be had now. Moses' measure in this chapter stands in exact contrast to the lesson of chapter 20 — there the hand reached too far; here the hand knows how to be held in. The same pair of hands learns both lessons within a year: do not strike the rock that is not to be struck, and do not push the boundary that is not to be pushed.

(Argument) This chapter has two "fires," and read together they are a hidden thread the author leaves behind (reading layer): one is the fiery serpents of verse 6, fire among the people, which is punishment; the other is the fire of verse 28 that went out of Heshbon — "For there is a fire gone out of Heshbon, a flame from the city of Sihon" — fire upon the enemy's city, which once burned Moab and now burns back upon the man who set it. Besides the fire of punishment and the fire of battle, Scripture also has the fire of the altar, the fire of the bush, the fire of the prophets — fire itself is neither good nor evil; upon whom it falls, and out of whose hand it comes, determines its nature. The two fires of this chapter warn a man on both sides: do not play with fire by speaking against God, and do not fear the enemy's fire; the same fire God can use to purge His own people, and can use to judge the king who resists Him. Sihon's lifetime pride was that fire that went out of Heshbon; he never imagined that when his own turn came, the text would not even trouble to write him a new obituary, but would borrow his own song of praise as the testimony — what a man boasts in, judgment takes as its evidence.

(Fact) Between the two victories, the comparison of the messengers' words is now gathered and closed (cross-reference layer): the two letters of chapter 20 and of this chapter share one form and one wording, the same "king's high way," the same promise not to turn aside and not to drink water; the king of Edom answers with refusal plus a threat of marching out, and Israel goes around; Sihon answers by gathering all his people and coming out into the wilderness to fight, and Israel meets him in battle and possesses the land. One and the same letter, written with measure, two outcomes — the difference lies not in the letter but in the man who receives it, and not in Israel's attitude but in God's differing arrangement for the two roads: on one road He tells them to turn aside; on the other He tells them to possess the land. Preaching this chapter, no one may set up "meet in battle and possess the land" as a general rule, just as preaching chapter 20 no one may set up "turn aside" as a general rule; read the two chapters together and there is only one rule: let the measure be fully kept first, and let the direction be heard from God.

### Part Five: Og King of Bashan — Fear Him Not (21:31-35) (56:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 31 gathers up the dwelling: "Thus Israel dwelt in the land of the Amorites." (21:31) In verse 32 they advance one station further north, as the base text gives it: "And Moses sent to spy out Jaazer, and they took the villages thereof, and drove out the Amorites that were there." (21:32) In verse 33 the company turns and goes up by the way of Bashan, and Og king of Bashan comes out of his own accord to meet them in battle: "And they turned and went up by the way of Bashan: and Og the king of Bashan went out against them, he, and all his people, to the battle at Edrei." (21:33) Before the battle opens, God speaks first — this is the second time in this chapter that God speaks in direct discourse (the first was at 21:8) — quoted in full verbatim: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Fear him not: for I have delivered him into thy hand, and all his people, and his land; and thou shalt do to him as thou didst unto Sihon king of the Amorites, which dwelt at Heshbon." (21:34) Registered item by item: the prohibition is "Fear him not," and the ground of it is that the delivering is already accomplished — "I have delivered him into thy hand": the battle is not yet fought and the account is already settled; the manner of fighting is given no new ordinance, only a pointing back to the last battle: "as thou didst unto Sihon." Verse 35 is the execution, quoted verbatim: "So they smote him, and his sons, and all his people, until there was none left him alive: and they possessed his land." (21:35) The whole chapter closes on one sentence of possessing land: from Hormah in the south to Bashan, the land from the Arnon northward lies in one piece — the first whole tract of land on which they dwell before entering Canaan.

(Argument) Half the weight of "Fear him not" lies below the line and half above it. God does not say that Og is not strong, nor that Edrei will be an easy battle; He simply sets the Sihon who has just been defeated before Moses' eyes: as thou didst unto Sihon. Yesterday's victory, in God's hand, is the textbook of today's faith — the thing He tells Moses to look back at is not his own courage but the record of the delivering: Sihon was delivered by Him, and Og is delivered by Him too. The people who in verse 4 were much discouraged because of the way, by the end of this chapter hold in their hands the memory of two victories. The road has not grown shorter; the people walking it have gained one thing more: a record to look back on. The next time the road is hard again, the method this chapter gives is not grim endurance but first counting backwards: Hormah, the serpent of brass, Beer, Sihon — and then lifting the head to look at the Og ahead.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Og's stature and the giant tradition of Bashan are rehearsed again in Deuteronomy 3 (Deut 3:11, cross-reference layer); this chapter says not one word of it, and no other chapter is used to fill this chapter's blank — see Endnote 8. The two battles of Sihon and Og are sung back side by side in the Psalms (Ps 135:10-11; 136:19-22, reading layer); how the land of the two kings was afterwards divided is the narrative of chapter 32 (cross-reference layer); this chapter records only the possessing and the dwelling.

(Fact) At the very end of the chapter the geographical account is gathered and closed: at the southern end, the one battle of Hormah, a victory in the south (21:1-3); in the middle stretch, the company goes around Edom, turns north from Mount Hor, and pitches along the line from Oboth to Pisgah (21:4-20); at the northern end, the land of Sihon from Arnon to Jabbok and the land of Og in Bashan pass in turn into Israel's hands (21:21-35). Within one chapter the company's position is wholly reversed: at the opening they are a company whose mourning has just stopped, with some of their people carried off by another; at the close they dwell on a whole tract of land they have possessed — this is the first time since the exodus that Israel halts on a piece of land in the posture of "dwelling" rather than "passing through." In chapter 22 they will pitch in the plains of Moab, with the promised land in sight across the river (cross-reference layer): chapter 21 is that doorway — only a people first given life can walk as far as the river; only a people that has learned to look back will dare to cross the river.

(Argument) The chapter closes by leaving one sentence for each of five kinds of hearer. First, for the man who has just lost a battle: the victory of Hormah begins with some people carried off as prisoners — losing first is not the final verdict; the man who vows in defeat, God hearkens to. Second, for the man who has grown tired of eating grace: the phrase "this light bread" first confesses half your sin for you — what has gone stale is not the bread but the heart; changing the bread cannot cure it; confessing the sin can. Third, for the man who has been bitten and is waiting for his circumstances to change: God may not first take your serpents away; He first lifts up His sign; do not lie on the ground waiting for the serpents to leave — lift your head first. Fourth, for those who lead: in this chapter Moses twice saves the whole camp — once by interceding, once by lifting up the serpent; a leader's work is half kneeling and half lifting, and neither of the two is striking. Fifth, for the man standing before a great enemy: the word God gives Moses is not an analysis of how strong Og is; it is a command to look back at Sihon — yesterday's record of delivering is today's reason for not fearing; count first the Hormah, the serpent of brass, the Beer, and the Sihon in your hand, and then go and face your Og.

## Devotional Excerpt

The people asked that the serpents be taken away; God gave them a serpent of brass lifted up instead. The serpents did not leave, the bites were still happening, and yet the road to life stood in the middle of the camp: if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived. The bitten man could do nothing at that moment — he could not walk, could not offer sacrifice, could hardly crawl to the door of the tent; all he had left was a pair of eyes. Moses did not tell him first to heal his wound, first to complete the procedures of cleansing, first to take back the words of complaint one sentence at a time — there was no time for any of that; the venom does not wait for men. This one look costs almost no strength; the only hard part is whether a man is willing to lift his head and turn his face toward it. The people's confession is also only one sentence: "We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD, and against thee" (21:7) — what they confess is their own mouth, with no excuse made of the hard road or the light bread; and once the words of confession are spoken, the eyes are lifted. God pressed the condition of life down this low: one look. As low as it can go, and yet as straight as it can go: the thing to be looked at is, of all things, shaped like the thing that bit him; the man who turns his face away cannot save himself. That thing in your heart today that you are unwilling to face squarely may be exactly the place where you are to lift your head. Do not first ask for the surroundings to be cleared clean; look first toward the sign God has given — His way is so often not to take away, but to lift up.

English (KJV-based):

The people prayed that the serpents be taken away; God instead lifted one up. "And Moses made a serpent of brass, and put it upon a pole, and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived." (Num 21:9, KJV) The bitten man could do nothing — he could not walk, sacrifice, or crawl to the tent. He had only his eyes left, and God set the price of life at a look. The thing he had to face was shaped like the thing that bit him. Do not first ask for the ground to be cleared; look where God has raised His sign.

## Summary

Within one chapter Moses sets out the whole order of faith: first hear (vv. 1–4), then love (v. 5), then keep the words in the heart and teach them to the children (vv. 6–9), then beware in the day of satiety (vv. 10–19), and at last have the answer ready when a son comes to ask (vv. 20–25). Deuteronomy 5 restated the Ten Words; chapter 6, following immediately, sets down the general outline of the whole faith — because He is the one LORD, the love owed to Him is with all the heart, all the soul and all the might; and that love must live in the heart, in the house and on the gates, stand firm in the day of satiety, and be able to answer the next generation. Hear, love, transmit, keep, answer — when these five steps are walked, faith has landed.

## Main Text

Introduction (00:00–05:00). (Fact) Deuteronomy 5 restated the Ten Words; chapter 6, following immediately, sets down the general outline of the whole faith. The chapter has twenty-five verses, and its structure is registered first: introduction and general outline, verses 1–3 (3 verses); the Shema and the commandment to love God, verses 4–5 (2 verses); the internalizing of the words and their transmission, verses 6–9 (4 verses); the warning against satiety, verses 10–19 (10 verses); the son's question and the father's answer, verses 20–25 (6 verses) — twenty-five verses in all (Endnote 1). This chapter's place in the Jewish tradition is without parallel: verses 4–9 are the first paragraph of the Shema, recited morning and evening every day (tradition registered, discussion layer); and Jesus Himself fixed verses 4–5 as "the first and great commandment" (Matt 22:38, registered at the New Testament Reading layer). (Argument) The relation of chapter 5 to chapter 6 is the relation of the text to the heart: chapter 5 says what must not be done and what must be done; chapter 6 says why all of it hangs on one God and one love. Without chapter 6 the Ten Words are a code; with chapter 6 they are the reply to a love letter — first "The LORD our God is one LORD," and only then "thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." (Summary) The governing sentence of this chapter can be put like this: within one chapter Moses sets out the whole order of faith — first hear (vv. 1–4), then love (v. 5), then keep the words in the heart and teach them to the children (vv. 6–9), then beware in satiety (vv. 10–19), and at last have the answer ready, waiting for a son to ask (vv. 20–25). Hear, love, transmit, keep, answer — when these five steps are walked, faith has landed.

### Part One: Hear, and Diligently Keep and Do (6:1–3) (05:00–13:00)

(Fact) Verse 1 is the doorplate of the whole chapter, quoted in full from the base text: "Now these are the commandments, the statutes, and the judgments, which the LORD your God commanded to teach you, that ye might do them in the land whither ye go to possess it:" (6:1) Registered item by item: first, the three names in a row — commandments (miṣwâ), statutes (ḥuqqîm), judgments (mišpāṭîm) — are the general name for the body of the law in the rest of the book (chapters 6–26) after the Ten Words of chapter 5; within this chapter this group of three names has two further forms (vv. 17, 20), the three forms each different, the account at Endnote 3. Second, "commanded to teach" joins two verbs: ṣāwâ (to command) plus the piel of lāmad (to teach) — God commands Moses, Moses teaches the people; the chain of transmission is in the same form as at 5:31. Third, the stage-word of this verse is "the land whither ye go to possess it": ʿābar (to pass over) plus yāraš (to possess) — the law is not spoken for hearing in the wilderness; it is spoken for doing after entering the land. (Fact) Verse 2 gives the purpose of the doing, quoted in full: "That thou mightest fear the LORD thy God, to keep all his statutes and his commandments, which I command thee, thou, and thy son, and thy son's son, all the days of thy life; and that thy days may be prolonged." (6:2) Registered item by item: first, the purpose chain has three links — fear (yārēʾ), keep (šāmar), days prolonged (ʾārak yāmîm). Second, the range of the hearers is "thou, and thy son, and thy son's son," three generations — the chapter's consciousness of transmission appears already at the second verse (in intertextual relation with the children's question and answer at vv. 7, 20). Third, the Chinese base text writes "statutes and commandments" together without an enumeration comma at this verse, itemized on record in its headnote (ii). (Fact) Verse 3 is the pre-call before the Shema, quoted in full: "Hear therefore, O Israel, and observe to do it; that it may be well with thee, and that ye may increase mightily, as the LORD God of thy fathers hath promised thee, in the land that floweth with milk and honey." (6:3) Registered item by item: first, "Hear therefore, O Israel" (šāmaʿ) — the Shema's proper sentence stands at verse 4; here the call comes once first: hearing, in this chapter, is a call given twice (Endnote 8). Second, "observe to do" is the pair šāmar plus ʿāśâ, in the same form as at 5:1, 32 (Endnote 4 of that manuscript); in this chapter it occurs twice, at verses 3 and 25. Third, "floweth with milk and honey" in the Hebrew is zāḇat ḥālāḇ ûḏəḇaš (flowing milk and honey), the fixed designation of the promised land. Fourth, the verb of the promise is the piel of dābar (to speak / promise) — what God has "spoken" is the promise; this verse answers, head to tail, to verse 19, "as the LORD hath spoken." (Argument) The inferences of these three verses: first, the order of the law is grace before law — verse 1 says the land is to be "possessed" (received freely), and verses 2–3 say blessing is the fruit of keeping the covenant: the commandments were never the price paid for the land; they are the way of life after moving into the land. Second, "hear" in this chapter is not the receiving of information but the posture of a relationship: the one who hears is to "observe to do"; hearing without doing, in this chapter's vocabulary, is not finished hearing (in intertextual relation with the people's own answer at 5:27, "we will hear it, and do it," registered as a reported summary). Third, the three-generation purpose sentence (v. 2) is a reminder: the unit of faith has never been the individual; it is the household — when Moses expounds the law, his eyes are on the children and grandchildren behind the hearers. (New Testament Reading) This part's motifs as echoed in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: James 1, "be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only" (registered as a reported summary), is in the same form as this part's hear–do chain; Hebrews 2, exhorting its readers to give the more earnest heed to the things heard, lest at any time they should let them slip (registered as a reported summary), stands in intertextual relation with the warning side of "hear." All are registered at the reading layer only. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) One matter set side by side for this part: the agreements and differences among the three names at verse 1 (commandments, statutes, judgments), the two names at 5:1 (statutes, judgments) and the three names at 5:31 (commandments, statutes, judgments) — the readings are set side by side: one reading takes the three names as a taxonomy of the law's literature (commandment as the general term, statutes as the cultic, judgments as the civil), their combination varying with the context; another takes the three names as a rhetorical piling-up of Deuteronomy's address style, not a strict classification, its evidence being that the order of the three forms varies within this very chapter (Endnote 3). Registered only, without adjudication. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: the opening three verses make three things clear first — by whom these words were commanded (the LORD your God), for whom they were prepared (thou, and thy son, and thy son's son), and where they are to be done (in the land whither ye go to possess it). The doorplate hung, the main hall can be entered: Hear, O Israel.

### Part Two: The Shema — One LORD, the Whole Person's Love (6:4–5) (13:00–24:00)

(Fact) Verse 4 is the crown of the whole chapter, quoted in full from the base text: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD:" (6:4) Registered item by item: first, the whole sentence in the Hebrew has only six words — Šəmaʿ Yiśrāʾēl, YHWH ʾĕlōhênû, YHWH ʾeḥād — call, name, assertion: three segments made into one sentence. Second, the Chinese base text closes "Hear, O Israel!" with an exclamation mark — the tone of the call stands in its punctuation; the KJV closes the call with a colon (see Base-text notes (i)). Third, "one" is ʾeḥād (H259, one); the Hebrew of this verse has no copula, and the Chinese base text's "is one LORD" is its supplied construction, the several syntactic readings being set down in the discussion layer. Fourth, this verse is the opening sentence of the Jewish congregation's daily recitation; in its scroll copies the final letter of "Hear" (šāmaʿ) and the final letter of "one" (ʾeḥād) are written large, the tradition registered in the discussion layer. (Fact) Verse 5 is the first and great commandment, quoted in full: "And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." (6:5) Registered item by item: first, the verb is the consecutive form of ʾāhaḇ (to love) — love, in this chapter, is not a suggestion about feeling; it is the verb of a commandment, joined directly to the assertion of verse 4: because He is one, the love must be of the whole person. Second, the threefold "all": in the Hebrew bəḵol-ləḇābekā (with all thine heart, lēḇāḇ), ûḇəḵol-nap̄šəkā (with all thy soul, nepeš), ûḇəḵol-məʾōḏekā (with all thy might, məʾōḏ) — "all" (kol) occurs three times, each of the three nouns furnished with an "all," leaving no remainder. Third, the base meaning of məʾōḏ is "very much / exceedingly"; here, made a noun, it denotes the whole of a person's strength and substance, itemized on record in the Chinese headnote (iv). Fourth, lēḇāḇ in biblical usage does not denote feeling alone; it denotes the seat of the will and the thoughts; nepeš denotes the whole living existence itself — the three words do not cut a person into three pieces; they say the same "all" from three directions. (Argument) The inferences of these two verses: first, the order cannot be reversed — first verse 4's "who He is," and only then verse 5's "what thou shalt do": monotheism is not a philosophical proposition; it is the ground of love; if He were not one, whole heart and whole might would not be logical. Second, "love" and "fear" in this chapter run side by side without contradiction: verses 2, 13, 24 speak of fear, this verse of love — fear guards against presumption, love against distance; Moses sets the two postures in one chapter, and the lack of either makes the other lopsided. Third, the threefold "all" measures the unit of love: the measure of love for God is not degree (love a little more) but range (all the heart, all the soul, all the might) — a love that keeps a remainder has nowhere to stand in this verse's syntax. (New Testament Reading) This part's citations in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only, and the densest place of New Testament echo in the whole chapter: in Matthew 22, Jesus, answering the lawyer's question which is the great commandment, cites verse 5 as "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind," and asserts: "This is the first and great commandment" and "On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets" (Matt 22:37–40, checked directly on their verse pages in the Chinese manuscript) — the New Testament reads məʾōḏ as "mind" (dianoia), registered on record; in Mark 12, Jesus Himself cites verses 4–5: "The first of all the commandments is, Hear, O Israel; The Lord our God is one Lord: And thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind, and with all thy strength: this is the first commandment" — the fourfold "all" set in a row (Mark 12:29–30, checked directly), and the scribe, repeating it, says "with all the heart, and with all the understanding, and with all the soul, and with all the strength" (12:33, checked directly); in Luke 10 the lawyer's own answer runs "with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy strength, and with all thy mind," the fourfold form, joined to the love of neighbour from Leviticus 19:18 (Luke 10:27, checked directly). The comparative account of the three records is at Endnote 10, registered only, without harmonizing. That the Lord Jesus twice Himself took this part as the head of the commandments (Matt 22, Mark 12) is registered as a fact of the text. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Three matters set side by side. First, the four readings of the syntax of verse 4: the Hebrew sets two subject-predicate groups side by side (YHWH ʾĕlōhênû / YHWH ʾeḥād) with no copula, and the readings are set side by side — (a) "The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (asserting His oneness); (b) "The LORD is our God, the LORD is one" (first confessing the relation, then asserting the oneness); (c) "The LORD our God, the LORD alone" (asserting His exclusiveness, ʾeḥād read as "the one and only"); (d) the Chinese Union Version's form, "The LORD our God is one LORD" (the two groups merged into one assertion, the form the KJV likewise carries). The four readings do not differ in their direction toward "one God" and "exclusive worship"; they differ in the centre of gravity of the assertion. This manuscript registers them only and does not adjudicate. Second, a registration of the scribal tradition: in Torah scrolls the final letter of "Hear" in this verse, ʿayin, and the final letter of "one," dālet, are written large, and the two letters read together make ʿēḏ (witness) — the scribal tradition marks this sentence by this means as the confession-sentence of the faith; this is a registration of a fact of the copies and of the traditional explanation, not an assertion about the text's original meaning. Third, the readings of məʾōḏ, set side by side: one reads it as strength (the Chinese base text's "might / force"; the KJV's "might"); one reads it as the noun form of "very much," denoting the whole of a person's substance (rabbinic tradition has the reading "with all thy wealth," registered as a traditional reading); the New Testament reads it as mind (dianoia). The three readings are set side by side; all point to "all"; there is no adjudication. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: the two shortest verses of the chapter are its weightiest words — an assertion in six words, and an answer in a threefold "all": because He is one, thy heart cannot be divided; because He is thy God, thy love can keep no remainder. The twenty verses that follow are all the unfolding of these two.


### Part Three: In Thine Heart — Teach Thy Children, Bind on the Hand, Write on the Posts (6:6–9) (24:00–34:00)

(Fact) Verse 6, quoted in full from the base text: "And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart:" (6:6) Registered item by item: first, "this day" (hayyôm) — in this chapter it occurs only twice (vv. 6, 24; Endnote 8): the words are commanded this day, and the life preserved is "as it is at this day" (v. 24); the two "this day"s lock head to tail. Second, the first landing-place of the words is not the hand, not the door, but the heart (ʿal-ləbābekā, upon thine heart) — the order registered: the heart before the hand (v. 8); the words are first inside, and only then bound outside. (Fact) Verse 7, quoted in full: "And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up." (6:7) Registered item by item: first, "teach them diligently" is šānan (H8150), whose base meaning is to sharpen, to rub repeatedly — the action of teaching is like the sharpening of a knife, produced by repeated friction, its lexical ground registered. Second, the occasions of talking are four: sitting in the house, walking by the way, lying down, rising up — two pairs of opposite actions (sit / walk, lie / rise) enclosing all time and place; there is no fifth, and no blank left. Third, the differences between the Chinese base text and its 2010 edition at "talk of / recite" and "sittest in thine house / whether sitting at home" are itemized on record in the Chinese headnote (vi). (Fact) Verse 8, quoted in full: "And thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thine hand, and they shall be as frontlets between thine eyes." (6:8) Registered item by item: first, the verb is qāšar (to bind) — the sign is bound on; it is an action, not a tattoo. Second, its parallels at Exodus 13:9, 16: Exodus 13:9, "And it shall be for a sign unto thee upon thine hand, and for a memorial between thine eyes, that the LORD's law may be in thy mouth: for with a strong hand hath the LORD brought thee out of Egypt." And 13:16, "And it shall be for a token upon thine hand, and for frontlets between thine eyes: for by strength of hand the LORD brought us forth out of Egypt." (both verses checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:8 in the Chinese manuscript) — the "sign / frontlets" form of this verse is wholly the same as at Exodus 13:16, registered side by side. Third, within this book, 11:18 restates it in broadly the same form: "Therefore shall ye lay up these my words in your heart and in your soul, and bind them for a sign upon your hand, that they may be as frontlets between your eyes." (checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:8 in the Chinese manuscript). (Fact) Verse 9, quoted in full: "And thou shalt write them upon the posts of thy house, and on thy gates." (6:9) Registered item by item: first, the verb changes to kātaḇ (to write) — kept in the heart, talked of with the mouth, bound on the hand, written on the posts: four verbs, four carriers. Second, the range moves from inside outward: heart (v. 6) → mouth (v. 7) → hand and front (v. 8) → the posts of the house → the gates (v. 9) — from the individual body to the household, and then to the city's public space, the circles registered. Third, the address-quotation of this part shows its closing mark at the end of this verse (for the anchor registration see Endnote 1); from verse 10 the text turns to the satiety-warning part. (Argument) The inferences of this part: first, the faith's method of preservation is "friction": the lexical meaning of šānan exposes an illusion — words do not stay because they have been heard once; the way they stay is to be talked of every day, everywhere, and in front of the children; transmission is not a ceremony but the result of a density of life. Second, the paired occasions guard against "piety by time-slot": sitting and walking are the two poles of space, lying down and rising up the two poles of time — the text of this part knows no division between "worship time" and "secular time"; talking of God's words is the default state at all hours. Third, the circles from heart to gates disclose the order of responsibility in speaking: what has not entered the heart cannot be bound on the hand, and what has not been bound on the hand cannot be written on the posts — the truth of the outward sign is guaranteed by the inward order. (New Testament Reading) This part's echoes in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: when the Lord Jesus rebuked the Pharisees He said, "But all their works they do for to be seen of men: they make broad their phylacteries, and enlarge the borders of their garments," (Matt 23:5, checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:8 in the Chinese manuscript) — the saying presupposes the concrete wearing practice of the sign of verse 8 in that day (a fact registered at the reading layer); what is rebuked is the motive, not the sign itself; 2 Timothy's record that Timothy had known the holy scriptures from a child (registered as a reported summary) stands in intertextual relation with this part's motif of teaching children. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters set side by side. First, the dispute between the literal and the figurative at verses 8–9, this chapter's longest-running interpretive division, three readings set side by side: (a) the literal reading — binding on the hand, wearing on the front, writing on the posts are all concrete practices, whose consequence is the two ritual objects of Jewish tradition, the phylacteries (təfillin) and the doorpost box (məzûzâ), whose source lies precisely here (tradition registered); (b) the figurative reading — "hand," "front" and "door" are symbolic words for conduct, thought and household, in the same binding-and-engraving idiom of wisdom literature as Proverbs 3:3 and 7:3 (their verse positions checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:8 in the Chinese manuscript), their purport being constant remembrance; (c) the parallel reading — the ancient Near East itself had a background custom of wearing signs and inscribing doorways (registered in scholarship); the text borrows the custom to speak of remembrance, and the two readings need not exclude each other. That a concrete practice existed by New Testament times is attested (Matt 23:5, reading layer); but whether the text's original meaning is literal or figurative is still set side by side in scholarship, and this manuscript does not adjudicate. Second, the meaning of ṭôṭāpōt, set side by side: some read it as a band worn on the front, some as a turban-like band, some take it to be related to an Arabic root denoting something that goes round; its exact meaning is undetermined (itemized record (vii) in the Chinese manuscript); the versions' "frontlets" (KJV) and the Chinese base text's and its 2010 edition's concrete renderings are two later concretizing renderings, registered. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: God's words have four dwellings in this part — first they dwell in the heart, then in the mouth, then on the hand and the front, and at last on the posts and the gates; the address moves from inside outward, and no place may be left empty.

### Part Four: When Thou Shalt Have Eaten and Be Full, Beware (6:10–19) (34:00–47:00)

(Fact) This part is the warning part, registered in three sections: the inventory of satiety (vv. 10–11), the command not to forget (vv. 12–15), and the close on not tempting and on diligent keeping (vv. 16–19). (Fact) Verse 10, quoted in full: "And it shall be, when the LORD thy God shall have brought thee into the land which he sware unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give thee great and goodly cities, which thou buildedst not," (6:10) Registered item by item: first, "shall have brought thee into" is the hiphil of bôʾ (to bring in) — the subject of the action of entering the land is God. Second, the three fathers are named in full in a row — in this chapter "fathers" occurs four times (vv. 3, 10, 18, 23), and only this verse gives all three names (Endnote 5). Third, the first entry of "not thine own work": cities which thou buildedst not (bānâ) — in this part the "not thine" entries make a chain of four in all (Endnote 7). (Fact) Verse 11, quoted in full: "And houses full of all good things, which thou filledst not, and wells digged, which thou diggedst not, vineyards and olive trees, which thou plantedst not; when thou shalt have eaten and be full;" (6:11) Registered item by item: first, four kinds of property: cities (v. 10), houses full of good things, wells, vineyards and olive trees — from city to house, from house to water source, from water source to field produce, the whole domain of life registered. Second, "all good things" is the concretizing of ṭûḇ (goodness, good things), of the same root as "for our good always" at verse 24, answering back and forth. Third, the peak verb of this part is śāḇaʿ (to eat and be full) — the warning is not issued in the time of hunger but in the time of satiety, the sequence registered: the "then" of verse 12 follows immediately upon eating and being full. (Fact) Verse 12, quoted in full: "Then beware lest thou forget the LORD, which brought thee forth out of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage." (6:12) Registered item by item: first, "beware" is the niphal form of šāmar, hiššāmer (take heed to thyself) — of šāmar's five occurrences in this chapter this is the only reflexive, self-guarding form (Endnote 4). Second, satiety's pathology is one word: šākaḥ (to forget) — what is forgotten is not an article of the law but the LORD Himself, "which brought thee forth." Third, the Exodus designation in this verse is "out of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage," answering, surface and depth, to verse 21's "We were Pharaoh's bondmen in Egypt." (Fact) Verse 13, quoted in full: "Thou shalt fear the LORD thy God, and serve him, and shalt swear by his name." (6:13) Registered item by item: first, three positive duties against the one danger of verse 12: fear (yārēʾ), serve (ʿāḇaḏ), swear by his name (šābaʿ) — swearing here is the rite of allegiance: by whose name a man swears, to him he belongs. Second, this verse is in broadly the same form, word for word, as Deuteronomy 10:20: "Thou shalt fear the LORD thy God; him shalt thou serve, and to him shalt thou cleave, and swear by his name." (checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:13 in the Chinese manuscript) — the difference between the two places is that 10:20 has one clause more, "and to him shalt thou cleave," and its swearing clause runs "and swear by his name" (as in this verse); itemized and registered, without adjudication. (Fact) Verses 14–15, quoted in full: "Ye shall not go after other gods, of the gods of the people which are round about you; (For the LORD thy God is a jealous God among you) lest the anger of the LORD thy God be kindled against thee, and destroy thee from off the face of the earth." (6:14–15) Registered item by item: first, at verse 14 the hearers are addressed in the plural, "ye," alternating with this part's singular "thou," the account at Endnote 9. Second, "jealous" (qannāʾ) occurs for the third time in this book (the "consuming fire" part at 4:24, the second commandment at 5:9, and this verse): in this chapter jealousy's domain of definition is exclusiveness — to go after other gods is not to worship one more, but to betray the One. Third, the consequence verb is šāmad (to destroy), and the object destroyed is removed from "the face of the earth" (ʾăḏāmâ) — answering, positive against negative, to verse 1's "the land whither ye go to possess it": the covenant-keeper obtains the land; the covenant-breaker is destroyed from the land. (Fact) Verse 16, quoted in full: "Ye shall not tempt the LORD your God, as ye tempted him in Massah." (6:16) Registered item by item: first, nāsâ (to tempt) occurs twice in this verse — once in the prohibition, once in the historical event. Second, "Massah" (massâ) is a pun on place-name and meaning (tempting): Exodus 17:7 registers the origin of its naming, quoted in full: "And he called the name of the place Massah, and Meribah, because of the chiding of the children of Israel, and because they tempted the LORD, saying, Is the LORD among us, or not?" (checked directly on the Exodus 17:7 page through the verse page for 6:16 in the Chinese manuscript). Third, this verse opens another address-quotation section, running to the end of verse 19 (Endnote 1). (Fact) Verses 17–19, quoted in full: "Ye shall diligently keep the commandments of the LORD your God, and his testimonies, and his statutes, which he hath commanded thee. And thou shalt do that which is right and good in the sight of the LORD: that it may be well with thee, and that thou mayest go in and possess the good land which the LORD sware unto thy fathers. To cast out all thine enemies from before thee, as the LORD hath spoken." (6:17–19) Registered item by item: first, the keeping of verse 17 is the intensified form šāmôr tišmərûn (diligently keep), the weightiest in tone of šāmar's five occurrences in this chapter. Second, verse 18's "right and good" sets hayyāšār (right) and haṭṭôḇ (good) side by side — the standard of the doing is not in one's own sight but "in the sight of the LORD." Third, verse 19's "to cast out" (hādap) stands opposite verse 10's "brought thee into": God brings thee in, God casts the enemy out; through the whole process of entering the land the subject has never changed. (Argument) The inferences of this part: first, this part discloses the sharpest single line of Deuteronomic theology — the danger is not in the time of lack but in the time of abundance: the wilderness tests faith; entering the land tests memory; cities, houses, wells and orchards, not one of them is sin; sin occurs only in the moment when "not thine" is read as "what I have earned" (in intertextual relation with 8:17–18, "the power to get wealth is what He gives thee" — its short quotation checked directly through the cross-reference column in the Chinese manuscript, registered). Second, "beware" here is not nervousness of feeling but a work of memory: remembrance's antonym in this part is not forgetting an article but rewriting a source — entering grace to one's own account. Third, the swearing of verse 13 deserves a note of its own: in this chapter, swearing by the LORD's name is listed as a positive act of worship — the oath of allegiance and the vain taking of the third commandment (5:11) form a positive and negative pair: His name must not be taken in vain, yet men are to swear by His name; the name is to be used in allegiance, not in empty talk. (New Testament Reading) This part's citations in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: in His temptation the Lord Jesus twice answered the devil from this chapter — Matthew 4:7 cites verse 16 as "Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God" (Matt 4:7 and Luke 4:12 both checked directly on their verse pages in the Chinese manuscript); Matthew 4:10 cites verse 13 as "Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve," and says expressly "it is written" (Matt 4:10 and Luke 4:8 checked directly). Both citations stand within this part, registered on record — the words that answer temptation in the wilderness come precisely from the part that warns men not to tempt God in the wilderness; this is a registration of a fact of textual position. Psalm 95:8–9, "Harden not your heart, as in the provocation, and as in the day of temptation in the wilderness: When your fathers tempted me, proved me, and saw my work," and its re-citation at Hebrews 3:8–9 (both checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:16 in the Chinese manuscript), are the historical-psalm echo of the Massah motif, registered at the reading layer. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters set side by side. First, the tension between this part's chain of four "not thine" entries and the narrative of the conquest of Canaan, set side by side: this chapter says the cities and houses were none of them built by Israel, but an inheritance received; Joshua's conquest narrative, by contrast, often records taking and burning (registered). Two readings set side by side — one takes this chapter's wording as theological rhetoric stressing the graciousness of the inheritance, referring to the cities not burned but left for dwelling (Joshua 24:13, "And I have given you a land for which ye did not labour, and cities which ye built not, and ye dwell in them; of the vineyards and oliveyards which ye planted not do ye eat," is in the same form as this chapter, registered as a parallel text); another takes the two books' records to differ in the range of cities and the periods covered, and holds that they need not be harmonized. The disagreement is registered only; to whom the land belongs and how the cities changed hands, this manuscript does not adjudicate. Second, a registration of the prophetic echo of the "satiety forgets God" motif: Hosea 13:6 (its Chinese base text checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:12 in the Chinese manuscript) records of Israel that according to their pasture they were filled, they were filled and their heart was exalted, and they forgot Him — the motif's later transmission is a fact of the text; its source-relation to this chapter belongs to scholarly discussion, registered without adjudication. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: this part is the longest in the chapter, yet the lesson it writes is the most cautious — the day of eating and being full is the most dangerous day; the prescription Moses gives is one sentence only: beware, lest thou forget.

### Part Five: In Time to Come, When Thy Son Asketh Thee (6:20–25) (47:00–57:00)

(Fact) Verse 20, quoted in full: "And when thy son asketh thee in time to come, saying, What mean the testimonies, and the statutes, and the judgments, which the LORD our God hath commanded you?" (6:20) Registered item by item: first, "in time to come" in the Hebrew is māḥār (tomorrow / in time to come), occurring once — the question is not today but tomorrow, and Moses prepares for it in advance. Second, what the son's question asks is the "meaning": testimonies (ʿēḏōt), statutes (ḥuqqîm), judgments (mišpāṭîm) — the three names appear here in their third transformed form (Endnote 3). Third, the form of the question is one a child can ask — not a challenge but a seeking of explanation; verse 20 of this chapter opens another address-quotation section, closing the whole chapter at the end of verse 25 (Endnote 1). (Fact) Verse 21, quoted in full: "Then thou shalt say unto thy son, We were Pharaoh's bondmen in Egypt; and the LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand:" (6:21) Registered item by item: first, the answer's first sentence is not an explanation but a claiming of identity — "We were bondmen": the answerer's self-narrative begins from slavery. Second, "with a mighty hand" (yāḏ ḥăzāqâ) is in the same form as the bringing-out formula of Exodus 13:9, 16 (checked directly through the cross-reference column in the Chinese manuscript); in this chapter, together with the bringing forth of verse 12 (the hiphil of yāṣāʾ), the account closes: the bringing out occurs three times (Endnote 7). Third, the quotation of the answer is opened, embedded, from within this verse, and closes the whole chapter at the end of verse 25 with a double closing (Endnote 1). (Fact) Verse 22, quoted in full: "And the LORD shewed signs and wonders, great and sore, upon Egypt, upon Pharaoh, and upon all his household, before our eyes:" (6:22) Registered item by item: first, the pairing of signs (ʾōṯôṯ) and wonders (môpəṯîm) is the fixed phrase-group of the Exodus narrative, and "before our eyes" marks this as no legend but the relaying of an eyewitness narrative — what the father relays to the son is precisely what the previous generation, "we," saw with their own eyes (the narrative level registered: eyewitness in the fathers' generation, hearing in the sons' generation; the question and answer of verse 20 are exactly the bridge between these two levels). Second, the Hebrew of "great and sore" is gəḏōlîm wərāʿîm (great and harmful), its "harm" being toward Egypt, itemized on record in the Chinese headnote (xiii). (Fact) Verse 23, quoted in full: "And he brought us out from thence, that he might bring us in, to give us the land which he sware unto our fathers." (6:23) Registered item by item: first, out (yāṣāʾ) and in (bôʾ) stand in parallel clauses within one verse — salvation's two directions: out from slavery, in toward the promise. Second, "to give" (nātan) is in the same form as verse 10's "to give thee" the land — the land is, from beginning to end, given (Endnote 7). (Fact) Verse 24, quoted in full: "And the LORD commanded us to do all these statutes, to fear the LORD our God, for our good always, that he might preserve us alive, as it is at this day." (6:24) Registered item by item: first, not until the fourth verse of the answer does the reason for the statutes appear — the reason is "for our good always" (ləṭôḇ lānû kol-hayyāmîm, all the days): the law's purpose sentence is spoken plainly here — it is good, and it is the preserving of life (the piel of ḥāyâ: to keep alive, preserve). Second, the hayyôm of "as it is at this day" locks with verse 6's "which I command thee this day" (Endnote 8) — the command is this day, and the preserving is also as this day; word and life run side by side on the same day. Third, the fear (yārēʾ) of this verse is this chapter's third occurrence (vv. 2, 13, 24; Endnote 8). (Fact) Verse 25, quoted in full: "And it shall be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments before the LORD our God, as he hath commanded us." (6:25) Registered item by item: first, "righteousness" (ûṣəḏāqâ) stands in the Hebrew at the head of the answer-sentence; it is the whole chapter's last stress. Second, "observe to do" is the second occurrence of this chapter's šāmar + ʿāśâ pair (vv. 3, 25) — head and tail, one before the Shema and one at the end of the question and answer, framing the whole chapter. Third, this verse's syntax is conditional: "if we observe to do" — the statement of righteousness here is a conditional sentence, not an automatic one, its grammar registered. (Argument) The inferences of this part: first, the order of the question and answer is this chapter's template for all the world's faith education: the son's question is "What mean the testimonies, and the statutes, and the judgments," yet the father's answer is nine-tenths history (vv. 21–23), one-tenth reason (v. 24), and only at the close identity (v. 25) — Moses' method of teaching men to answer questions of faith is to tell the story first and the reasoning after; the law's intelligibility lies not in its articles but in the Exodus. Second, "We were Pharaoh's bondmen" is the answer's first sentence, and this point must not be passed lightly: the starting point of faith's transmission is not a sense of superiority but the identity-memory of one who has received grace — only a father who remembers that he himself was a bondman can answer his son's question. Third, the "righteousness" of verse 25 must be read by this chapter's own context: it speaks of the state of being reckoned right in relation before God when the covenant people diligently observe and do (in the same wording as Deuteronomy 24:13, "it shall be righteousness unto thee before the LORD thy God," checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:25 in the Chinese manuscript; Psalm 106:31 records of Phinehas's deed that it "was counted unto him for righteousness," registered in the same form) — this verse's function within this chapter is to give "observe to do" a name and standing within the covenant; its relation to the doctrine of justification belongs to the level of New Testament reading and doctrinal discussion, set down at the reading and discussion layers; this manuscript does not adjudicate it at the fact layer. (New Testament Reading) This part's echoes in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only, all checked for their comparative relation through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:25 in the Chinese manuscript: Leviticus 18:5, "which if a man do, he shall live in them," is cited by Paul at Romans 10:5 as the description of "the righteousness which is of the law" ("For Moses describeth the righteousness which is of the law, That the man which doeth those things shall live by them"), and cited again at Galatians 3:12 as "The man that doeth them shall live in them," set in contrast with faith — how the New Testament takes up the vocabulary of "live by doing" and of "righteousness" is a registration of the reading layer; verse 25's own context still stands by this chapter, without reading back. At Luke 10:28 the Lord says to the lawyer, "Thou hast answered right: this do, and thou shalt live" — the Shema's question and answer open another question and answer in the New Testament; this too is registered at the reading layer. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters set side by side. First, the source and course of the question-and-answer form at verses 20–25: this chapter's question and answer, together with Exodus 12:26–27 (the Passover question), 13:14 (in the same form, "when thy son asketh thee in time to come," its verse position checked directly through the cross-reference column of the verse page for 6:20 in the Chinese manuscript) and Joshua 4:6 (the question of the heap of stones in the Jordan), form a group of catechetical forms, "the son asks, the father answers" (form registered); scholarship takes its background either as the question-and-answer rite of the Passover household meal, or as the rhetorical frame of Deuteronomy's address — the two accounts set side by side; over its function in the text (answering articles with narrative) there is no disagreement. Second, a comparison of the use of ṣəḏāqâ at 6:25 and Genesis 15:6 (registered as a reported summary: Genesis 15 records that Abram believed the LORD, and He counted it to him for righteousness): the relation, within the Old Testament text itself, between the two forms, "believe and be counted righteous" and "observe and be counted righteous" — three readings set side by side: (a) the harmonious reading: the two places differ in subject and context (the patriarch's faith / the covenant people's observing), and "counted for righteousness" is two entrance accounts of one and the same covenant relation; (b) the tension reading: the two forms represent two poles of the understanding of faith, and the tension between them continues to be handled by later texts (the prophetic books, the New Testament); (c) the semantic reading: ṣəḏāqâ in both places denotes "being proved right within a relation," and is not necessarily the forerunner of the later doctrinal term. The three readings are set side by side, and this manuscript does not adjudicate — the fact layer confirms only this: both verses use the counted-righteousness construction with the word ṣəḏāqâ; all else is set down below. (Summary) One governing sentence for this part: this text rehearses faith's most moving scene — the child opens his mouth to ask, the father opens his mouth to tell: tell that we were bondmen, tell of that mighty hand, tell that this land was given, tell that the law is for our good; and at the very end the father says: if we observe to do, it shall be our righteousness.

Conclusion (57:00–60:00). (Summary) The whole chapter drawn together: Deuteronomy 6's twenty-five verses are one complete circuit of faith — hearing (vv. 1–4) begets love (v. 5); love must live in the heart, in the house, on the posts (vv. 6–9); having moved in, it must still be kept firm on the most dangerous day (vv. 10–19); having been kept, it must still be able to answer the next generation's question (vv. 20–25). Wherever a link breaks, the circuit breaks. (Argument) The closing charge picks out three things only: first, the Shema's order cannot be exchanged: only by first confessing that He is one can whole-heartedness be spoken of; confessing God first and loving God after is this chapter's bone. Second, satiety's lesson cannot be exempted: the chapter's longest warning is given not to men short of water in the wilderness but to men who have eaten and are full after entering the land — most of today's hearers are not in the wilderness but in satiety, and verse 12's "Then beware" is written for today. Third, the son's question cannot be waited for unprepared: the question of verse 20 is already on the road — "What mean these?" This chapter has prepared, for every father and every teacher, the shape of the answer: do not recite the articles; first tell that we were bondmen, and how He brought us out. (Fact) The conclusion closes on the chapter's own closing sentence, quoted again from the base text as the seal of the whole sermon: "And it shall be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments before the LORD our God, as he hath commanded us." (6:25) — Hear, O Israel: having heard, keep it in thine heart; having kept it in thine heart, teach thy children; having taught thy children, beware in the days of eating and being full; beware to the end, and thou shalt surely be able to answer that question in time to come.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotional use)

"And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up." (Deut 6:6–7, KJV)

Moses did not hand God's words to men to be kept in a cupboard; he let them dwell in the heart. Words that dwell in the heart walk outward of themselves: to the table, to the road, to the child's bedtime and rising. Faith is not a matter of leaving God an hour or two each week; it is talking at home, talking on the road, talking lying down, talking rising up — four occasions, and not one of them left to forgetting.

Why talk on like this without stopping? Because Moses has already given the answer further on: doing all these statutes is "for our good always, that he might preserve us alive, as it is at this day" (Deut 6:24). That thou art still alive today, and still hast strength to open thy mouth and talk, is itself the proof of His preserving. The more the words are talked of, the more firmly the good is remembered; the more firmly the good is remembered, the less easily the heart forgets Him in the days of eating and being full.

Today, try speaking one verse aloud: one sentence at the meal, one on the road, one before sleep. It need not be long; like the sharpening of a knife, sharpen it every day, and the words will be keen, and the heart bright. And when one day a child opens his mouth to ask, "What does this mean?" thou wilt find that the answer has long been talked familiar on thy lips.

Prayer: Lord, write Your words on my heart; let me not only know them, but open my mouth to talk of them, move my hands to do them, and teach them to my children. Sitting in my house, walking by the way, lying down and rising up, may Your words be with me. In Jesus' name, Amen.

### Devotional Extract (English, KJV-based)

"Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD: And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." (Deut 6:4–5, KJV)

Moses does not give Israel a list first; he gives them a confession. Before any commandment can be kept, Someone must be known: the LORD our God is one. Love that is divided among many masters is not love at all, so the first word is not "do" but "hear" — hear who He is, and the whole heart will follow.

And hear what keeping His word is for: "And the LORD commanded us to do all these statutes, to fear the LORD our God, for our good always, that he might preserve us alive, as it is at this day. And it shall be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments before the LORD our God, as he hath commanded us." (Deut 6:24–25, KJV) His commands are not a tax on life; they are the guardrail of life — for our good always.

So today, say the Shema aloud once, slowly. Let the hearing come before the doing, and let the doing be love's answer.

Prayer: Lord, You alone are God. Teach my heart to hear You first, to love You with all that I am, and to keep Your words as life itself. In Jesus' name, Amen.

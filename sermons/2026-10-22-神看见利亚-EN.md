## Summary (about 100 words)

Jacob met Rachel at the well in Haran and served seven years to marry her, counting them as only a few days; on the wedding night Laban substituted his elder daughter Leah for her. Leah was unloved, yet the LORD saw her and enabled her to bear Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah. The four names tell all of her sorrow and hope, and also make clear: the one people favor less, God does not overlook, and at the end of tears grows the lineage of kings and priests.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter Jacob met God in the wilderness, and God said, wherever you go, I will keep you. Today he arrived at his destination, Haran. When he left he had nothing; after he arrived he soon had work, a person he loved at first sight, and a home he thought would soon be his. But the Bible's pen does not hurry to write a perfect ending; instead it places two things before us: a well, and a wedding in which the bride was substituted. The well is full of the sweetness of love, and the wedding is full of the scheming of the human heart and the tears of a woman.

In this chapter, the main character on the surface is Jacob, but the one whom God treats with the most tender strokes is a woman who from beginning to end was never properly loved by anyone — Leah.

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several key passages:

"And Jacob served seven years for Rachel; and they seemed unto him but a few days, for the love he had to her." (Genesis 29:20, excerpt, KJV)

"And it came to pass in the evening, that he took Leah his daughter, and brought her to him; and he went in unto her. ... And it came to pass, that in the morning, behold, it was Leah: and he said to Laban, What is this thou hast done unto me? did not I serve with thee for Rachel? wherefore then hast thou beguiled me?" (Genesis 29:23-25, excerpt, KJV)

"And when the LORD saw that Leah was hated, he opened her womb: but Rachel was barren. And Leah conceived, and bare a son, and she called his name Reuben: for she said, Surely the LORD hath looked upon my affliction; now therefore my husband will love me." (Genesis 29:31-32, excerpt, KJV)

"And she conceived again, and bare a son: and she said, Now will I praise the LORD: therefore she called his name Judah; and left bearing." (Genesis 29:35, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today we are going to hear the story of a person who was not loved, and also hear how You cared for her. Comfort every one among us who feels overlooked, replaced, and neglected, and let them know that human eyes miss people, but Your eyes do not miss anyone. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Love at the Well and Seven Years as a Few Days (29:1-20)

First look at Jacob's situation when he arrived in Haran. He "lifted up his eyes" and saw a well, and beside the well were three flocks of sheep waiting to be watered. A fugitive arrived in the territory of his relatives, and the first thing he saw was a well. Stories at wells in the Bible are never simple: Abraham's servant found Rebekah for Isaac at a well, Moses met Zipporah at a well, and today Jacob met Rachel at a well. A well was a place where nomads gathered, a place where women came out to draw water, and also a place where a solitary person was received again into a family.

Rachel was a shepherdess; she came driving her father's sheep. As soon as Jacob saw her, he did something very unlike his former character: he went up alone, rolled the great stone from the well's mouth, and watered the flock of his uncle Laban. That stone normally required several shepherds working together to move it; the Bible says it was rolled away only when all the shepherds had gathered. Jacob rolled it away by himself — love made his strength greater too. Then he kissed Rachel and lifted up his voice and wept. What was this weeping about? It was the first loosening before the grievance of twenty years away from home had even arrived: he had fled all this way, hidden all this journey, and at last arrived at his mother's family home and saw his relatives. Jacob the deceiver wept at the well like a child. This is how people are: no matter how calculating a person is, there is also a place in his heart that only wants to be properly received by someone.

He made his identity known, entered Laban's house, and stayed for a month. Laban first filled the family affection to the full: "Because thou art my brother, shouldest thou therefore serve me for nought?" Then they talked about wages. Jacob offered a price that surprised everyone: "I will serve thee seven years for Rachel thy younger daughter." Seven years — not a sum of money, but a stretch of life. At that time marrying a wife required paying a bride price; Jacob the fugitive could not produce money, and the only thing he could produce was himself: he used seven years of labor as the bride price.

Here the Bible gives a comment that has been handed down for thousands of years: "And Jacob served seven years for Rachel; and they seemed unto him but a few days, for the love he had to her." Please savor this sentence carefully. It does not say that seven years were short; of course seven years are long, and every day's work was real: pasturing sheep, shearing, keeping watch at night, guarding against wild beasts. What it says is that, because of deep love, the long time had its feeling rewritten by love. Love does not cancel hardship; love changes the taste of hardship. Serving for the sake of the person you love, even the longest days have hope; days without love are hard to endure however short they are. Whether our years of serving God, serving family, and serving the church are long does not depend only on the number of years, but on whether there is love inside them. Jacob's seven years are the brightest page about love in Genesis.

But we must also speak honestly about the other side of the brightness: Laban had two daughters. "Leah was tender eyed; but Rachel was beautiful and well favoured." The Hebrew descriptor (rakkoth, "tender/delicate") has traditionally been understood of her eyes as weak and without radiance. The Bible introduces the two women, writing about the eyes of one and the overall beautiful and well-favored appearance of the other; the contrast is already very clear. Even clearer is the next sentence: "And Jacob loved Rachel." From this moment the hearts in this household were divided into near and distant. Leah had not yet done anything wrong; she innately stood on the side that was not favored. Brothers and sisters, this is how the world divides people: by appearance, by ability, by origin, by whether or not one is likable. Leah's tragedy was not that she did something bad; it was that she was born in a home that measured people by favoritism, and married into a marriage that continued to measure people by favoritism. The heaviest place in this chapter is right here: favoritism itself is a kind of chronic harm; it does not need to lift a hand to make a person feel superfluous for a lifetime.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Substitution on the Wedding Night — The Deceiver Is Deceived (29:21-30)

When the seven years were fulfilled, Jacob confronted Laban: "Give me my wife, for my days are fulfilled, that I may go in unto her." He spoke very directly. Laban held a wedding feast and invited all the men of the place, a respectable wedding. The problem came at night: "And it came to pass in the evening, that he took Leah his daughter, and brought her to him." The person brought in at night, veiled, was Leah, not Rachel. "And it came to pass, that in the morning, behold, it was Leah." The Bible places the most piercing sentence of the whole chapter in the morning. Seven years waiting for the wrong person, one night married to the wrong bride. Jacob rushed to question Laban: "What is this thou hast done unto me? did not I serve with thee for Rachel? wherefore then hast thou beguiled me?"

Please pause on this word: "beguiled." The original uses the meaning of deceiving. Who is the person saying this? It is Jacob. Deceiving was what Jacob was best at in his life: he had beguiled his blind father, impersonated his elder brother, carried in false savory food, and worn false skins. Now he stood before Laban and for the first time tasted what it was like to be beguiled by someone, and in a major matter of his own life. Even more wonderful is the correspondence of details: in that year he used his father's blindness to impersonate the elder son in the dark; now Laban used the darkness of night to bring in the elder daughter in the dark. In that year he substituted for his brother's place; now the one substituted was his bride's place. In that year his mother arranged it behind the scenes; now her father arranged it behind the scenes. The Bible does not have a single comment saying this is retribution, but the narrative itself is the comment: whatever a person sows, that he also reaps. God disciplined Jacob without thunder, without an earthquake; a mirror was enough: let him personally taste what it felt like, what he had put on others.

Laban's answer can be called seasoned: "It must not be so done in our country, to give the younger before the firstborn." First he pushed responsibility to custom, then he offered a new condition: serve me another seven years for this woman, and I will give you Rachel also. Jacob agreed. He first fulfilled Leah's week of the wedding feast, then married Rachel, and served Laban another seven years. The Bible adds a defining sentence: "And he loved also Rachel more than Leah." The tone of this chapter was already set: in one home, two wives, one loved and one not loved; one husband, whose heart leaned completely to one side.

We must stop and say a fair word for Leah. In this substitution, could she decide anything? She was a pawn in her father's hand. Whomever her father said to send, that one was sent; she had no room even to refuse. On the morning after the wedding, when her husband saw it was her, he turned away at once to settle accounts — as she listened in that room, what did she feel in her heart? From the first day she knew that she had been stuffed into this marriage, that she was her sister's substitute, a number on her father's abacus. The Bible does not record a single word of rebuttal from her; all her words are in the names she later gave her children. When people are wounded most deeply by favoritism, they are often unable to speak. Being unable to speak does not mean it does not hurt.

This passage also gives parents a heavy blow. Laban used his daughter as a bargaining chip and exchanged her for fourteen years of his son-in-law's labor. He thought he was shrewd and had won two weddings and fourteen years of free labor. But from then on this home buried decades of division: two wives contending, more than ten sons divided into factions, Joseph sold, almost the destruction of the family — all drawn up from this well of favoritism and scheming. Parents push a child with their hand, and the child must spend a lifetime repaying it. Parents, may God give us clean hands: do not use children's marriages as transactions, do not lift up one child in front of another, do not treat favoritism as a matter of course. The account of favoritism is always paid by the children.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The LORD Saw Leah — The Names of Four Sons (29:31-35)

The turning point of the whole chapter is only one sentence, yet it is like a beam of light splitting the whole story: "And when the LORD saw that Leah was hated, he opened her womb: but Rachel was barren."

First, chew on the wording. "Hated," in the original sane' (שָׂנֵא, H8130), to hate, detest, be unloved, be unfavored [1]. This word carries a very heavy weight; ordinarily it simply means "hate." Read in context, the word here does not imply active abuse: it was not that Jacob beat or scolded Leah, but that his heart was not with her, and she was in a position of being neglected and looked down upon in the marriage. The Bible uses such a heavy word to make us face this squarely: neglect is not a small matter. In God's eyes, a person who has long not been loved by her husband has a situation that deserves to be measured with the word "hate." Cold violence in marriage and favoritism in the family are all recorded by God, and God regards them all as debts.

Then look at that verb: "saw." In the original ra'ah (רָאָה, H7200), to see, look upon, care for [2]. God's seeing is never merely seeing; God saw Hagar in the wilderness, and Hagar called Him "the God who sees me"; God saw the affliction of the Israelites in Egypt, and came down to deliver them. Seeing is the beginning of caring. Leah was used by her father, neglected by her husband, and outshone by her sister; everyone took things from her, and no one properly looked at her even once. Yet the Scripture says: the LORD saw. Brothers and sisters, this is a preview of the gospel more than a thousand years in advance: the one people favor less, God does not overlook; the one people overlook, God insists on seeing. God's fairness is not that everyone receives exactly the same, but that upon the person who has been shortchanged, He Himself makes up that portion of regard that no one gave.

How did God make it up? "He opened her womb." In that era when childbearing was a woman's value, God first gave the most valued blessing to the least valued person. Leah bore four sons in succession, and the names she gave each child are her autobiography — four chapters, each chapter moving higher than the one before.

The eldest son Reuben, Re'uven (רְאוּבֵן), she said: "Surely the LORD hath looked upon my affliction; now therefore my husband will love me." [3] The first character of the name is "see." Her first hope still hung on her husband: having borne the eldest son, surely he should love me now. Many wounded people pray like this at first: Lord, please make him change; please make them approve of me. God heard the first half — He had seen her affliction; the second half, her husband's heart, did not go as she wished.

The second son Simeon, Sim'on (שִׁמְעוֹן), she said: "Because the LORD hath heard that I was hated, he hath therefore given me this son also." [4] From seeing it advances to hearing. Her hope became a little smaller: whether her husband loved her or not, let that go for now; at least God had heard. Words that receive no response among people all find their place with God. Leah began to learn the most important thing: to move her reliance on people, inch by inch, onto God.

The third son Levi, Lewi (לֵוִי), she said: "Now this time will my husband be joined unto me, because I have born him three sons." [5] The word "joined" carries the meaning of adhesion. Her hope was still struggling: three sons now, surely he should be close to me. This is how the human heart is: knowing clearly that hope has fallen empty twice, the third time it still cannot help hoping once more. We need not laugh at her; her struggle is exactly our struggle: we too always want to beg from people a satisfaction that ought to be obtained from God.

The fourth son Judah, Yehudah (יְהוּדָה), she said: "Now will I praise the LORD." [6] Please note, in her words she mentioned her husband for the first time not at all. It was not "my husband will love me," nor "my husband will be joined unto me," but "I will praise the LORD." Four children, four names, one road of the heart: from asking her husband to love her, to knowing God had heard, to hoping again for union, and finally landing on praising God alone. She did not wait until Jacob changed his heart to praise; she learned to praise first, while her situation had not changed. Only then did she stop bearing — the narrative stops on a posture of worship.

And God's arrangement was far deeper and longer than Leah's tears. Among the sons she bore, Levi became the tribe of priests, and the office of Israel's worship came from her; Judah became the tribe of kings, David came from him, and the Messiah came from him. The end of the Book of Ruth records the descendants of Judah, and Revelation calls the Lord Jesus "the Lion of the tribe of Juda." As for Rachel, who was favored at the well, her son Joseph was indeed noble and saved the whole family; but at the very center of the lineage of salvation — priests and kings — God entrusted it to unloved Leah. Brothers and sisters, human casting chooses the beautiful and well favored; God's casting chooses the one who was overlooked by people yet learned to praise amid tears. There is no monument on Leah's grave, but the most glorious line in the whole Bible passed through her womb. If today you feel you are a substitute, a backup choice, the one no one wants, please listen well: God sees, God hears, and God insists on accomplishing in you what people cannot imagine.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: people who feel they are not loved. Neglected in marriage, unable to rank in their family of origin, always the one left over in a crowd. Today's Scripture is read for you: the LORD saw that Leah was hated. Your situation does not need to be beautified; God calls it by the heaviest word, and God also cares for you with the most solid action. You may wait in vain for a human heart; come first before God — His seeing is never late.

The second kind: people who are practicing favoritism. Which child parents favor, which student a teacher favors, which co-worker a leader favors — you know clearly in your heart, and God also knows clearly. The wounds favoritism creates are passed from one generation to the next; Jacob's family paid the account for decades. May God stop this chain today: the regard you owe that child, the fairness you owe that co-worker, while there is still time, make it up.

The third kind: people who live by scheming. Laban worked his abacus all his life, and in the end his children were alienated and his family enterprise empty; Jacob schemed against people in his youth, and in middle age was schemed against. What you seize by scheming, sooner or later you will taste back with principal and interest. Change to a different way of living as early as possible: honest wages, clean hands. When God disciplines those He loves, sometimes He does not need heavy words; a mirror is enough.

The fourth kind: people serving in a long wait. Seven years as a few days was because there was love; there are also people whose seven years grow longer day by day, because the person they wait for is not worth it and the thing they hope for has no visible end. Please redeem your years from people and place them in God's hand. Serving people will exhaust you; serving God has reward. God has counted all your days, and not one day will be missed.

The fifth kind: people who stake all their hope on people. Learn Leah's four steps to the end: you may tell God your sorrow, you may tell God your lack of favor, but in the end you must arrive at the fourth name — now will I praise the LORD. Praise is not a program that comes only after the situation improves; praise is, while the situation has not yet improved, first settling your heart with God. Human love misses; God's love does not miss.

The sixth kind: people who feel they are substitutes. Leah was stuffed into a marriage, Joseph was sold into Egypt, Moses was placed in a river — God's lineage is full of people regarded by others as second-class. What your origin is, what your starting point is, and how others define you are not the final outcome. The final outcome is written in that sentence, "the LORD saw." A person seen by God is never a backup choice.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have seen a well, a wedding, and four names. The love at the well was real, the scheming at the wedding was real, Leah's tears were real, and the most real was that sentence, "the LORD saw that Leah was hated." The one people favor less, God does not overlook; what people substitute in the dark place, God turns over in the bright place. At the end of tears, Leah held Judah and said: Now will I praise the LORD.

Let us pray together: Lord who cares for people, thank You; You saw Hagar, You saw Leah, and You also see every one among us who is not loved, not chosen, and not remembered. Heal the wounds left by favoritism, stop the disasters brought by scheming, and let us move our hope back from people to You. Teach us, like Leah, while our situation has not yet changed, to open our mouths and say: Now will I praise the LORD. In the name of the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] sane': Hebrew שָׂנֵא (H8130), to hate, detest, not love, be unfavored; Genesis 29:31 "the LORD saw that Leah was hated" and 29:33 Leah's self-description "I was hated" both use this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The sense in context includes "not being favored, being neglected"; the third part of this manuscript, "neglect deserves to be measured by hate," is a homiletical exposition based on the weight of this word, and is noted as exposition.

[2] ra'ah: Hebrew רָאָה (H7200), to see, look upon, care for; Genesis 29:31 "the LORD saw Leah" and 29:32 Leah said "the LORD hath looked upon my affliction"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Genesis 16:13, where Hagar called God "the God who sees me," and Exodus 3:7, where God saw the affliction of the people, as the scriptural basis for this manuscript's "God's seeing is the beginning of His caring."

[3] Re'uven: Reuben, Hebrew רְאוּבֵן, Genesis 29:32 Leah said "the LORD hath looked upon my affliction"; the name can be explained as "see, a son," and is also associated by wordplay with "He has seen my affliction"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[4] Sim'on: Simeon, Hebrew שִׁמְעוֹן, Genesis 29:33 Leah said "the LORD hath heard"; the name derives from the verb shama (שָׁמַע, H8085, to hear); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[5] Lewi: Levi, Hebrew לֵוִי, Genesis 29:34 Leah said "my husband will be joined unto me"; the name is associated by wordplay with the verb lawah (לָוָה, H3867, to join, to attach oneself); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note. See Numbers chapter 18, where the tribe of Levi was set apart as holy for service, as the basis for this manuscript's "the tribe of priests came from Leah."

[6] Yehudah and yadah: Judah, Hebrew יְהוּדָה (Yehudah), Genesis 29:35 Leah said "Now will I praise the LORD," and therefore named him; his name is associated by wordplay with the verb yadah (יָדָה, H3034, to give thanks, to praise); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Genesis 49:8-10, Jacob's blessing on Judah, and Revelation 5:5, "the Lion of the tribe of Juda," as cross-evidence for this manuscript's "the lineage of kings came from Leah."

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And when the LORD saw that Leah was hated, he opened her womb: but Rachel was barren. And Leah conceived, and bare a son, and she called his name Reuben: for she said, Surely the LORD hath looked upon my affliction; now therefore my husband will love me." (Genesis 29:31-32) "And she conceived again, and bare a son: and she said, Now will I praise the LORD: therefore she called his name Judah; and left bearing." (Genesis 29:35)

Leah's story is told for everyone who feels overlooked. She had done nothing wrong, yet she was never the favored one: her father used her as a pawn, and her husband's heart belonged to her sister. The four names she gave her sons trace a road from tears to praise. At first she longed for her husband to love her; then she learned that God had heard her; at last she no longer spoke of her husband at all, but said: This time I will praise the LORD. Human love is partial and human eyes miss people, but God neither favors nor overlooks. The honor you never received from people, God Himself makes up to you. Do not keep staring at the closed door today. Learn Leah's fourth sentence first: before the situation changes, I will praise. For the one whom God sees, no tear is wasted, and your Judah is on the way.

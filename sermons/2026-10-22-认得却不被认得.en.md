## Summary (about 100 words)

Famine came upon Canaan, and Jacob sent ten sons down to Egypt to buy grain, keeping only Benjamin behind. Joseph knew his brothers, but they did not know him. He remembered the dreams of his youth and tested them with stern words, and under interrogation the brothers at last spoke the words buried for twenty-two years: we are verily guilty concerning our brother. Confession is the door to the turning point.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter we saw Joseph standing before Pharaoh. Thirteen years of prison, of being sold and falsely accused, were all turned over in that one day: he interpreted Pharaoh's dream, put on vestures of fine linen and a gold chain, and became ruler over Egypt. Through the seven years of plenty he went throughout all the land of Egypt and gathered grain as the sand of the sea, very much. But the Bible's pen does not stop at glory. As soon as the plenty ended, famine came — and not famine in Egypt alone, but famine in all lands. In today's chapter the camera turns from the palace of Egypt back to a tent in Canaan: old Jacob sits in hunger, looks at his ten sons, and tells them to go down to Egypt.

This chapter has a deeply moving title: he knew them, but was not known. The one who knew the brothers stood right before them, interrogating them; those being interrogated could not for the life of them recognize that this Egyptian ruler was the younger brother they had sold with their own hands twenty-two years ago. On one side, complete knowledge; on the other side, complete ignorance. On one side, tears held back; on the other side, a conscience held down for twenty-two years. Through this reunion God was going to do something far greater than buying grain: He was going to bring into the light a sin this family had hidden for twenty-two years.

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several key passages:

"Now when Jacob saw that there was corn in Egypt, Jacob said unto his sons, Why do ye look one upon another? And he said, Behold, I have heard that there is corn in Egypt: get you down thither, and buy for us from thence; that we may live, and not die." (Genesis 42:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And Joseph's brethren came, and bowed down themselves before him with their faces to the earth. And Joseph saw his brethren, and he knew them, but made himself strange unto them, and spake roughly unto them." (Genesis 42:6-7, excerpt, KJV)

"And they said one to another, We are verily guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the anguish of his soul, when he besought us, and we would not hear; therefore is this distress come upon us." (Genesis 42:21, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob their father said unto them, Me have ye bereaved of my children: Joseph is not, and Simeon is not, and ye will take Benjamin away: all these things are against me." (Genesis 42:36, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, twenty-two years have passed; what people thought was turned over, You have not turned over; the sin people thought was buried, You will dig up in Your time — not to condemn us, but to save our lives. Speak to us in the same way today; awaken the conscience that has slept so long within us, and let confession become the beginning of our receiving grace. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Famine Comes, and Ten Sons Go Down to Egypt (42:1-6)

First look at the Canaan side. "Jacob saw that there was corn in Egypt." In years of famine, news is life: where there is grain, there is a way to live. When Jacob heard there was grain in Egypt, he urged his sons to set out, in words carrying both rebuke and an old father's anxiety: "Why do ye look one upon another?" You look at me, I look at you, and after all the looking the pot is still empty. Brothers and sisters, looking at one another is the most useless posture in a famine. When disaster comes, the fault people most easily commit is not having no way out, but standing still looking at each other and waiting for someone else to move first. Jacob's question also asks us today: God has already pointed out the way for you — the grain is in Egypt, the road is under your feet — why are you still looking one upon another? You have prayed, you have heard sermons, you understand in your heart, yet you delay setting out; such looking delays the life of a whole family.

One word in Jacob's speech deserves attention: the grain bought, for which the text repeatedly uses a particular term. In the original it is shever (שֶׁבֶר, H7668), whose basic meaning is grain, corn, especially grain offered for sale, for people to buy [1]. This word appears many times in chapter 42, beating like a drum: Egypt has grain, Canaan has none; to live, one must bow his head and buy. What a household this once was: the estate Isaac had gathered, flocks and herds in multitudes; now ten sons must push their baggage and queue before the storehouse of foreigners. Famine respects no person; it lines the rich household and the poor up in the same queue. Sometimes God permits famine to come upon a family not to shame them, but to push them out of the tent, to a place they would never ordinarily go — before the old account, and before grace.

Those who went were ten. "And Joseph's ten brethren went down to buy corn in Egypt." Why ten? Because one did not go: Benjamin. The text deliberately explains: "But Benjamin, Joseph's brother, Jacob sent not with his brethren; for he said, Lest peradventure mischief befall him." One "lest peradventure" tells all of the old father's wounds of twenty-two years. As to how Joseph disappeared, Jacob to this day knew only one version: a wild beast had torn him, and the bloody coat was still there. Yet in one place in the father's heart, which even he could not explain, he dared not again hand over this last son left by Rachel to those ten. "Lest peradventure mischief befall him" — this sentence is the father's silent verdict on the ten sons: of the younger brothers you took out, one was not brought back. What the ten brothers felt in their hearts when they heard this sentence, the Bible does not write; it leaves it for us to ponder: in a family, the deepest after-effect of sin is not one quarrel, but decades in which people cannot trust one another. What sin destroys is never only one event; it is the peace of all the days that follow.

Consider another layer: in keeping Benjamin, Jacob seemed to be protecting his son, but this was also a foreshadowing of the lesson this family had to walk through. Today he would on no account let him go, but God's arrangement would not stop at the father's fear. Benjamin would go down to Egypt in the end, and this family would in the end stand whole before Joseph, not one missing. The family God heals accepts no trial in absence, and accepts no permanent missing of one. Note this point first; when we come to Jacob's refusal at the end of the chapter, we will return to it.

The ten brothers arrived in Egypt, mixed among the crowds from every land who had come to buy grain. The text gives an extremely important note: "And Joseph was the governor over the land." The original word for governing carries the sense of ruling and exercising authority. At this moment the authority over grain and over government in Egypt were both in the hands of this young man of Hebrew slave origin. Those who sold him would buy grain from him; those who harmed him would seek a way to live from him — this is not coincidence; it is the closing of God's long line of arrangement over twenty-two years.

"And Joseph's brethren came, and bowed down themselves before him with their faces to the earth." Please look carefully at this bow. More than twenty years ago, seventeen-year-old Joseph had a dream: his brothers' sheaves bowed down to his sheaf; and another dream: the sun, the moon, and the eleven stars bowed down to him. When the brothers heard it at the time, they hated him bitterly: shalt thou indeed reign over us? Or shalt thou indeed have dominion over us? His father also rebuked him. Now the dream was fulfilled word for word, at the very moment they bowed with their faces to the earth — and they themselves had no idea at all that they were fulfilling the dream they had mocked and sold for twenty pieces of silver. Brothers and sisters, when God's word is fulfilled, it is often not fulfilled with drums and gongs; it is fulfilled quietly, inch by inch, in days when people are wholly unaware. The promise you mocked and the word you thought long voided will not fall to the ground one syllable when God's appointed day comes. So do not despise words God has spoken to you through lowly people and in ways you dislike; and do not, because the wait is long, conclude that the dream is dead. Joseph waited twenty-two years; how many years have we waited?

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: He Knew Them, But Was Not Known — Interrogation Awakens the Conscience (42:7-24)

The heart of the whole chapter begins to beat from verse 7: "And Joseph saw his brethren, and he knew them, but made himself strange unto them."

Here there is a Hebrew wordplay in which the key to the whole chapter is hidden. "Knew" is in the original nakar (נָכַר, H5234), to recognize, identify, know; yet another form of the same root means to make oneself strange, to pretend not to be acquainted [2]. Verse 7 says Joseph knew them; verse 8 says again that Joseph knew his brothers, but his brothers did not know him. The same word: on Joseph it is knowing; on the brothers it is not knowing. The precision of the Bible's wording is enough to make one tremble: the one who knew pretended not to know, and those who did not know were wholly unaware that they stood before the one who knew.

Why could they not recognize him? Twenty-two years had passed. The one they sold was a seventeen-year-old youth in a coat of many colours, with a child's face; the one sitting before them now was a ruler of about forty, shaved, clothed in Egyptian fine linen, speaking Egyptian, bearing the Egyptian name Zaphnathpaaneah. The years had changed his face, status had changed his dress, language stood between them, and an interpreter stood there as well. The deeper reason is that they never thought to recognize him. In their ledger, Joseph's page had long been settled — either dead, or a slave in some nameless corner; he could on no account be sitting on the throne of Egypt. People fail to recognize God's work mostly not because God's work is too hidden, but because in their hearts they have already written the footnote for the ending. The footnote the brothers wrote for Joseph was slavery and death; what God wrote was rulership and salvation. Brothers and sisters, our failure to recognize God is sometimes like this: by experience, by probability, by what that person used to be, we draw the boundary in advance for what God can do, and whatever lies outside the boundary we simply cannot recognize.

And Joseph? He recognized them at a glance, and the text immediately says: "And Joseph remembered the dreams which he dreamed of them." Knowing his brothers, what he remembered first was not settling the old account, but God's word. This point is extremely important. What sustained Joseph through these twenty-two years was not a list for revenge, but the dreams God gave him in his youth — in the pit, the dreams did not die; in Potiphar's house, the dreams did not die; in prison, the dreams did not die. Now the dreams were fulfilled before his eyes, and what he remembered first was that God had known the ending from the beginning. What enables a person to wait for God's time is not comfort given by circumstances, but the fire of God's word not extinguished in his heart. The one who is known learns first to wait — at this moment Joseph fully had the power to overturn the table on the spot: revenge, condemnation, throwing all ten into prison, all at one word. But he did not. He first had to see one thing: were these ten men still the same ten men of twenty-two years ago? Was his father still alive? What had become of his younger brother Benjamin? God had placed the authority of judgment in his hand, yet he used it first to examine hearts. This is maturity: having authority, one is not in a hurry to use it; having wounds, one is not in a hurry to repay them.

Then came that stern accusation: "Ye are spies; to see the nakedness of the land are ye come." "Spies" is in the original meraggelim (מְרַגְּלִים, H7270), spies, men who spy out [3]. Here we must distinguish right from wrong clearly, lest we misread Joseph. Saying they were spies was, at the level of fact, not true: they really had come to buy grain, and Joseph knew it in his heart. His words were not a verdict of condemnation, but a probe of testing — like a doctor's percussion hammer, struck down to hear the echo within. His purpose had three layers: first, to ascertain the true situation of his father and Benjamin — if they really were spies, they would never report the true details of a whole household, old and young; second, to examine whether their hearts had changed, using pressure to force out what was hidden inside; third, to leave order for the reunion — his father and the whole family were to move to Egypt, and this game of chess had to be played step by step, not thrown into chaos at the door of the storehouse. So in reading this chapter we must neither read Joseph as a powerful man taking private revenge, nor read his sternness as cold-hearted ruthlessness. The text itself will shortly testify for him: when he heard his brothers confess, he turned away and wept. The sternness was the outer garment; the tears were the true heart. God Himself often treats people this way too: His rebuke sounds heavy, and underneath it is cushioned a heart that wants to save us.

What did the brothers say under pressure? They first reported their family: "Thy servants are twelve brethren, the sons of one man in the land of Canaan; and, behold, the youngest is this day with our father, and one is not." Please note those last words: "one is not." Twenty-two years later, introducing their family members to outsiders, it was still this same formula: one is not. "Is not" — such light words, behind which lay a youth's pleading in the pit, a coat of many colours dipped in goat's blood, and a father's tears of twenty-two years. With the words "is not" they had covered a sin for twenty-two years. But the more it was covered, the heavier the lid became. Joseph, however, seized their own words and returned them: if you are true men, let one brother stay in prison, let the rest go back, and bring your youngest brother, and then I will know that you are not spies. The words "true men" were like a needle. What these ten men lacked most in their lives was precisely truthfulness: not truthful to their father, not truthful to their younger brother, and not necessarily truthful among themselves. Now an Egyptian ruler was examining their truthfulness to their faces.

The test fell first on prison: Joseph put them all in ward three days. On the third day he changed the arrangement: keep one, let the other nine take grain back to relieve the famine of their households, and bring the youngest brother. Why the change? The text gives the reason: "for I fear God." This was the first confession of faith Joseph spoke before his brothers. The change of arrangement was not softness of heart; it was that a man who fears God refuses to treat people in an excessive way, even when the other party is guilty. He could have kept all ten and examined them slowly, but at home there were old people and children starving. Fearing God made his righteousness carry mercy: the examination went on as before, and the grain was released as before. Brothers and sisters, this is a lesson for everyone of us who holds a little authority: the person who fears God is stern with measure; the person who does not fear God will do even good deeds harshly.

Just as they were preparing to set out and conferring among themselves, the most important sentence of twenty-two years finally came out of their own mouths: "We are verily guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the anguish of his soul, when he besought us, and we would not hear; therefore is this distress come upon us."

"Guilty" is in the original 'asham (אָשֵׁם, H818), guilty, bearing guilt, deserving punishment [4]. This was not a sentence complaining about fate; it was a sentence of confession. They did not say our luck is bad; they did not say this Egyptian is too cruel; what they said was: we are guilty. They even remembered the details: he besought us; we saw the anguish of his soul, and we would not hear. "Saw, yet would not hear" — the scene of the sin, recounted by them without a hair's breadth of error. The conscience can sleep for twenty-two years, but it does not die in twenty-two years; it is only waiting for an occasion, for a word of interrogation, for a distress, to wake it up. What woke it today was the stern words of the Egyptian ruler and three days in prison. To awaken a person's conscience God often uses not thunder, but a situation one cannot get around: you are placed in the very position you once placed another, and only then do you suddenly understand what exactly you did in that year. In that year they treated their brother's pleading as wind past the ear; today, pleading their case in a foreign palace, no one would hear them either — between this requiting and being requited, God let them experience in their own bodies what it means for pleading to find no door.

At this point Reuben interjected: "Spake I not unto you, saying, Do not sin against the child; and ye would not hear? Therefore, behold, also his blood is required." The elder brother's words were half innocence and half evasion: in that year he truly had advised them, and truly had intended to come back and rescue Joseph; but after advising, where did he go? He did not persist to the end, did not tell his father the truth, and joined the others in acting out the play for twenty-two years. Reuben's tragedy is the tragedy of many people: they understand right and wrong perfectly, but at the critical moment refuse to pay the price; afterwards they are the most skilled at saying, I said so long ago. Brothers and sisters, having advised does not equal being guiltless; having objected in one's heart does not equal having clean hands. When the conscience is awakened, even a half-step like Reuben's must be entered in the account. Yet we must also be fair: at least at this moment the old matter was being brought up by someone, and this family's silence of twenty-two years cracked open from this day. Once words of confession are spoken, even carrying evasion, they are stronger than twenty-two years of dead silence — because light enters precisely through such cracks.

While they were speaking, there was one more person standing by who heard everything: "And they knew not that Joseph understood them; for he spake unto them by an interpreter." The interpreter was Joseph's arrangement; the Joseph on stage did not understand Hebrew, but the Joseph off stage understood every word. When he heard that sentence, "we are verily guilty concerning our brother," "he turned himself about from them, and wept." Please engrave this weeping in your heart. The man interrogating them wept first. What did he weep for? He wept for this sentence of confession, finally awaited after twenty-two years; he wept that his own pleading in his youth had in fact been heard by his brothers, and remembered to this day; and he wept that these ten men had changed — they had begun to know how to confess. Tears here are the seal of the turning point: the hard side did not remain hard to the end, and the examining side softened first. After weeping, he wiped his tears, returned, and had Simeon bound before their eyes and kept as a hostage. Note the order: first weeping, then binding. His sternness, having been washed by tears, was not revenge but discipline; his arrangements, carrying tears, never for one step departed from the original intention of saving this whole family.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Silver Returned and Jacob's Refusal — Grace Within Fear, and a Wound Not Yet Healed (42:25-38)

When the interrogation reached a pause, Joseph commanded to fill their sacks with corn, and did something no one would have imagined: "restore every man's money into his sack, and to give them provision for the way." The silver returned in full — this was grace hidden in the baggage, not yet signed with a name. Brothers and sisters, God's grace often comes to people first in this way: before recognizing who He is, one first discovers in one's sack something that should not be there; before learning gratitude, one first learns fear.

Sure enough, at the lodging place one of them opened his sack to give his ass provender and saw that his money was in his sack's mouth, and said to his brothers, my money is restored. Their reaction at that moment was not joy, but "their heart failed them, and they were afraid," saying one to another, "What is this that God hath done unto us?" Free of charge should have been good news, but for a group of men whose conscience had just been awakened, grace of unknown origin was more frightening than a bill. Their theology at this moment was remarkably unanimous: behind all of this was God. The interrogation was God, the prison was God, and now the silver in the sacks was also God. Sinners look at the world and everywhere see requital; they did not yet understand that God's hand could also be a hand preparing reunion. But this step was already remarkable: for the first time in twenty-two years they connected what befell them with God, and for the first time trembled over a small matter. The beginning of reverence often is not having figured things out, but first knowing fear. A person with no fear of God at all has a conscience that cannot be awakened; a person who begins to know fear has a story with a turning point in it.

They returned to Canaan and reported the whole journey to their father, item by item: the lord of the land had spoken roughly, had kept Simeon, and required Benjamin himself to go down before he would release him and sell them grain again. After the report, one last blow awaited the whole family: as they emptied their sacks, they found that every man's bundle of money was in his sack, and "when both they and their father saw the bundles of money, they were afraid." Nine sacks, nine bundles of silver, not a fraction short. This was no oversight; it was the deliberate arrangement of that ruler. Jacob's household stood before the silver and feared together — they dimly sensed that a hand they did not know was pushing their whole family in a direction they did not want to go. Brothers and sisters, have you had such a moment: thing after thing, coinciding beyond coincidence, pushing you to face the person and the matter you least want to face? That is most likely not fate playing tricks on you; it is God arranging you. The hand with which He pushes people sometimes hides in returned silver.

Jacob's reaction is the most grievous passage of the whole chapter: "Me have ye bereaved of my children: Joseph is not, and Simeon is not, and ye will take Benjamin away: all these things are against me." The old father began naming names and settling accounts, each name a pang. The father bereaved of his children said, all these things are against me — in these words there was real suffering, and also an inaccurate attribution. Joseph's "not being" was not heaven playing tricks on men; it was the work of the sons standing before him. Simeon's detention was not the inconstancy of fate; it was the lesson this family had to walk through. But Jacob did not know the truth; he only knew that he was losing, one after another. The words of his old age grew more and more like Job's words, reckoning everything as a burden that had come upon himself. Reading here, let us not hurry to blame the old man's weak faith. A man who has lived twenty-two years holding a bloody coat has a fear with a history. God had compassion on such fear, and so in the story that follows God did not rebuke Jacob; He healed him with facts, little by little: first Simeon would come back, then Benjamin would come back, and at last even Joseph, whom he thought dead for twenty-two years, would stand alive before him.

Reuben stood forward once again, and this time he offered an astonishing price: "Slay my two sons, if I bring him not to thee: deliver him into my hand, and I will bring him to thee again." Reuben's surety (compare the guarantee passages of Scripture) [5] sounded resolute, yet carried a recklessness: pledging the lives of his own two sons as collateral to comfort the heart of a grandfather who had just lost a son — this account was calculated in such a way that even the comfort became a fright. Jacob refused outright, which was no surprise. Reuben's problem was not lack of zeal; it was that his surety carried no weight: in the past twenty-two years he had paid no price at all for his brother's disappearance, and his credit in this family had long been overdrawn. Brothers and sisters, a guarantee must use the credit of ordinary days as its capital. A person who remembers to declare his resolve only at the critical moment is usually already too late; words that carry true weight are redeemed by small matters, one by one, on ordinary days. In the later story, likewise a matter of surety, the words Judah spoke were completely different from Reuben's, and the father's response was completely different too — that is the matter of the next chapter; today we only bury the foreshadowing here: the headship of this family was quietly changing hands; the one willing to pledge himself for his brother is the one fit to bring his brother out. Reuben was the firstborn, yet in half-step after half-step he yielded the place of the firstborn.

Jacob's refusal was spoken with finality: "My son shall not go down with you; for his brother is dead, and he is left alone: if mischief befall him by the way in the which ye go, then shall ye bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to the grave." And the whole chapter ends on this refusal. No reunion, no reconciliation — only a father clutching his last son, refusing at all costs to let go. The story stops here, stopping in a way that blocks the heart. But this is precisely the rhythm with which God writes stories: He is not in a hurry to end where people think it should end. Jacob said he shall not go down, but the famine would not stop because of a father's refusal — the grain at home would be eaten up, Benjamin would go down in the end, and Joseph's tears would in the end be wiped on his father's neck. The lesson this chapter leaves the congregation lies precisely in this unfinished ending.

First, listen to your conscience early; do not wait for it to be awakened by distress. The brothers' confession was twenty-two years late, and in those twenty-two years the father wept, the younger brother was in a foreign land, and their own household lived its days in fear. The most expensive thing about sin is the time it steals. If in your conscience there is still an apology never spoken and an old account never settled, do not learn from them and wait until prison and interrogation arrive to open your mouth. Confession is the door to the turning point; put the other way, it is equally true: without confession, the turning point cannot come in.

Second, before being recognized by people, first learn to wait for God's time. Joseph recognized everyone, yet held himself back and did not lift the veil ahead of time. Very often God is not refusing to let us be reconciled; the time has not yet come: the other's conscience has not yet awakened, and the conditions for reunion are not yet complete. To govern one's own hand in the waiting — not running ahead, not taking revenge, not proving oneself — is the lesson those whom God uses must finish. If in your hand too you are clutching an old account, a truth, a chance to overturn everything, ask God first: has Your time come? If the time has not come, tears may flow, but the hand must be held back.

Third, refusing God's arrangement often comes from being wounded, not from unbelief. Jacob's "shall not" was wounds in every word. When in the church we meet people who have shut their hearts tightly, do not first condemn their little faith; first see their bloody coat. Everyone may be holding a bloody coat of twenty-two years in his arms. Stand with him for a while; wait for the famine to walk the road through; wait for God to lay out the facts — God has patience, and we must have patience too. And if we ourselves are precisely the one holding the bloody coat, may God also give us a measure of courage today: the day we hand over our last Benjamin may be precisely the day the whole family's reunion begins.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Genesis chapter 42: famine pushed ten sons down to Egypt, interrogation pushed a sin of twenty-two years onto the stage, the returned silver pushed fear back to Canaan, and a father's refusal stopped the story halfway. Joseph knew them, and they did not know him; yet precisely in the place of not knowing, God let confession happen first. "We are verily guilty concerning our brother" — this sentence was the first true words this family had spoken in twenty-two years, and also the first brick of reunion. Brothers and sisters, do not fear God's awakening of your conscience; His awakening of the conscience is never the end of the story, but the entrance of grace.

Let us pray together: Lord, You know us more clearly than we know ourselves. Our old accounts, our bloody coats, that sentence of guilt we cannot speak — You know them all. Do not let our conscience sleep on; awaken us in Your time and by Your method; and teach us, like Joseph, when we have the power to settle accounts, first to learn to wait, and in sternness not to lose our tears. May this family of ours, and this church, starting from one honest confession, walk into the reunion You have prepared. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[5] Note on surety (compare 'arev / 'arav): Hebrew עָרֵב (root 'arav, H6148 related forms, to give surety, to guarantee); in this chapter, at 42:37, Reuben pledged the lives of his two sons to Jacob as surety, in words impulsive and ineffective; at Genesis 43:9, Judah instead pledged himself to bear the blame forever before his father as surety, and Jacob finally permitted Benjamin to go with them. This note serves only as a foreshadowing comparison; the exposition of this chapter does not cross into the content of chapter 43; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT, and the comparative judgment is this manuscript's exposition.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And they said one to another, We are verily guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the anguish of his soul, when he besought us, and we would not hear; therefore is this distress come upon us." (Genesis 42:21, KJV)

For twenty-two years Joseph's brothers had learned everything except how to say this one sentence. Now, in the palace in Egypt, after the interrogation and the prison, the sentence finally came out of their own mouths. Confession never sounds pleasant, yet it was the first brick of this family's reunion: only when sin is spoken aloud can it be forgiven, and only when the conscience is awakened do tears know where to flow. When Joseph heard it, he turned away and wept — the man interrogating them had waited twenty-two years for this sentence. Brother, sister, is there also a sentence pressed down in your heart for a long time — an apology, an old account you dare not touch? Do not wait for the next famine or the next interrogation. While it is still today, bring it before God and speak it honestly. Start today with this one honest sentence, and give your heart back to the God who has always known us and has always been waiting for us, and let Him Himself lead us through the road home. God awakens our conscience not to push us away, but precisely to bring us back. Confession is a door, and behind the door is the reunion He has long prepared.

Deuteronomy 24 is a miscellany within the middle of the legal detail (chs. 12–26): a bill of divorcement, a newlywed, a millstone, a man-stealer, leprosy, Miriam, a pledge, wages, collective punishment, perverted judgment, a forgotten sheaf, olives, grapes — thirteen scenes in twenty-two verses. Beneath the miscellany run two threads. One is "remember": remember Miriam in v. 9, and twice remember "that thou wast a bondman in Egypt" in vv. 18 and 22. The other is the list of the weak: the wife who is sent away, the new bride, the neighbour who borrows, the poor man, the hired servant, the stranger, the fatherless, the widow — the edge of these regulations almost always falls on the hand of the one who has property against the one who has no support. Verse 13, at the chapter's exact centre, gives all of this a definition: return the poor man's pledge before the sun goes down, and "it shall be righteousness unto thee before the Lord thy God."

## Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Fact Layer] The chapter opens with a bill of divorcement: "When a man hath taken a wife, and married her, and it come to pass that she find no favour in his eyes, because he hath found some uncleanness in her: then let him write her a bill of divorcement, and give it in her hand, and send her out of his house." (24:1) Then the scene changes again and again: the newlywed stays at home, the millstone is not taken to pledge, the stolen brother is to be recovered, leprosy is handled as the priests teach, Miriam's case is to be remembered; the pledge does not stay overnight, wages do not wait for sunset, the death penalty does not reach the children, judgment is not perverted against the stranger, and the forgotten sheaf is not fetched back. In twenty-two verses one can count thirteen groups of regulations; the prohibitions — counted in the KJV by their prohibitive clauses ("may not," "shall not," "shalt not," "No man shall," "neither shall," "nor") — number sixteen (Note 2), the chapter's most-used form of words.

[Theological Development Layer] One general inference is set down first (this is an inference): the common object of the chapter's thirteen groups of regulations is "the person who has the capacity to harm the weak" — the husband toward the wife, the creditor toward the debtor, the employer toward the hired servant, the landowner toward the gleaner, the propertied toward the stranger. What the law does repeatedly in this chapter is draw a line for the stronger hand: you may exercise your right, but the right has a boundary. The ground of the boundary is written in the twice-repeated closing sentence: "thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman in Egypt" (24:18, 22).

[Summary Layer] Deuteronomy 24: thirteen groups of regulations in twenty-two verses; remembering that you were a bondman is the root, the list of the weak is the direction, drawing a boundary for the strong hand is the business; and one sentence at the centre serves as the definition: return the pledge before the sun goes down — it shall be righteousness unto thee.

## 1. The Bill of Divorcement and the Bar on Taking Her Again (Deut 24:1–4; 05:00–16:00)

[Fact Layer] These four verses form one Masoretic unit (samekh closing at the end of v. 4) and, syntactically, one long case sentence: vv. 1–3 are all conditional clauses, and the main clause lands in v. 4. The order of the case is: first, after a man has taken a wife, he "hath found some uncleanness in her" and "she find no favour in his eyes" — the "some uncleanness" is in the original ʿerwat dāḇār (ʿerwâ, H6172; dāḇār, H1697), literally "some shameful thing," and the text gives no further definition; "find no favour" in the original is "she finds no favour in his eyes" (ḥēn, H2580). Second, a three-step procedure: "let him write her a bill of divorcement, and give it in her hand, and send her out of his house" — the bill of divorcement (sēper kərîṯûṯ, H5612 + H3748) is literally a "bill of cutting off," and all three steps are expressly recorded. Third: "And when she is departed out of his house, she may go and be another man's wife." (24:2) Fourth, the two later paths: "And if the latter husband hate her, and write her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand, and sendeth her out of his house; or if the latter husband die, which took her to be his wife;" (24:3) — only now does the main clause fall: "Her former husband, which sent her away, may not take her again to be his wife, after that she is defiled; for that is abomination before the LORD: and thou shalt not cause the land to sin, which the LORD thy God giveth thee for an inheritance." (24:4) The section's only prohibition is that the former husband may not take her again; the grounds are twofold: "that is abomination before the LORD" (tôʿēḇâ, H8441), and the defiling of the land (ṭāmēʾ, H2930; inheritance, nāḥălâ, H5159). What must be kept distinct: the text does not command divorce, and it does not define "some uncleanness"; what it establishes is a procedure (there must be a written bill) and a prohibition (he may not take her again).

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the weight of this section's law lies not on "whether divorce is permitted" but on "after the divorce" (this is an inference): three and a half of the four verses lay out the scenario, and the law's teeth fall only in the final sentence — he may not take her again. Reading this section as either a licence for divorce or an endorsement of divorce misses where the main clause stands; the section adds a written procedure and a bar on turning back to a practice that already existed (divorce). Second, that the bill falls "in her hand" is a detail that may not be passed over (this is an inference): the document is not the husband's memorandum; it is the woman's evidence in her own hand — what she carries away when she leaves his house is a document that can prove her status and that she may marry again. Third, the direction of "defiled" in this section must be registered according to the original (this is an inference): the verb in v. 4, "after that she is defiled," is in the passive form — she is the one placed in the defiled position, not the agent of defilement; the prohibition's object is the former husband ("may not take her again"), and the land's defilement is stated as consequence.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, the interpretation of ʿerwat dāḇār is this section's longest-running discussion: in the Jewish tradition the school of Shammai read it as a matter of unchastity, the school of Hillel read it broadly as anything that displeased the husband, and Akiva broader still, down to "he has found another fairer than she." The readings are registered only; the sermon text follows the base text's literal wording only. Second, two readings of this section's syntax are set side by side: (a) the long-conditional reading — vv. 1–3 as a whole are a hypothetical scenario, and the only normative content is the prohibition of v. 4, so "let him write her a bill of divorcement" belongs to the scenario's description, not to an authorization; (b) the procedural-permission reading — vv. 1–3 are read as regulating the divorce procedure. The two readings stand side by side, registered only, with no ruling. Third, a register of the ancient Near Eastern comparison for the bill of divorcement: divorce documents genuinely existed in ancient Near Eastern legal life (their existence is registered only; no clauses are quoted); scholarship discusses both the document's evidentiary and protective function for women, and the fact that the section as a whole stands within a patriarchal structure — both sides registered only, with no ruling.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Four registrations, strictly registration only — no reading backwards, and no ruling on contemporary marriages. First, the echo within Deuteronomy 22: Deut 22:19, of the man who brings an evil name upon a bride — "and she shall be his wife; he may not put her away all his days"; Deut 22:29, of the man who humbles a damsel not betrothed — "and she shall be his wife; because he hath humbled her, he may not put her away all his days" (both clauses KJV verbatim; the full verses stand in the anchor file). The two occurrences of "he may not put her away all his days" and this section's "write her a bill of divorcement … and send her out" form a within-book comparison (the "put away" and "send away" are the same word in the original, šālaḥ, Note 4): in those two cases the power to send away exercised in this section is revoked for life — comparison registered only, the passages are not used to gloss each other, and no ruling is made. Second, the echo in Matt 19:3–9: the Pharisees frame their question around "put away" (19:3, 7 registered by verse reference only), and two sentences in Jesus' answer were verified directly: "Moses because of the hardness of your hearts suffered you to put away your wives: but from the beginning it was not so." Fourth, Mal 2:13–16, on dealing treacherously with the wife of one's youth (registered by verse reference and theme only) — set alongside, registered only, with no ruling.

[Summary Layer] Section 1: one case in four verses — he takes a wife, finds some uncleanness in her, writes a bill of divorcement and gives it in her hand; the woman marries again; the latter husband either divorces her or dies; and the section's only prohibition falls at the end: the former husband may not take her again.

## 2. What May Not Be Taken — a Man's Life: The Newlywed, the Millstone, and a Brother (Deut 24:5–7; 16:00–26:00)

[Fact Layer] These three verses are each their own Masoretic unit (samekh at the end of both v. 5 and v. 6; v. 7 is the first half of unit 7–8), and the three regulations concern, respectively, time, a tool, and a person. Verse 5, on the newlywed: "When a man hath taken a new wife, he shall not go out to war, neither shall he be charged with any business: but he shall be free at home one year, and shall cheer up his wife which he hath taken." — Two exemptions: going out to war (in the original, "going out to the host," milḥāmâ, H4421) and "any business"; the state is "free at home" (nāqî, H5355) for one year; the purpose clause is "shall cheer up his wife which he hath taken" (śāmaḥ, H8055, piel form). Verse 6, on the millstone: "No man shall take the nether or the upper millstone to pledge: for he taketh a man's life to pledge." — The millstone (reḥayim, H7347) and the upper millstone (rāḵeḇ, H7393) are banned alike; the reason clause calls the thing a life (nepeš, H5315): to take the millstone is to take a man's life to pledge. Verse 7, on man-stealing: "If a man be found stealing any of his brethren of the children of Israel, and maketh merchandise of him, or selleth him; then that thief shall die; and thou shalt put evil away from among you." — "Stealing" (gānaḇ, H1589) is literally "to steal"; the object is limited to "any of his brethren of the children of Israel"; the offence has two forms: he "maketh merchandise of him," or "selleth him" (mākar, H4376); the penalty is that the thief "shall die" (mûṯ, H4191, hophal form); the close is the put-away-evil formula (bāʿar, H1197).

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the common object of these three regulations is the three things that stand under the word "life" (this is an inference): the year of a new marriage, the stone that grinds the flour, a brother's free body — in the text's accounting, none of the three may be requisitioned, distrained, or sold. Verse 6 says the accounting aloud: the millstone is a "life," because a household's daily bread comes out from under that stone. Second, the exemption of v. 5 is a whole year's allowance given to the new household (this is an inference): what is lifted is not only military service but "any business" as well — public life's power to requisition the individual is suspended wholesale in the first year of a marriage; and the purpose clause does not land on the husband but on "shall cheer up his wife which he hath taken": the beneficiary expressly recorded is the wife. Third, the wording of v. 7 is worth pausing over at the word "steal" (this is an inference): stealing a brother shares a root in the original with the verb for stealing goods — the law files man-stealing under theft because the stolen person's status is first degraded to merchandise; to steal a brother is to steal a life, and it belongs to the same "life" account as v. 6.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, the relation between 24:5 and Deut 20:7 is, per discipline, set side by side only: Deut 20:7 is a proclamation before battle — "And what man is there that hath betrothed a wife, and hath not taken her? let him go and return unto his house, lest he die in the battle, and another man take her." (Deut 20:7, KJV verbatim) — its object is a man betrothed but not yet married, its moment is the eve of battle, its ground is the fear he may die; 24:5's object is a man who has taken a new wife, its form is a year-system of being free at home, its ground is that he cheer up his wife. One is a wartime exemption proclamation, the other a post-marriage year-system — side by side only; the exemptions of Deut 20:5–6 and the mirror sentence of Deut 28:30 are registered by verse reference only. Second, Exod 21:16 set side by side: "And he that stealeth a man, and selleth him, or if he be found in his hand, he shall surely be put to death." (Exod 21:16, KJV verbatim) — there the object is not limited to "brethren," the offence has the additional form "if he be found in his hand," and the close has no put-away-evil formula; here the object is limited, the offence has two forms, and the formula is present. The two are set side by side only, with no ruling. Third, a material register on the millstone: the stone mill was the basic household tool for the daily processing of grain, and flour was what the day's food depended on — registered as material background only.

[New Testament Reading Layer] One registration. ## 3. Take Heed, Remember, and the Pledge (Deut 24:8–13; 26:00–38:00)

[Fact Layer] These six verses comprise three Masoretic units — v. 8 is the second half of unit 7–8, v. 9 is its own unit, and vv. 10–13 are a whole unit. First, disease and remembrance (vv. 8–9): "Take heed in the plague of leprosy, that thou observe diligently, and do according to all that the priests the Levites shall teach you: as I commanded them, so ye shall observe to do." (24:8) — Plague (negaʿ, H5061) and leprosy (ṣāraʿaṯ, H6883) are named together; authority to deal with it lies with the priests the Levites (kōhēn, H3548; lēwî, H3881): their office is to teach (yārâ, H3384, hiphil form), and the people's duty is to take heed, observe diligently, and do (šāmar, H8104). The example that follows immediately is a command to remember: "Remember what the LORD thy God did unto Miriam by the way, after that ye were come forth out of Egypt." (24:9) — Remember (zākar, H2142) occurs three times in this chapter; this is the first. "By the way" points to the wilderness journey. Then the four pledge verses (vv. 10–13): "When thou dost lend thy brother any thing, thou shalt not go into his house to fetch his pledge." (24:10) — Lend (lāwâ, H3867); KJV "thy brother," where the CUNPSS base reads "neighbour" (rēaʿ, H7453; base-text entry 7); pledge (ʿăḇôṭ, H5667); the line is drawn at his house door. The procedure follows: "Thou shalt stand abroad, and the man to whom thou dost lend shall bring out the pledge abroad unto thee." (24:11) A poor man's pledge has its own clause: "And if the man be poor, thou shalt not sleep with his pledge:" (24:12) — Poor (ʿānî, H6041). The deadline for return and its ground are in v. 13: "In any case thou shalt deliver him the pledge again when the sun goeth down, that he may sleep in his own raiment, and bless thee: and it shall be righteousness unto thee before the LORD thy God." — Raiment (śalmâ, H8008) in the original is "his garment"; the effect is threefold: he sleeps in it, he blesses you, and it is counted righteousness before God.

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the line in the four pledge verses stands on the threshold (this is an inference): the debt is real and the pledge is real, and the law abolishes neither — it only stops the creditor's hand outside his house door: you may stand abroad and wait; he brings it out to you from inside. Second, v. 13 gives "righteousness" an operable definition (this is an inference): righteousness is not an emotion, not a stance, but the walk back with the garment before sunset — it has a time (sunset), an object (the poor man), an action (deliver it again to him), and effects (he sleeps, he blesses, God counts it righteousness). The chapter's title comes from here, and the reason is here: in twenty-two verses, God himself gives the word "righteousness" to an evening act. Third, the juxtaposition of vv. 8–9 has its own logic (this is an inference): on leprosy the text gives no diagnosis; it gives authority and procedure — follow what the priests teach; then it immediately gives a negative remembrance: Miriam. In Numbers 12, Miriam's disease is bound up with her speaking against the leader, speaking beyond her place (theme registered only); this chapter sets "remember" after the disease regulation: dealing with the plague rests, first, on observing the teaching, and second, on remembering the earlier warning.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, a register of the semantic range of ṣāraʿaṯ: in Lev 13–14 the word covers skin conditions and mildew on garments and houses (verse references registered only); its reference is broader than leprosy in modern medicine; the base text's rendering is an inherited translation name, and the text is not revised backwards by a modern disease name. Second, Numbers 12 set side by side: Miriam and Aaron speak against Moses; Miriam becomes leprous and is shut out of the camp seven days, and the people wait for her (Num 12:10, 13, 14, 15, registered by verse reference and theme only, no quotation) — the narrative is there, the example is here; side by side only, with no ruling. Third, Exod 22:26–27 set side by side: "If thou at all take thy neighbour's raiment to pledge, thou shalt deliver it unto him by that the sun goeth down:" (Exod 22:26, KJV verbatim) "For that is his covering only, it is his raiment for his skin: wherein shall he sleep? and it shall come to pass, when he crieth unto me, that I will hear; for I am gracious." (Exod 22:27, KJV verbatim) — there only one pledge, a garment, is in view, and the ground rests on "his covering only" and on God's hearing the cry; here the rule extends to any pledge whatever, the poor man's clause is listed separately, and the effects add "bless thee" and "righteousness." The two are set side by side only, with no ruling.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Two registrations. First, a register of the vocabulary echo of righteousness: Gen 15:6, "And he believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness." (KJV verbatim), and Deut 6:25, on keeping the commandments, "it shall be our righteousness" (verse reference registered only) — the three "righteousness" accounts each have their own context (believing, keeping, returning a pledge); registered side by side only, not harmonized, not used to gloss each other. Second, the echo of the word "neighbour" in Matt 22:39 and in the narrative of Luke 10 (verse references and theme registered only); no settled relation to the pledge regulation is asserted.

[Summary Layer] Section 3: in leprosy, follow what the priests teach, and remember what was done to Miriam; when you lend to your brother (CUNPSS: your neighbour), do not go into his house to fetch his pledge, and a poor man's pledge shall not stay the night — in any case deliver him the pledge again when the sun goeth down: it shall be righteousness unto thee.

## 4. Wages, Guilt, and the Vulnerable (Deut 24:14–18; 38:00–49:00)

[Fact Layer] These five verses comprise three Masoretic units — 14–15, 16, and 17–18 (for the samekh at the unit ends, see the opening note). First, the hired servant (vv. 14–15): "Thou shalt not oppress an hired servant that is poor and needy, whether he be of thy brethren, or of thy strangers that are in thy land within thy gates:" (24:14) — The hired servant (śāḵîr, H7916) is described by two stacked words, poor (ʿānî, H6041) and needy (ʾeḇyôn, H34); the objects are two classes: "thy brethren" and "thy strangers that are in thy land within thy gates" (gēr, H1616; the CUNPSS base shortens this to "sojourning in your town," base-text entry 9); the prohibition is "thou shalt not oppress" (ʿāšaq, H6231). The rule for paying the hire: "At his day thou shalt give him his hire, neither shall the sun go down upon it; for he is poor, and setteth his heart upon it: lest he cry against thee unto the LORD, and it be sin unto thee." (24:15) — Hire (śāḵār, H7939; KJV "hired servant" in v. 14 and "his hire" in v. 15); "setteth his heart upon it" in the original is "he lifts up his nepeš toward it" (nepeš, Note 4); he cries unto the LORD, and the result is "it be sin unto thee" — sin (ḥēṭʾ, H2399) is here defined by withheld wages. The next verse is a judicial principle (v. 16): "The fathers shall not be put to death for the children, neither shall the children be put to death for the fathers: every man shall be put to death for his own sin." — Fathers (ʾāḇ, H1) and children (bēn, H1121) are balanced in both directions, and the scope of those put to death is limited to "his own sin." The last group is the vulnerable three (vv. 17–18): "Thou shalt not pervert the judgment of the stranger, nor of the fatherless; nor take a widow's raiment to pledge:" (24:17) — Pervert (nāṭâ, H5186, hiphil form) the judgment (mišpāṭ, H4941); the stranger and the fatherless (yāṯôm, H3490) stand before the judgment seat, the widow's (ʾalmānâ, H490) raiment (beḡeḏ, H899) on the pledge shelf — both banned in the same verse. The close is the remembrance formula: "But thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman in Egypt, and the LORD thy God redeemed thee thence: therefore I command thee to do this thing." (24:18) — Redeemed (pādâ, H6299): the regulation of v. 17 is grounded in the salvation history of v. 18.

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, v. 15 classifies withheld wages as sin, not as a dispute (this is an inference): when the employer delays the hire, the hired man has no strike and no lawsuit to file first — the path the text gives him is "cry … unto the LORD," and the verdict it gives the employer is "it be sin unto thee." Sunset is the chapter's moral clock: in v. 13 the pledge is limited by sunset, in v. 15 the hire is limited by sunset — how the propertied man's day ends is seen in what of another's he is still holding. Second, the position of v. 16 in this section is no idle stroke (this is an inference): regulations protecting the weak stand before and after it, and in the middle a judicial boundary is erected — guilt is personal, collective punishment is abolished. Without this boundary, protecting the weak would turn into a new name for the strong oppressing the weak; in its most merciful section, the law also binds the hand of vengeance. Third, the syntax of vv. 17–18 is "regulation + remembrance" (this is an inference): no reasoning first, the prohibition first, and only then "thou shalt remember" — remembrance is not a decorative ending but the source of enforcement: a nation that remembers it was a bondman in Egypt will hear the stranger's case as its own case. The "redeemed" of v. 18, pādâ, is the chapter's only occurrence of a verb of deliverance, and it does not land on the Exodus itself — it lands before the judgment seat.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Four items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, Lev 19:13 set side by side: "Thou shalt not defraud thy neighbour, neither rob him: the wages of him that is hired shall not abide with thee all night until the morning." (Lev 19:13, KJV verbatim) — there it reads "shall not abide with thee all night until the morning," while this section reads "at his day … neither shall the sun go down upon it," and adds both the reason clause (he setteth his heart upon it) and the sin clause; the two are set side by side only. Second, a register of v. 16 alongside the tradition of collective punishment: collective and family liability genuinely existed in the ancient world (the case of Achan in Joshua 7, registered by verse reference and theme only); this verse establishes the principle of individual guilt in legal form, and its relation to that narrative is variously read by era-stratification and by genre-division — registered only, with no ruling. Third, the correspondence of Exod 22:21–27 with vv. 17–18, per discipline set side by side only: "Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him: for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt." (Exod 22:21, KJV verbatim) "Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child." (Exod 22:22, KJV verbatim) "If thou afflict them in any wise, and they cry at all unto me, I will surely hear their cry;" (Exod 22:23, KJV verbatim) "And my wrath shall wax hot, and I will kill you with the sword; and your wives shall be widows, and your children fatherless." (Exod 22:24, KJV verbatim) "If thou lend money to any of my people that is poor by thee, thou shalt not be to him as an usurer, neither shalt thou lay upon him usury." (Exod 22:25, KJV verbatim) Together with the two pledge verses of Exod 22:26–27 (already set side by side in Section 3) — there the backing is God's hearing the cry and his wrath waxing hot, and the ground is the history of sojourning in Egypt; here the ground is remembrance of the bondman history in Egypt. Correspondence set side by side only, with no ruling. Fourth, a register of the enforcement echo of v. 16: 2 Kings 14:6 records that King Amaziah slew the murderers of his father "but the children of the murderers he slew not," and the quotation stands within that verse: "The fathers shall not be put to death for the children, neither shall the children be put to death for the fathers; but every man shall be put to death for his own sin." (2 Kings 14:6, quoted portion, KJV verbatim) — one explicit quotation-and-execution of the law within the royal narrative; registered only.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Three registrations. First, the echo in James 5:4: "Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth." (James 5:4, KJV verbatim) — in one line with the "cry … unto the LORD" of v. 15; registered only, not read backwards. Second, a register of the prophetic echoes in Ezek 18:20 and Jer 31:29–30: "The soul that sinneth, it shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him." (Ezek 18:20, KJV verbatim); Jer 31:29–30, on the abolition of the sour-grapes proverb (verse reference registered only) — both stand with v. 16 on the individualizing of guilt; registered side by side only. Third, the within-book echo in Deut 10:18: "He doth execute the judgment of the fatherless and widow, and loveth the stranger, in giving him food and raiment." (Deut 10:18, KJV verbatim); and the curse sentence of Deut 27:19 and the judgment warning of Deut 16:19 (verse references registered only) — registered only, not used to gloss each other.

[Summary Layer] Section 4: the hired servant's hire is paid at his day, not to wait for sunset; those put to death die only for their own sin, and a son does not die for his father; the judgment of the stranger and the fatherless is not perverted, and a widow's raiment is not taken to pledge.

## 5. What Is Left Behind at Harvest (Deut 24:19–22; 49:00–57:00)

[Fact Layer] These four verses comprise two Masoretic units — 19–20 (samekh at the end of v. 20) and 21–22 (samekh at the end of v. 22, closing the chapter). Three harvests, three "not agains." The first is the forgotten sheaf in the field: "When thou cuttest down thine harvest in thy field, and hast forgot a sheaf in the field, thou shalt not go again to fetch it: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow: that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all the work of thine hands." (24:19) — Cut down (qāṣar, H7114), harvest (qāṣîr, H7105), forgot (šākaḥ, H7911), a sheaf (ʿōmer, H6016); the effect is the promise of blessing (bārak; at v. 19, "may bless thee," base-text entry 12). The second is the olive: "When thou beatest thine olive tree, thou shalt not go over the boughs again: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow." (24:20) — Beat (ḥāḇaṭ, H2251) the olive tree (zayiṯ, H2132); the original pāʾēr (H6286) literally means to strip the branches bare (Note 8); the KJV renders it "thou shalt not go over the boughs again." The third is the grape: "When thou gatherest the grapes of thy vineyard, thou shalt not glean it afterward: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow." (24:21) — Gather (bāṣar, H1219), vineyard (kerem, H3754); the original ʿôlēl (H5953) refers to the gleaning of what remains after the first gathering (Note 8); the KJV renders it "thou shalt not glean it afterward." The close is remembrance again: "And thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman in the land of Egypt: therefore I command thee to do this thing." (24:22) — against v. 18, there is no verb of redemption here (Note 3).

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, this section's manner of giving is "not taking," not "handing out" (this is an inference): the regulation does not order the landowner to measure out so much from the barn to the poor; it orders one less pass at harvest — the forgotten sheaf is not fetched back, the boughs are not beaten again, the vineyard is not gleaned again. Before the leavings ever become the landowner's property, they are already assigned to the three classes; mercy here is a step in the harvest process, not goodwill after the harvest. Second, the order of the three regulations' objects does not vary by a word (this is an inference): the stranger, the fatherless, the widow — repeated in the same order three times in vv. 19, 20, 21, and the end of the chapter joins head-to-tail with the list in Section 4, v. 17: Section 4 protects their rights in litigation and their raiment; Section 5 protects their food. Third, the remembrance of v. 22 is shorter than that of v. 18 (this is an inference): the "redeemed" sentence is missing — not an abbreviation, but a different landing place: the remembrance of v. 18 upholds justice; the remembrance of v. 22 lands on farm work — a man reaping in his field is to remember, the better his harvest, how he came out of the land of bondage. The forgetfulness that abundance most easily breeds is exactly what the law here stands guard against.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, Lev 19:9–10 and 23:22 set side by side: "And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corners of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleanings of thy harvest." (Lev 19:9, KJV verbatim) "And thou shalt not glean thy vineyard, neither shalt thou gather every grape of thy vineyard; thou shalt leave them for the poor and stranger: I am the LORD your God." (Lev 19:10, KJV verbatim) "And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not make clean riddance of the corners of thy field when thou reapest, neither shalt thou gather any gleaning of thy harvest: thou shalt leave them unto the poor, and to the stranger: I am the LORD your God." (Lev 23:22, KJV verbatim) — there the command is to leave the corners and the gleanings, the objects are "the poor and stranger," and the backing is "I am the LORD your God"; here the command is not to gather a second time, the objects are "the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow," and the backing is the promise of blessing and the remembrance of Egypt. The two groups are set side by side only, with no ruling. Second, the narrative of Ruth 2 set side by side: Boaz commands his young men, "And let fall also some of the handfuls of purpose for her, and leave them, that she may glean them, and rebuke her not." (Ruth 2:16, KJV verbatim) — in the narrative a landowner enacts the spirit of this section, and exceeds its letter (handfuls pulled out of the sheaves for her); Ruth's status is precisely that of a sojourning widow (theme registered only). Set side by side only, with no ruling. Third, a register of the two senses of ʿōmer: it is also the name of a measure (Exodus 16, verse reference registered only); in this verse it carries the sheaf sense, "a sheaf," and the two are not used to gloss each other.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Two registrations. First, a register of the echo in 2 Cor 9:6–8: "Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver." (2 Cor 9:7, KJV verbatim), together with 9:6, "he which soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; and he which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully," and 9:8, "God is able to make all grace abound toward you; that ye, always having all sufficiency in all things, may abound to every good work" (phrases KJV verbatim, full verses in the anchor file) — the New Testament teaching on giving and this section's promise of blessing are set side by side in the reading layer only; registered only. Second, the echoes in Luke 6:35, 38 and 1 John 3:17–18 (verse references and theme registered only): lending hoping for nothing again, and opening a heart of compassion to a brother in need, mirror this section's "leave it" ethic — registered only, not read backwards.

[Summary Layer] Section 5: the sheaf forgotten in the field — do not go back to fetch it; the beaten olive boughs — do not go over them again; the gathered vineyard — do not glean it afterward: what is left belongs to the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow. The chapter also ends as one of its threads began, with remembrance: you were a bondman in the land of Egypt.

## Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Fact Layer] A recount of the whole chapter: the bill of divorcement and the bar on taking her again, one case in four verses (vv. 1–4); the newlywed, the millstone, and man-stealing — three things that may not be taken (vv. 5–7); leprosy, Miriam, and the four pledge verses (vv. 8–13); wages, guilt, and the vulnerable three (vv. 14–18); the three harvests and the remembrance close (vv. 19–22). "Remember" occurs three times, set at two places — mid-chapter and chapter-end (v. 9; vv. 18, 22) — and serves as the chapter's structural rivet.

[Theological Development Layer] One closing general inference (this is an inference): this chapter's righteousness is righteousness with a sunset deadline. Verse 13 says returning the pledge is righteousness; v. 15 says clearing the hire at his day is righteousness; v. 19 says not going back for the forgotten sheaf brings blessing — righteousness in this chapter is never abstract: it is an evening, a cash payment, a field not reaped clean. And the engine of it all is one sentence: remember that thou wast a bondman in Egypt. Memory is the master key to every regulation in this chapter.

[Summary Layer] Conclusion: Deuteronomy 24 — the bill of divorcement must be written clearly, and the former husband may not take her again; the newlywed is exempt for a year, the millstone is not taken to pledge, a brother may not be stolen and sold; the pledge is returned before sunset and the hire cleared before sunset; sin belongs to the person himself, and the harvest is not reaped clean.

## Devotional Extract

The most moving word in Deuteronomy 24 is a time of day: sunset. A poor man has left his garment with you as a pledge, and the law says: give it back before the sun goes down — not when the debt is settled, but before dark, because he must sleep in it tonight. A hired man has worked your field all day, and the law says: pay him his hire that same day, before the sun goes down — his family's supper is in that wage. You are reaping your harvest and a sheaf is forgotten in the field, and the law says: do not go back for it — it already belongs to the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow. The God of this chapter does not stay above us with great abstractions; He stands at your doorway, at your strongbox, at the edge of your field, governing the small things that must be done before sunset. Why so much detail? Because He remembers, and He commands you to remember: you were a bondman in Egypt. You too have waited for a wage, slept without a covering, and gleaned at the edge of another man's field. Now the field is yours. How you treat the portion of the weak in your hand shows whether you still remember Egypt. The first year of a marriage is left to the wife; the millstone stays in the mill; a brother may not be stolen away; a case may not be bent against the poor: all the tenderness of this chapter is hidden in these "thou shalt nots." May we all live before sunset: return what must be returned, pay what must be paid, leave what must be left. "In any case thou shalt deliver him the pledge again when the sun goeth down, that he may sleep in his own raiment, and bless thee: and it shall be righteousness unto thee before the Lord thy God." (Deut 24:13, KJV)

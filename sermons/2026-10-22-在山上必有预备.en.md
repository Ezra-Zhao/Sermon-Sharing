## Summary (approx. 100 words)

God tested Abraham, commanding him to take his only and beloved son Isaac to the land of Moriah to offer him as a burnt offering. Abraham rose early in the morning, split the wood, prepared the fire, and journeyed three days with his son. When Isaac asked where the lamb was, he answered: God will provide for Himself. Arriving at the place, he built an altar, laid the wood in order, bound his son, and stretched out his hand to take the knife—the Angel called out from heaven to stop him. He lifted his eyes and saw a ram caught in a thicket, took it, offered it up instead of his son, and called the name of the place Jehovah-Jireh (The Lord Will Provide). This sermon looks at three things: only when a test falls upon what is most beloved can reverence be measured; faith climbs the mountain holding unresolved questions; and when man lays down the knife, God provides on the mountain.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

Today's chapter is the mountain summit of the entire patriarchal narrative. Throughout the preceding twenty-one chapters, God called, promised, made covenants, and gave a son—leading Abraham upward all the way. Reaching this day, God speaks, demanding that he surrender the most important thing on that summit: "Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah . . . and offer him there as a burnt offering."

The Scriptures let us in on the secret first: "Now it came to pass after these things that God tested Abraham." A test—we as readers know beforehand that it is a test, but Abraham did not know. All he heard was a command. When we read this chapter, our hearts must be divided in two: one half knowing the outcome, and the other half walking with him through those three days of journey where the outcome was unknown.

Let us first read several key passages of Scripture:

"Then He said, 'Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains of which I shall tell you.'" (Gen. 22:2, excerpt)

"So Abraham rose early in the morning and saddled his donkey, and took two of his young men with him, and Isaac his son; and he split the wood for the burnt offering, and arose and went to the place of which God had told him." (Gen. 22:3, excerpt)

"But Isaac spoke to Abraham his father and said, 'My father!' And he said, 'Here I am, my son.' Then he said, 'Look, the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?' And Abraham said, 'My son, God will provide for Himself the lamb for a burnt offering.' So the two of them went together." (Gen. 22:7–8, excerpt)

"And Abraham stretched out his hand and took the knife to slay his son. But the Angel of the Lord called to him from heaven and said, 'Abraham, Abraham!' So he said, 'Here I am.'" (Gen. 22:10–11, excerpt)

"Then Abraham lifted his eyes and looked, and there behind him was a ram caught in a thicket by its horns. So Abraham went and took the ram, and offered it up for a burnt offering instead of his son." (Gen. 22:13, excerpt)

Let us pray: Lord, today we ascend Mount Moriah. We pray that You would let us see Abraham's fear of God, and also see Your provision; teach us to place what we love most into Your hands, for You Yourself will provide. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: The Test Falls on What Is Loved Most—A Single Word on a Three-Day Journey (22:1–6)

First, let us lay hold of the word "test." In the original Hebrew, it is *nasah* (H5254), meaning to test, try, or prove.[1] When God tests a person, it is not to acquire information—God knows all things. The test is so that the one being tested may see his own heart, and so that those who come after may see what faith looks like. In Exodus, God tested the Israelites with manna; Deuteronomy looks back and says: to test you, to know what was in your heart. A test is a mirror that reveals us.

Listen further to the three qualifiers in the command: "your son—your only son—Isaac, whom you love." Each weighs heavier than the last. "Only," in Hebrew *yachid* (H3173), means only, unique, or solitary.[2] Ishmael had already been sent away; according to the line of promise, Isaac was the only one. God spoke with such precision not out of cruelty, but so Abraham himself would be clear: what was being surrendered this time was not leftovers, but the very vessel of the entire promise. In the matter of sacrifice, this is what God has always measured: whether what you surrender is what you are most reluctant to part with. Saul spared the best of the oxen and sheep, and God took no pleasure in it; the widow gave two small mites, yet the Lord Jesus commended her. The altar does not weigh mass; it weighs the heart.

Regarding Abraham's response, Scripture gives only actions, not psychology: he rose early in the morning, saddled the donkey, split the wood, and set out. People often imagine that giants of faith have no struggles—yet the text says he rose "early in the morning." A man who wrestled throughout the night still walked toward the mountain as soon as day broke. The act of splitting the wood shows the deepest depth of character: splitting piece by piece, binding them together, taking them on the road. This was wood he would use himself; it was his own son being offered, and the wood was split by his own hands. Brothers and sisters, the hardest part of obedience is not shouting slogans, but splitting the wood piece by piece—it is carrying out those preparations step by step, knowing full well they lead to the altar.

A three-day journey. On the first day Beersheba could still be seen; on the second day they were still walking; only on the third day did he see the place afar off. Throughout those three days, Scripture records not a single word spoken. Imagine it: Isaac walking beside him, eating the provisions he had prepared, asking this and that. A father walking three days with the son he was to sacrifice—the Epistle to the Hebrews opens a window into heaven for us: Abraham was "concluding that God was able to raise him up, even from the dead" (Heb. 11:19, excerpt).[9] It was not that he had no answer; he walked holding onto an answer: the God who gave the son had the power to bring that son back from the dead. This faith was not mere optimism; it was calculated: God's word cannot contradict itself—He said that offspring would come through Isaac, yet He also said to offer Isaac; therefore, the only solution was resurrection. Faith means refusing to let go of either side when God's words seem to war against God's words.

When they reached the foot of the mountain, he said to the young men: "Stay here with the donkey; the lad and I will go yonder and worship, and we will come back to you." "We will come back to you"—notice the verb in this sentence; in the Hebrew original, it is in the first-person plural: "we will come back to you." He did not say "I will come back," but "we." Two went up; two would come down. These words were not a hollow excuse to appease the servants; this was his faith: this boy will come back down the mountain with me.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part 2: Where Is the Lamb?—The Father and Son Walking Together and That Answer (22:7–10)

The servants remained behind; the father placed the wood upon his son's shoulders, while he himself took the fire and the knife. "So the two of them went together"—these words appear twice in this passage, driven deep like two iron nails. In the silence, Isaac was the first to speak: "My father!" Abraham answered, "Here I am, my son." Then came one of the most heart-wrenching questions in all of Scripture: "Look, the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?"

The child asks about the offering; the father hears the knife. Abraham's answer is the key to the entire chapter: "My son, God will provide for Himself the lamb for a burnt offering." "Provide," in the original Hebrew *ra'ah* (H7200), fundamentally means to see—God will Himself see, Himself look after it. Rendering it as "provide" captures the theological meaning: God's seeing is His provision. He said this to his son, and he said it to himself as well. Having said this, "So the two of them went together"—for the second time. The question has no answer, yet the journey continues forward. Brothers and sisters, this is the normal state of faith: walking on together carrying unanswered questions. The issues in your hands today—concerning your children, your health, your business—may not necessarily have a resolution today; but you can first learn this sentence: God will provide for Himself. Say it to your family, and say it to yourself.

"Then they came to the place of which God had told him." Abraham built an altar, placed the wood in order, bound his son, and laid him on the altar upon the wood. The verbs in the text follow one after another, without pause, without sentimentality. Binding—Jewish tradition calls this passage the *Akedah*, "the binding" [10]. Isaac by this time must have been a youth, capable of carrying a bundle of wood, while his father was over a hundred years old—had he resisted, the old man might not have been able to bind him. The Bible does not record him struggling. We can only speak from the text: this son was bound by his father, and there was also the element of him lying down on his own. The text leaves a blank; we do not add drama on his behalf [8], but this silence itself inspires awe.

"And Abraham stretched out his hand and took the knife to slay his son." The narrative freezes on this single frame: the hand is stretched out, the knife is raised. Twenty-five years of waiting, a three-day journey, a lifetime of promises, all compressed into this one hand.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: Do Not Lay a Hand—The Ram, the Naming, and the Second Oath (22:11-19)

Right at this frame, heaven opens: "the Angel of the LORD called to him from heaven and said, 'Abraham, Abraham!' So he said, 'Here I am.'"—"Here I am," in Hebrew *hinneni* [6], is already the third time he has answered this way in this chapter: once to God (22:1), once to his son (22:7), and once to the angel. Whoever calls—whether it is God commanding him to offer his son, his son asking where the lamb is, or the angel restraining his hand—his answer is the exact same phrase: Here I am. A person who fears God is present and responsive toward God, toward people, and toward restraint.

The angel's words must be listened to sentence by sentence: "Do not lay your hand on the lad, or do anything to him; for now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son, your only son, from Me." "Now I know"—God did not just now acquire this information; this is the verdict of the test: through this act, fear of God became a visible reality. The Epistle of James recounts: "Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered Isaac his son on the altar?" (James 2:21). Faith and works converge here: the faith of chapter 15 grew into the form of works in chapter 22. And there is mercy in the latter half of the sentence: God specifies "your only son"—God knows full well the weight of what was offered. He never looks lightly upon what you surrender.

"Then Abraham lifted his eyes and looked"—the two times he lifted his eyes in this chapter are both entrances of grace (cf. lifting his eyes in Gen. 13). This time he saw a ram [5] caught in a thicket by its horns. He took the ram and offered it up for a burnt offering "instead of his son" [4]. Substitution—this is the deepest seed planted in this chapter: the question of the lamb has an answer, and that answer is substitution. God will provide for Himself, not by providing a well or a meal, but by providing a sheep to die in man's place. Abraham named that place Jehovah-jireh, *Yahweh-yireh* (H3070)—the LORD will provide, the LORD will see [3]. The text adds an editorial note: "as it is said to this day, 'In the Mount of the LORD it shall be provided.'" This mountain of Moriah later became the Temple Mount (2 Chron. 3:1 records Solomon building the house of the LORD in Jerusalem on Mount Moriah) [7]; and later still, on the mountains of this very region, God laid His own Son upon the wood—the difference being that this time, no angel called out to stop, because that Lamb was God Himself, provided for us. If we preach this chapter today without making this clear, we do not reach the core: the knife that Abraham lowered, God did not lower at Calvary; Isaac carrying the wood up the mountain was the type; the One carrying the cross up the mountain is the substance.

The blessing immediately follows, and it comes with the solemnity of an oath: "and said: 'By Myself I have sworn, says the LORD, because you have done this thing, and have not withheld your son, your only son—blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply your descendants as the stars of the heaven and as the sand which is on the seashore; and your descendants shall possess the gate of their enemies. In your seed all the nations of the earth shall be blessed, because you have obeyed My voice.'" (22:16-18, excerpted). Notice the final phrase: "because you have obeyed My voice." The anchor of the entire blessing is obedience. The calling is grace, the covenant is grace, but this time, God explicitly wrote "because you have obeyed" into the oath.

The ending is remarkably quiet: "So Abraham returned to his young men, and they rose and went together to Beersheba; and Abraham dwelt at Beersheba." Where is Isaac? The text makes no further mention of him coming down the mountain—the eye of the narrative remains on the father. We only remember that sentence at the foot of the mountain: "We will come back." They rose and went together—within that "together," Isaac was there.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

First, those currently walking the three-day journey. You have already received your "Moriah," the road is still long, and there is no answer yet. Learn from Abraham: split the wood beforehand, keep your footsteps moving, and let "God will provide for Himself" be your only line throughout these three days.

Second, those clutching their most beloved things too tightly. Children, career, savings, face—what God wants is not necessarily the thing itself; He measures its place in your heart. Whatever comes before God will, sooner or later, have to go up Mount Moriah; surrendering it early is far better than having it taken away.

Third, those making faith decisions for their children. Isaac walked up carrying the wood himself. The faith of our children cannot be inherited; we can only accompany them along the way. Let him carry the wood, let him ask the questions, speak "God will provide" into his ears—and then surrender the outcome back to God.

Fourth, those who doubt when they encounter restraint. Sometimes God tells you to go up the mountain; sometimes He calls out to stop when your hand is raised. Both voices are His. Fearing God does not merely mean understanding commands; it also means understanding "Do not lay your hand"—obedience includes stopping in time.

Fifth, those who always feel that God has shortchanged them. Did God take Isaac away? No. Having measured Abraham's fear of Him, He returned him just as he was, and added a ram, an oath, and the name of a mountain. Whatever you think is lost once surrendered is kept safely in God's hands.

Sixth, we who stand before the substance. Every stick of wood in this chapter points to Golgotha. The son whom Abraham did not ultimately sacrifice, God sacrificed. The reason we can lay down our knives and release our tight grips today is that His Lamb has already been provided. Are you still afraid that He will not provide for your small matters tomorrow?

## (58:00 - 60:00) Summary and Closing Prayer

Today we have ascended Mount Moriah: we have seen the test fall upon what is most beloved, we have seen faith walking on while holding unanswered questions, and we have seen God Himself providing the ram when the knife was raised. From that day on, this mountain bore a name—In the Mount of the LORD it shall be provided.

Let us pray together: God of Jehovah-jireh, we thank You. You test us to reveal fear of You; You restrain our hands to reveal mercy; You provided a ram to substitute for Isaac, and You provided Your Son to substitute for us. Lord, we place into Your hands that which we are most reluctant to let go—our children, our tomorrows, the things we have clutched for too long. We ask that You accept our obedience, and let us hear that ancient saying on every mountain: In the Mount of the LORD it shall be provided. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] nasah: Hebrew נָסָה (H5254), to test, to prove; Gen. 22:1 "God tested Abraham"; definitions per BDB, HALOT. Cf. usage in Exod. 20:20; Deut. 8:2; 13:3; the homiletical exposition in Part 1 of this manuscript, "A test is a mirror," is based on the usage of this word.

[2] yachid: Hebrew יָחִיד (H3173), only, only-begotten, solitary; Gen. 22:2, 12, 16 three times refer to Isaac as "your only son"; definitions per BDB, HALOT. The LXX translates this word in 22:2 as *agapetos* (beloved), which mirrors the Chinese Union Version's "whom you love," specifically noted here.

[3] ra'ah and Yireh: Hebrew רָאָה (H7200), to see, to look after, to provide; Gen. 22:8 "God will provide for Himself" literally means "God will see for Himself (the lamb)"; 22:14 place name יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (H3070, Yahweh-yireh), translated in the Chinese Union Version as "Jehovah-jireh," with the footnote "meaning The Lord will provide"; definitions per BDB, HALOT.

[4] 'olah: Hebrew עֹלָה (H5930), burnt offering, whole burnt offering; Gen. 22:2–3, 6–8, 13; definitions per BDB, HALOT.

[5] 'ayil: Hebrew אַיִל (H352), ram; Gen. 22:13 "there was a ram caught in a thicket by its horns"; definitions per BDB, HALOT.

[6] hinneni: Hebrew הִנֵּנִי, "Here I am," formed from *hinneh* (הִנֵּה, H2009, behold) plus the first-person singular suffix, expressing readiness to obey; occurs three times in Gen. 22:1, 7, 11; definitions per BDB, HALOT. "The one who fears God is present all three times" in this manuscript is a homiletical observation based on this repetition.

[7] Regarding Moriah and the Temple Mount: 2 Chron. 3:1 explicitly states that Solomon began to build the house of the Lord in Jerusalem on Mount Moriah; this manuscript connects Gen. 22 with the subsequent place of sacrifice on this basis, specifically citing the reference here.

[8] Regarding Isaac's age and reaction when bound: Gen. 22 does not record Isaac's age or whether he struggled; this manuscript treats this as textual silence and deliberately refrains from dramatizing beyond the text; "a youth capable of carrying a bundle of firewood" is a reasonable inference based on the narrative of 22:6, already marked at the sermon review stage as an inferential statement.

[9] Regarding the interpretation of Heb. 11:19 (Abraham concluding that God was able to raise Isaac up, even from the dead): Part 1 of this manuscript takes this as the explanatory basis for Abraham's faith during the three-day journey, not quoting the entire verse directly, but excerpting it.

[10] Akedah: Hebrew עֲקֵידָה (*aqedah*), "binding," the technical term in Jewish tradition for the offering of Isaac in Gen. 22, derived from the verb *aqad* (עָקַד, H6123, to bind, to tie; Gen. 22:9 "bound Isaac his son"); this term is widely used in Jewish traditional literature and biblical scholarship, specifically noted here.

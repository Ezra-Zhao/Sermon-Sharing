# Would God That All the LORD's People Were Prophets — A Sermon on Numbers 11

- Text: Numbers 11:1-35
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending cross-review — marked as a first translation awaiting proofreading (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Numbers 11; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible and cross-checked against the same edition as carried on Bible Gateway, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations; verses not checked word for word are given in summary only, never as quotations.
- Note on the title: The Chinese title is drawn, under this series' rule, from an unbroken phrase of the Chinese base text. Candidates were checked verse by verse in Numbers 11: "Taberah" (v. 3) names a place and covers only verses 1-3; the Chinese phrase for "the graves of the people that lusted" (the base text's own gloss at v. 34) covers the judgment line but not the line of the shared Spirit; "fell a lusting" in its Chinese wording (v. 4) covers the first half of the chapter; "bear alone" in its Chinese wording (vv. 14, 17) is the hinge word, but it belongs to the complaint side of the chapter. At verse 29 the Chinese base text reads, in Moses' wish, a five-character unbroken phrase — "that all … prophesy by inspiration" — answering to verse 25, where the elders in fact do so when the Spirit rests on them. The burden line (vv. 14, 17), the Spirit-sharing line (vv. 17, 25), and the large-hearted line (v. 29) all gather under that phrase, and the Chinese title was fixed on it. The English title renders the same verse from the KJV's own wording: "would God that all the LORD's people were prophets" (Num 11:29, KJV). The two titles are drawn each from its own base text and are not literal glosses of one another. See Endnote 2.
- A base-text correction carried into both manuscripts: briefing material for this chapter gave one elder's name in a Chinese form that the base text does not use, described the quails' measure at verse 31 with a Chinese numeral the base text does not use, and paraphrased verses 14 and 17 as though they were quotations. Checked against the base texts, the Chinese reads Eldad with a different first character than the briefing's form (KJV: Eldad), verse 31 reads "two cubits" (in Chinese, the formal numeral, not the colloquial one), verse 17 reads, in Chinese, that the LORD will take of the Spirit set upon Moses and bestow it on the elders, and verse 14 reads that the burden of governing the people is too heavy for Moses to bear alone. Both manuscripts record the verses as their base texts give them. See Endnote 3.
- Independent-review note: This English manuscript was prepared with its own check of the Chinese original's counts, verse references, and Strong's numbers against the KJV and the lexica. Its findings — all counts confirmed, one famous divergence at verse 25 between the KJV and the Chinese base text flagged in both manuscripts — are set out in section III and Endnotes 4, 6, and 10.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

At the end of chapter 10 the host has only just left Sinai behind the ark and the cloud; chapter 11 opens with the travellers already complaining. First an unnamed complaint kindles wrath, and fire burns to the uttermost parts of the camp; the place is called Taberah. Then the mixt multitude leads a lusting, and Israel weeps again for flesh, reciting Egypt's menu — the fish, the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlick — while before their eyes there is nothing beside the manna. The weeping lands on Moses, and Moses lays the burden straight before God: he is not able to bear all this people alone. God's answer runs on two roads. To Moses: gather seventy men of the elders, and I will take of the spirit which is upon thee and put it upon them, that they bear the burden with thee. To the people: ye shall eat flesh — not one day, nor two days, but even a whole month, until it come out at your nostrils. Moses doubts where flesh for six hundred thousand footmen for a whole month can come from, and God answers with one question: Is the LORD's hand waxed short? Then the Spirit rests on the seventy elders and they prophesy; Eldad and Medad, who remained in the camp, prophesy there; Joshua would forbid them, and Moses answers: would God that all the LORD's people were prophets. At the close a wind brings quails from the sea; the people gather all that day and night and the next day; and while the flesh is yet between their teeth, the plague strikes, and the place is called Kibrothhattaavah — there they buried the people that lusted. From there the people journey to Hazeroth. This sermon reads the chapter in its own order: first, how complaint and lusting spread from the camp's edge and Egypt's menu; then, Moses' burden and God's answer of a shared Spirit and a shared load; then, the largeness of Moses' heart when the Spirit rests inside and outside the tent; and last, the single step between the quails and the graves. The thread it traces is this: a burden that cannot be borne alone is answered when God shares His Spirit so that men bear it together, while a lust that cannot be filled turns the very ground of gift into a burial ground — in one chapter, the Spirit shared out is the road of life, and the craving that knows no end is the road of death.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 From the fire at the camp's edge to Egypt's menu: complaint burns up, and lusting counts its dishes

(Fact) The chapter opens with no reason given — only the people's complaint and God's anger. Verse 1 stands in the KJV: "And when the people complained, it displeased the LORD: and the LORD heard it; and his anger was kindled; and the fire of the LORD burnt among them, and consumed them that were in the uttermost parts of the camp." (Num 11:1, KJV) Mark the text's restraint: what the people complained about, this verse does not say — no lack of water, no lack of bread, no length of road; only that they complained, and that what they said reached the LORD's ears. The fire's reach is stated as precisely: it burnt among them and consumed those in the uttermost parts of the camp; the verse does not say how many died or how long it burned, only that the people cried to Moses, Moses prayed, and the fire was quenched (v. 2, Summary). Verse 3 nails the event into a place name, in the KJV: "And he called the name of the place Taberah: because the fire of the LORD burnt among them." (Num 11:3, KJV) The name Taberah is the proper noun taḇʿērâ (Strong's H8404), and the text at once explains it by the burning: the verb is bāʿar (Strong's H1197), the very verb of verse 1's "burnt." Name and event answer one another in one root; both pairings were checked individually for this sermon (see Endnote 7). A place name here is a marker: at every stage of the march, the stage's name keeps the stage's lesson for Israel. Taberah keeps this one: the journey had barely begun, and complaint had already burned.

(Fact) The fire is quenched; the lusting follows — and this time the text names the source, the words, and the list. Verses 4-6 stand in the KJV: "And the mixt multitude that was among them fell a lusting: and the children of Israel also wept again, and said, Who shall give us flesh to eat? We remember the fish, which we did eat in Egypt freely; the cucumbers, and the melons, and the leeks, and the onions, and the garlick: But now our soul is dried away: there is nothing at all, beside this manna, before our eyes." (Num 11:4-6, KJV) Layer by layer. First, the source: "the mixt multitude that was among them" — it was not the whole people who began it; the mixt multitude among them fell a lusting first, and Israel was carried along and wept again. Lusting is contagious, and the text records even the order of the infection. Second, the list, counted item by item for this sermon: the fish, the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlick — six items in all, one of fish and five of the garden (see Endnote 4). The list's price tag is "freely": the Egypt they remember is an Egypt that remembers the eating and forgets the beating — brickyards, taskmasters, and bondage are on no list; only free fish and five vegetables remain. Third, their verdict on the present: "our soul is dried away" — the manna is not absent; it is daily. The trouble is not that there is no food, but that there is only this one food: "there is nothing at all, beside this manna, before our eyes." The eye of lusting reads a daily provision as a monotonous lack: the one thing that is there is called "nothing at all," and the six things that are not there are counted as though they were all still on the table.

(Fact) In the middle of the weeping the text sets down a description of the manna (vv. 7-9), as if to measure the "nothing at all" on the spot. Verse 7 stands in the KJV: "And the manna was as coriander seed, and the colour thereof as the colour of bdellium." (Num 11:7, KJV) The two verses after it, in Summary: the people went about and gathered it, ground it in mills or beat it in a mortar, baked it in pans and made cakes of it, and its taste was as the taste of fresh oil; and when the dew fell upon the camp in the night, the manna fell upon it (vv. 8-9, Summary). This description stands between the weeping and Moses' complaint, and its position is itself a commentary: the manna has form and colour — as coriander seed, as bdellium; it has ways of preparation — ground, beaten, baked, made into cakes; it has a taste — as fresh oil; it has a rhythm — it falls with the dew by night and is there in the morning. The people said there was nothing beside it; the text describes that one thing in sight, sound, and flavour: the provision was not poor; the appetite had gone poor first. The manna's origin and rules stand in Exodus 16 (Summary): there, in the wilderness of Sin, the LORD gave quails and manna, the manna falling with the dew, gathered by every man's eating, double on the sixth day and none on the sabbath. This sermon marks that passage as background only and does not read it back as this chapter's text; this chapter's own strokes stop at form, preparation, taste, and the hour of falling (for the counted cross-references, see Endnote 9).

(Application) Taberah gives travellers a first warning: complaint often starts after the start and before any hardship. In chapter 10 the cloud had only just been taken up and the host had only just broken camp; the journey had barely begun, and complaint burned ahead of every actual want. The text does not even give this complaint a subject — as if to remind us that complaint usually has no subject at first: it is a weariness with things as they are, which then goes looking for subjects; and the fire that reaches the uttermost parts of the camp is that weariness burning out to the edge of the community's life. A man who begins to be weary on the tail of a comfortable season will find the road ahead narrower and narrower, because his eye has been set to a fault-finding focus: at provision, he sees only monotony; at leading, only delay; at his companions, only the mixt multitude. Moses' prayer quenched the fire, but it did not reset the focus — which is why, the fire scarcely out, the lusting begins. Prayer can quench a moment's fire; the heart's focus must be reset by a daily counting of the provision: what the manna was like, what it tasted of, how it fell. The text counts it once for the people; we must learn to count it for ourselves.

(Application) Egypt's menu deserves a slow reading, too. Of its six items, not one is a luxury: fish is the Nile's common fare, and cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions, and garlick are the cheapest produce of the field. Lusting beguiles not by rarity but by familiarity — it can produce exact names, exact flavours, and an exact price: freely. That a man in the wilderness should long for the free fish of the house of bondage is itself the deepest irony: that fish was never free; its price was a whole people's backs, only the account was kept in bricks and not at the table. Our own nostalgia has the same fault: it remembers the past's benefits and deletes the past's bondage; it remembers the present's monotony and deletes the present's freedom. The text records the list without comment and lets the reader hear it: a delivered nation, weeping to go back — for a menu. When we long for something, may we first read the whole list — together with the price written on its back.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) On the naming of Taberah, commentators commonly note the narrative's way of explaining a name by the event: the place name's sound answers to a keyword of the story — here bāʿar, "to burn" — fastening the event into the name as an aid to memory in oral transmission. Such explanations are the narrative's memory-craft and need not be read as strict etymology by modern standards. This sermon records the drift of that discussion only: the text's own stated reason is "because the fire of the LORD burnt among them," and the sermon's argument rests on that sentence, not on etymology. The naming of Kibrothhattaavah at verse 34 is treated in the same way; see section IV and Endnote 7.

### II. 08:00-24:00 Moses says it is too heavy: the burden laid before God, and God shares Spirit and load alike

(Fact) The weeping lands next on Moses. Verse 10 stands in the KJV: "Then Moses heard the people weep throughout their families, every man in the door of his tent: and the anger of the LORD was kindled greatly; Moses also was displeased." (Num 11:10, KJV) The weeping's place is specified: not assembled at the tabernacle door with a petition, but every man in the door of his own tent — a tent-door of weeping in every family, until the whole camp is a sea of tears. What Moses hears is not one complaint; it is a campful. Then comes Moses' speech to the LORD (vv. 11-15), read verse by verse for this sermon; its questions are concentrated in verses 11-13 and were counted one by one: five questions in all — two at verse 11 (why hast thou afflicted thy servant? and why have I not found favour in thy sight, that thou layest the burden of all this people upon me?); two at verse 12, in the KJV: "Have I conceived all this people? have I begotten them, that thou shouldest say unto me, Carry them in thy bosom, as a nursing father beareth the sucking child, unto the land which thou swarest unto their fathers?" (Num 11:12, KJV); and one at verse 13, where the people cry to him, "Give us flesh, that we may eat" (Num 11:13, KJV, the people's cry within the verse). After the five questions comes the sum of the matter, verse 14, in the KJV: "I am not able to bear all this people alone, because it is too heavy for me." (Num 11:14, KJV) At verse 15 Moses even asks to die: if thou deal thus with me, kill me out of hand, if I have found favour in thy sight, and let me not see my wretchedness (v. 15, Summary). This speech must be characterised exactly: it is not treason and not resignation; it is a leader laying the burden's true weight, unvarnished, before God — he does not turn on the people, he does not boast before men; he tells God alone: I am not able to bear it. And note the word: the "burden" of this people (vv. 11, 17) is massāʾ (Strong's H4853) — the very word of chapter 4 for what the Levites were to bear (Num 4, Summary). A burden is a thing carried on the shoulder; what Moses says is that this load is more than one pair of shoulders can carry. This sermon has checked the word's appearances in this passage: the "burden" of verse 11, the burden-bearing language of verse 14, and verse 17's "bear the burden of the people with thee, that thou bear it not thyself alone" all stand within this one root-field (see Endnote 7).

(Fact) God's answer comes to Moses first, and to the people second. The part for Moses stands at verses 16-17. Verse 16, in the KJV: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Gather unto me seventy men of the elders of Israel, whom thou knowest to be the elders of the people, and officers over them; and bring them unto the tabernacle of the congregation, that they may stand there with thee." (Num 11:16, KJV) Mark the standard first: not any seventy men gathered at random, but men Moses knows to be the elders of the people and officers over them — men already in office and already known; and the place is the tabernacle, the posture, standing there with Moses. Verse 17 continues, in the KJV: "And I will come down and talk with thee there: and I will take of the spirit which is upon thee, and will put it upon them; and they shall bear the burden of the people with thee, that thou bear it not thyself alone." (Num 11:17, KJV) Three layers, one by one. First, the way of sharing the load is not the sharing of office first, but the sharing of the Spirit first: God issues no commissions and draws no districts before He takes of the spirit which is upon Moses and puts it upon them. The "spirit" in the Hebrew is rûaḥ (Strong's H7307); the verb of taking-and-setting, ʾāṣal in its stem form, this sermon records by its form and use only, without a number, its number not having been individually verified (see Endnote 7). Second, the result of the shared Spirit is a shared burden: "they shall bear the burden of the people with thee" — the Spirit comes down, and then the load is divided; the order admits no reversal: it is not that men first divide the work and then seek the Spirit, but that the Spirit first rests, and the work becomes bearable in company. Third, the purpose clause is frank: "that thou bear it not thyself alone" — God Himself confirms Moses' diagnosis: this burden was never meant for one man's bearing. What is to change is not Moses' attitude but the burden's establishment.

(Fact) The part for the people stands at verses 18-20: first the people are told to sanctify themselves against tomorrow and eat flesh (v. 18, Summary), for their weeping has come up into the LORD's ears, and He will give them flesh. For how long, verses 19-20 say, in the KJV: "Ye shall not eat one day, nor two days, nor five days, neither ten days, nor twenty days; But even a whole month, until it come out at your nostrils, and it be loathsome unto you: because that ye have despised the LORD which is among you, and have wept before him, saying, Why came we forth out of Egypt?" (Num 11:19-20, KJV) The terms were counted one by one: one day, two days, five days, ten days, twenty days — five stated terms, rising step by step, and landing at last on "a whole month" (see Endnote 4). The tone of this speech must be read rightly: it is not the tone of reward but of surrender to their will — what you wept for, you shall have in full, until it is loathsome to you; for what you despised was not the manna but the LORD who is among you. Flesh eaten until it comes out at the nostrils: the text deliberately makes the picture nauseating. The end of a lust granted is not satisfaction but disgust: only when the thing wept for has been eaten to vomiting does a man learn that what he wept for was wept for in error.

(Fact) Hearing this, Moses' doubt rises before his obedience (vv. 21-22, Summary): the people among whom he is are six hundred thousand footmen — "six hundred thousand footmen" is the verse's own phrase — and shall they eat flesh a whole month? Shall flocks and herds be slain for them, to suffice them? Or shall all the fish of the sea be gathered together for them, to suffice them? The six hundred thousand is Moses' own figure, the reckoning of the numbered host of chapter 1 (Num 1, Summary): so many men, so many days — where is the flesh to come from? The reckoning is not wrong; what is wrong is that God is not in the reckoning. God's answer is one sentence, verse 23, in the KJV: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Is the LORD's hand waxed short? thou shalt see now whether my word shall come to pass unto thee or not." (Num 11:23, KJV) The sentence falls into two halves. The first half is a counter-question — Is the LORD's hand waxed short? — turning the question from "where is the flesh?" back to "who is God?": if the supply lies in God's hand, the question is not the storehouse but the length of the arm. The second half is a promise of verification: thou shalt see now whether my word shall come to pass — Moses need not first work it out; he need only watch. Every verse that follows in this chapter is a record of that verification: the Spirit resting on the elders is the word come to pass; the wind bringing quails is the word come to pass — and the judgment half of it lies within the verification too.

(Application) Moses' complaint is the Bible's honourable pattern of a breakdown for everyone who serves. Where is its honour? First, he breaks down before God, not before men: in a camp full of weeping he does not stand at the camp gate and curse back, nor resign and walk away; he goes into prayer and says his five questions and his death-wish to God alone. Second, he tells the truth: the burden is too heavy; I am not able to bear it alone — not a polite fiction but a measurement. A leader who owns that he cannot carry a thing is far more honest than one who stiffens himself until he collapses, and resents everyone after the collapse. Third, God does not rebuke him for the speech: throughout the chapter God's anger falls on the lusting people, never on the complaining Moses; and God's way of answering Moses is to change the establishment at once — gather seventy men. In God's account, "I am not able to bear it" is not a sin; it is a subject for prayer: weigh the load truly and bring the weight to Him, and He appoints fellow-bearers by that weight. Many of us fail at the two extremes: at one end, stiff-necked endurance until the endurance turns to bitterness, still calling itself faithfulness; at the other, throwing the load down the moment it grows heavy. Moses is neither: he does not stiffen, and he does not quit. He weighs — and having weighed, hands the weight to God and stands in his place to await God's answer.

(Application) The order of the sharing of the Spirit and the sharing of the burden must be pressed further, because we are most apt to reverse it. When we arrange fellow-workers, we think first of hands, posts, and procedures, and remember last to seek the Spirit's fullness. The text's order is the reverse: the Spirit is first taken and put upon them, and only then can the burden be borne in company. The seventy were already elders — office they had, and men who knew them; what they lacked was not a title but the spirit that was upon Moses, put upon them too. The real unity of fellow-workers is never the unity of a rota; it is the unity of having received one Spirit: the same sense of burden, the same tears, the same patience with the people, from the same Spirit. And the spirit upon Moses, shared out, left Moses no less: through the whole chapter Moses remains Moses — the word still comes by him, the prayer is still his. The Spirit is the one thing that, shared with seventy men, leaves its first bearer undiminished. Here lies the sharpest difference between spiritual leadership and worldly power: power shared out leaves its holder the less, and so it is clutched; the Spirit shared out leaves its holder undiminished, and so Moses can later say, would God that all were prophets. Whoever clutches must first ask himself: what I am clutching — is it power, or is it the Spirit?

### III. 24:00-42:00 The Spirit rested upon them: seventy elders prophesy, and two men in the camp prophesy too

(Fact) Verse 24 records Moses' execution of the command, in the KJV: "And Moses went out, and told the people the words of the LORD, and gathered the seventy men of the elders of the people, and set them round about the tabernacle." (Num 11:24, KJV) Verse 25 records the Spirit's descent, in the KJV: "And the LORD came down in a cloud, and spake unto him, and took of the spirit that was upon him, and gave it unto the seventy elders: and it came to pass, that, when the spirit rested upon them, they prophesied, and did not cease." (Num 11:25, KJV) Layer by layer. First, the manner of the descent matches the promise: verse 17 said, "I will come down and talk with thee there: and I will take of the spirit which is upon thee," and verse 25 records the coming down in a cloud, the speaking, and the giving — every verb of the promise has its landing place. Second, the sign of the Spirit's resting is prophesying: in the Hebrew it is the reflexive form of nābāʾ (Strong's H5012), speaking forth under the Spirit's moving. The Chinese base text renders this one verb in two ways inside the chapter — at verse 25 as inspired speaking (in the sermon's Chinese phrase, "received inspiration and spoke"), and at verses 26-27 as prophesying; the KJV renders it prophesy and prophesied throughout verses 25-27, and as the noun "prophets" at verse 29. The Chinese manuscript checked this distribution verse by verse (see Endnote 6); each manuscript stands on its own base text and neither is bent to match the other. Third — and here this English manuscript records its own independent finding, made in checking the Chinese original — the verse's last clause diverges famously between the two base texts: the KJV reads "and did not cease," while the Chinese base text reads, in its own words, that they did not do so again afterwards. On its face the one says the prophesying continued; the other says it was not repeated. Each manuscript follows its own base text and states the divergence openly, here and in Endnote 10, without altering a letter of either. On the Chinese reading, this prophesying is a once-for-all sign — a seal that the Spirit has been shared and the burden now has fellow-bearers — and when the sealing is done, the sign is withdrawn; on the KJV's wording the stress falls the other way. The sermon's argument rests on what both texts say alike: the Spirit was given to the seventy, and the elders prophesied.

(Fact) Next comes the scene inside the camp (vv. 26-29). Verse 26 stands in the KJV: "But there remained two of the men in the camp, the name of the one was Eldad, and the name of the other Medad: and the spirit rested upon them; and they were of them that were written, but went not out unto the tabernacle: and they prophesied in the camp." (Num 11:26, KJV) The facts, item by item: these two were not men outside the list — "they were of them that were written"; their names stood in the register of the seventy. They simply had not gone out to the tabernacle; they remained in the camp. And the Spirit did not withhold Himself for the wrong location: the spirit rested upon them, and they prophesied in the camp. At verse 27 a young man ran and told Moses that Eldad and Medad were prophesying in the camp (v. 27, Summary). At verse 28 Joshua speaks, in the KJV: "And Joshua the son of Nun, the servant of Moses, one of his young men, answered and said, My lord Moses, forbid them." (Num 11:28, KJV) Joshua's standing is written out in full: the servant of Moses, one of his young men, the son of Nun — no outsider gossiping, but the nearest aide moved by zeal for his master. Verse 29, Moses' answer, is the chapter's summit, in the KJV: "And Moses said unto him, Enviest thou for my sake? would God that all the LORD's people were prophets, and that the LORD would put his spirit upon them!" (Num 11:29, KJV) Verse 30 closes the scene: Moses returned to the camp, he and the elders of Israel (v. 30, Summary). A scene that might have hardened into a factional quarrel settles in one sentence of Moses': no punishment for Eldad and Medad, no rebuke for Joshua, and no recall of the sharing of the Spirit.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) The relation of the seventy to the two men in the camp has long been discussed; this sermon sets out the facts and the readings and decides for neither. The factual layer is three sentences only: God commands seventy men to be gathered (v. 16); Moses gathers seventy men and sets them round about the tabernacle (v. 24); and two men remained in the camp, were of them that were written, and went not out to the tabernacle (v. 26). By bare arithmetic: if the written total is seventy, then sixty-eight stood at the tabernacle and the two in the camp complete the seventy — sixty-eight and two make seventy, and that is one reading. If the seventy stood at the tabernacle complete, and these two are besides, the total becomes seventy-two — and that is another reading. The text nowhere states how many actually stood before the tabernacle. This sermon sets out the "68 + 2" only as one reading's arithmetic and does not write it as the text's own statement; commentators hold both readings side by side. The sermon's argument does not rest on this sum: on either reckoning, the text says plainly that the spirit rested on the two in the camp, that they prophesied in the camp, and that Moses did not forbid them. Why the two remained in the camp, the text does not say at all: modesty, retirement, another cause — all is conjecture. This sermon leaves the blank blank and invents no motives for them.

(Application) Joshua's question and Moses' answer are the touchstone of a leader's heart. Joshua's motive was not bad: he was guarding Moses' honour. Inspired prophesying looked like the badge of Moses' own line; now two absentees were prophesying in the camp, and in Joshua's eyes that broke the order and stole the orthodoxy. Many quarrels begin in exactly this kindness: in the name of guarding the master, doing the work of forbidding; in the name of keeping order, quenching first the Spirit's work elsewhere. Moses' answer changes the measure altogether: Enviest thou for my sake? The problem is not whether Eldad and Medad were present; it is whether there is in your heart a fire of fighting for my territory. Moses does not envy — because he has just shared the Spirit out with his own hands. A man who can share the Spirit with seventy men without a pang will not grudge two more in the camp. His wish is far larger than Joshua's anxiety: would God that all the LORD's people were prophets — not seventy, but all; not only before the tabernacle, but in the camp as well. A leader with this heart leaves no room in his company for "absent, therefore invalid," nor for "not of my line, therefore no shining allowed"; there is only this: where the Spirit rests, there a man speaks — and I rejoice, and do not envy. This one sentence of Moses belongs on every leader's desk: what are you guarding — the Lord's glory, or your own ground? The text has written the answer for us: the man who truly guards his Lord wishes that all would prophesy.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 A wind brings quails, and the flesh is yet between their teeth: from Kibrothhattaavah to Hazeroth

(Fact) The elders' matter settled, the promise of flesh is verified next. Verse 31 stands in the KJV: "And there went forth a wind from the LORD, and brought quails from the sea, and let them fall by the camp, as it were a day's journey on this side, and as it were a day's journey on the other side, round about the camp, and as it were two cubits high upon the face of the earth." (Num 11:31, KJV) Item by item. First, the agent: "there went forth a wind from the LORD" — the verification of verse 23's "Is the LORD's hand waxed short?" comes by no other instrument than a wind; the wind is the arm's tool. Second, the source: from the sea; the quails (in the Hebrew, śəlāw, recorded by its form only for this sermon, its Strong's number not having been individually verified; see Endnote 7) are brought by the wind from over the sea. Third, the extent: by the camp and round about the camp, a day's journey on this side and a day's journey on the other — the camp at the centre, about a day's journey each way. Fourth, the measure, exactly as the text gives it: "two cubits" — the cubit is ʾammâ (Strong's H520); the text says only that it was about two cubits upon the face of the earth, and this sermon records it as it stands, without converting it to a modern length and without deciding whether the two cubits measure the height of the flying or the depth of the fallen — that belongs to the discussion layer below. Verse 32 records the gathering, in the KJV: "And the people stood up all that day, and all that night, and all the next day, and they gathered the quails: he that gathered least gathered ten homers: and they spread them all abroad for themselves round about the camp." (Num 11:32, KJV) The time was counted segment by segment: all that day, all that night, and all the next day — three segments of gathering without pause (see Endnote 4). And the floor of the harvest is staggering: he that gathered least gathered ten homers — a homer is ḥōmer (Strong's H2563), a dry measure; the text gives its name and this sermon records the name without conversion (the estimates are in the discussion layer). And they spread them abroad for themselves round about the camp: the camp's surround, which once held the marching order of the tabernacle, is now covered edge to edge with drying quails. The camp is the same camp; what surrounds it has changed.

(Fact) The end stands at verses 33-35, each verse shorter than the last and every sentence a nail. Verse 33, in the KJV: "And while the flesh was yet between their teeth, ere it was chewed, the wrath of the LORD was kindled against the people, and the LORD smote the people with a very great plague." (Num 11:33, KJV) The moment is fixed between the teeth: not after they had eaten, not after they had tired of it, but while the flesh was yet in the mouth, unchewed — the thing lust had demanded was scarcely in, and the judgment was there; the warning of verses 19-20, of flesh eaten to loathing, did not even wait for the month to run before its nature was verified at the first mouthful. Verse 34, in the KJV: "And he called the name of that place Kibrothhattaavah: because there they buried the people that lusted." (Num 11:34, KJV) The name is the proper noun qiḇrôṯ ha-taʾăwâ (Strong's H6914); the Chinese base text carries its own gloss at this verse — "the graves of the people of lust" — and its second half is that same word of verse 4, taʾăwâ (Strong's H8378), "lust": the chapter's head and tail are sewn together by one word — at the head, a great lusting of the heart; at the tail, burial in the graves of lust (for the checking of the numbers, see Endnote 7). Verse 35, in the KJV: "And the people journeyed from Kibrothhattaavah unto Hazeroth; and abode at Hazeroth." (Num 11:35, KJV) The chapter's thirty-five verses end on a plain itinerary note: the host must still move on — only now it moves with a company thinned by the graves of lust, and with a stage-name that warns for ever. The chapter's two place names stand head to tail against each other: Taberah remembers fire; Kibrothhattaavah remembers graves — the one is complaint's starting point, the other is lusting's end.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) Three measures and phenomena in this passage are much discussed; this sermon keeps the readings and decides for none. First, the "two cubits": some read the quails' low flying height — about two cubits off the ground, precisely the height for catching by hand, agreeing with the people's gathering all day; others read the depth of the fallen birds upon the ground. The versions' phrasings follow their readings; this sermon records the KJV's "two cubits high upon the face of the earth" as it stands and takes neither side. Second, the conversion of "ten homers": the homer as a dry measure has been estimated very variously, the modern-volume equivalents running from a little over two hundred litres to several hundred; this sermon records the text's own figure, ten homers, and keeps every conversion in this layer as reference only, building nothing on any litre count: what the text means the reader to marvel at is the words "he that gathered least." Third, the phenomenon of wind-borne quails: commentators commonly note that quails migrate seasonally, cross the sea on the wind, and, exhausted, fly low and fall in great numbers, easy to catch — a natural phenomenon documented in the ancient Near East. The text assigns the timing, the wind, and the scale to the wind that "went forth from the LORD," and this sermon builds on the text's own attribution; the natural background stands in this discussion layer only, neither diluting nor replacing the text's own account. Psalms 78 and 106 afterwards retell the event (Ps 78; Ps 106, Summary) as a warning against lust and judgment — an echo within the Old Testament, marked here as its own layer; the New Testament reading stands in section V (for the counted list, see Endnote 9).

(Application) The most piercing thing in this passage is its moment: the flesh yet between their teeth. God did not withhold the flesh — He gave it overwhelmingly: a day's journey of range, two cubits of lowness, ten homers as the minimum — every figure a proof that the hand was not waxed short. Nor did God change His mind after giving: verses 18-20 had said it beforehand — you have despised the LORD which is among you; this flesh is surrendered flesh, not blessed flesh. The judgment falls between the teeth because the problem was never the flesh; it was the heart that ate it. The same quails, received with thanksgiving, would have been provision; seized by lust, they are the material of graves. The energy of the gathering — all day, all night, all the next day — is the same energy as the weeping that demanded: without, weeping to have; with, scrambling to gather; and never once a pause to ask: is this truly what I need? Is this what God is pleased for me to eat? The most terrible thing about lust is not that it asks for the wrong thing; it is that it abolishes the pause: weep, demand, seize, eat — in one breath, until the plague steps on the brake for it. We may not die on quails today, but we live constantly in that pauseless rhythm: scroll, want, order, receive, tire, scroll again — the last thing still between our teeth while the next is already on its way. The name Kibrothhattaavah stands in the wilderness to leave every generation a pause: before your hands go out to gather, look first at what this place is finally called.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: fellow-bearers for the burden, and the graves for a warning

(Fact) The chapter's thirty-five verses gather now into three lines. First, the line of complaint and lusting (vv. 1-9): a complaint without a subject; fire at the camp's edge; the name Taberah; the mixt multitude leading a great lusting; a six-item Egyptian menu set against the manna that falls daily; weeping taking shape. Second, the line of burden and shared Spirit (vv. 10-30): after five questions Moses says plainly that he is not able to bear it alone; God gathers seventy elders, takes of the spirit upon Moses and puts it upon them so that they bear the burden with him, promises flesh for a whole month, and answers Moses' doubt with "Is the LORD's hand waxed short?"; the Spirit rests on the seventy elders and they prophesy; Eldad and Medad prophesy in the camp; and Moses settles Joshua's plea to forbid them with "would God that all the LORD's people were prophets." Third, the line of promise and judgment (vv. 31-35): a wind goes forth from the LORD; quails come from the sea; the people gather through three segments of time, he that gathered least gathering ten homers; while the flesh is yet between their teeth the plague strikes; the place is called Kibrothhattaavah; and the host moves on to Hazeroth. The three lines are sewn together on two words: the burden, massāʾ — shared out, and there are fellow-bearers; and the lust, taʾăwâ — never filled, and there are fellow-burials.

(Application) Let the sermon close in three sentences. First: if you are not able to bear it, say you are not able to bear it. Moses' five questions and his death-wish earned him not a rebuke but seventy fellow-workers; to weigh the load truly before God is the first step of its relief. Second: when the Spirit is shared out, the heart widens. Moses could say "would God that all were prophets" because he had first known a sharing of the Spirit that left him undiminished; only the man who insists on enjoying alone can envy; the man who shares can only rejoice. Third: before your hands gather, remember the place names. Taberah's fire and Kibrothhattaavah's graves stand at the road's two ends as a reminder: complaint's end, unrepented, is lusting's grave, while the road of provision, thankfully received, finds the daily manna enough to reach the promised land. May we, a generation on the road, unload our burdens in the right place, widen our hearts when the Spirit comes, and steady our hands when appetite stirs — stage by stage toward Hazeroth, and toward the land of promise.

(New Testament reading) Three New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. The first stands between Joel and Acts: Joel prophesies that God will pour out His Spirit upon all flesh, and sons and daughters shall prophesy (Joel 2:28, Summary), and at Pentecost in Acts 2 Peter cites that word as fulfilled (Acts 2, Summary) — Moses' wish, "would God that all the LORD's people were prophets," answers across the Testaments to the promise of the Spirit poured on all flesh: one man's wish in the wilderness became, at Pentecost, a multitude's reality. The second stands at Luke 9: John forbids a man casting out devils in the Lord's name because he does not follow with the apostles, and the Lord answers, forbid him not (Luke 9:49-50, Summary) — set beside Joshua's "My lord Moses, forbid them" and Moses' answer, the pictures almost overlap: the disciple who guards his master would forbid the work outside the company, and the Master will not have it forbidden. The third stands at 1 Corinthians 10: Paul says the wilderness generation's lusting after evil things is our example, to the intent we should not lust after evil things as they also lusted (1 Cor 10:6, Summary) — the graves of Kibrothhattaavah are formally set up in the New Testament as the specimen of warning. (Application) But the layers must be kept: Numbers 11 is first of all itself — the weeping at the tent doors, the shared Spirit of the seventy elders, the flesh between the teeth. The New Testament's reading is the light in which the church, in Christ, looks back. The more solidly this chapter is read in its own facts — its numbers checkable letter by letter, its place names verifiable verse by verse, its great wish quoted as spoken — the more weight the reading layer's correspondences carry: what Moses wished and did not see, the church afterwards saw with its eyes; and the breadth Moses would not allow to be forbidden became the Lord's own rule taught to His disciples.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's hands. In your hands there may lie a burden you are not able to bear alone — in the home, in the church, in the work: you can hear the weeping at every family's tent door, and you cannot conjure the remedy. This chapter says to you: do not stiffen, and do not throw it down; learn from Moses — go before God, tell Him the weight item by item, and wait for Him to gather your seventy fellow-workers. Or you may be standing in Joshua's place: a heart that guards its master, watching a man shine in the camp outside the recognised company, wanting to say "forbid them." This chapter says to you also: ask first, for whose sake are you envious? On whom the Spirit rests is not ours to fence; would God that all were prophets — that is the heart a fellow-worker should have. Or your hands may be reaching out to gather something: a chance, a pleasure, a possession — not yet in hand, and the heart already gone after it. This chapter says to you a third time: before you gather, recite once the name of that place — the graves of the people that lusted. May we unload our burdens in the right place, widen our hearts, and steady our hands, and walk on through the wilderness, stage by stage, to Hazeroth and toward the land of promise.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 11 stays with me not first for the quails, but for Moses' one sentence: "I am not able to bear all this people alone, because it is too heavy for me." Moses heard the people weep throughout their families, every man in the door of his tent, and he had no flesh to give them; so he carried the matter straight to the LORD: "Have I conceived all this people? have I begotten them?" God did not rebuke him for laying the burden down in prayer. Instead He gathered seventy men of the elders, and took of the spirit that was upon Moses and put it upon them, that they should bear the burden of the people with him. I have often thought that a servant of God must simply clench his teeth and bear alone, and that to falter is to lack faith. This chapter tells me otherwise: God's way out of an unbearable burden is often not to remove the burden, but to share His spirit with fellow labourers, so that the burden is borne together. Even Eldad and Medad, who remained in the camp, had the spirit rest upon them — and Moses was not envious. He said, "would God that all the LORD's people were prophets, and that the LORD would put his spirit upon them!" So I pray today: Lord, for the portion I am not able to bear, keep me from stiffening my neck and from hiding in shame; put Thy spirit upon the fellow labourers beside me, that we may bear together the burden Thou hast measured out to us; and give me the heart of Moses, who saw others prophesy in the camp, and only rejoiced, and was not envious, until every burden is borne steadily together to the end.

# Ye Shall Be Holy

Scripture: Leviticus 11:1-47 (KJV, aligned verse by verse with the Chinese original)

Translator's note: Translated from the Chinese original manuscript of the same date. This is a first translation awaiting proofreading.

Summary: Leviticus chapter 11 opens the purity legislation. Through the clean and unclean among beasts, creatures of the waters, birds and insects, God teaches His people to discern at the daily table. The standards of separation finally gather into one sentence: ye shall be holy, for I am holy. Holiness is not a rule detached from life; it is an identity that comes from redemption, trained in the most ordinary acts of eating and drinking.

---

## I. The Lesson of Discernment: From the Door of the Tabernacle to the Table 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, after the first ten chapters we stand at the door of the tabernacle: we have watched the priests consecrated, the fire of the altar descend, and the grievous scene of Nadab and Abihu offering strange fire and falling dead on the spot. At the end of chapter 10 God gave Aaron a programmatic word: "That ye may put difference between holy and unholy, and between unclean and clean" (Lev 10:10, KJV). That word was spoken to the priests: the priest was to discern within the sanctuary, and to teach that discernment to the people. Chapter 11, which follows immediately, is that word coming down to earth: the lesson of discernment walks out of the door of the tabernacle, into the tent, into the kitchen, and to every table. From chapter 11 to chapter 15, Scripture spends five whole chapters on cleanness and uncleanness: chapter 11 treats food and the carcasses of animals, chapter 12 childbirth, chapters 13 and 14 skin diseases and mould in garments and houses, chapter 15 bodily discharges. This is the purity legislation at the centre of Leviticus, and chapter 11 is its opening — a main gate, as it were: the categories and vocabulary of everything that follows about cleanness are established in this chapter.

Let us first fix the chapter's place in the book. This is a structural observation, not a preacher's arbitrary association. The first ten chapters of Leviticus tell how man comes before God with sacrifice and how the priests minister; chapters 11 to 15 tell how the people keep, in daily life, the state in which they may come before God; chapter 16, the Day of Atonement, carries the book to its climax; from chapter 17 the Holiness Code follows, telling how holiness is lived into ethics and feasts. The discernment of the first ten chapters happens in the sanctuary; from chapter 11, discernment enters life itself. God's requirement of His people was never merely to come to the tabernacle on feast days, but that what they eat at home during the week, what they touch, and how they handle things, all stand under His teaching. Worship has its hours, but no inch of life lies outside God's gaze. This is the first reminder chapter 11 gives us: the truth or falsity of faith is often shown not at the door of the tabernacle, but at the table.

The chapter opens with great simplicity: "And the LORD spake unto Moses and to Aaron, saying unto them, Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, These are the beasts which ye shall eat among all the beasts that are on the earth" (Lev 11:1-2, KJV). Note that the words are addressed to two men, Moses and Aaron. Moses is the prophet who receives the revelation; Aaron is the priest who ministers. The dietary ordinances are entrusted not only to the administrator but also to the teacher, because these ordinances must be explained, discerned, and taught from generation to generation. And those who carry them out are all the children of Israel. God does not say that this chapter is an internal rulebook for priests' eyes only; He says, "Speak unto the children of Israel." This is a chapter written for the people's daily life: the affairs of the kitchen, what is in the housewife's hands, what the hunter sees, what is in the fisherman's net — all are solemnly written into Scripture and become part of the fear of God. From this verse on, we must learn to read with the same solemnity: do not despise the details. When God Himself writes details into revelation, details are no longer mere details.

In reading this chapter, one pair of words must be clarified first, or the whole chapter will be misread. "Clean" in Hebrew is tahor (טָהוֹר, H2889); "unclean" is tame' (טָמֵא, H2931). This pair occurs with the highest frequency in Leviticus; it is the skeleton vocabulary of the whole book. One crucial distinction must be made plain here: tahor and tame' are ritual categories, not moral categories. A person who is ritually unclean is not thereby guilty of a moral sin. A woman is unclean after childbirth, yet she has committed no offence; a man who touches the carcass of an animal is unclean, yet he may only have done his duty in disposing of it; a priest defiled by attending to the burial of his kin is not morally corrupt. Conversely, a person ritually clean cannot thereby prove that his heart is just and upright; the prophets repeatedly rebuke precisely those whose hands are clean but whose hearts are full of violence. Ritual uncleanness is a state bearing on one's fitness to draw near to the sanctuary; it has a beginning and an end, a term and a rite of cleansing. Moral sin, by contrast, requires confession, atonement and forsaking. Confuse these two levels, and chapter 11 becomes a label-book of human worth: eat rightly and you are a good person; touch wrongly and you are a bad one. The text means nothing of the kind. This chapter teaches the discernment of states, not the grading of persons. With that distinction clear, we can read the chapter honestly: it deals with how one draws near to the holy God who dwells among His people, and with what preparation and boundaries that requires at the level of body and daily life.

Why, then, does drawing near to God require such preparation? The answer is given by God Himself at the end of the chapter: "For I am the LORD your God: ye shall therefore sanctify yourselves, and ye shall be holy; for I am holy" (Lev 11:44, KJV). Remember this sentence; we will return to it at the end. The forty-seven verses of the chapter, from beasts to creeping things, look like nothing but classification and prohibition, yet their conclusion is not a dietary summary: it is a declaration about God Himself, and a calling concerning the people's identity. The ordinances have a root, and the root is God's holiness and His redemption: "For I am the LORD that bringeth you up out of the land of Egypt, to be your God" (Lev 11:45, KJV). First the exodus, first His bringing them up; only then, "ye shall be holy." The order cannot be reversed. This chapter is not a price list by which men buy God's favour with diet; it is the pattern by which a people already redeemed learn to live according to the character of the God who redeemed them. As we read this chapter today, we too must stand in that order: we do not win God's pleasure by discerning; God first set us apart, and only then do we learn to discern in daily life.

## II. Parting the Hoof and Chewing the Cud: The Double Standard for Beasts and Water Creatures 08:00-24:00

The ordinances begin with the beasts. The standard God gives is clear enough to be tested on the spot: "Whatsoever parteth the hoof, and is clovenfooted, and cheweth the cud, among the beasts, that shall ye eat" (Lev 11:3, KJV). Two standards, and neither may be lacking. Parting the hoof: the hoof is divided into two distinct toes. Chewing the cud: rumination, bringing the swallowed fodder back into the mouth to chew again. These are the text's own observational standards: the hoof that can be seen, the chewing that can be observed. One purpose of the standards is precisely that the people can judge by them without relying on abstruse knowledge. The herdsman looks at the hoof; the shepherd watches the chewing; everyone can learn to discern. This fits the chapter's character exactly: discernment is not the priest's monopoly; it is the whole people's lesson.

The text then lists the four most instructive cases — what we may call the half-qualified: each has one standard and lacks the other. First, the camel: "the camel, because he cheweth the cud, but divideth not the hoof; he is unclean unto you" (Lev 11:4, KJV). The camel's foot is a broad pad, fitted for desert travel, but it has no divided hoof. Second, the coney (the hyrax, shaphan, שָׁפָן, H8227): it chews the cud, so to speak, but does not divide the hoof. The hyrax is a small creature dwelling among the rocks, about the size of a hare; its jaw is often seen moving as if chewing, and the ancients classed it, by outward appearance, among those that chew the cud. Third, the hare: likewise classed as chewing the cud, but without a divided hoof; the hare's lips and jaw move constantly, giving the appearance of rumination. Fourth, the swine: "Though he divide the hoof, and be clovenfooted, yet he cheweth not the cud; he is unclean to you" (Lev 11:7, KJV). The swine's hoof is divided as plainly as can be — of the four cases it looks most like a qualifier — yet it fails on the second standard. These four cases must be stated honestly, especially the "cud-chewing" of the hyrax and the hare: this is ancient observational classification, not rumination in the sense of modern zoology. Neither the hyrax nor the hare ruminates by modern taxonomy; the classification of Scripture rests on the text's own observational standard, the chewing motion seen by observers of that age. This sermon states both plainly: it does not argue with modern zoology, nor does it force the text into a zoological treatise. The basis of the classification is appearance; the authority of the ordinance comes from God's command, not from the frontier of taxonomy. Acknowledge this, and the text is read the more honestly.

Set side by side, the four cases reveal their teaching edge. Chewing without hoof: not permitted. Hoof without chewing: not permitted either. The standard is double, and lacking one means exclusion. At the factual level this is a rule of classification; at the level of homiletical exposition we may extend it: the life of faith likewise fears half-measures. Outward marks without inward digestion, or inward impressions without distinct outward steps, are not complete obedience. Reading and chewing over Scripture, meditation and digestion, are the inward exercise; dividing the hoof, step by step with clear distinction, is the outward witness. But the levels must be marked: this is a homiletical extension, not the direct meaning of verses 3 to 8. The direct meaning is dietary classification; the extension is a reminder for life. The extension is legitimate, but it must not masquerade as the original meaning. With the levels distinguished, the application has roots.

After the four cases, the text lists the other clean beasts — cattle, sheep, deer and the various edible wild animals among them — and says plainly: "Of their flesh shall ye not eat, and their carcase shall ye not touch; they are unclean to you" (Lev 11:8, KJV). Here the section on beasts buries a thread that runs onward: defilement by carcasses. This thread will break out in force in the second half of the chapter; note it now: uncleanness is transmitted not only by eating but also by touching. Eating is active taking-in; touching is passive contact. God's teaching covers both, showing that the lesson of discernment governs not only the choices of the table but also the contacts of life. A man may not eat a thing, yet may touch it every day. If the boundary is kept only at the mouth, it will not hold at the hand.

For the creatures of the waters, the standard changes: "Whatsoever hath fins and scales in the waters, in the seas, and in the rivers, them shall ye eat" (Lev 11:9, KJV). The double standard for water creatures is fins and scales: fins (senapir, סְנַפִּיר, H5579) propel the fish; scales (qashqeset, קַשְׂקֶשֶׂת, H7193) cover its body; where both are present, it may be eaten. Whatever has neither is called an abomination: "they shall be even an abomination unto you; ye shall not eat of their flesh, but ye shall have their carcases in abomination" (Lev 11:11, KJV). Here appears another key word of the chapter: "abomination," sheqets (שֶׁקֶץ, H8263), denoting that which is to be detested and kept away from in religious feeling. The word does not describe a moral evil; it is the term of rejection within the ritual classification: this thing is to be separated from the people's diet and from their approach to God. It is close to tame' yet distinct: tame' speaks of the state of uncleanness; sheqets speaks of the verdict that a thing is to be detested. Brief as the water ordinance is, it preserves the testability of the standards to the end: the fisherman hauls in his catch, looks for fins and scales, and judges on the spot. Men cannot see through the world of the waters, yet God gave a standard that can be executed above the surface. The people's discernment thus extends from their own pastures to waters where they cannot linger: there is no need to see the depths; it is enough to discern on the shore by the standard given. Much of a man's life is just so: the waters are too deep to fathom, but the standard has been given; discernment does not rest on seeing through everything, but on obeying what is already known.

Set the two classes already treated back into the chapter's whole pattern, and a structural observation deserves mention. The four classes of animals correspond to four domains of habitation: beasts on the land, water creatures in the waters, birds in the air, and creeping things moving close to the ground. The sermon takes only the main line and does not expand class by class, but the tidiness of the pattern is a fact of the text: the ordinances cover the whole of the people's living world. Read against the order of creation in Genesis chapter 1, the pattern stands out further: Genesis 1 arranges the creatures by waters, sky and land, and distinguishes kinds within each domain; Leviticus 11 arranges the edible and the inedible by the same world-pattern, as if, within the created order, an order of worshipping life were drawn once more. This sermon marks that comparison as an analogy at the level of structural observation: it helps us see the wholeness and intention of the ordinances, but it is not asserted as a conclusion, and we do not claim that the reason for the classification is to replicate the order of creation. Observation may be proposed; forced parallels must stop. That the people's daily eating and drinking are set within so whole a pattern is itself teaching: God's separation is not confined to one corner, but extends through the whole world in which man dwells; wherever a man makes his living — at the land's edge, at the water's edge, in the air, in the soil — there is discernment to learn and a boundary to keep.

## III. The Birds, for Whom There Is Only a List, and Three Explanations Set Side by Side 24:00-42:00

The third class is the birds, and here the text changes its method, producing the most noteworthy textual feature of the chapter: for the birds that may not be eaten, the text gives only a list of names and no criterion of judgement. The beasts have a double standard, the water creatures have a double standard; with the birds the standard is suddenly absent, and only a string of names remains: the eagle, the ossifrage, the ospray, the vulture, the kite, the raven after his kind, the owl, the night hawk, the cuckow, the hawk after his kind, the little owl, the cormorant, the great owl, the swan, the pelican, the gier eagle, the stork, the heron after her kind, the lapwing, and the bat (Lev 11:13-19, KJV). This feature must be pointed out, because it determines how we read: for beasts and water creatures the people take the standard and judge for themselves; for birds the people take the list and check item by item. The two modes of teaching stand side by side, showing that the ordinances do not have only one form. Sometimes God gives a principle and calls men to exercise discernment; sometimes He gives a list and calls men simply to obey it as received. The life of faith contains both: principles that can be applied by analogy, and commands that cannot be re-derived by one's own reasoning but only received. Most of the birds on the list are raptors, water birds and carrion-eaters — a common tendency not hard to observe; but since the text does not write the tendency into a standard, the sermon states the tendency only, and announces no standard. The list even includes the bat: the bat is a mammal by modern classification, yet it stands at the end of the bird list here. This confirms again that the basis of classification is ancient appearance and function: what flies belongs to the domain of the air. The text classifies by the world as seen, not by modern disciplines; the reader should respect the text's own classificatory language and not be anxious on its behalf.

The ordinance on edible insects is the exception among exceptions in this chapter. Among winged creeping things that go on all four, most may not be eaten; only those whose legs have joints above their feet, to leap withal upon the earth, may be eaten: "the locust after his kind, and the bald locust after his kind, and the beetle after his kind, and the grasshopper after his kind" (Lev 11:22, KJV). Note the form of the standard: it is not that insects in general may be eaten, but that one class marked by a physical sign may — the jointed long legs (kera'ayim, כְּרָעַיִם, H3767) are the instruments of leaping upon the earth. This exception shows that the ordinances do not take man's disgust at appearances as their standard: the locust, at which many blanch, is among the permitted, while many creatures pleasing to the eye are outside. The standard of separation lies in God's word, not in man's taste. If men rewrite the ordinances by preference, the ordinances become a menu; if men receive them by God's word, a menu becomes an exercise in reverence. The people in the wilderness did in fact eat locusts, and John the Baptist's locusts and wild honey in the wilderness are an echo of this ordinance: the food that had been set apart sustained men in the wilderness. The ordinances are not showy austerities; they cover even the hardest days: when there is no grain to eat, the leaping insects may still fill the belly. Within God's boundary there is God's own provision.

At this point we must face squarely the question every reader asks: why these standards? Why may what parts the hoof and chews the cud be eaten, while lacking one excludes? Why are fins and scales clean, and their absence an abomination? Scholarship offers three main explanations. This sermon sets them side by side honestly, asserts none as settled, and marks the evidence and the limits of each.

The first is the hygienic explanation. It holds that the ordinances carry considerations of public health: swine and the like may transmit disease; scaleless water creatures spoil easily and cause sickness; raptors and carrion birds have contact with carcasses; abstaining from these protects the people's health. Among its supporters, Milgrom's attitude is the most cautious and the most representative: in his commentary he acknowledges that some ordinances do objectively have hygienic benefits, yet he explicitly rejects hygiene as the legislative reason for the ordinances. The limits of the evidence must be stated honestly. First, the text never states health as the reason for any ordinance; its only stated reason is holiness. Second, if health were the point, the ordinances should be revised as knowledge advances, yet the text fixes the standards at the level of outward appearance. Third, provisions such as uncleanness from touching a carcass until evening, and the washing or breaking of vessels, are very strained when explained hygienically. The hygienic explanation may account for some incidental effects; it cannot account for the whole of the ordinances.

The second is the symbolic or separation explanation, represented by the reading of the anthropologist Mary Douglas: the classification system symbolises order and wholeness. Each domain has its proper form and mode of movement; what conforms to its class is clean, and what crosses boundaries or mixes forms is unclean. The norm for beasts is the divided hoof together with cud-chewing; the norm for fish is scales together with fins; what departs from the norm is separated out. Through this, the ordinances engrave the order of creation into daily eating: at every meal the people rehearse that God is a God who separates, and that the world has boundaries appointed by Him. This reading has the greatest explanatory power for the text's structure — it accounts especially well for the tidiness of the double standards and the four-domain pattern. Its limit is that the symbolic correspondences are, in the end, the interpreter's reconstruction: the text itself gives no symbolic meaning item by item, and Douglas herself later revised parts of her early account. This explanation is best used as a reference for structural observation, not proclaimed as the sole key to the ordinances.

The third is the ethical-discipline explanation, represented by Wenham: the dietary ordinances are a daily ethical training, bound up with the order of creation and with an attitude toward death. By daily choices the people learn to respect life, to restrain appetite, and to keep away from what is associated with death; birds that feed on carrion and torn flesh are separated out, as if at the table the people practised daily a way of life that does not keep company with death; and the testability of the standards, with their constant repetition, turns abstract holiness into a memory of the muscles. The strength of this reading is that it connects the ordinances with the ethical teaching of the second half of Leviticus: holiness is not only in ritual but also in character. Its limit is that the text does not state an ethical symbolism for each standard for beasts and water creatures; the symbolism outweighs the explicit text. The honest conclusion of setting the three side by side is this: the reason for the ordinances is given by the text in one sentence only — ye shall be holy, for I am holy. The other explanations are illumination from different angles by later readers; they may be consulted, but they cannot replace that one sentence. If preaching presents any one of them as settled, it has crossed the line the text itself drew.

## IV. Touching Carcasses and Defilement: The Transmission of Uncleanness and the Gathering-In of Holiness 42:00-56:00

The second half of the chapter turns from what may and may not be eaten to defilement. "And for these ye shall be unclean: whosoever toucheth the carcase of them shall be unclean until the even. And whosoever beareth ought of the carcase of them shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until the even" (Lev 11:24-25, KJV). The carcasses of unclean beasts and of creeping things spread their reach further: wood, garments, skin, sacks — any vessel on which a dead body falls must be put into water and is unclean until evening; if it falls into an earthen vessel, whatever is in the vessel is unclean and the vessel itself must be broken; water that has touched a carcass defiles, though a fountain or a pit of gathered water remains clean; an oven or ranges defiled must be broken down; seed that is dry and unwetted remains clean, but seed that has been watered and then touched by a carcass is unclean (Lev 11:29-38). If a clean beast dies of itself, he who touches its carcass is unclean until evening, and he who eats of it must wash his clothes and be unclean until evening (Lev 11:39-40). And of every creeping thing the text gathers its conclusion in the strongest tone: ye shall not eat, nor make yourselves abominable or unclean through them (Lev 11:41-43).

From this large body of defilement provisions the sermon takes only three main points. First, uncleanness is transmitted: contact defiles, regardless of man's will. The one who touches may not approve, may not wish it, yet his state has changed. Scripture's view of sin and defilement has always been so: defilement needs no signed consent; draw near to the wrong contact, and a man loses, without noticing, his fitness to approach the sanctuary. This reminds us that the meaning of a boundary lies beforehand: discernment must happen before contact, not after defilement. Second, defilement has a term, and cleanness has a way out. "Unclean until the even" is the phrase that recurs through the chapter: sunset, washing, waiting — the state can be restored. Nowhere does this chapter say that the defiled person is expelled forever. The severity of the ordinances is that their boundaries are clear; their gentleness is that the way back is clear: wash your clothes, put it in water, wait until evening. God sets boundaries not to shut men out, but to teach them how to return. Third, the grading of vessels shows the precision of the separation: wooden vessels may be washed; earthen vessels must be broken. Earthenware is porous; the defilement soaks into its substance and cannot be washed away, so it must be shattered. This is not waste; it is the cost of the boundary. Some defilement can be washed away; some defilement requires the vessel itself to be given up. In the kitchen the people learned this lesson — the cost-accounting of reverence: not everything can be kept, and holiness sometimes requires a man to break the earthen vessel he is used to using.

Now the whole chapter gathers into verses 44 and 45, its summit, toward which the preceding forty-three verses all flow: "For I am the LORD your God: ye shall therefore sanctify yourselves, and ye shall be holy; for I am holy: neither shall ye defile yourselves with any manner of creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth. For I am the LORD that bringeth you up out of the land of Egypt, to be your God: ye shall therefore be holy, for I am holy" (Lev 11:44-45, KJV). "Holy" is qadosh (קָדוֹשׁ, H6918), whose root sense is to be set apart, assigned to God, distinct from the common. The root of holiness is not in man's effort but in God's own nature: "for I am holy." Nor does the path of holiness begin with the ordinances; it begins with redemption: "I am the LORD that bringeth you up out of the land of Egypt." God is first the Redeemer, then the Lawgiver; first the gracious bringing-out, then the holy following. These verses reach back to Exodus 19:6: "And ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation." The word in Exodus was spoken before the law was given at Sinai; it is the declaration of the covenant. The word in Leviticus 11 puts that declaration down upon the table; it is the daily training of a holy nation. Later, Leviticus 19:2 repeats it: "Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy"; and Leviticus 20:26 says again: "And ye shall be holy unto me: for I the LORD am holy, and have severed you from other people, that ye should be mine." Three passages, one main line: holiness is identity first and training second; God's separation first and man's discernment second. A man does not become a holy people by eating rightly; because he has first been separated out to God, he learns the discernment of holiness in his eating and drinking. Read these two verses after the defilement provisions, and the temperature of the ordinances changes completely: they are no longer a cold list, but the daily leading of a Redeemer over His own people — I brought you out in order that you should be like me, and your road to being like me begins with the most concrete routines of every day.

## V. The New Testament Reading and Today's Table 58:00-60:00

Finally, by this sermon's consistent rule, the New Testament reading is marked off on its own level: what follows is New Testament reading and homiletical use; it is not read back into this chapter's original meaning, and it certainly does not say that the chapter's original meaning was to abolish the dietary ordinances. The chapter's original meaning, within the framework of the old covenant, was to teach Israel to live with holy discernment. The New Testament writers, in the new situation after Christ has accomplished redemption, dealt afresh with the boundaries of food: in Mark's account of the dispute about unwashed hands, the evangelist adds the parenthesis that Jesus thereby declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19); in Acts chapter 10, Peter sees in a vision a great sheet descending, with all kinds of beasts, creeping things and birds in it, and hears a voice saying, "What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common" — and the destination of that vision is the acceptance of the Gentiles, the opening of the gospel across the boundary; in Romans chapter 14, Paul treats the freedom of eating and not eating, and mutual non-judgement; in Colossians 2:16, Paul says, "Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink." Homiletical use of these passages carries this chapter's themes of holiness and discernment into the life of believers under the New Testament order of grace: the focus of our discernment today is no longer the menu, but whether the heart, conduct, and all that we draw near to agree with the character of the holy God. Such use is a legitimate bridge; reading it back into the chapter is a false reading. Distinguish these two levels, and the ordinances of the Old Testament are neither cheaply discarded nor wrongly laid as a burden on believers' shoulders.

We close with today. Verse 47, the last verse of the chapter, states the chapter's purpose in its own words: "To make a difference between the unclean and the clean, and between the beast that may be eaten and the beast that may not be eaten" (Lev 11:47, KJV). Forty-seven verses of ordinances gather into one act: separation. God set this lesson at the table because the table is where a man returns three times a day; God entrusted this lesson to the whole people because being set apart unto holiness is not the profession of a few, but the common life of His people. May we go out from here today not carrying a longer list of forbidden foods, but a pair of trained eyes: grateful at food, careful in contact, and mindful of our identity in every choice. We are a people brought up out of Egypt, a people set apart to God. "Ye shall be holy, for I am holy." That sentence, written four thousand years ago beside the tabernacle in the wilderness, is still written today beside every one of our tables. Let us bow our heads and pray: Holy Lord, You redeemed us first, and only then taught us. Give us a discerning heart, we pray, so that in daily eating, drinking and touching we do not drift with the stream and do not defile ourselves, but are set apart to You alone, holy as You are holy. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

1. tame' and tahor: tame', Hebrew טָמֵא, transliterated tame', adjective, unclean, Strong's number H2931; its noun form tum'ah, Hebrew טֻמְאָה, transliterated tum'ah, uncleanness (as a state), Strong's number H2932. tahor, Hebrew טָהוֹר, transliterated tahor, adjective, clean, Strong's number H2889. Lexical data after BDB: tame' is listed as unclean, defiled, used in both ritual and moral domains according to context; HALOT: ritually unclean. In Leviticus 11-15 the usage is chiefly ritual: a state that temporarily unfits a person to approach the sanctuary, arising from contact with carcasses, childbirth, disease or discharges, having a term and a rite of cleansing, and distinct from moral guilt — the distinction between the ritual and moral categories drawn in the sermon rests on this.

2. sheqets: Hebrew שֶׁקֶץ, transliterated sheqets, noun, detestable thing, that which is to be abhorred, Strong's number H8263. Lexical data after BDB: detestation, abomination, especially of idols and unclean things; HALOT: detestable thing, abomination. Leviticus 11 uses the word repeatedly of scaleless water creatures, birds and creeping things (11:10-12, 20, 23, 41-42); it is the term of rejection within the classification, not a designation of moral evil as such. The parallel list in Deuteronomy 14 uses the same family of words.

3. ma'aleh gerah: the Hebrew phrase מַעֲלֵה גֵרָה, transliterated ma'aleh gerah, literally "that which brings up the cud," i.e. that which chews the cud. gerah, Hebrew גֵּרָה, transliterated gerah, noun, the chewed mass brought back up, Strong's number H1625. Lexical data after BDB: cud; HALOT: cud, what is chewed again. Leviticus 11:3-8 and Deuteronomy 14:6-8 use the phrase together with the divided hoof as the double standard for beasts.

4. mafris parsah: the Hebrew phrase מַפְרִיס פַּרְסָה, transliterated mafris parsah, literally "that which parts the hoof," i.e. that which divides the hoof. parsah, Hebrew פַּרְסָה, transliterated parsah, noun, hoof, Strong's number H6541. Lexical data after BDB: hoof, cloven hoof; HALOT: hoof. The "parting" of the sermon refers to the hoof's being fully divided into two toes (cf. the wording of 11:3 and 11:7), in contrast to the camel's padded foot.

5. qashqeset and senapir: qashqeset, Hebrew קַשְׂקֶשֶׂת, transliterated qashqeset, noun, scale, Strong's number H7193. Lexical data after BDB: scale (of fish); HALOT: scale. The other standard for water creatures is senapir, Hebrew סְנַפִּיר, transliterated senapir, noun, fin, Strong's number H5579; BDB: fin. Leviticus 11:9-12 makes fins and scales the double standard for water creatures, structurally parallel to the double standard for beasts.

6. The four half-qualified cases: camel, gamal, Hebrew גָּמָל, Strong's number H1581; hyrax (coney), shaphan, Hebrew שָׁפָן, transliterated shaphan, Strong's number H8227, glossed by BDB as rock badger, hyrax; hare, arnevet, Hebrew אַרְנֶבֶת, transliterated arnevet, Strong's number H768, glossed by BDB as hare; swine, chazir, Hebrew חֲזִיר, transliterated chazir, Strong's number H2386. All four appear in 11:4-8: the camel, the hyrax and the hare are classed as chewing the cud but do not divide the hoof; the swine divides the hoof but does not chew the cud. The "cud-chewing" of the hyrax and the hare is ancient observational classification: the chewing motion of their jaws resembles rumination in appearance, while modern zoology does not count them as ruminants. Milgrom and Wenham both note that the basis of the classification is the text's observational standard; this sermon states both plainly and does not set the text against modern taxonomy.

7. The bird list without a standard: 11:13-19 lists only the names of the birds that may not be eaten, and the bat, without laying down a criterion of judgement — a different literary procedure from that for beasts and water creatures, where the standard is given first and examples follow. This is a factual observation about the text's form. Many of the Hebrew bird names in the list are of disputed identification, such as nesher (eagle, vulture), peras (ossifrage) and ozniyyah (ospray); BDB and HALOT mark the uncertain entries in each case. The bat, atalleph, Hebrew עֲטַלֵּף, Strong's number H5847, stands at the end of the list, showing that the classification assigns it to the domain of the air by the function of flight.

8. The edible insects: 11:21-22 permits only those with jointed legs for leaping. kera'ayim, Hebrew כְּרָעַיִם, transliterated kera'ayim, noun, legs (especially the hind legs), Strong's number H3767. The four names listed — arbeh (locust, H697), sol'am, chargol and chagav — are all noted by BDB and HALOT as insects whose exact species cannot be determined with certainty; the renderings follow the common versions. The morphological standard of the ordinance lies in the jointed leaping legs, not in human impressions.

9. sherets: Hebrew שֶׁרֶץ, transliterated sherets, noun, creeping things, small creatures moving in swarms, Strong's number H8318. Lexical data after BDB: swarming things, teeming expanse; HALOT: small animals moving in swarms. The same root is used in Genesis 1 for what the waters and the earth teem with; in Leviticus 11:29-31 and 41-43 it denotes the creeping things of the earth, whose carcasses' power to defile is one of the themes of the chapter's second half.

10. neveilah and the defilement procedures: carcass, Hebrew נְבֵלָה, transliterated neveilah, noun, carcass, dead body, Strong's number H5038; BDB: carcase, corpse. The defilement procedures of 11:24-40 include uncleanness until evening, washing of clothes, putting vessels into water, and the breaking of earthen vessels; keli, Hebrew כְּלִי, vessel, implement, Strong's number H3627. Hartley explains the breaking of earthen vessels by their porous material, which absorbs defilement beyond washing; Milgrom explains the exception for fountains and pits of water by the classificatory notion of living water as self-purifying. The details are set side by side; the sermon takes the main points.

11. qadosh and the holiness declaration: qadosh, Hebrew קָדוֹשׁ, transliterated qadosh, adjective, holy, set apart as holy, Strong's number H6918; the root sense is separation, being marked off for God; BDB lists holy, sacred, and HALOT holy, set apart. The verbal form in 11:44 is וְהִתְקַדִּשְׁתֶּם ("ye shall sanctify yourselves"). Intertexts: Exodus 19:6, a kingdom of priests and a holy nation; Leviticus 19:2, "Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy"; Leviticus 20:26, "ye shall be holy unto me... and have severed you from other people, that ye should be mine." Together with 11:44-45 these belong to the declarative structure in which holy identity precedes the training of conduct; the sermon's account of the redemption ground (11:45, "I am the LORD that bringeth you up out of the land of Egypt") rests on this chain of intertexts.

12. Sources for the New Testament reading level: Mark 7:19, "purging all meats," is the evangelist's parenthesis; the destination of Peter's vision in Acts 10 is the acceptance of the Gentiles; Romans 14 treats freedom in eating and mutual non-judgement; Colossians 2:16 grounds the refusal of judgement in matters of food and drink. All four belong to the New Testament's fresh handling of food boundaries after Christ has accomplished redemption; in homiletical use they must be marked as a New Testament reading and application level, and not read back into the original meaning of Leviticus 11 within the old covenant framework.

## Devotional Excerpt

At breakfast today I thought again of that word, set apart. God did not hand me a list first and then decide whether to love me; He brought me out of Egypt first, and only then taught me how to discern at the table. Holiness is not an examination but an identity: I am one whom He has set apart for Himself, so what I eat, what I touch and what I draw near to are all worth a slower question — does this agree with His character? Some things meet only half the standard, like the swine, which parts the hoof but does not chew the cud; so I too may look respectable outwardly while never chewing His word over within. Lord, give me trained eyes today, and a heart willing to digest: to take Your word in and chew it slowly, until it yields both flavour and obedience. And if I am defiled, I need not fear: You have left the way of washing and waiting, and at evening I may be clean again and come before You once more. May every meal today be more than filling; may it be a remembrance — I am one whom He brought out, and I will be holy, for He is holy.

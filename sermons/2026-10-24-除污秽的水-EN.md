# The Water of Separation — A Sermon on Numbers 19

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Draft — awaiting cross-review (Sermon Production Batch 3, 24 October 2026, unpublished)
Text: Numbers chapter 19 (22 verses in full, in the numbering of the Chinese and English Bibles; the Hebrew canon was checked book by book and agrees with this chapter and these verses — see Base-text notes and Endnote 1)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), checked verse by verse against the whole chapter on 24 October 2026; the companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (Simplified, Shen edition), verse by verse. The English text is not a back-translation of the Chinese: every quotation below is KJV wording, and where the two base texts differ in wording, order, or distribution, the difference is registered, not harmonized, and each manuscript follows its own base text.
Title verification: The title is taken from the name given to the ashes' use in verse 9, read as it stands in the base text: the ashes of the heifer are kept "for the congregation of the children of Israel for a water of separation: it is a purification for sin." (19:9). The name "water of separation" occurs five times in the KJV of this chapter (19:9, 13, 20, and twice in 19:21; at 19:12 the KJV has only the pronoun "with it," where the Chinese base text names the water expressly — the distributions differ and are registered in Endnote 4). The first half of the chapter is the making of this water (19:1-10), the second half its use and the refusal of it (19:11-22); the whole chapter, both halves, hangs on this one vessel. Candidates examined verse by verse and set aside: "a red heifer" (19:2) governs only the first part of the making paragraph — once the heifer has been burned to ashes she appears no more, and the name cannot cover the sprinkling and cutting-off cases; "it is a purification for sin" (19:9) is only the characterising clause of the ashes and its reach ends at verse 9; "his uncleanness is yet upon him" (19:13) covers only the side of the man who refuses cleansing, not the making paragraph; "shall be unclean until the even" occurs five times in the chapter but is the time-limit sentence for handlers and touchers of the water — the chapter's closing cadence, not its eye. The title is therefore verse 9's "water of separation." The Chinese title is taken independently from the same verse in its own base text, where the water is named by its function (the water that removes uncleanness); the KJV names it by the literal sense of its Hebrew name (separation). The two titles point to the same thing in different wording; each follows its own base text, and neither is a translation of the other. Full record in Endnote 2.
Base-text notes (where the two base texts or common paraphrase differ, the base text governs): KJV 19:2 gives the heifer's conditions in the order "a red heifer without spot, wherein is no blemish, and upon which never came yoke" — red first, spotlessness stated twice ("without spot," "wherein is no blemish"), the yoke last; the Chinese base text gives three coordinate conditions in the order no blemish, never yoked, purely red, with red last and spotlessness stated once. The orders and the doubling differ; each manuscript registers its own text. KJV 19:3 reads "ye shall give her unto Eleazar the priest," the title before the name, as in verse 4. KJV 19:4 reads "take of her blood with his finger, and sprinkle of her blood" — "of her blood," and the verb is "sprinkle." KJV 19:5 lists "her skin, and her flesh, and her blood, with her dung," four items. KJV 19:6 lists "cedar wood, and hyssop, and scarlet" — "scarlet" stands alone, where the Chinese reads the two-word phrase "scarlet thread." KJV 19:7-8 give the order washing first and uncleanness until the even last ("Then the priest shall wash his clothes... and the priest shall be unclean until the even"); the Chinese base text gives the opposite order, declaring the uncleanness first and the washing after. The two differ, and neither manuscript rewrites the other. KJV 19:9 reads "a purification for sin." KJV 19:11 reads "shall be unclean seven days," where the Chinese order is "seven days unclean" — the word order differs, each text as it stands. KJV 19:12 states the act expressly on the third day only ("He shall purify himself with it on the third day"), the seventh day being the result ("and on the seventh day he shall be clean"); verse 19 states the sprinkling on both the third day and the seventh. The two verses divide the labour differently and are not merged in either manuscript. KJV 19:13 names what is defiled "the tabernacle of the LORD"; KJV 19:20 names it "the sanctuary of the LORD" — two words within one chapter, in both base texts (tabernacle/sanctuary), each verse as it stands. KJV 19:15 reads "every open vessel, which hath no covering bound upon it, is unclean." KJV 19:16 lists the field cases as "one that is slain with a sword... or a dead body, or a bone of a man, or a grave," and closes "shall be unclean seven days." KJV 19:17 reads "running water shall be put thereto in a vessel." KJV 19:19 reads, of the seventh day, "he shall purify himself," which on its face can be read as the unclean man's self-purifying; the Chinese base text reads "shall make him clean," with the clean person as subject — the two readings differ and are registered, not harmonized. KJV 19:20 reads "cut off from among the congregation," where the Chinese reads "from among the assembly" with a shorter phrase. KJV 19:21 gives the statute's recipients as "unto them," where the Chinese reads "unto you" — the pronouns differ. KJV 19:22 closes the chapter: "whatsoever the unclean person toucheth shall be unclean." Chapter division: checked book by book, the Hebrew canon has this same chapter 19 with the same 22 verses, beginning and ending where the Chinese and English numbering begins and ends (the displacement of chapter boundary at chapter 17 has long been closed before this chapter); this sermon uses the Chinese-English numbering throughout and records here that the chapter was checked and found the same.

## Summary

Chapter 18 established the offices and their provision; chapter 19 follows at once with the most practical statute of all: when a man dies, what are the living to do? The LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron (19:1) — the chapter's only speech introduction — and everything under it is one single statute. The statute falls into two halves. The first half (19:2-10) is the making of the water of separation: the children of Israel bring a red heifer without spot, wherein is no blemish, and upon which never came yoke, and give her to Eleazar the priest, who brings her forth without the camp, where she is slain; the priest takes of her blood with his finger and sprinkles it directly before the tabernacle of the congregation seven times; the heifer is burned whole, and the priest casts cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet into the burning; a man that is clean gathers up the ashes and lays them up without the camp in a clean place, to be kept for the congregation for a water of separation. Everyone who handles any of this — the priest, the burner, the gatherer of the ashes — is himself unclean until the even. The second half (19:11-22) is the use of the water: he that touches the dead body of any man is unclean seven days and must purify himself with it on the third day, and on the seventh day he shall be clean; when a man dies in a tent, all that come into the tent and all that is in it are unclean seven days, and every open vessel likewise; in the open fields, whoever touches one slain with a sword, a dead body, a bone, or a grave is unclean the same seven days. For such a one they take of the ashes of the burnt heifer and put running water thereto in a vessel, and a clean person takes hyssop, dips it in the water, and sprinkles the unclean on the third day and on the seventh, and on the seventh day he is clean. The man who is unclean and will not purify himself shall be cut off from among the congregation, because he has defiled the sanctuary of the LORD. The chapter's undertone is an arrangement that looks like a contradiction: the water that makes others clean comes out of a burned heifer, and the men who handle the cleansing thing are themselves the first to contract uncleanness. Death in the camp was an everyday matter; God's way out was not to tell men to keep away from the dead, but to have the ashes of purification prepared beforehand and a procedure of sprinkling laid down, so that a man who had touched death could still come back into the midst of the congregation.

## Main Text

### 1. A Red Heifer: Slain Without the Camp, Her Blood Sprinkled Seven Times (Numbers 19:1-4) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) The chapter speaks only once. Verse 1, as it stands in the base text: "And the LORD spake unto Moses and unto Aaron, saying," (19:1). Chapter 18 had four utterances of God, three addressed to Aaron and one directing Moses; this chapter has the one introduction at its head, addressed to Moses and Aaron together, and the twenty-one verses under it (19:2-22) are all the content of that one speech, running from the making of the heifer to the cutting off of the refuser in a single breath. The count is registered first: the speech introduction occurs once in the chapter (19:1), standing in exact contrast to chapter 18's four — the offices had to be directed paragraph by paragraph, but the statute of cleansing is issued at once as one whole set, because it is the making and the using of one and the same vessel, and taken apart it cannot be read. Verse 2 sets up the statute's heading, quoted in full: "This is the ordinance of the law which the LORD hath commanded, saying, Speak unto the children of Israel, that they bring thee a red heifer without spot, wherein is no blemish, and upon which never came yoke:" (19:2). Registered item by item: the statute's source is the law which the LORD has commanded, and its nature is one ordinance within it; the first step of execution lies with the people — the children of Israel are to bring the heifer; the heifer's conditions, in the KJV's own order, are red, without spot and with no blemish in her, and never having borne a yoke. Read in that order: red first, the colour's singleness; then spotlessness, stated twice over, the body's wholeness; last the yoke she never came under — never used, never entered into man's labour. (The Chinese base text orders the three conditions otherwise — no blemish, never yoked, purely red — and states spotlessness once; the difference of order and doubling is registered in the head of this sermon, and each manuscript reads its own text.) Taken together, this heifer is not one drawn at random from the stall: she is whole, unused, and of one colour — the text nails the standard of choosing into its first verse, and the thoroughness of the burning that follows (19:5) answers exactly to this strictness of choosing: first wholeness, and the burning can be clean.

(Fact) Verse 3 hands the heifer over, quoted in full: "And ye shall give her unto Eleazar the priest, that he may bring her forth without the camp, and one shall slay her before his face:" (19:3). The three acts belong to three parties: she is given to Eleazar the priest — the priest who actually handles the statute in this chapter is not Aaron but Eleazar, and this sermon registers his name as the base text gives it, adding no explanation beyond his title; she is brought forth without the camp — the place of slaughter and burning is outside the camp, and "without the camp" occurs twice in the chapter (19:3, 9): the slaughter is here and the laying up of the ashes is here, and the making of the cleansing vessel is not carried on inside the camp; and one slays her before his face — the slayer is named in the text only as "one," without a name; Eleazar's office is presence and the taking of the blood, and the knife is not in his hand. Verse 4 is the act the priest does with his own hand, quoted in full: "And Eleazar the priest shall take of her blood with his finger, and sprinkle of her blood directly before the tabernacle of the congregation seven times:" (19:4). Three items registered: the manner of taking the blood is with his finger; the direction is directly before the tabernacle of the congregation — the heifer is slain without the camp, but the blood is sprinkled toward the tabernacle within it, and the place of making and the place the blood faces are bound together by that direction; the number is seven times — the chapter's numbers make their first appearance here, before the seven days that follow. The blood is sprinkled at this one place only, and seven times only; the heifer is then burned whole, and the blood has no other use; the sprinkling rite of this chapter uses ashes and water (19:17-19), not blood — blood and ashes go each its own way in this chapter, set side by side as the base text gives them, with no relation arranged for them beyond that order.

(Application) The first lesson of this section for today's hearers is preparation before need. The water of separation was not mixed on the spot when someone happened to touch a dead body: the statute was issued first, the heifer burned to ashes first, the ashes laid up beforehand without the camp in a clean place (19:9), and only then did a man come on the third day to use them (19:12, 17). God's way of dealing with uncleanness is to prepare the way out first and then wait for the man who needs it. In the camp men died every day — that is precisely the premise of the cases from verse 11 onward — and God neither waited for death to happen before improvising a response, nor told every man who touched a corpse to go and find a red heifer of his own. The church's vessels for holding up fallen men are the same: the arrangements for prayer, the hands ready to visit, the procedures that catch the weak, are all to be set up before a man falls; to begin discussing who should go and how, only after he is already lying down, makes the discussion itself a second delay. Ashes prepared first, men waited for after — the order itself is mercy.

(Application) The second lesson stands at "without the camp." The heifer is brought forth without the camp to be slain and burned there, and the ashes are laid up there; the cleansing water comes, of all places, from outside the camp. A people centred on the tabernacle and keeping themselves clean have the vessel of their cleansing produced beyond the boundary of their own camp. Today's hearers have their own "without the camp": the hospital ward, the hospice room, the funeral place, the cemetery, the bedside of the dying — places men would rather not go in ordinary days and count as taboo. This chapter speaks of them very calmly: God did not tell the people to pretend these places do not exist, nor to carry the dead hastily out and be done; he told the priest himself to go out of the camp and finish the work of preparing cleansing there. Serving men at the edge of death — sitting with the dying, conducting funerals, comforting the bereaved — is not, on this chapter's map, miscellaneous work outside the camp; it is the place where the priest himself must stand. And the blood is sprinkled toward the tabernacle besides: the work outside the camp and the worship inside it are two ends of one and the same thing.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Heifer" is pārâ (H6510), specifically a cow; wherever the Chinese base text expressly says "mother cow" (19:2, 5, 9, 10) this is the word. The word-form for "red" is ʾădummâ, "red" in its feminine form, agreeing with the heifer's gender; its number was not separately verified before this manuscript was written, and the form alone is therefore given. The word behind the blemish clause is mûm (H3971), a bodily defect or blemish. "Yoke" is ʿōl (H5923). "Sprinkle" in verse 4 is nāzâ (H5137), to sprinkle by flicking with the finger: the sprinkling of the blood in verse 4 uses this word, and so does the sprinkling of the water in verses 18, 19, and 21; note that verses 13 and 20, saying the water "was not sprinkled" upon a man, use a different word, zāraq (H2236) — the division of labour between the two words in this chapter is registered as the original gives it. "Slay" is šāḥaṭ (H7819). All numbered items above were verified one by one before writing; the register is in Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The standing of the red heifer statute in later tradition is set out without adjudication: Jewish tradition counts this chapter among the least fathomable statutes of the law; the Second Temple period has traditional records of the making and distributing of the ashes, and the number ever made across the ages is, in the traditional literature, extremely small — all of this belongs to the tradition and discussion layers and is not used in the sermon text as fact. At this chapter's fact layer only one thing stands: the base text issues the manner of making and using and records not a single actual occasion of the making; to argue back from the fewness or manyness of later practice to the force of this chapter's clauses belongs to the discussion layer and is not adopted here.

### 2. Burned to Ashes: The Ashes of Purification and the Uncleanness of the Handlers (Numbers 19:5-10) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 5 is the burning's inventory, quoted in full: "And one shall burn the heifer in his sight; her skin, and her flesh, and her blood, with her dung, shall he burn:" (19:5). Three items registered: the burning is carried on in Eleazar's sight, the supervision never leaving the place; the burning's range is the whole heifer — skin, flesh, blood, and dung named one by one, blood and dung not excepted: the blood has already been taken and sprinkled (19:4), and the remaining blood is burned with the heifer; no skin is kept, no flesh is kept, no part may be put to another use. The thoroughness of this verse answers, head to tail, to the strictness of the choosing in verse 2: a heifer whole, unused, and of one colour goes whole into the fire and becomes wholly ashes. In verse 6 the priest must cast three more things into the fire, quoted in full: "And the priest shall take cedar wood, and hyssop, and scarlet, and cast it into the midst of the burning of the heifer." (19:6). The three are quoted as they stand: cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet, all cast into the midst of the burning, burned with the heifer and reduced to ashes with her. The text explains no symbolism of the three things, and this sermon registers the fact only, as its rule is: of these three, only one returns later in the chapter — the hyssop, which the clean person in verse 18 takes to dip in the water and sprinkle with; the cedar wood and the scarlet appear in this verse once only, are finished with the burning, and are given no sequel here.

(Fact) Verses 7-10 are a string of parallel closings, four verses of almost identical construction, quoted verse by verse. Verse 7: "Then the priest shall wash his clothes, and he shall bathe his flesh in water, and afterward he shall come into the camp, and the priest shall be unclean until the even." (19:7) Verse 8: "And he that burneth her shall wash his clothes in water, and bathe his flesh in water, and shall be unclean until the even." (19:8) Verse 10 is quoted after the laying up of the ashes. The order of the formula is registered first: the KJV puts the washing first and the uncleanness until the even last; the Chinese base text puts the order the other way round, declaring the uncleanness first and the washing after. The two differ, and the English fact layer follows the KJV only. In content, one thing must be seen still more clearly: the men who transact this cleansing business contract uncleanness themselves first — the priest who presides over the burning is unclean, the man who does the burning is unclean, and the man who gathers the ashes below is unclean too (19:10). The making of the cleansing vessel is not, in this chapter, a sterile procedure: the water is for removing other men's uncleanness, yet the men who make the water must spend the time until the even in uncleanness. The text offers no explanation and no apology for this; it registers it man by man, as if it went without saying — and this sermon states that "without saying" exactly as it stands, without softening its wording.

(Fact) Verse 9 is the chapter's pivot: the laying up of the ashes and the naming of the water are both here, quoted in full: "And a man that is clean shall gather up the ashes of the heifer, and lay them up without the camp in a clean place, and it shall be kept for the congregation of the children of Israel for a water of separation: it is a purification for sin." (19:9). Registered item by item: the gatherer of the ashes must be a man that is clean — the last link of the making chain expressly names the handler's state; the place of keeping is without the camp, in a clean place — outside the camp, yet clean, the two standing side by side: the location is outside the camp, the state must be clean; the ashes' use is to be kept for the congregation for a water of separation — the name "water of separation" appears in this verse for the first time, and the chapter's title is taken from here; and the characterising clause: "it is a purification for sin" — these ashes, this water, are entered in the law's account under the head of purification for sin, and this sermon registers that characterisation in the base text's own wording, neither enlarging it nor reducing it. Verse 10 closes the making paragraph, quoted in full: "And he that gathereth the ashes of the heifer shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until the even: and it shall be unto the children of Israel, and unto the stranger that sojourneth among them, for a statute for ever." (19:10). The gatherer of the ashes washes his clothes and is unclean until the even, under the same rule as the priest and the burner; and the statute's range reaches to the stranger who sojourns among them — the water of separation is not a vessel for Israel's own household only; the sojourner stands within the statute too. With this verse the making paragraph is finished: from the bringing of the heifer to the laying up of the ashes, nine verses in all (19:2-10), one vessel prepared and ready, waiting to be used.

(Application) The first lesson of this section is that cleansing has a price, and the price falls on its agents. The priest is unclean until the even, the burner is unclean until the even, the gatherer of the ashes is unclean until the even — the chapter repeats the sentence three times without tiring of it. The man who prepares cleansing for others must himself go into uncleanness for a while first: that is this chapter's plainly stated fact, and it does not need the New Testament before it can be understood. The men who transact cleansing business in the church today — those who counsel, who intercede, who handle disputes, who clear up wreckage — all know the same experience: the business is done, but the sense of defilement stays on their own persons; they must wash, and wait, until the even. The text promises no exemption from contagion for the men who transact cleansing; what it promises is this: the contagion has a term, until the even; and the washing has a procedure, wash the clothes and bathe the flesh in water. Let the servant neither think himself forsaken because ashes have stuck to him, nor stiffen himself and say he is unaffected — let him wash according to the procedure and wait according to the term, and the even will pass.

(Application) The second lesson stands in "it is a purification for sin." Ashes are ashes; chemically considered they are no different from hearth ash; yet the text gives them a characterisation: a purification for sin. They are a purification for sin not because there is anything in the ashes, but because God has placed the office of cleansing upon these ashes, in this water. A vessel's worth lies not in its material but in its appointment. Many of the vessels by which men are cleansed in the church today are just as ordinary: a cup of water, a piece of bread, a spoken word of confession, a prayer with the laying on of hands — the materials are all ordinary; the appointment is not. If a man prices them by their material, he will slight these vessels, thinking them too coarse and too simple to transact so great a matter; the ashes of this chapter stand there to remind him: when God transacts great things, he is accustomed to use what is left over from burning. That ordinary thing in your hand, even that ash-like thing — if he has appointed it to transact cleansing, do not be ashamed of it on its behalf beforehand.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Ashes" is ʾēper (H665), the ash left after burning; the ashes of verses 9, 10, and 17 are all this word (at verse 17 the Chinese base text renders it within the phrase "ashes of purification for sin"). "Burn" is śārap̄ (H8313); the burning of the heifer in this chapter and the "burnt" of verse 17 rest on this same root. "Cedar wood" is ʾerez (H730); "hyssop" is ʾēzôb (H231), twice in the chapter (19:6, 18); "scarlet" is šənî tôlāʿat — šənî (H8144), scarlet, with tôlāʿat (H8438), the worm from which the dye-stuff comes, the two words read together where the Chinese base text reads "scarlet thread." The Hebrew of "water of separation" is mê niddâ: niddâ (H5079) means, at root, separation, a state set apart as unclean, and the water is named after the thing it deals with; the KJV's "water of separation" takes precisely the word's literal sense. "Purification for sin" is ḥaṭṭāʾt (H2403), a word which in the law denotes both sin and the offering that purifies from sin; it stands at verses 9 and 17 of this chapter, rendered in the KJV "a purification for sin" at the one place and within "of purification for sin" at the other; this sermon registers the word-form and the renderings only and builds no double-meaning sermon on the one word here. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing.

(New Testament Reading) The two facts of the burning and of "without the camp" have one explicit echo at the New Testament reading layer, registered at that layer only: the thirteenth chapter of Hebrews treats of the bodies of the sin-offering beasts being burned without the camp, and joins it to Jesus suffering without the gate (Heb 13:11-12, New Testament reading layer) — the New Testament writer reads "burned without the camp" as a recognisable shape. At this chapter's fact layer only the chapter's own facts are registered: the heifer slain without the camp and burned without the camp, the ashes laid up without the camp; how that shape is taken up in the New Testament belongs to the reading layer, and this sermon does not lay it down beforehand at the fact layer.

### 3. He That Toucheth the Dead Body Shall Be Unclean Seven Days: The Cases of Uncleanness (Numbers 19:11-16) (24:00-42:00)

(Fact) The second half of the chapter opens with a general rule; verse 11, quoted in full: "He that toucheth the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days." (19:11). Registered item by item: the defiling thing is the dead body of a man — this chapter's uncleanness is tied from first to last to human death, not to the carcasses of beasts (the statute for beast carcasses stands elsewhere, cross-reference layer, see Endnote 6); the act is touching — contact counts, whether deliberate or not; the term is seven days, the chapter's longest cycle of cleansing, laid down from here, standing in exact two-grade contrast with the handlers' "until the even": the uncleanness contracted in transacting cleansing is limited by the even, the uncleanness contracted by touching a dead body runs to seven days. Verse 12 gives those seven days their way out, quoted in full: "He shall purify himself with it on the third day, and on the seventh day he shall be clean: but if he purify not himself the third day, then the seventh day he shall not be clean." (19:12). Read this verse's own division of labour as it stands: the act expressly stated falls on the third day — he shall purify himself with it, "it" being this water of separation (the KJV carries the reference by pronoun, where the Chinese base text names the water expressly at this verse; registered in Endnote 4); the seventh day is the result — he shall be clean. The conditional is read as it stands too: if he purify not himself the third day, then the seventh day he shall not be clean — the third day's act is the precondition of the seventh day's cleanness, and a man who skips the third day will not find the seventh arriving of itself. As for the concrete manner of the sprinkling — a clean person sprinkling on both the third day and the seventh — verse 19 has its own express text; the two verses divide the labour differently, and this sermon sets them side by side without merging: this verse lays down the man's own responsibility and time-limit, verse 19 the agent's procedure.

(Fact) Verse 13 is the chapter's heaviest-toned verse, quoted in full: "Whosoever toucheth the dead body of any man that is dead, and purifieth not himself, defileth the tabernacle of the LORD; and that soul shall be cut off from Israel: because the water of separation was not sprinkled upon him, he shall be unclean; his uncleanness is yet upon him." (19:13). Registered item by item: what is punished is not the act of touching a dead body — touching a dead body brings only seven days' uncleanness (19:11); any man may touch, and there is a way for the man who has touched; what is punished is "purifieth not himself": water available and not used. What is defiled is the tabernacle of the LORD: a man who carries uncleanness and will not be cleansed has his uncleanness entered in God's account as a defiling of the tabernacle — uncleanness is no longer his private state; it is a matter between him and the sanctuary. The penalty is cutting off: cut off from Israel, twice in the chapter (19:13, 20), with the wording and the object changing once more at verse 20. The reason clause is read as it stands: "because the water of separation was not sprinkled upon him" — the ground of condemnation is not the depth of the uncleanness but that the water was not sprinkled; and the closing clause, "his uncleanness is yet upon him," stands in exact contrast with verse 12's way out: on the man the water is sprinkled, the seventh day is cleanness; on the man it is not sprinkled, the uncleanness stays exactly as it was.

(Fact) Verses 14-16 are three applied cases, quoted verse by verse. Verse 14, the tent case: "This is the law, when a man dieth in a tent: all that come into the tent, and all that is in the tent, shall be unclean seven days." (19:14). When a man dies in a tent, the uncleanness reaches the whole space: those in it are of course unclean, and those who go in afterwards are unclean too, the term likewise seven days — this case widens the range of "touching": a man need not touch the dead; to have entered a tent where a man died is contagion enough. Verse 15, the vessel case, is one sentence: "And every open vessel, which hath no covering bound upon it, is unclean." (19:15). Of the vessels in the tent, the open one with no covering bound upon it is unclean; of the covered and bound one the text does not say it is unclean — this sermon registers the open side only, as the base text gives it, and adds no verdict for the bound side. Verse 16, the field case: "And whosoever toucheth one that is slain with a sword in the open fields, or a dead body, or a bone of a man, or a grave, shall be unclean seven days." (19:16). The four cases are quoted as they stand: one slain with a sword, a dead body, a bone of a man, a grave. The place moves from the tent to the open fields and the object from a whole dead man to a bone and a grave — the remains and the markers of death defile alike, and the term is still seven days. Taken together, the three cases show this chapter's register of uncleanness to be systematic: the person (v. 11), space (v. 14), vessels (v. 15), remains in the field (v. 16) — express text at every level, with not one place filled in by inference.

(Application) The first lesson of this section is to distinguish condition from choice. Touching a dead body is never, in this chapter, a sin: burying a father or mother requires touching, laying out the dead requires touching, and in the fields a man may touch a bone or a grave without meaning to — the law's whole characterisation of all this is "unclean seven days," with a way out given and no condemnation pronounced. The man who is actually cut off is the man who refuses cleansing. Men today habitually confuse these two things: at one end, treating a condition as a sin — having undergone a bereavement, touched ill luck, lived through a breaking — a man thinks himself abhorred by God and dares not come near; at the other end, treating a choice as a condition — with the road of confession, reconciliation, and return lying plainly before him, a man says he cannot help himself. This chapter's account is kept with complete clarity: a condition is entered as seven days only; a choice is what is entered as cutting off. What you have touched, God does not hold against you in condemnation; whether you are willing to go to the water's edge is your real account.

(Application) The second lesson stands at the third day. Whether the seventh day can bring cleanness depends on whether a man will purify himself on the third. God arranges cleansing as a procedure with a before and an after, not as a spell-like immediacy: first the sprinkling received on the third day, and only then the cleanness of the seventh. For men in a hurry to turn the page at once this is a lesson: after confession, some consequences, relationships, and habits must be settled slowly within the days; the third day's act done, the seventh day will come; and there are men who do nothing but wait for the seventh day — wait for feelings to pass, for time to dilute, for the other party to speak first — and the text says: purify not himself, and the seventh day he shall not be clean; time itself washes nothing. The days must be walked through in full and the act must not be skipped: that is the bone of this chapter's doctrine of cleansing.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Touch" is nāgaʿ (H5060), to make contact, to come against; the touching of verses 11, 13, 16, 18, 21, and 22 is all this word (what is touched in verse 22 being things and persons). The construction behind "dead body" is nepeš (H5315) with a form of the word for death: nepeš means at root life, a person; at verses 11 and 13 the Chinese base text renders it "the dead body of a man," a man who has died. The root of "unclean" is ṭāmēʾ: verb form H2930, adjective form H2931, occurring throughout the chapter — the densest root in the whole chapter; the corresponding root of "clean" is ṭāhēr, whose adjective ṭāhôr (H2889) serves for "a man that is clean" and "a clean place." "Cut off" is kārat (H3772), at root to cut down, to cut off, twice in the chapter (19:13, 20). "Tent" is ʾōhel (H168): three times in verse 14, and at verse 18 once in the KJV's wording ("upon the tent"), the persons there being named separately (the Chinese base text has the word twice at verse 18, within "the persons in the tent"; the distributions are registered in Endnote 4). "Vessel" is kəlî (H3627), at verses 15, 17, and 18 alike. "Grave" is qeber (H6913), once each at verses 16 and 18; "bone" is ʿeṣem (H6106), likewise once each at the two verses. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The relation of this chapter's cases of uncleanness to the cleansing statutes of Leviticus is much discussed in scholarship, set out without adjudication: Leviticus 11 treats the contagion of beast carcasses (the toucher unclean until the even, Lev 11:24-28, cross-reference layer) and Leviticus 21 the priest's distance from the dead (Lev 21:1-4, cross-reference layer), and both differ in weight from this chapter's seven days for a human corpse; whether the several statutes divide the labour or accumulated in layers belongs to scholarly discussion. This sermon, preaching this chapter, follows this chapter only: this chapter's seven days and its third-day and seventh-day procedure form, within the Pentateuch, the most complete set of corpse-cleansing law in its own right, and its agreements and differences with the statutes elsewhere are set side by side in Endnote 6, not harmonized into one general rule.

(Application) The third lesson stands at the tent and the vessels. When a man dies in a tent, those in the tent are unclean, those who enter the tent are unclean, and even the open vessels are unclean — uncleanness in this chapter takes up space: it does not cling to the dead man only; it spreads through a household, a room, a set of everyday utensils. When a household meets death and breaking today it is the same: one has gone, and in the space left behind everything carries the memory — the chair, the bowls and chopsticks, the smell of the room — and it wells up the moment a man comes in at the door. God did not tell Israel to burn the tent down in one fire, nor to leave it uninhabited from then on; what he gave was sprinkling and seven days: space can be cleansed, vessels can be used again, days can begin again. A house that death has spread through need not be demolished; sprinkle according to the procedure, wait according to the days, and it can still be lived in, still have meals set in it, still receive guests. The stroke about the open vessel is especially fine: what has no covering bound upon it is infected first — the man whose heart has no covering lets grief and defilement march straight in most easily; to bind a covering on the heart is not coldness, but keeping, amid bereavement and disturbance, one stretch of quiet set apart as holy, so that when the sprinkling day comes, the man is still there.

### 4. Ashes and Running Water: The Sprinkling Rite of the Third Day and the Seventh (Numbers 19:17-19) (42:00-56:00)

(Fact) Verse 17 restates the recipe and puts it into effect, quoted in full: "And for an unclean person they shall take of the ashes of the burnt heifer of purification for sin, and running water shall be put thereto in a vessel:" (19:17). Registered item by item: the ashes' origin is pointed out once more here — the ashes of the burnt heifer of purification for sin are the very heifer ashes laid up in verse 9, taken out days later when needed; the ashes are taken, and running water is put thereto in a vessel — "running water" read as it stands, the KJV's rendering, both base texts pointing to flowing water, not dead water; the recipe's proportions the text does not give, and this sermon registers that blank exactly as it stands: how much ash, how much water, this chapter does not say, the executor has nowhere in this chapter to look it up, and no later hand may fill tradition in as though it were text. Verse 18 is the execution of the sprinkling rite, quoted in full: "And a clean person shall take hyssop, and dip it in the water, and sprinkle it upon the tent, and upon all the vessels, and upon the persons that were there, and upon him that touched a bone, or one slain, or one dead, or a grave:" (19:18). Registered item by item: the sprinkler must be a clean person, under the same rule as the gatherer of the ashes in verse 9 — the man who transacts cleansing must himself first be clean; the instrument is hyssop, the very one of the three things cast into the fire in verse 6, now returning as the instrument of sprinkling; the objects sprinkled fall into two groups: one group is things and space — the tent, all the vessels, and the persons that were there; the other is the man of the field cases — him that touched a bone, or one slain, or one dead, or a grave, the four cases answering at a distance to verse 16 (verse 16 reads "one that is slain with a sword... or a dead body," this verse "one slain, or one dead" — the wording differs slightly, and each verse is registered as its own base text gives it). The sprinkling's range gathers the cases of verses 14-16 in together: persons, tent, and vessels are handled in one and the same sprinkling rite.

(Fact) Verse 19 lays down the sprinkling's days and its closing, quoted in full: "And the clean person shall sprinkle upon the unclean on the third day, and on the seventh day: and on the seventh day he shall purify himself, and wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and shall be clean at even." (19:19). Registered item by item: the sprinkling is twice, on the third day and on the seventh day, sprinkled by the clean person upon the unclean — read together with verse 12 the division of labour is clear: verse 12 speaks from the man's own side, "he shall purify himself with it," this verse from the agent's side, the clean person sprinkling; the two verses are two faces of one and the same procedure; the seventh day's result is given in the words "he shall purify himself" — on its face the KJV wording at this point can be read as the unclean man's purifying of himself (his own washing and bathing follow in the same verse), where the Chinese base text reads "shall make him clean," with the clean person making the unclean man clean; the two base texts read the clause differently, and the head of this sermon has registered it, without harmonizing; the sprinkled man's own closing acts are to wash his clothes and bathe himself in water, and he shall be clean at even — the great seven-day cycle closes on an "at even," answering at a distance to the small cycle of the handlers (19:7-10): within one statute, two kinds of uncleanness and two terms, and both end at last in washing and waiting.

(Application) The lesson of this section is that cleansing needs an agent. The man who sprinkles must be a clean person — the unclean man cannot sprinkle himself clean; this chapter's procedure has no self-service item in it: the ashes must be laid up by another, the water sprinkled by another, the days kept and counted by another on your behalf. That is precisely the lesson today's hearers are least used to: we would rather handle our own defilement ourselves — digest it ourselves, forget it ourselves, acquit ourselves — because letting another man know, and letting another man act as agent, wounds our dignity too much. This chapter's arrangement pointedly sets dignity aside: you touched a dead body, and the whole camp knows that on the third day and the seventh you must stand in the camp and be sprinkled; cleansing is completed before the eyes of all, under an agent's hand. Confession and restoration in the church should be the same: not hiding a man away to rot slowly, but a clean person, an open procedure, and days that can be counted. To be willing to stand before the sprinkler is the first step of cleansing; from the man who hides from the company, the water can never reach — "the water of separation was not sprinkled upon him" (19:13).

(Application) The second lesson stands in the running water. Dead ashes, mixed with living water, become the water of separation. The ashes are the past — burned out, finished, nothing left but residue; the running water is the present — flowing, fresh, to be taken and used at this moment. God's recipe for cleansing men requires both: without the ashes, a man forgets where this cleansing comes from and what shape its price took; without the running water, ashes are only ashes, which cannot be sprinkled out and can transact nothing. Some men today have ashes only: they keep a past failure, a past testimony, a past experience, and look at it over and over, and the more they look the drier it gets; others have water only: they want only this moment's feeling and a fresh experience, and will not look back at where the ashes come from. This chapter puts the two in one vessel: own your ashes, and receive the running water; the past is to be confessed, the present is to flow — mixed together, then it is the water of separation that can be sprinkled upon a man.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Running water" is mayim ḥayyîm, ḥayyîm being the plural form of ḥay (H2416), "living, life"; the Chinese base text renders it "living water" and the KJV "running water," the two renderings pointing alike to flowing water. "Sprinkle" in verses 18 and 19 is nāzâ (H5137), the same word as the sprinkling of the blood in verse 4; the matter of sprinkling the water in this chapter uses nāzâ for the positive administration (19:18, 19, 21) and zāraq (H2236) for the negative not-sprinkled (19:13, 20) — the two words' division of labour was registered in Section 1 and is only noted here as the correspondence. "Bathe" is rāḥaṣ (H7364), to wash in water; the "bathe his flesh" of verses 7 and 8 and the "bathe himself" of verse 19 rest on this same word. "Wash his clothes" is kābas (H3526), at verses 7, 8, 10, 19, and 21 of this chapter alike. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing.

### 5. The Man Who Refuses Cleansing Shall Be Cut Off from Among the Congregation: The Closing of Contagion (Numbers 19:20-22) (56:00-60:00)

(Fact) The last paragraph's three verses first lay down the refuser's sentence. Verse 20, quoted in full: "But the man that shall be unclean, and shall not purify himself, that soul shall be cut off from among the congregation, because he hath defiled the sanctuary of the LORD: the water of separation hath not been sprinkled upon him; he is unclean." (19:20). Set verse by verse against verse 13, the division of labour registered as it stands: verse 13 speaks of the man who touched a dead body and purifies not himself, its penalty is cutting off from Israel, what he defiles is the tabernacle of the LORD, and its reason is given as "his uncleanness is yet upon him"; this verse speaks of any man that shall be unclean and shall not purify himself, its penalty is cutting off from among the congregation, what he defiles is the sanctuary of the LORD, and its closing clause is "he is unclean." The two verses are two promulgations of one sentence: the object widens from the corpse-toucher to every unclean man, the place moves from the tabernacle to the sanctuary (the distinction between the two words is registered as the base text gives it, and this sermon sets up no scale of rank between them), and the ground remains one sentence throughout — the water of separation has not been sprinkled upon him. The chapter's heaviest penalty, twice over, front and back, never falls on contagion; it falls only on refusal.

(Fact) Verse 21 makes the statute's final confirmation, quoted in full: "And it shall be a perpetual statute unto them, that he that sprinkleth the water of separation shall wash his clothes; and he that toucheth the water of separation shall be unclean until even." (19:21). Two closings: first, the statute's recipients are given as "unto them" — verse 10 gives them as the children of Israel and the stranger that sojourneth among them, this verse as "them," the near and the far, each verse as its own base text gives it (the Chinese base text reads "unto you" at this verse; registered in the head of this sermon); second, the paradox closes the ring: he that sprinkles the water of separation shall wash his clothes, and he that touches the water of separation shall be unclean until even — the water that makes others clean makes the man who touches it himself unclean until the even instead, answering head to tail to the handlers' cases of verses 7-10: this water, from its making to its use, has not one handler excepted. Verse 22 is the chapter's last sentence, quoted in full: "And whatsoever the unclean person toucheth shall be unclean; and the soul that toucheth it shall be unclean until even." (19:22). The chain of contagion is closed here: the unclean person touches a thing, and the thing is unclean; a soul touches the thing, and the soul is unclean until even. The chain's length is read as it stands: thing-to-person contagion is registered for one link only, until even and no further, spreading no further without limit; and the term is read as it stands too: second-hand contagion is limited by the even, standing in its own grade beside the corpse-toucher's seven days and the handler's one even. The chapter closes on this contagion law: a statute that began with one red heifer lands at last on the everyday touching and being touched — the government of uncleanness is, in the end, the daily lesson of every man in the camp.

(Application) The closing lesson is: do not refuse. The water has long been prepared: the ashes laid up for years, the water to be mixed at any time, the sprinkling man right there in the camp, the days counted out with complete clarity. In this chapter a man loses his place in the congregation not because his uncleanness is too heavy — however heavy the uncleanness, seven days and two sprinklings and it is clean — but because he will not. The forms of refusal today are mostly unshowy: not a public "I do not believe," but delay — later, when I feel better, when the other man bows his head first; and as the days of delay lengthen, uncleanness passes from condition into identity, from seven days into "yet upon him." This chapter's warning and hope to the delayer are two faces of one sentence: where the water is not sprinkled, the uncleanness still remains; where a man lets the water be sprinkled, on the seventh day he is clean. The decision is not on the water's side — the water's side was decided long ago; the decision is whether you are willing to walk before the sprinkling man, stand still, and let him sprinkle.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The nature of cutting off (kārat) is much discussed in later ages, set out without adjudication: one reading takes it as extinction of congregational standing (struck from the roll of the people), another as a penalty executed by God himself (a cutting off no human hand can perform), another as containing both; this chapter lays down the sentence itself only — cut off from Israel, cut off from among the congregation — and records no procedure of execution, and the sermon text argues from the sentence only, the accounts of the execution level being kept on record. Again, this chapter's relation to the putting-out in chapter 5 (Num 5:2-3 puts those unclean by a dead body out of the camp, cross-reference layer): there the spatial isolation is laid down, here the temporal procedure of cleansing; the complete handling is seen only with the two together, and in preaching this chapter the cross-reference is registered only, without reading that putting-out into this chapter's fact layer.

## Devotional Excerpt

The man who touched a dead body was not a wicked man. He may have just buried his father, or found a corpse in the open field with none to bury it — the law's whole characterisation of him is a condition, not a charge: uncleanness for seven days, with the way out prepared beforehand — ashes laid up without the camp, running water to be mixed, a clean person to sprinkle him on the third day, and cleanness on the seventh. What this chapter fears is never the touching; it is the refusing: the water stands ready, and the man simply will not come. So let the reader who carries something today that he cannot wash off — a stretch of the past, a single failure, a debt of guilt he cannot say aloud — learn of the man who touched the dead, and not of the refuser: come at the appointed day, stand before the LORD, confess, receive. The ashes are dead, the water is living, and thy LORD has prepared both already; only come, and on the seventh day thou shalt be clean.

English (KJV-based, as it stands in the companion Chinese manuscript):

The man who touched a dead body was not a wicked man. He may have just buried his father, or found a corpse in the open field with none to bury it; the law's whole verdict on him is one sentence: "He that toucheth the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days." (Num 19:11) Uncleanness was a condition, not a charge; and before he ever touched the dead, God had prepared the way out: a red heifer burned without the camp, its ashes laid up and mixed with running water, waiting for him to come on the third day and let the water be sprinkled upon him, that on the seventh day he might be clean. What ruins a man in this chapter is never the touching; it is the refusing — "the water of separation hath not been sprinkled upon him; he is unclean." (19:20) If today thou carriest something thou canst not wash off — a past, a failure, a debt of guilt thou canst not name aloud — do not learn of the refuser, and let uncleanness harden into identity. Learn of the man who touched the dead: come at thy day, stand before the LORD, confess, receive, and be sprinkled. The ashes speak of death; the water is living; thy LORD has prepared both already. Only come — and on the seventh day thou shalt be clean.

Cross-references, each one counted and its layer marked — eight in all: (1) Numbers 5:2-3 — those unclean by a dead body are put out of the camp (law cross-reference layer): that passage lays down spatial isolation, this chapter the procedure of cleansing. (2) Leviticus 11:24-28, 39-40 — the toucher of a beast carcass unclean until the even (law cross-reference layer): there the small cycle of one even, here seven days for a human corpse — the weights set side by side, not confused. (3) Leviticus 14:4-7, 49-52 — the cleansing rite for leprosy uses cedar wood, hyssop, scarlet, and living water (law cross-reference layer): the combination of instruments overlaps with 19:6 and 19:17 of this chapter at several points and is the closest parallel procedure in the Pentateuch; the overlap is registered only, no symbolism erected on it. (4) Leviticus 16:27-28 — the sin-offering bullock and goat are carried forth without the camp and burned, and the burner washes (law cross-reference layer): parallel with this chapter at two points, burning without the camp and the handler's washing (the New Testament reading layer takes the line up again at Hebrews 13; see the reading-layer paragraph of Section 2). (5) Numbers 6:6-9 — the Nazarite must not come near a dead body, and if any man die very suddenly by him he must shave his head on the seventh day (law cross-reference layer): the stricter version, within the same book, for a person of special standing. (6) Numbers 31:19-24 — after the war, abiding without the camp seven days and purifying on the third day and the seventh (narrative cross-reference layer): one actual execution of this chapter's procedure within narrative. (7) Ezekiel 36:25 — "Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean" (reading layer): the prophetic taking-up of the sprinkling image. (8) Hebrews 9:13-14 — the ashes of an heifer sprinkling the unclean sanctify to the purifying of the flesh (New Testament reading layer): this chapter's anchor cross-reference in the New Testament; the sermon's reading is developed at this layer and at the application layer only, and is never poured back into this chapter's fact layer.
7. Supplement: the handlers' uncleanness, item by item against the base text: the priest Eleazar (19:7, washes his clothes, bathes his flesh in water, afterward comes into the camp, unclean until the even); the man who burns the heifer (19:8, washes his clothes in water, bathes his flesh in water, unclean until the even); the man who gathers the ashes (19:10, washes his clothes, unclean until the even); the man who sprinkles the water of separation (19:21, washes his clothes); the man who touches the water of separation (19:21, unclean until even); the soul who touches a thing the unclean person has touched (19:22, unclean until even). Six classes of handlers or touchers in all, every term limited by the even; beside them stand the corpse-touchers' seven days (19:11, 14, 16) — two grades in all, set side by side as the base text gives them, with no scale of grades erected here beyond the text's own.
8. Blanks of this chapter, registered as blanks: the proportions in which ashes and running water are mixed are not stated in this chapter; the day from which the seven-day cycle is reckoned (whether the day of touching counts in) is not expressly stated — the chapter lays down the two nodes of the third day and the seventh only; the heifer's age is not stated; the symbolism of "red" and of the three things (cedar wood, hyssop, scarlet) is not explained in the text, and this sermon registers word-forms and facts only, laying down no conclusion; the procedure for executing cutting off is not recorded. Later tradition and preaching that supply any of these belong to the tradition or reading layers; neither the sermon text nor these notes adopt them, and only the blanks themselves are registered.

## Summary (approx. 100 words)

The latter half of Genesis 11 presents another genealogy: generation after generation of Shem's descendants, with lifespans shortening from generation to generation, yet the list steadily narrows down, finally settling upon the family of Terah. Terah took Abram and Sarai out of idolatrous Ur, intending to go to the land of Canaan, but when they came to Haran, they stopped and dwelt there until he died there. Furthermore, Sarai was barren, and the line of promise seemingly reached a dead end. This sermon examines three things: God moves through genealogies without the slightest haste; the greatest danger on the path of calling is stopping halfway; where man stops is not the end, for God Himself would call Abram out once again to finish the journey his father left unfinished.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

In the previous message, we stood by the construction site of Babel: the tower ceased, the work dispersed, and people speaking languages they could not understand from one another went in all directions. Reading to that point, it is easy to get the false impression that the story has ended and God's plan has been disrupted.

Turn the page, and you will see that this is not so. In the second half of chapter 11, without a single word of complaint, the Bible begins counting names again: Shem begot Arphaxad, Arphaxad begot Salah, Salah begot Eber... counting down generation after generation for ten generations, down to a man named Terah, and down to Terah's son, named Abram. While the people of Babel were busy making a name for themselves, God was quietly journeying forward through the list of names—His line was never broken; you only need the patience to read through the genealogy.

Yet at the end of this list of names lies a sigh-inducing sentence: Terah's family departed "to go to the land of Canaan," came to Haran, and "dwelt there"; Terah died in Haran. They came out, but they never arrived. They walked, but they only went halfway. Today's message focuses on this halfway journey so that we may examine ourselves.

Let us first read the key passages of Scripture (quotations follow the Chinese Union Version in the original):

"This is the genealogy of Shem: Shem was one hundred years old, and begot Arphaxad two years after the flood. After he begot Arphaxad, Shem lived five hundred years, and begot sons and daughters." (Gen. 11:10-11)

"Terah begot Abram, Nahor, and Haran. Haran begot Lot." (Gen. 11:27)

"Then Abram and Nahor took wives: the name of Abram’s wife was Sarai... But Sarai was barren; she had no child." (Gen. 11:29-30, excerpt)

"And Terah took his son Abram and his grandson Lot, the son of Haran, and his daughter-in-law Sarai, his son Abram’s wife, and they went out with them from Ur of the Chaldeans to go to the land of Canaan; and they came to Haran and dwelt there. So the days of Terah were two hundred and five years, and Terah died in Haran." (Gen. 11:31-32)

Today we will examine this in three parts:

First, 11:10-26, the genealogy of Shem—moving sentence by sentence through "lived" and "begot"; lifespans are shortening, yet the line of promise is steadily narrowing down from family to family;
Second, 11:27-30, Terah's family—coming out of idolatrous Ur, yet within the home are two fractures: one son died early, and one daughter-in-law was barren;
Third, 11:31-32, stopping upon reaching Haran—the place where the father stopped becomes the very starting point where God calls Abram in the next chapter.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. When Babel dispersed, You did not disperse; while men grew old and died generation after generation within the genealogies, Your promise moved forward step by step. Today, we ask that You open our eyes to see our own journey that has stopped halfway: what we once left, what we have not finished, what remains stuck in Haran, reluctant to move. We also thank You that You do not despise those who have gone only halfway; You speak again in Haran to call people back onto the road. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: God Journeys Through the List of Names (11:10-26)

### 1.1 "This Is the Genealogy of Shem": A Reappearing Genealogy, Taking a Different Path

This opening in verse 10 is one we have seen in chapter 10: "genealogy" (or "generations"), the Hebrew *toledot* (H8435, generations, descendants, genealogies) [1]. The list in chapter 10 spread out horizontally—all nations dispersing in every direction; this list moves vertically downward—passing down from generation to generation. Chapter 10 shows how broad God's heart is, whereas this chapter shows how focused God's plan is: all nations are in His heart, yet the line of promise must narrow down to a single family, ultimately to bless all nations through this one family. Breadth and focus are never contradictory in God's hands.

Reading this genealogy has a distinct rhythm; every generation follows the exact same formula: "So-and-so lived so many years, and begot so-and-so. After he begot so-and-so, so-and-so lived so many years, and begot sons and daughters." Lived, begot, lived again, begot sons and daughters—it sounds plain, yet within it is the way God works: He does not advance history through grand chronicles; He does it through ordinary people living well, bearing and raising children, and bringing them up. Brothers and sisters, when God's great plan lands upon the earth, it often takes this very shape: there are no spotlights, just one generation raising up the next and passing the faith along.

Warren W. Wiersbe, in his commentary on Genesis, when explaining the genealogy of Shem, reminds readers to note that although human lifespans were continually decreasing, God's chosen line continued to advance through the descendants of Shem, demonstrating that God faithfully preserves His promise. (cf. Warren W. Wiersbe, *The Bible Exposition Commentary: Genesis*)

### 1.2 Lifespans Steadily Shrinking: Post-Flood Generations Grew Shorter by Generation

Laying out the numbers, one sees a downward trajectory: Shem lived 600 years, Arphaxad 438 years, down to Eber at 464 years, Peleg down to 239 years, Reu 239 years, Serug 230 years, Nahor 148 years, and Terah 205 years. Compared to the lifespans in chapter 5 before the flood—which frequently exceeded 900 years—the days of man were noticeably shortening after the flood.

Scripture offers no explanation for why, merely laying the numbers before us. Psalm 90 contains the words Moses wrote as he looked back on these generations: "The days of our lives are seventy years; and if by reason of strength they are eighty years" (Ps. 90:10, paraphrased excerpt). Moses also offered a prayer: "So teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom" (Ps. 90:12). Brothers and sisters, what is shrinking in the genealogy is not just numbers, but a reminder: the reality that our days are numbered becomes more vivid with each passing generation. By the end of the genealogy, every generation must hand off the baton—and whether you pass it well is far more important than how long you live.

There is another name in the list that warrants a pause: Eber (already appeared in 10:24, recorded again here in 11:16–17). "Eber," Hebrew *Eber* (H5677)[2], is linked to the root meaning "to cross over, pass through"; later, the designation "Hebrew" came from him (cf. Gen. 14:13, where Abram is called a Hebrew). There is also Peleg; as mentioned in chapter 10, his name means "division," commemorating the dividing of the earth among people (cf. Gen. 10:25). You see, even such a distressing event as dispersal was preserved by God in a name and made part of the genealogy—He does not tear out the unsightly pages; He keeps writing on every single page.

### 1.3 The End of the List Is a Family: Narrowing the Focus in Order to Widen It Again

Counting down ten generations, the camera lens ultimately settles on one family: Terah and his three sons. The cinematic sense of the biblical narrative is very clear here: from all nations to one clan; from one clan to one family; from one family to one man—Abram. First narrowing, then widening: in chapter 12, God will say to this one man that all the families of the earth shall be blessed in him (cf. Gen. 12:3).

Here is an application for you and me: do not underestimate the place of your generation in the list. You might feel that your page is utterly mundane: going to work, providing for the family, raising children, caring for elderly parents. The way to read genealogies tells you that God's line moves forward precisely through these ordinary, mundane days. When you pass the faith conscientiously to the next person, your page has not been written in vain.

To summarize the first part in a single sentence: **The genealogy of Shem shows that God is pressing forward on His journey through the list: lifespans are shortening, but His line does not shorten; the focus narrows to one family so that from this one family He may once again bless all the nations.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Family Coming Out of Ur, Bearing Two Fractures (11:27–30)

### 2.1 What Kind of Place Was Ur: Being Called Means First Coming Out of an Idolatrous Background

To understand the family of Terah, we must first know where they came from: "Ur of the Chaldeans" (Gen. 11:31). Ur was a major city in Mesopotamia at that time, boasting a sophisticated city-state religion that worshiped idols such as the moon god. Joshua 24:2 puts it unsparingly: Joshua said to the people that their fathers, including Terah the father of Abraham and the father of Nahor, dwelt on the other side of the River in old times, and they "served other gods." Nehemiah 9:7 also looks back to settle this account: God chose Abram and brought him "out of Ur of the Chaldeans."

This matters greatly to people today: Abram was not born into a multi-generational Christian family, nor was he a child raised in Sunday school from youth. He came from an idol-worshiping home. God’s election does not depend on pedigree; He goes in person into the city of idols and calls a person out. Brothers and sisters, your background, your family's past, what you were like in the years before you came to faith—none of these can hinder God's call. If He could find Abram in Ur, He can find someone in any background today and begin writing their genealogy anew.

"Abram," Hebrew *Abram* (H87)[3], means "exalted father." Please keep in mind the tension inherent in this name: a man named "exalted father," yet his wife was barren, without even a single child. The name and the reality stood in stark contradiction—this is the very foreshadowing of the next chapter, where God will intervene to change his name and change his destiny.

### 2.2 The First Fracture in the Family: Haran Died Before His Father (11:27–28)

Verses 27 and 28 first announce a death: "Haran begot Lot. And Haran died before his father Terah in his native land, in Ur of the Chaldeans."

The white-haired burying the black-haired—a parent outliving a child—is the most agonizing inversion of order in human life. The Bible records this event in just a single sentence, offering no explanation, and moves on. But notice that Lot, the son left behind by Haran, followed Abram from then on—in Abram's company there was always a nephew who had lost his father. A called family is not a family without wounds: in this household that was to be greatly used by God, there was premature death, there was an orphan, there were tears. God did not wait for this family to heal completely before starting; He set out on the journey with their wounds.

### 2.3 The Second Fracture in the Family: Sarai Was Barren (11:29–30)

In verse 30, the Bible records the heaviest matter in the briefest sentence: "But Sarai was barren; she had no child."

Only when we place this sentence back into its context does its full weight emerge: throughout the preceding ten generations of genealogy, sentence after sentence declared "begot, begot, begot sons and daughters"; the line of promise was passed down entirely through childbearing. Yet when it reached the most crucial family, the most pivotal couple—they could not bear children. A line that advanced through childbearing was broken right here. It was not severed by an external enemy; it was severed within the body, broken in the kind of grief that weeps behind closed doors at night.

"Barren," for related meaning, see the Hebrew *akarah* (H6135, a barren woman)[4], a word used elsewhere in Scripture to describe a woman who cannot bear children (cf. usage in Exod. 23:26, etc.); in Genesis 11:30, the Chinese Union Version translates literally as "was barren, having no child." The Lu Zhenzhong version serves as a comparative translation reference for this verse, though its rendering is not directly quoted in this manuscript.

Later in the Bible, the same pattern appears repeatedly—Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah, Elizabeth; the promise often comes first to those who "cannot bear." It is not that God is late; rather, God deliberately chooses to begin His work where human strength reaches its end, so that people may know that children are a grace, not an achievement earned by human effort. If your home is experiencing this kind of pain of being "cut off" today—a matter prayed over for so long with no response, a door knocked on with great effort that remains shut—do not rush to conclude that God has forgotten you. Reading the genealogy up to chapter 11, we discover precisely this: where the line is severed is the very place where God Himself will reconnect it in the next chapter.

Brothers and sisters, someone sitting here today faces a fracture right in their home: a loved one who departed too soon, a child who never comes, a matter prayed over for years without an answer. You feel that your genealogy has come to an end with you. On the authority of this chapter of Scripture, I say to you: Abram's genealogy was also "broken"—broken by his wife's barrenness, broken by his brother's untimely death—yet it was from this very fracture that God brought forth blessing for all nations. Your fracture is not the end; it is God's favorite place to go to work. Today, stop using your fracture to pass judgment on yourself; lay the fracture open before God, and let Him continue writing from the break. His hand that writes genealogies has never trembled.

To summarize the second part in a single sentence: **Terah's family came out of idol-worshiping Ur, bearing two fractures—untimely death and barrenness; God's election does not depend on pedigree nor does it wait for wounds to heal, and the line of promise will be reconnected by Him personally precisely where human eyes see it severed.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: Coming to Haran and Settling Down (11:31–32)

### 3.1 "To Go to the Land of Canaan": Terah's Direction Was Originally Right

Verse 31 must be read slowly: "And Terah took his son Abram and his grandson Lot, the son of Haran, and his daughter-in-law Sarai, his son Abram’s wife, and they went out with them from Ur of the Chaldeans to go to the land of Canaan;"

Please note: departing from Ur and heading toward Canaan—this direction was first taken under Terah. When Stephen recounted this history in Acts 7 in the New Testament, he said that when Abraham was in Mesopotamia, God's call had already come to him, telling him to leave his country and his relatives and go into the land that God would show him (cf. Acts 7:2–3). That is to say, the call came first to Abram, and Terah led the whole family in response. The father took the lead, the direction was right, and the whole family set out—what a wonderful beginning! How many family stories begin this way: someone first hears the call, the whole family moves in response, luggage is packed, houses are disposed of, relatives say farewell, and tears and hope set out together on the road.

Acts adds a crucial timing detail: Abram departed from Haran only after Terah died (cf. Acts 7:4). In other words, the stop in Haran was not just a matter of a few days; it was an interruption that lasted an entire generation, until the father who led the company passed away and the journey could resume.

### 3.2 "They came to Haran and dwelt there": The Most Dangerous Place to Stop Is a Good Place Halfway

The problem lies in the second half of the sentence: "they came to Haran and dwelt there."

Haran was not a bad place. Situated on the way from Ur to Canaan, it was an established commercial city, similar to Ur in language, customs, and moon-god worship—comfortable to live in, requiring no readjustment. Looked at on a map, Haran is roughly at the midpoint of the journey: one has already left the old land, yet has not arrived at the destination. Brothers and sisters, the halfway point is the hardest to leave. It is far enough that you can say, "I have already left," and close enough that you do not have to pay the price of "arriving." It gives people the illusion of having already obeyed, while sparing them the hardship of continuing to obey.

Terah ultimately died in Haran (11:32), and Scripture intentionally records his place of death: he died neither in Ur nor in Canaan, but in Haran—he died halfway along the way. For a man recorded in the genealogy, the location of his tombstone became a signpost for all believers in future generations: having once left does not equate to having arrived.

The words of the Gospels are fittingly placed here for self-examination: the Lord Jesus said, "No one, having put his hand to the plow, and looking back, is fit for the kingdom of God" (Luke 9:62). Putting one's hand to the plow is already setting out; looking back is the reason for stopping. What kept Terah's family in Haran was not necessarily that the road was too long, but more likely a backward glance at the old life: the moon-god temple there, the trade there, the circle of relatives there—everything was similar to Ur. As they went on living there, Canaan changed from a destination into a mere catchphrase.

### 3.3 Man Stopped, but God Did Not: Haran Is a Comma, Not a Period

Yet the final hope of this passage is hidden outside the name of Terah: the story did not stop in Haran. Turning to chapter 12, the very first sentence is God speaking: "Now the Lord had said to Abram: 'Get out of your country, from your family and from your father’s house, to a land that I will show you'" (Gen. 12:1).

Where the father stopped, God stepped in to take up the baton Himself. The calling was not nullified by the pause of one generation; He brought it upon the son in a more direct way. Brothers and sisters, this is an immense comfort both to parents and to children: the road the parents' generation left unfinished, God can reopen in the children; the stretch where you yourself have paused for years, God has not crossed out either. He can still speak the original word to you once again today.

When Hebrews 11:8 looks back to summarize Abraham, its very first sentence says: "By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called to go out to the place which he would receive as an inheritance. And he went out, not knowing where he was going." Please notice: what Hebrews commends is not Terah's half-journey, but Abram's setting out anew. The relay of faith is often passed along like this: the previous runner stops in Haran; the next runner hears the voice and takes up the stride again.

Brothers and sisters, Haran is not far from us at all. Having believed in the Lord for many years, your zeal stops halfway; your marriage is maintained until it is "passable," and there you settle down; the calling was once heard, stirred your heart, made you weep, and then you bought a house and put down roots in Haran, with Canaan reduced to a mere line in the prayer meeting. On behalf of chapter 11, I ask you today: How long have you been stopped in Haran? Five years? Ten years? Do not wait until the leader is gone; do not wait until the days have worn you down. Today—this very day—hear that word anew: "Get out." God has not stopped; He is waiting for you in Haran to set out again. That step you have paused for years—take it today. Not tomorrow, not waiting until all the conditions are met, but hearing today and moving today.

To sum up the third part in one sentence: **Terah had the right direction and a good start, yet he stopped halfway in Haran until his death; the place where man stops is not the finish line—God Himself spoke again in Haran, entrusting the unfinished road to Abram to complete.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

Today's chapter speaks to six categories of people:

**To seekers:** You may feel that faith is something handed down across generations in other people's families, having nothing to do with an "outsider" like you. Abram's household came out of idol-worshiping Ur; it was the first generation, possessing no background whatsoever. When God seeks someone, He does not look at what was written earlier in your genealogy; He looks at whether you are willing to follow Him starting from this page. Your genealogy can completely begin anew from you.

**To new believers:** Believing in the Lord is a departure, but after departing there is still a long road ahead. Beware of a kind of complacency: feeling that "I have already left my old life" is enough. The lesson of Haran is that leaving is merely getting on the road, not arriving. Write down that clearest stirring of your heart from when you first believed, and bring it out after some time to check: Am I still moving forward, or have I already settled down in Haran?

**To brothers and sisters who have been in the Lord for many years:** The place long-time believers are most familiar with is Haran: meetings as usual, serving in turn as usual, but that inner drive of "going to Canaan"—who knows in which year it vanished. Today's chapter is an annual checkup for us: find the station where you stopped—whether it is your prayer life, your heart for evangelism, or a calling half-answered—and take another step this week. It does not need to be big; just one step will do.

**To parents:** Terah gives us an example that is both warm and piercing: he led his whole family on the journey, and the direction was right; yet he stopped in Haran, and his children stopped with him. The parents' footsteps are the children's earliest map. You do not have to be the one who finishes the entire course, but by no means be the one who settles the whole family halfway. Even if you walk slowly, let your children see that you are constantly edging in the direction of Canaan.

**To those striving in the workplace:** The Haran in the workplace is called the "comfort zone": a decent income, a passable position, slowly settling down the person who once wanted to accomplish something. Today is not telling you to resign; it is telling you to be honest: Is your current pause the rest that God has granted you, or is it a Terah-like settling down? After rest, one must journey on; settling down is taking the midway station as the destination. Tell the difference, and then keep walking where you ought to walk.

**To those currently in suffering:** The fractures in your family may be just as concrete as those in Terah's household: someone passed away early, what was sought has never arrived, a road was traveled halfway and could not be continued. Hear the word of this chapter: the fractures were written into the genealogy, not deleted; those who stopped in Haran were not deleted by God either. His way of working is to continue writing across the breaks and midway along the journey. Do not rush to pronounce the final verdict on your page; yield this page back to the One who writes the genealogy.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Summary · Response Prayer

Let us review today's three statements:

First, God is pressing forward on His journey through the list of names—though lifespans shorten, His line does not shorten, and the narrowing of focus is for the purpose of zooming out again;
Second, Terah's family came out of Ur bearing two fractures: early death and barrenness—God's election is not selective about background, and where the line breaks He joins it Himself;
Third, to go as far as Haran and stop there is the most dangerous way to stop—man stopped, but God did not; Haran is a comma, not a period.

Now let us bow our heads and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. After the scattering at Babel, You pressed forward step by step through the list of names, pressing right up to the doorstep of each one of our homes. We ask You to forgive the days we have stopped in Haran: having once left, we thought we had arrived; having once been stirred, we thought we had obeyed. We ask You to speak to us anew today, as clearly as You spoke to Abram. We entrust the fractures in our families to You; we entrust the half-traveled road in our hearts to You. Lead us to set out again, to finish the road left unfinished, until we reach the land You will show us. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

## Chapter-End Notes

[1] toledot: Hebrew תּוֹלְדוֹת (H8435), descendants, generations, genealogy; see Gen. 2:4; 5:1; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10; semantic definitions according to BDB and HALOT.

[2] Eber: Hebrew עֵבֶר (H5677), Eber, a personal name; the association of his name with the root meaning "to cross over, pass through" (abar, H5674) is a common exposition, included in this manuscript for background explanation only; for the designation "Hebrew," see Gen. 14:13.

[3] Abram: Hebrew אַבְרָם (H87), Abram, personal name, meaning "exalted father" or "high father"; see Gen. 11:26, etc.; lexical meaning according to BDB and HALOT.

[4] akarah: Hebrew עֲקָרָה (H6135), barren (woman); Gen. 11:30 in the Chinese Union Version renders it "Sarai was barren; she had no child," and this note explains the relevant semantic range.

[5] Regarding Gen. 11:31 "to go to the land of Canaan; and they came to Haran and dwelt there": Chinese Union Version text; English comparison: KJV reads "they came unto Haran, and dwelt there"; NIV and NRSV are similar in sense (English citations are recorded according to publicly standard texts). Lu Zhenzhong's translation of this verse is referenced, though not directly quoted in this manuscript.

[6] Regarding Terah's age and the timing of Abram's departure from Haran (a comparative reading of Gen. 11:26, 32 and Acts 7:4), commentators have historically handled this in different ways; this manuscript presents it according to the narrative of Acts 7:4 that Abram left Haran after Terah died, without asserting this as the only possibility.

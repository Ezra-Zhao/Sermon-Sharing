## Main Text

### Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Fact] The chapter's place in the book is registered first: Deuteronomy 12–26 is the body of the law, and within it the group of office sections runs from the end of chapter 16 — 16:18–20, the judges in each town; 17:8–13, the central court; 17:14–20, the king; 18:1–8, the priestly Levites; 18:9–22, the prophet. Officers, king, priest, prophet — four offices legislated one by one, and every one of them fenced about by this same book of the law: the judge must not wrest judgment (16:19), the king must read in it all the days of his life (17:19), the priest has no inheritance (18:2), the prophet's words are not his own (18:18). This chapter stands at the close of the group: the law of the king has just been completed, and the pen returns to the altar and to the prophet. The holy name "the LORD" occurs 20 times in the chapter in the Chinese base text, distributed over 14 verses (1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 21, 22); the account of its forms (as corrected in the Chinese strong re-verification): "the LORD thy God" with the fuller possessive, 5 times (vv. 5, 12, 13, 14, 15); "the LORD thy God" in the shorter form, 2 times (vv. 9, 16); "the LORD my God," once (v. 16, in the people's words at Horeb); "the LORD his God," once (v. 7, of the Levite who comes to minister); without a possessive, 11 times. (On the KJV letter the name occurs 19 times in the same 14 verses, the one fewer standing at verse 2, where the KJV reads "as he hath said unto them" without repeating the name; the KJV does not distinguish the two Chinese possessive forms, reading "the LORD thy God" at all seven of those places — the difference is registered in Endnote 2 and not harmonized.) One last item is worth recording at the outset: the chapter's only "my God" stands not in Moses' mouth but in the people's — when the people ask for a mediator, what they say is "the voice of the LORD my God" (v. 16).

[Argument] This is a summary argument: chapter 17 asks "who rules," chapter 18 asks "who carries the word." A nation can be without a king — the Israel of this chapter stands east of the Jordan, and no king is yet set up; but a nation cannot be without the word of God coming to it. The priest guards the channel of sacrifice, the prophet guards the channel of speech; this chapter sets up the keepers of both channels together, and only then is Deuteronomy's map of offices complete. This is an inference (drawn by induction from the arrangement of the office sections of chapters 16–18; it is not a doctrine self-evident in any single verse).

[Summary] One sentence takes hold of the whole chapter: the man without an inheritance keeps the altar, the man with a word to carry keeps the mouth — the priest has God himself as his inheritance, the prophet has God's word as his; what the people are to do is not to learn the arts of the nations, but to hear the man God raises up. The four parts follow: first, the Levites' having no part and no inheritance, and the portions due to them (vv. 1–8); then, the abominable things of the nations that must not be learned (vv. 9–14); then, the raising up of a prophet like me (vv. 15–19); last, the test of truth and falsehood — presumption and fulfilment (vv. 20–22).

### Part One: No Part nor Inheritance — the LORD Is Their Inheritance (Deuteronomy 18:1–8; 05:00–20:00)

[Fact] Verse 1 sets the key for the whole part: "The priests the Levites, and all the tribe of Levi, shall have no part nor inheritance with Israel: they shall eat the offerings of the LORD made by fire, and his inheritance." Two nouns are distinguished first: ḥēleq (part) is the plot allotted when the land is divided; naḥălâ (inheritance) is the property held by descent — the Levites have neither. This verse does not say why none is given; it gives the substitute: what they eat is not the produce of the land but the produce of the altar — "the offerings of the LORD made by fire" (ʾiššê YHWH) and "his inheritance" (in the original, naḥălāṯô, "his inheritance": God turns the portion that belongs to himself over to the Levites as their inheritance, and the original here forms a verbal circle — the Levites' naḥălâ is YHWH's naḥălâ). Verse 2 makes this plain: "Therefore shall they have no inheritance among their brethren: the LORD is their inheritance, as he hath said unto them." In the original this stands in the singular — "he shall have no inheritance… the LORD is his inheritance," the subject being the tribe taken as a whole in the singular (for the singular/plural entries, see the Base texts note and Endnote 4). This sentence is read against what God said to Aaron in person at Numbers 18:20 (checked directly in this round): "And the LORD spake unto Aaron, Thou shalt have no inheritance in their land, neither shalt thou have any part among them: I am thy part and thine inheritance among the children of Israel." The two places are registered side by side: Numbers is God's declaration to the high priest alone; Deuteronomy is the law's statement to the whole tribe; from one household to one tribe, the sentence changes from "I am" to "the LORD is," and the difference of person is entered as it stands.

[Fact] Verses 3–4 are the details of the maintenance. Verse 3 opens: "And this shall be the priest's due from the people, from them that offer a sacrifice, whether it be ox or sheep; and they shall give unto the priest the shoulder, and the two cheeks, and the maw." In the original, mišpāṭ hakkōhănîm, "the judgment / the due of the priests" — the same word mišpāṭ as the "sentence of judgment" of the central court in chapter 17: the priest's income is not a gratuity; it is a right fixed by judgment. The items are three — zərôaʿ (the shoulder, literally the arm), ləḥāyîm (the two cheeks), qēḇâ (the maw, rendered in the base text "the stomach"). Verse 4 is the portion from the land: "The firstfruit also of thy corn, of thy wine, and of thine oil, and the first of the fleece of thy sheep, shalt thou give him." Four things of the "first" order: rēʾšîṯ (firstfruit, the first share) leads off the three produce of the land — corn (dəḡān), wine (tîrôš), oil (yiṣhār) — together with the first of the fleece of the flock (gēz ṣōʾn). Note that the sources of the two verses stand in exact contrast: verse 3 comes from the altar (the sacrifices the people offer), verse 4 from the land (the produce the people gather in) — those who sacrifice and those who farm alike maintain the same landless company. Verse 5 gives the reason for the maintenance, resting not on need but on election: "For the LORD thy God hath chosen him out of all thy tribes, to stand to minister in the name of the LORD, him and his sons for ever." bāḥar (to choose) is the same word as God's choosing of the king at 17:15, and of the same root as "the place which the LORD shall choose"; the posture of the service is "to stand" (ʿāmaḏ) with "to minister" (šārēṯ), and its term is kol-hayyāmîm — "all the days," rendered in the base text "for ever." The Levites' landlessness is a permanent arrangement, not a temporary relief: as long as the election, so long the maintenance.

[Fact] Verses 6–8 treat a man easily overlooked: the Levite scattered among the towns. "And if a Levite come from any of thy gates out of all Israel, where he sojourned, and come with all the desire of his mind unto the place which the LORD shall choose; Then he shall minister in the name of the LORD his God, as all his brethren the Levites do, which stand there before the LORD." In the body of the law the Levites are distributed among the towns (the "any of thy gates" of verse 6 of this chapter and the "gates" of 17:2 belong to the same picture); the Levites in permanent attendance at the central sanctuary are only a part of them. If the scattered one comes "with all the desire of his mind" (in the original, bəḵol-ʾawwaṯ nap̄šô, "with all the desire of his soul") to the place which the LORD shall choose, his qualification to minister is wholly equal to that of those in permanent attendance — the sentence is a double "as / alike": alike in ministering in the name, alike in standing. Verse 8 settles his income on the same level: "They shall have like portions to eat, beside that which cometh of the sale of his patrimony." In the original, ḥēleq kəḥēleq, "portion like portion" — his portion is equal to their portion; and one item is entered separately: he may in addition have what comes from the sale of his patrimony (a scattered Levite may hold saleable family property — this and the "no part nor inheritance" of verses 1–2, which speak of having no allotted land, are registered on two levels and do not cancel each other).

[Argument] This is an inference: the logic of maintenance in this part is exactly the mirror image of the three prohibitions of the law of the king. Chapter 17 forbids the king to multiply "to himself" horses, wives, and silver; this chapter sets up the opposite shape for the Levites: no accumulation — not even land is allotted; his security is not in the storehouse but at the altar, not in a hereditary property but in the sacrifices offered daily. Those who maintain him are thereby reminded as well: the firstfruit is given to him first, the first fleece is given to him first — in giving the "first" to the Levites, the people confess that the produce of their own land is not wholly their own either. This is an argument (the "mirror image" is a structural inference drawn from the contrast of the "to himself" usage across chapters 17 and 18; the text itself nowhere states the pairing).

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Two matters are registered. First, the differences among the books in the details of the Levites' maintenance: Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy each record the portions due to priests and Levites with differing fullness (the priest's portion in Leviticus 7, the whole portion of priests and Levites in Numbers 18, this part in Deuteronomy 18); this sermon has checked directly only Numbers 18:20, and registers the remaining places by chapter number only; as to the relation among the several lists (facets of one system, or stages in the history of a system), scholarly positions differ, and this sermon sets them out side by side without arranging lines of derivation. Second, the singular/plural variant of the sentence "the LORD is their inheritance" (see Endnote 4): the grammatical relation of the original's singular subject to the collective noun of the tribe, the pluralizing treatment in the base text and in the KJV, and the 2010 version's return to the singular — the three readings are registered as they stand, without adjudicating which is right.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report) No verbatim New Testament citation of this part can be registered. In later readings, the sentence "God himself as inheritance" is often read together with Psalm 16:5, the LORD as one's inheritance and the portion of one's cup (registered as a report, not checked word for word), and 1 Corinthians 9:13–14, where the apostle, arguing that those who preach the gospel should live of the gospel, points back to the old arrangement by which those who serve at the temple eat of the things of the temple (registered as a report, not checked word for word). One point of the reading is registered only: the New Testament's pointing back uses the maintenance principle of "eating alike of the things of the altar," and does not on that account rewrite this part's tribal setting. This sermon does not expand the point.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part One: the Levites have no land, because their land is God himself; the people offer the firstfruit and shear the first fleece to maintain a company standing before the altar — having no part and no inheritance is not ill-treatment; it is the shape of election.

### Part Two: The Abominations of Those Nations — Thou Shalt Not Learn to Do After Them (Deuteronomy 18:9–14; 20:00–33:00)

[Fact] Verse 9 — the second group of quotation marks opens, and the scene moves from the altar to the border: "When thou art come into the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee, thou shalt not learn to do after the abominations of those nations." The verb is "learn" (lāmaḏ) — the same word as the king's "learn to fear" at 17:19: the word "learn" is used in two directions, in one place learning reverence, in the other a prohibition on learning abominations; entered as it stands. The forbidden list stands in verses 10–11, set out in one breath across two verses: "There shall not be found among you any one that maketh his son or his daughter to pass through the fire, or that useth divination, or an observer of times, or an enchanter, or a witch. Or a charmer, or a consulter with familiar spirits, or a wizard, or a necromancer." The original is registered item by item: making a child pass through the fire (maʿăḇîr bənô-ûḇiṯtô bāʾēš, making his son or his daughter pass through the fire); one who uses divination (qōsēm qəsāmîm, one who divines divinations); an observer of times (məʿônēn); an enchanter (mənaḥēš); a witch (məḵaššēp̄); a charmer (ḥōḇēr ḥāḇer, one who binds spells and charms); a consulter with familiar spirits (šōʾēl ʾôḇ, one who asks of a ghost); a wizard (yiḏḏəʿōnî, a knower, a spirit-medium); a necromancer (dōrēš ʾel-hammēṯîm, one who seeks unto the dead). The base text names nine items (five in verse 10, four in verse 11), and the original's headwords are likewise nine — eight of them participles, yiḏḏəʿōnî being a noun (a knower); šōʾēl ʾôḇ and yiḏḏəʿōnî are rendered in the base text as two items, "consulter with familiar spirits" and "wizard," aligned with the original's nine. The common point of the list is stated first: all of them are techniques of "asking" — asking the fire, asking omens, asking by arts, asking the dead — and what is asked is in every case what has not yet happened and what cannot be known. Verse 12 gives the list its character: "For all that do these things are an abomination unto the LORD: and because of these abominations the LORD thy God doth drive them out from before thee." tôʿăḇâ (abomination) occurs three times in this chapter (once in verse 9, twice in verse 12); the causation is here made plain: the Canaanites are driven out (yāraš) not because Israel is strong, but because of these things; and if Israel learns to do them, this verse's causation points at once at Israel itself: the man who does these things is to be driven out of this land — the close of this passage stands on a sentence of geography, not on a sentence of penalty (this level is a registration of sentence-pattern contrast).

[Fact] Verse 13 is the close of the whole part, and the shortest command in the chapter: "Thou shalt be perfect with the LORD thy God." In the original, tāmîm tihyeh ʿim YHWH — "thou shalt be wholly, perfectly, with the LORD"; tāmîm in the sacrificial context denotes an offering without blemish (the opposite of the mûm of 17:1), and here denotes a man's being wholly without divided heart toward God. Note the syntactic position: the list set out nine kinds of "asking," and the command gives only one sentence, "be perfect" — the law supplies no substitute technique item by item; it gives one whole posture: be perfect toward God, and there is no need to ask elsewhere. Verse 14 carries the passage forward and hands it on: "For these nations, which thou shalt possess, hearkened unto observers of times, and unto diviners: but as for thee, the LORD thy God hath not suffered thee so to do." The contrast in the original is strong: on one side, "these nations hearkened"; on the other, "but as for thee" — what God gives Israel is not a better divination but something else entirely; and that something comes on stage at once in verse 15: the prophet. Part locks into part at this point: the forbidden-arts passage does not end with a prohibition; it opens with a promise (verse 15 follows without a seam; the joint is registered here, and the prophet part is unfolded in Part Three).

[Argument] This is an inference: what this list forbids is, on the surface, arts; underneath, it is "asking after what God has not said." The anxiety of divination is the wish to know the answer before God has spoken; the anxiety of consulting the dead is the wish to go round God and ask the source direct. This chapter's answer to these two anxieties is not to hide the answer deeper, but to change the channel: when God wills to speak, he himself raises up a man to speak; when God has not spoken, the perfect man waits. This is an argument (a theological inference drawn from the junction of verses 9–14 with verse 15; the text itself nowhere states the reasoning).

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Two matters are registered. First, the comparison of the list with Leviticus 19 (**set out side by side only, without adjudication**): Leviticus 19:26 (checked directly in this round): "Ye shall not eat any thing with the blood: neither shall ye use enchantment, nor observe times." Leviticus 19:31 (checked directly in this round): "Regard not them that have familiar spirits, neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them: I am the LORD your God." Three points are set out side by side: the relevant prohibitions of Leviticus 19 stand divided between two verses, four items in all (enchantment, observing times, familiar spirits, wizards), and are interleaved with everyday holiness provisions such as eating blood and defilement; the list of Deuteronomy 18 stands gathered in one place, nine items in all, and is placed within the office sections, immediately before the promise of the prophet. The differences of fullness, position, and context between the two lists are registered only; as to their derivation and their order of composition, this sermon does not adjudicate. Second, what "making children pass through the fire" refers to: scholarship holds two readings — the offering of children in sacrifice, and a rite of passage through fire (a purification or divination rite in which children are made to pass over the fire); this sermon sets the two readings out side by side and registers them, without adjudication (the relation to the child-offering records in Leviticus 18 and in Kings has not been checked place by place; this sermon registers only the books to which those chapters belong).

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report) No verbatim New Testament citation of this part can be registered. Acts 16:16–18 records the slave girl possessed with a spirit of divination, who brought her masters gain by soothsaying, and Paul's casting out the spirit in the name of Jesus (registered as a report, not checked word for word) — one point of comparison in the readings is registered only: in the New Testament narrative, the art of divination belongs to the side that is cast out, in the same direction as this part's characterization; Galatians 5:20 lists "witchcraft" among the works of the flesh (registered as a report, not checked word for word). This sermon does not on the New Testament's account rewrite this part's setting.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part Two: the first lesson on entering Canaan is not warfare but not learning — none of the nine arts of asking after the future is to be learned, because Israel's future rests not on asking but on hearing; and whom to hear, the next part says.

### Part Three: I Will Raise Them Up a Prophet, Like Unto Me (Deuteronomy 18:15–19; 33:00–47:00)

[Fact] Verse 15 — the promise of the prophet is spoken for the first time: "The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken;" Every word has been weighed: "will raise up" (the causative of qûm — God is the one who raises up; the prophet is not self-appointed); "from the midst of thee, of thy brethren" (miqqereḇ ʾaḥeḵā — in the same form as setting up the king "from among thy brethren" at 17:15: the king comes out from the brethren, and the prophet comes out from the brethren too); "like unto me" (kāmōnî, like me — Moses speaking of himself: a prophet who, like him, acts as mediator, and who, like him, receives the word face to face); and the command falls on the people: "unto him ye shall hearken" (šāmaʿ with its preposition, hearing that obeys). Verse 16 supplies the promise with its historical ground — this promise does not come out of nowhere; it is established in answer to the people's request at Horeb: "According to all that thou desiredst of the LORD thy God in Horeb in the day of the assembly, saying, Let me not hear again the voice of the LORD my God, neither let me see this great fire any more, that I die not." The people's words are read against the Exodus record (Exodus 20:19, checked directly in this round): "And they said unto Moses, Speak thou with us, and we will hear: but let not God speak with us, lest we die." — at the foot of Horeb (Sinai), what the people feared was facing God's voice and fire directly, and so they asked for a mediator; Deuteronomy 5:27–28 records that, after God heard these words, he approved them and set Moses up as mediator (checked directly in this round): "Go thou near, and hear all that the LORD our God shall say: and speak thou unto us all that the LORD our God shall speak unto thee; and we will hear it, and do it." Verse 17 of this chapter is exactly the second half of that scene — God says to Moses, "They have well spoken that which they have spoken." (Deuteronomy 5:28 records the same matter, God's words running, "I have heard the voice of the words of this people, which they have spoken unto thee: they have well said all that they have spoken" — checked directly in this round; the two places are registered side by side.) The origin of the prophetic office is, in this chapter, traced back to a request: men feared to face God directly, and God set up a man standing in between.

[Fact] Verse 18 — the promise is spoken a second time, and in God's own mouth to Moses: "I will raise them up a Prophet from among their brethren, like unto thee, and will put my words in his mouth; and he shall speak unto them all that I shall command him." Read against verse 15, the agreements and differences are entered separately: the subject changes from the third person ("The LORD thy God will raise up") to the first person ("I will raise them up"); the hearer changes from the people to Moses himself; and one further layer of mechanism is added — how God makes the prophet a prophet: "I will put my words in his mouth" (in the original, "I will put my words in his mouth"), together with the prophet's obligation: "he shall speak unto them all that I shall command him." The definition of the prophet is here written in three full strokes: the words are not his (God puts them in); the words are the whole portion (all that I shall command); the words are directed to the people (he shall speak unto them). Verse 19 — the seriousness of hearkening: "And it shall come to pass, that whosoever will not hearken unto my words which he shall speak in my name, I will require it of him." The word rendered "require" is, in the original, dāraš — the very word used at verse 11 for "seeking unto the dead," and the word of enquiry into cases in chapter 17: seeking unto the dead is a forbidden art; God's requiring is judgment; the man who does not hear the prophet's words, God himself comes to "ask" of him. This verse also raises the "unto him ye shall hearken" of verse 15 from exhortation to responsibility: not to hear is not to miss information; it is to stand under God's requiring.

[Argument] This is an inference: the most moving structure of this part is the promise's double entrance. One and the same promise is first proclaimed by Moses to the people (v. 15), and then restated by God to Moses (v. 18) — the former like a testament, the latter like a commission. Moses knows he is to die (this chapter stands precisely in the setting of his final legislation); what he leaves the people is not his own portrait but a prophet to come; and God takes up Moses' words and speaks them again in the first person — as much as setting his seal upon the testament. Consider again the weight of "like unto me": Moses is set up in Deuteronomy as the pattern of the prophet, and his prophetic office is bound to the mediator event at Horeb — the essence of the prophet here is not foretelling the future, but standing between God and men carrying the word. This is an argument ("testament / commission" is a structural inference drawn from the order of verses 15 and 18).

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Two matters are registered. First, the range of reference of "a Prophet": nāḇîʾ in verses 15 and 18 is in both places singular, and at least three scholarly readings stand side by side — the continuous institution of the prophetic office (the company of prophets raised up in each generation, the singular being used generically); one particular successor after Moses; and one ultimate, Moses-like prophet. The three readings each have their textual ground (the command of 18:15 is spoken to the people of that day and the responsibility of verse 19 takes immediate effect — this is the ground of the continuous reading; the uniqueness of the expression "like unto me / like unto thee" and the chain of singular verbs — this is the ground of the particular reading); this sermon sets the three readings out side by side, without adjudication — the direction of the two New Testament citations is registered in this part's New Testament Reading layer, belongs to the New Testament's own reading, and is not read back as this part's historical meaning. Second, the relation of this part to the Horeb narratives (Exodus 20; Deuteronomy 5): this chapter ties the origin of the prophetic office to the people's request for a mediator, and the three records (Ex 20:19; Deut 5:23–31; Deut 18:16–17) differ in fullness and wording; this sermon sets out only the directly checked texts side by side, without arranging lines of derivation.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report) The promise of this part is expressly cited at two places in the New Testament, both registered from the original text checked directly, verse page by verse page. Acts 3:22 — Peter, preaching in Solomon's porch, cites Moses' words: "For Moses truly said unto the fathers, A prophet shall the Lord your God raise up unto you of your brethren, like unto me; him shall ye hear in all things whatsoever he shall say unto you." — the citation read against Deuteronomy 18:15: the subject stands as "the Lord your God," and the command is expanded to "him shall ye hear in all things whatsoever he shall say unto you" (connected in its context with the requiring sentence of 18:19 — Peter immediately cites at 3:23 the sense of "shall be destroyed" for the one who will not hear; this sermon has checked directly only 3:22, and registers 3:23 only). Acts 7:37 — Stephen, in his defence before the council, cites the same sentence: "This is that Moses, which said unto the children of Israel, A prophet shall the Lord your God raise up unto you of your brethren, like unto me; him shall ye hear." — the citation is embedded in a narrative sentence, its subject reduced to "the Lord your God," and it points to the prophetic identity of "this Moses" himself. The common direction of the two citations (Peter and Stephen both read the promise onto Jesus) belongs to the New Testament's own reading; this sermon registers only the fact of citation and the differences in the citations' forms (the form of the subject, the expansion of the command sentence, the narrative embedding), and does not on the New Testament's account rewrite this part's historical meaning within Deuteronomy; the three readings registered in the scholarly layer still stand side by side.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part Three: to a people afraid to hear his voice, God raises up a prophet like Moses — the words are God's, put into his mouth; the portion is the whole portion; the man comes from among the brethren; the people's one business is to hearken.

### Part Four: The Presumptuous Shall Die; Only What Comes to Pass Counts (Deuteronomy 18:20–22; 47:00–57:00)

[Fact] Verse 20 — the close of God's words in single quotation marks, and the false prophet's two ways of death: "But the prophet, which shall presume to speak a word in my name, which I have not commanded him to speak, or that shall speak in the name of other gods, even that prophet shall die." Two forms are registered side by side: first, falseness of content — speaking in the LORD's name a word the LORD has not commanded him to speak (the question is the word's source); second, falseness of name — speaking in the name of other gods (the question is in whose name). The verb is "presume" (yāzîḏ, the causative of zûḏ: deliberate transgression) — of the same word-family as the "presumptuously" (zādôn) and "do presumptuously" (zûḏ) of the central-court passage at 17:12–13; both places in the book where "presumption" occurs fall on offences against the holy channel: in one place, refusing to hear the sentence of the priest and the judge; in the other, forging the prophet's words; the correspondence of the word-family is registered only. The penalty is death, of the same grade as the sin of idolatry in chapter 13 and refusal of the final court in chapter 17 — forging God's words is, in the law's eyes, the same sin as serving other gods, because both tamper with one and the same channel. Verse 21 — the law hears the difficulty in the people's heart and asks it for them: "And if thou say in thine heart, How shall we know the word which the LORD hath not spoken?" This is a rare considerateness in Deuteronomy: if the death sin of verse 20 had no means of discernment, the people could only suffer at both ends, between "fearing to hear wrongly" and "fearing not to hear" — verse 19 says the one who does not hear will be required of, verse 20 says the one who speaks falsely will be put to death; then by what are those who hear to discern? Verse 22 gives the rule: "When a prophet speaketh in the name of the LORD, if the thing follow not, nor come to pass, that is the thing which the LORD hath not spoken, but the prophet hath spoken it presumptuously: thou shalt not be afraid of him." The test is of the after-the-fact kind: the word must wait to be tested by whether it comes to pass (bôʾ, "to come, to be fulfilled"); the verdict is double — "the thing which the LORD hath not spoken" (a judgment of source) together with "the prophet hath spoken it presumptuously" (a judgment of motive; yāzîḏ, the same word as in verse 20); and the close addressed to the people is not a penalty sentence but a comfort and a release: "thou shalt not be afraid of him" (lōʾ ṯāḡûr, gûr) — the false prophet's commonest weapon is intimidation, and the law first takes this weapon out of his hand.

[Fact] This test and the test of chapter 13 are here set out side by side (**side by side only, without adjudication**). Deuteronomy 13:1–3 (checked directly in this round): "If there arise among you a prophet, or a dreamer of dreams, and giveth thee a sign or a wonder, And the sign or the wonder come to pass, whereof he spake unto thee, saying, Let us go after other gods, which thou hast not known, and let us serve them; Thou shalt not hearken unto the words of that prophet, or that dreamer of dreams: for the LORD your God proveth you, to know whether ye love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul." The two tests stand side by side: chapter 13's case is "the sign has come to pass, and still he must not be heard," because the content he carries has broken the covenant — the test is of loyalty; chapter 18's case is "if the thing spoken does not come to pass, it is not God's word" — the test is of fulfilment. One test governs content (though it come to pass, covenant-breaking is false); one test governs source (if it does not come to pass, sourceless is false); the two tests coexist in Deuteronomy, and this sermon registers only the form of their division of labour, without adjudicating which takes precedence and which is fundamental. One further structural fact is registered: this chapter's verse 20, "shall speak in the name of other gods," joins in its charge to chapter 13's "let us go after other gods" — of the false prophet's two ways of death, the second leads directly to chapter 13's sin; and the death-penalty passages of the three chapters (13, 17, 18) all close on the question of the "channel" — registered only, and not argued as an editor's intent.

[Argument] This is an inference: the protection this chapter gives the people is two-layered. One layer is vertical: verse 19's requiring warns the people not to make light of the true prophet; one layer is horizontal: verse 22's "thou shalt not be afraid of him" releases the people from any need to fear the false prophet. The two layers together complete the people's posture before the prophet: true words are to be heard to the end; false words need not be feared one whit. Note too the patience of the test: the test of fulfilment is waited out — the law gives the people no technique of instant identification; it gives them time and a record: write the word down, and see whether it comes to pass. This stands in exact contrast to the forbidden list of Part Two: divination wants an instant answer; discernment wants waiting and verification. This is an argument (an inference drawn from the contrast of verses 19 and 22 with the list of Part Two).

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Two matters are registered. First, the range of application of the test of fulfilment: verse 22's test is directly effective for words of the "prophecy" kind; how it applies to words of exhortation and warning (whose fulfilment commonly carries conditions) is much discussed in scholarship and in the interpretive tradition (Jonah's prophecy against Nineveh and the variable of repentance are often cited as the case in discussion; this sermon has not checked Jonah directly, and registers the book's name only); this sermon registers the question only, and lays down no general rule. Second, the relation between the promise of "a prophet like me" and this part's means of discernment: if the promise refers to the continuous prophetic office, then verses 20–22 are the office's standing rules of discernment; if it refers to one particular prophet, then this part is the guardrail for the people as they await that one — the part's function differs under the two readings, and the matter is registered in connection with the three readings of Part Three's scholarly layer; this sermon still does not adjudicate.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report) John 1:21 records that the Jews asked John the Baptist whether he was "that prophet," from which it can be seen that the expectation of "that prophet" as a title had already taken form in the Second Temple period (registered as a report, not checked word for word); Matthew 7:15–20, on beware of false prophets — "by their fruits ye shall know them" — is the New Testament's reading of discernment, close in direction to this part's test of fulfilment but different in standard (registered as a report, not checked word for word). Both places register only that the reading exists; neither is used to rewrite this part.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part Four: the false prophet's two roads are both roads of death — forging in the Lord's name, and speaking in the name of other gods; and the people need fear neither: write the word down, and see whether it comes to pass; what does not come to pass is not God's speaking — thou shalt not be afraid of him.

### Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Fact] The chain of the chapter's closing sentences is looked back over once: verse 2 closes on "the LORD is their inheritance"; verse 5 closes on election and ministering for ever; verse 13 closes on "Thou shalt be perfect"; verse 19 closes on "I will require it of him"; verse 22 closes on "thou shalt not be afraid of him." The chapter's first sentence is having no part and no inheritance; its last sentence is not being afraid — from before the altar to the prophet, the law first settles those who keep the channel (they have an inheritance: God himself), and then settles those who hear by the channel (you shall not be afraid).

[Argument] This is an inference: Deuteronomy 18 draws a complete picture of "how God speaks to men": there is an altar, and the priest stands before it, receiving the portion God turns over to him; there is a mouth, and the prophet is raised up among the people, carrying the words God puts into his mouth; there is a prohibition, and the nine arts of private asking are sealed off without exception; there is a test, and every self-styled word must pass the gate of fulfilment. The centre of the picture is neither the art nor the test, but the God who raises up the prophet — he is willing to speak, and willing to be heard. This is an argument.

[Summary] The final governing sentence: A prophet like unto me — the last gift Moses leaves Israel is a promise and a measure: the promise, that God will surely raise up a man to speak; the measure, that the word must come to pass, the heart must be perfect, and the ear must hearken.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotional use)

**Scripture (Deuteronomy 18:2, KJV)**: "Therefore shall they have no inheritance among their brethren: the LORD is their inheritance, as he hath said unto them."

**Reflection**: Of the twelve tribes, Levi alone received no land. On the day the boundaries were measured and the markers set for the other tribes, the Levites stood by and watched — and the law gave them only one sentence: "the LORD is their inheritance." At first hearing it sounds like a word of comfort; read on, and it proves to be an arrangement: what they ate was the portion offered to God at the altar; what they wore and where they lived all came from their service. They did not live on the harvest of a plot of land; they lived on the firstfruits of grain and the first fleece of wool that men daily brought to the altar. The people with no inheritance were precisely the people who could least afford to leave God's presence — leave it, and they had nothing. We too are always counting our "inheritance": savings, houses, positions, connections — and as we count, the heart settles, and quietly drifts away. Levi's lesson runs in the opposite direction: hands empty, yet the heart knowing that he himself is the inheritance itself. And there is a more moving scene still: the Levite scattered in some far town, if he comes "with all the desire of his mind," may pack up and come to the place God has chosen, and eat one equal portion and minister in one equal service with the brethren who live there always — God has not forgotten the one who is far away. When we are far from the altar, the road is open: come willingly, and there is a portion. Pray today like this: Lord, the things I have been counting are too many, and they are not you; teach me, like the Levites, first to confess that you are my portion, and only then to live the days of empty hands in contentment.

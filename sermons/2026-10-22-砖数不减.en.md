## Summary (about 100 words)

Moses and Aaron came before Pharaoh for the first time, asking that the people be let go to hold a feast in the wilderness. Pharaoh answered: Who is the LORD? He then stopped the supply of straw while the tale of bricks was not reduced, and the oppression escalated in the name of efficiency. The officers were beaten, pleaded in vain, and turned to blame Moses; Moses returned before God and questioned Him: Why have You mistreated this people? You have not delivered them at all. The first step of obedience brought a heavier load, and God gave no explanation on the spot.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The closing picture of the previous chapter still remains in our hearts: the elders heard that the LORD had visited the Israelites and had seen their affliction, and they believed the word and bowed their heads in worship. After several hundred years of slavery, they heard for the first time that God Himself had come by name to see them; that morning of prostrate worship must have been full of tears and hope. By our usual way of thinking, the story should now move upward: since God has already acted, Pharaoh should surely relent, the people should surely breathe a little easier, or at the very least there should be some sign of improvement.

But chapter 5 opens by giving us not relenting, but escalation. Moses and Aaron went to see Pharaoh and finished speaking, and the number of bricks was not reduced in the least, while the load on their backs immediately doubled. Straw — no longer given; bricks — handed over as before; and the people — beaten besides. They believed, they worshiped, they obeyed, they went and spoke, and the result was greater suffering. Brothers and sisters, this is exactly the chapter we must face together today. It does not flatter us, it speaks no empty comforting words; it lays bare before us the hardest stretch of the road of faith: why is the first step of obeying God so often not relief, but added weight?

Let us read together several key passages from today:

"And afterward Moses and Aaron went in, and told Pharaoh, Thus saith the LORD God of Israel, Let my people go, that they may hold a feast unto me in the wilderness." (Exodus 5:1, excerpt, KJV)

"And Pharaoh said, Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice to let Israel go? I know not the LORD, neither will I let Israel go." (Exodus 5:2, excerpt, KJV)

"And Pharaoh commanded the same day the taskmasters of the people, and their officers, saying, Ye shall no more give the people straw to make brick, as heretofore: let them go and gather straw for themselves. ... And the tale of the bricks, which they did make heretofore, ye shall lay upon them; ye shall not diminish ought thereof." (Exodus 5:6-8, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses returned unto the LORD, and said, Lord, wherefore hast thou so evil entreated this people? ... neither hast thou delivered thy people at all." (Exodus 5:22-23, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today we will not go around the difficulty, nor invent explanations for You. Teach us through this chapter still to come before You when the load grows heavier, and still to speak the truth to You when there is no answer. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Let My People Go — Pharaoh's Question Is the Question of the Whole Book (5:1-5)

First look at the opening words of this audience. Moses and Aaron stood before Pharaoh and spoke, not in the tone of pleading, but in the tone of delivering a message: "Thus saith the LORD God of Israel, Let my people go, that they may hold a feast unto me in the wilderness." In this short sentence there are three weights. First, the speaker behind it is God; this is not a slogan Moses invented. Moses was only the voice carrying it; he brought into the palace, just as it was, the word he had heard at the burning bush. Second, the address is "my people," not "your slaves." In Pharaoh's eyes these people were labor in the brickfields, numbers in a ledger; in God's word they are His people, people He claims in person. The same crowd, two ways of seeing them, and the whole conflict begins right here. Third, the demand is "go and hold a feast." On the surface the request is not large: let the people go into the wilderness, a journey of three days, to sacrifice and hold a feast to their God. This is what God had instructed Moses at the bush; see Exodus 3:18. Not a departure of everyone at once, not a rebellion to seize power, only worship.

But Pharaoh's answer, in one sentence, states the title of the whole book: "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice to let Israel go? I know not the LORD, neither will I let Israel go." Please read this sentence twice more. Pharaoh was not asking a question of knowledge; he was refusing a question of sovereignty. To his ears, a name he had never heard actually dared to come into his palace and issue commands, actually dared to speak about his population and his labor force — this in itself was an offense. So his answer is double: I do not know Him, therefore I will not let them go. On one side he denied God's identity; on the other side he denied the people's way out.

Here there is a word at which we must stop and look closely. The first word of Pharaoh's question in the original is cut-and-dried: "Who is the LORD?" In the original it is mi YHWH, meaning precisely: Who is the LORD [1]. This sentence stands in sharp contrast with chapter 3, where God named Himself. In chapter 3 Moses had also asked a question about a name: when I come to the Israelites and they ask me what His name is, what shall I answer? God answered him: I AM that I AM, and gave him that covenant name. This time, the one asking about the name was Pharaoh. Moses asked about the name in order to know it and to carry it; Pharaoh asked about the name in order to refuse it and not obey. The same name — received on one side, resisted on the other. Brothers and sisters, this is not a coincidence of rhetoric; this is the outline of the whole Book of Exodus. Hereafter the ten plagues, the crossing of the sea, the giving of the law, the building of the tabernacle — piece by piece, all of them answer Pharaoh's question: Who is the LORD? Pharaoh said, I do not know Him, and God let him come to know Him step by step. Before each plague came down, there was a sentence like this: that you may know that I am the LORD. Pharaoh's question was arrogant, yet he asked in the right place. He asked in advance, in chapter 5, the question the whole book must answer.

We must also honestly hear another layer in Pharaoh's words. He said, "that I should obey his voice." In the Old Testament, to hear is not only to perceive with the ears; it is also to obey and to act accordingly. What Pharaoh refused was not information, but sovereignty. He did not need to investigate this God's background first and then decide; his heart was already settled: Egypt has only one king, and in the palace only one voice is heard. Any command from outside, even if it comes in God's name, is to be treated first as a threat. This is the instinct of power: what it fears most is not hardship, but loss of control. For the people to go into the wilderness for three days was, in Pharaoh's reckoning, not a religious activity but labor leaving its post — slaves acting for the first time in the form of an independent community. Today they ask leave for worship; tomorrow they might walk out for freedom. So he would not grant even three days. In his eyes, to let them go for three days was to admit that this crowd had another master, and this was something he could on no account admit.

Moses and Aaron added one more layer of explanation: the God of the Hebrews has met with us. If we do not go, He may fall upon us with pestilence or with the sword. This was spoken very restrainedly, even with a tone of consideration for Pharaoh: letting them go would be good for you too. But when Pharaoh heard it, he changed the subject — not theology, but management. He looked at Moses and Aaron and said something very practical: "Wherefore do ye, Moses and Aaron, let the people from their works? get you unto your burdens." And he said: the people of the land are now many, and you make them rest from their burdens. In these few sentences Pharaoh translated worship into absenteeism, holding a feast into resting from work, and a spiritual request into a problem of production. Once the translation was changed, refusal appeared justified: it is not that I do not revere God; it is that they cannot stop work. Brothers and sisters, this rhetoric is not unfamiliar to us. In every age, oppression seldom says nakedly, I simply want to oppress you; it always changes into another set of clothes first: efficiency, order, schedule, bad influence. Once a request is redefined, refusal puts on the outer garment of reasonableness.

At the end of this passage, the camera does not stay in the palace; it is about to go to the brickfields. Pharaoh's "I know not the LORD" would not remain only on his lips. Arrogance with only words cannot yet wound anyone; arrogance with power in its hand falls upon the people at the very bottom. Moses had delivered the message and thought his duty done; he did not yet know that the bill for those words would first be paid by the people who gathered straw, the people who made bricks, and the people who were beaten. This is precisely the heaviest lesson in acting by faith: those who stand in front and speak — the price is often paid by those standing behind. Before God's servants speak, they should think of this first; after speaking, even more, they should stay and stand together with those who pay the price, and not speak and walk away. Moses indeed stayed after this; only, the first thing he faced in staying was the angry faces of his own people.

There is one more thing in these opening verses that is easily read past, yet deserves our slowing down. When Moses stood before Pharaoh, he actually held no bargaining chips at all. He had no army, no embassy, no conditions to trade; behind him stood a crowd of slaves whose names on Egypt's ledgers were only numbers. His only credential was God's word: God said, let My people go. Obedience is very often like this: God's word goes first, the conditions are not all in place, and the person steps out first and speaks afterward. Before stepping out, Moses had already made excuses several times at the bush; it was not that he did not know Pharaoh was hard to deal with, still less that he did not know this trip might win him no good. He went anyway. Brothers and sisters, do not underestimate this step. Many people's faith sticks right here: the teaching is all understood, the conditions are always lacking, the feet never move, and the days pass year after year in the same place. Moses' feet moved, even though the price of moving came at once. The deliverance of the whole book afterward began from this step not being taken back. Pharaoh could refuse God's word, but he could not make the spoken word be taken back. The word had entered the palace, like seed fallen into the ground; whether it sprouts first or is first pressed down by the soil is no longer Pharaoh's alone to decide. God often works this way: He lets the word go out first, and lets the events follow; those on the scene often hear only refusal first and see only added pressure first, but if the word is God's word, it will not return empty. This layer of hope is not stated plainly in chapter 5, and we must not state it too fully either; we can only stand at the doorway of the narrative and look in this way: refusal is not the ending, and added pressure is not the ending, because the One who spoke has not finished speaking.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: No More Straw, No Fewer Bricks — Oppression Escalates in the Name of Efficiency (5:6-19)

On the same day, Pharaoh issued a new command. Note this "same day": he did not wait, did not consult, allowed no buffer. Once the command came down, the sky over the whole brickfield changed. Let us hear the command clearly. First: you shall no longer give the people straw to make brick as before; let them go and gather straw for themselves. Second: the tale of bricks they made before, you shall still require of them; not one shall be reduced. Third: if it falls short at all, it still counts against them, and the reason is ready-made: because they are idle, therefore they cry, saying, let us go and sacrifice to our God.

First, let us explain the craft of brickmaking. Egyptian bricks were made of mud mixed with chopped straw, dried in the sun or fired. In a brick, straw is not mere filling; it acts as the sinew, making the mud brick firm and less likely to crack. The word for this straw in the original is teven, meaning straw, stubble [2]. Formerly, the straw was supplied by the authorities, and the brickmakers only mixed the mud, set it in molds, and dried it; the processes were divided. Now the straw had to be gleaned in the fields by themselves, gathered by themselves — which is to say, on top of the original work, an entire preceding process was added: walking, searching, cutting, binding, carrying it back, and only then could the mixing of mud begin. Where did the time go? On the road, in searching everywhere. And the number of bricks to be delivered? Not one character changed; the word used is matkonet, meaning the prescribed number, the quota [3]. On one side, withdraw the supply of raw material; on the other side, nail down the output quota; the stretch of time squeezed out in between can only be squeezed from human beings, from their sleep and their breathing. This is not a lapse of management; this is calculated escalation: with the same quota, consume all the people's remaining strength, so that they have no time left even to lift their heads and cry out.

Those who carried out the command were two layers of people: taskmasters and officers. The taskmasters were Egyptians; in the original they are called noges, whose basic sense is to drive, to oppress, to press [4]. Holding the command, they went everywhere pressing the people, scattering them into the fields to gather straw, repeating the same sentence: fulfill your works, deliver the full amount daily. The officers were Israelites chosen to manage affairs, caught in the middle: above them the taskmasters pressed with the number, below them the people looked at them with tears. When the bricks fell short, they were the first to be beaten. The Bible records: Pharaoh's taskmasters beat them, demanding, why have you not fulfilled your task in making brick, both yesterday and today, as before? The beaten officers went to Pharaoh and cried: Why do you deal this way with your servants? There is no straw given to your servants, yet they say to us, make brick; we cannot make them, and we are beaten, but the fault is in your own people. In this cry, every word is blood: the straw was stopped by you, the number was set by you, we are squeezed from both ends, and in the end the fault falls on our heads. But Pharaoh would not listen even to this. He returned only one sentence, repeated twice in the text itself, nailed down over and over: "Ye are idle, ye are idle" [5]. The original word is nirpim, meaning slack, idle, feeble [5]. The people were plainly exhausted and drained, yet in Pharaoh's mouth they became idle; plainly the raw material had been taken away, yet in Pharaoh's mouth it became a problem of attitude. To call a structural impossibility a defect of character, and to call pressure motivation — this is the laziest speech an oppressor can make: as long as the problem is fixed on you, he never has to change his command.

Brothers and sisters, as we read this passage we must read out its edge for today. Pharaoh's method has not gone out of date in several thousand years. The work is increased, the staff is not added, the deadline is unchanged — and it is called training; resources are withdrawn, targets are not lowered — and it is called optimization; a person collapses from exhaustion, and it is not the system that must be examined, but the individual who was not positive enough. When oppression escalates, it often does not wear armor; it wears a suit, holds a spreadsheet, and speaks fluently of efficiency. It does not need to shout about killing; it only needs to take the straw away and then nail the number down, and people will run themselves breathless between field and brickfield on their own. More ruthless still, it also wants to seize the legitimacy of people's crying out: you want to go and worship? That is your idleness. You want to catch your breath? That is your slackness. Even why you suffer, it explains for you, and the right of explanation is forever in its hand. The text records Pharaoh's word "idle" twice in repetition, not out of verbosity, but so that we may see clearly: when a system repeatedly uses one word to define a group of suffering people, we should be alert — that word is mostly not a diagnosis, but a whip.

There is one more detail we must not let go. When the officers cried out, they called Pharaoh king and called themselves servants; their tone had already sunk into the dust. But a low, submissive voice bought no mercy, because what Pharaoh wanted was never an explanation; it was the number. After the plea failed, the officers came out of the palace and met, face to face, Moses and Aaron. They were standing there, waiting to hear the news. The news was only bad to the very bottom. When people are at their most desperate and see the ones they had pinned their hopes on, the fire in their hearts cannot be hidden. The conflict of the next passage begins right at the palace gate. And what Moses was about to face was no longer Pharaoh's cold face, but the burning, angry eyes of his own people. This is much harder than seeing Pharaoh.

Let us pause here and say a fair word for the officers and the people. Their suffering was real suffering, not suffering caused by insufficient faith. The straw really was gone, the road really was far, the bricks really could not be made up in number, and the rods really fell on their backs. To speak about faith in such a situation, if it is spoken as "you must work harder, be more positive, be more upbeat," that becomes Pharaoh's word, not God's word. The Bible itself never speaks this way. The Bible records their cry exactly as it was and records Pharaoh's injustice exactly as it was, without making a single excuse for Pharaoh. God's word first stands on the side of the suffering, acknowledges suffering as suffering and injustice as injustice, and only then can there be talk of hope. The honesty of this chapter is what we most need to learn today.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: You Have Made Us Abhorred — Moses Brings the Question Back Before God (5:20-23)

As soon as the officers came out of the palace, they lashed out at Moses and Aaron. They said: The LORD look upon you and judge, because you have made our savor to be abhorred in the eyes of Pharaoh and in the eyes of his servants, to put a sword in their hand to slay us. These words are heavy, and every sentence has its history. "Made abhorred" means like a foul odor that people loathe. Originally, though the people were slaves in Egypt, their hard days still had the old practice to follow: straw was supplied by someone, bricks had a fixed number, and by enduring they could still live. Now the old practice was broken, the way of living thrown into disorder, and the officers had also lost face before Pharaoh, becoming offenders who could not meet the number. In their view, the turning point of all this was that audience of Moses and Aaron. The words were spoken by you; the disaster is suffered by us; the sword was handed over by you. In their blame there is grievance, and there is also fact: after the audience, the situation really did become worse — a fact no one can deny.

For a leader, the hardest moment is often not being refused by the enemy, but being misunderstood by his own people. Pharaoh's refusal Moses was long prepared for; God had already foretold at the bush that Pharaoh's heart would be hardened. But for the people to turn back and blame him was a lesson Moses had never rehearsed. He had gone to deliver the word in order to save; now, in the people's mouth, he had become the one who handed over the sword. If he defended himself on the spot, saying, this is all God's will, you must endure it, he would probably pour oil on the fire; if he gave up on himself and turned back to Midian, the people would truly have no one to lead them. The Bible gives us Moses' third choice, and the most important scene of the whole chapter: Moses returned unto the LORD.

Returning before God, he spoke no fine words; he spoke a question. He said: Lord, why have You so mistreated this people? Why is it that You have sent me? For since I came to Pharaoh to speak in Your name, he has mistreated this people, and You have not delivered Your people at all. This prayer is only two verses long, yet its weight is very great; let us listen to it sentence by sentence. "Why have You mistreated this people" — the original wording is very direct; in plain speech it is: why have You brought harm and calamity on this people [6]. Moses did not stop at laying the responsibility on Pharaoh; he asked all the way to God: at the scene of the people's suffering, where are You, and why do You permit it? "You have not delivered them at all" — the original uses an emphatic construction that repeats and intensifies the word for deliver, meaning: You really have not delivered, not even the slightest movement of deliverance [6]. This is not a polite prayer; this is a prayer carrying blood and breath, a prayer that moves disappointment before God exactly as it is.

Brothers and sisters, we must vindicate prayer like this. In many people's imagination, the prayer of a person of faith should not carry questions; it should carry only thanks, praise, and submission. But the Bible does not teach this. Job questioned from the ashes, the psalmist in Psalm 73 nearly slipped, and Moses questioned here too. What their questioning has in common is that the address of the questioning is right: not spreading bitterness among people, not turning away and leaving, but returning before God and finishing the words to God. Complaint and questioning prayer may look alike on the surface, but their bones are different: complaint shuts God outside the door and ferments among people; questioning prayer brings the grievance inside the door and spreads it before God — even if the words are heavy, the footsteps still face toward God. This one turn of Moses set the base color of his whole life of service: the people's anger he did not vent back on the people, nor bottle up in his own heart; he took it to God. A leader stands sandwiched between God and people, and the most important skill is to learn to translate pressure from the ground into prayer toward heaven.

At the same time, we must honestly guard the boundary of this chapter: in chapter 5, God gave no explanation. Moses asked why, and God did not answer why in this chapter. There was no explanation of why Pharaoh was permitted to escalate, no explanation of why the people had to suffer this extra stretch, and not one comforting sentence to close. The ending of chapter 5 is Moses' question hanging in the air, and the people's bricks still piled in the field. The Bible leaves a blank here not because God has nothing to say, but because the answer is in the next chapter: only in chapter 6 does God name Himself again and restate the words of the covenant, saying, now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh. Preachers must be especially careful here: explanations the narrative does not give, we must not invent on God's behalf. We cannot say that God deliberately added suffering in order to train the people — that would make God Pharaoh's accomplice; nor can we say that the suffering is all the fruit of the people's insufficient faith — that would be speaking in Pharaoh's tone. We can only honestly point out the direction of the narrative: God heard the question, God did not rebuke Moses for his directness, and in the next chapter God answered with His name and His promise — and the way He answered was not first to lecture them on reasons, but first to act to deliver. Before the answer arrives, what this chapter teaches us is how to hold fast in days without answers: the load may be called heavy, the words may be spoken straight, and the footsteps must not leave.

Now let us bring this chapter into our lives; I will speak of only three kinds of people. The first kind: people whose situation grew heavier after obedience. Following God's word you took the first step — to reconcile, to confess, to leave a wrong road, to begin a right thing — and what came was not relief but doubled difficulty: the family grew more tense, the work less smooth, the body more tired. You begin to doubt whether you took the wrong step. Today's Scripture tells you: a first step that brings added weight has precedent in the Bible, and it need not immediately be taken as evidence of failure. Pharaoh's escalation precisely shows that the word struck the vital point. The moment darkness is least willing to let go is often the very beginning of its being forced to let go — only those inside it could not see it at the time. The second kind: people caught in the middle taking pressure from both sides, like the officers, like every supervisor sandwiched between above and below, every parent, every church co-worker. You convey for those above and plead for those below; neither side is pleased, and the first rod to fall lands on you. Learn Moses' destination: do not forward human anger just as it is; before God is the place to unload. The third kind: people who want to pray but are left with only questions. Your prayer has no praise in it, only one sentence — why — and therefore you feel you have no faith and dare not open your mouth. Moses' prayer stands here: bringing questions before God is far more fitting than complaining before people, and far healthier than letting bitterness rot in the heart. God does not fear your heavy words; what He fears is that you no longer come. The words may be heavy; the footsteps must come back. Returning before God is itself evidence that faith has not gone out.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 5. Moses delivered the word; Pharaoh asked, "Who is the LORD?", and then stopped the straw and nailed down the tale of bricks; the officers were beaten and turned to blame Moses; Moses did not break up the company and did not merely grit his teeth and endure — he returned before God and spoke the heaviest words: Why have You mistreated this people? You have not delivered them at all. This chapter has no good news on the spot; what it leaves us is three things that still stand in the midst of suffering: God's name has already been declared, and Pharaoh's question God will answer with the whole book; the heavier load is not the ending of the story, only the hardest stretch at the opening of deliverance; and the prayer that brings questions back before God — God not only permits it, He will take it up in person with the promise of the next chapter.

Let us pray together: Lord, we confess that many times we obeyed, yet the load grew heavier; we spoke, yet the situation grew harder. Keep us from falling apart on this stretch of the road, and keep us from speaking in Pharaoh's tone to define those who suffer. Teach us, like Moses, to bring the questions, the tears, and the number we cannot meet back before You. You gave us no explanation in this chapter, so we will quietly wait for Your answer in the next; and since You have already declared Your name, we believe You will carry the answer through to the end. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Pharaoh said, Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice to let Israel go? I know not the LORD, neither will I let Israel go." (Exodus 5:2, KJV) "And Moses returned unto the LORD, and said, Lord, wherefore hast thou so evil entreated this people? ... neither hast thou delivered thy people at all." (Exodus 5:22-23, excerpt, KJV)

The first step of obedience sometimes brings not relief but a heavier load. The straw was stopped, the tale of bricks was not reduced, and the officers were beaten besides. If you are walking through days like these, remember two things first. Do not hurry to invent a reason for suffering: in this chapter God gave no explanation, and Moses did not force one; he simply returned to God. Much suffering has no answer for the moment, and forced answers only wound more deeply. And questions may be spoken, but they must be spoken to God. Moses' words were heavy, almost to the edge of irreverence, yet he brought them to the right place. Complaint left among people turns only into blame and scattering; questions brought before God become prayer. Tonight, place before God, just as they are, the quota you cannot meet and the why you have held back so long. He did not rebuke Moses, and in the next chapter He answered in person. Come back first. Coming back is the first step of faith.

## Summary (about 100 words)

At Rephidim the Israelites had no water to drink and strove with Moses, asking the deepest question of the whole chapter: Is the LORD among us, or not? Moses struck the rock at Horeb, and water came out. Then Amalek came to fight; Joshua led the people out to battle, Moses held up his hands on the hilltop, and Aaron and Hur held up his hands until sunset. Moses built an altar and called it Jehovah Nissi: victory does not rest in human hands, but under God's banner.

---

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter, God gave manna in the wilderness of Sin. Every morning the people gathered that day's portion, and in the most ordinary way God taught them one thing: the God who provides for you is faithful. But as soon as the lesson of food was finished, the lesson of water arrived. At the very beginning of Exodus chapter 17, the company has moved on at the LORD's command and camped at Rephidim — and there was no water there. That is how the wilderness journey goes: one station has one lack, one lesson has one difficulty. A person may have just given thanks for one thing, and turn around and panic over the next.

This chapter has two crises, one inside the camp and one outside it. The crisis inside the camp was no water: the people thirsted, grew desperate, and strove with their leader. The crisis outside the camp was an enemy: the Amalekites came while the company was weary and attacked from behind. Lack of water tested the people's faith in God's presence; battle tested their faith in God's victory. The two crises are joined together, each deeper than the other: first doubting whether God still cared for them, then doubting whether God could still save them. And between these two crises stands one man, Moses. Inside the camp he had to face a striving people; on the hill he had to hold up the hands of prayer. His own hands would also grow tired, would also droop, would also need someone to hold them up. What this chapter tells us is exactly this: how a man whose hands droop can, by God and by his fellow workers, stand until sunset.

Let us read several key passages of today's text together:

"And all the congregation of the children of Israel journeyed ... according to the commandment of the LORD, and pitched in Rephidim: and there was no water for the people to drink. Wherefore the people did chide with Moses, and said, Give us water that we may drink. And Moses said unto them, Why chide ye with me? wherefore do ye tempt the LORD?" (Exodus 17:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And the people thirsted there for water; and the people murmured against Moses, and said, Wherefore is this that thou hast brought us up out of Egypt, to kill us and our children and our cattle with thirst? ... And he called the name of the place Massah, and Meribah, because of the chiding of the children of Israel, and because they tempted the LORD, saying, Is the LORD among us, or not?" (Exodus 17:3, 7, excerpt, KJV)

"Then came Amalek, and fought with Israel in Rephidim. And Moses said unto Joshua, Choose us out men, and go out, fight with Amalek: to morrow I will stand on the top of the hill with the rod of God in mine hand." (Exodus 17:8-9, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses built an altar, and called the name of it Jehovahnissi." (Exodus 17:15, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, among us today there are also thirsty people, people whose hands are drooping, and people who doubt whether You are still here. Through the words of this chapter, let us see again: Your presence is not withdrawn because of our weakness, and Your victory does not depend on the strength of our arms. Hold up every pair of aching hands among us, until sunset, until victory. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: No Water at Rephidim — Beneath the Striving Was Doubt of His Presence (17:1-7)

First look at the journey. The text deliberately says that the whole congregation moved station by station according to the commandment of the LORD and camped at Rephidim. In other words, they did not lack water because they had taken the wrong road; they were walking on the road God was leading, and still there was no water. This point matters greatly. Many people assume that if you obey God, the road should be smooth; if you follow God, the supplies should be complete. The moment water is lacking, they conclude that God is not there. But the Bible writes the commandment and the lack of water in the same verse precisely to correct this idea: the road God leads also passes through thirsty ground. Presence does not mean lacking nothing along the way; presence means that in the lack, God is still there, still listening, and still has a way.

The people thirsted — that was real. No water in the wilderness is not affectation; it is a matter of life and death. People thirsted, children thirsted, livestock thirsted. The Bible never belittles people's real needs, and Moses later pleaded before God for this very need. So the problem was not that the people thirsted, nor that they opened their mouths to ask for water; the problem was the way they asked: they strove with Moses. This word "strove / chided" appears more than once in this chapter and later became part of a place name. This was not a calm appeal; it was an accusation that surrounded the leader and pressed in step by step. Their words also grew heavier and heavier: Why have you brought us up out of Egypt, to kill us with thirst? This sentence reversed the Exodus. The one who had brought them out of Egypt was the LORD; in their mouths the great deed of redemption became Moses' crime of harming people. When people grow desperate, they rewrite history: grace is forgotten, hardship is magnified, and the one who led them becomes the defendant. This is also the shape of many conflicts today: the difficulty is real, but once the words come out, a benefactor is described as an enemy.

Moses' answer cut to the point: "Why chide ye with me? wherefore do ye tempt the LORD?" Moses first named the true object of the striving: on the surface you are coming at me, but in fact you are tempting the LORD. To tempt is to put God in the defendant's seat and demand that He prove Himself on the spot. What the people wanted was no longer only a mouthful of water; they wanted a guarantee, a piece of evidence: if You do not give water at once, we have reason to say You are not among us. Moses too was desperate in his heart; the text records that he cried to the LORD, saying the people were almost ready to stone him. A leader is also human; surrounded by the very people he led, who want to stone him, that chill in the heart is something the Bible does not cover up for him. But Moses did not answer striving with striving; he brought the striving before God. Leaders, parents, and fellow workers should all learn this: when people come at you, do not rush first to defend yourself; first bring the matter to God.

How did God answer? God did not first rebuke the people's attitude; God first solved the people's thirst. He told Moses: take in your hand the rod with which you struck the Nile, take some of the elders, and go to the rock at Horeb; I will stand before you there upon the rock, and you shall strike the rock, and water shall come out of it, that the people may drink. Notice this picture of action. God's rod had once struck the Nile and turned Egypt's water to blood; now the same rod would strike the rock at Horeb and make a wilderness rock yield water. The rod that judged Egypt now became the rod that provided for Israel. Before the eyes of the elders Moses struck, the rock split, and water gushed out. "Rock," in the original tsur ( צוּר , H6697), a rock, a crag [3]. Inside dry, hard stone there is not a drop of stored water, yet God chose to bring water out of the very place where water was most impossible. Brothers and sisters, God's way of providing for people is often not in the warehouse we expected, but in the place we thought driest, hardest, and most hopeless. The elders saw it with their own eyes so that they would bear witness: this water was not a trick Moses performed; it was the LORD standing upon the rock, personally opening the spring.

Here we must honestly add one word about how to read this. In the New Testament, Paul looked back on this wilderness history and said the Israelites drank the same spiritual drink, and they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them, and that Rock was Christ (1 Corinthians 10:4). This is the New Testament's way of reading the Old Testament — the apostle looking back at the rock in the light of Christ: the One who followed the people and provided for them in those years is, in the New Testament revelation, identified as Christ Himself. This manuscript cites this reading as annotation presented according to the New Testament's reading of the Old, and does not force the later revelation back into the mouths of the people of that day. The people of that day knew only that the rock had yielded water; we today may know one layer more: the picture of the struck rock pouring out living water found its deepest echo in Christ — He was struck, and living water flowed to the thirsty.

There is one more layer that the later Bible makes plain for us. Psalm 95 looked back on this event and its tone suddenly grew severe: harden not your heart, as in the provocation, as in the day when your fathers tempted Me in the wilderness and saw My work. The psalmist says that generation went astray in their hearts and did not know God's ways, so that He swore in His wrath that they should not enter His rest. Notice: what the psalm cares about is not whether they were angry or thirsty that day, but that their hearts were hardened by one temptation. For a person in difficulty to ask once whether God is still there is not yet the most frightening thing; what is frightening is to ask so habitually that the heart is set in that shape, so that afterward, at every difficulty, the first reaction is not prayer but accusation, and the first sentence is not a cry for help but a verdict. The terror of Massah and Meribah is not a moment of grumbling; it is that grumbling can train a heart hard. So Moses named this place and nailed the shameful matter onto the map precisely so that later generations, arriving here, would be warned: water can be found again, but if a heart is hardened and, hearing God's voice, still refuses to turn back, that is the true thirst. What the wilderness lacked most was never only water; it was a soft heart. With a soft heart, however hard the rock, God has a way to make it yield water; with a hard heart, even manna in the mouth will be eaten into grumbling. This chapter left the place name behind as a mirror, so that every time we are tempted to test God, we may first look at our own hearts.

Finally look at the place names. Moses gave that place two names: Massah, and also Meribah. Massah ( מַסָּה , H4531) means the place of testing [1]; Meribah ( מְרִיבָה , H4808) means the place of striving [2]. When the Israelites named places, it was usually to commemorate what God had done; this time what was recorded was human failure. A place name is like posting the people's medical record on the map: later generations passing here would all remember that their ancestors here tested God and strove. Why record a shameful matter? Because the text immediately gives the eye of the whole chapter, the people's question: "Is the LORD among us, or not?" This is the deepest place in the chapter. The people's real crisis was never only having no water; it was doubting God's presence. When the water was gone, they inferred that God was gone; when circumstances dried up, they concluded that His presence had dried up too. Lack of water is the body's thirst; doubting His presence is faith's thirst — the latter is far deeper than the former, and far more dangerous. We today are often the same: when prayer is not answered at once, we ask whether God is still there; when illness does not heal at once, we ask whether God still cares; when a job is lost or a child is in trouble, we ask whether God has already left. The text records this sentence exactly as it was, not to shame the people, but to remind us: presence is not proved by circumstances; presence is a fact God Himself has promised. He said He is here, and He is here — even if the rock has not yet split, even if the water has not yet flowed.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Amalek Came to Attack — The Battle Below the Hill and the Hands on the Hill (17:8-13)

They had barely drunk the water when the battle came. Then Amalek came and fought with Israel in Rephidim. The Amalekites were descendants of Esau, a strong nomadic people of the wilderness. Why did they choose this moment to come? Deuteronomy later looked back on this battle and said it very plainly: Amalek met Israel on the way, when they were faint and weary, and struck down those who lagged behind, all who were feeble at the rear (Deuteronomy 25:18, cited in sense). That is, they specialized in striking when people were most tired, most thirsty, and weakest at the tail of the column. The enemy's tactic is the same in every age: he does not attack you at your strongest; he waits for you at your most exhausted. The people had just finished striving over water and the hearts in the camp had not yet settled when Amalek killed in from outside. Striving inside and battle outside — this is exactly when people are most likely to collapse.

Moses' deployment had two levels, upper and lower. Below the hill, he said to Joshua: Choose us out men, and go out, fight with Amalek. This is Joshua's first appearance in the Bible. Before this he was only Moses' assistant, without a name-bearing weight of his own in the narrative; from this battle on he was pushed to the front of the formation — choosing men, going out to war, swinging the sword, learning to be a leader amid real blades and real spears. There is no shortcut in the making of a new leader: not the classroom first, but the battlefield first; not a title first, but an apprenticeship first. When Joshua later took Moses' staff and led the people into Canaan, his lessons began with this battle at Rephidim. When God raises up successors, He often does it quietly: He first places them in a hard battle and lets them learn trust and responsibility on the front line. The young people of the church today are raised the same way: give them real battles to fight and real responsibilities to bear, and only then do they grow up.

On the hill was the other level. Moses said: Tomorrow I will stand on the top of the hill with the rod of God in my hand. So Moses took Aaron and Hur up to the top of the hill. What follows is the strangest scene in the whole chapter: when Moses held up his hand, Israel prevailed; when he let down his hand, Amalek prevailed. The swords below the hill had not yet decided the outcome, but the pointer of victory was already swinging with the hands on the hill. This picture tells us: warfare has a visible side and an invisible side. What is visible is Joshua fighting desperately in the valley; what is invisible is Moses holding up his hands on the hill. Before the battle on the ground had even begun, the battle in heaven had already decided half of it. So many things are like this: on the surface it looks like ability, resources, and strategy in contest, but underneath it is prayer holding up the whole situation. The church's ministry, a family's hard pass, a personal low valley — which of them is not like this? The battle at the front must be fought in earnest, and the prayer behind the scenes must not be missing even more. Without the hands on the hill, however fast the sword below, it cannot hold.

Here this manuscript must honestly pause and separate fact from interpretation. The text only states the correspondence between Moses holding up and letting down his hands and the course of the battle; it does not directly say that holding up his hands was prayer, and it records no words of prayer from Moses on the hill. Reading the raised hands as prayer, as reaching out to God and calling upon Him, is an interpretation common in later preaching and worship tradition, and this manuscript follows that homiletical interpretation, because raised hands toward heaven do carry the meaning of prayer elsewhere in the Bible; but we must state clearly that this is interpretation, not the text's own words. Separating them in this way does not weaken the message; it honors the text: what the text plainly says, we preach with full confidence; what the text does not plainly say, we honestly label as interpretation. The more a preacher reveres the text, the more at ease those who hear can be.

Then look at Moses' hands. Moses' hands were heavy. Holding up hands is not striking a pose for a photograph; it had to be held from morning until sunset. An old man of eighty, holding his hands above his head, would find even one hour hard, let alone a whole day. The text does not portray Moses as a superman who never tired; the Bible's figures are all real people: hands ache, arms tremble, and held up long enough, they droop. Just then Aaron and Hur did something seemingly very small that in fact saved the whole army: they brought a stone and put it under Moses, so that he sat on it; and one stood on one side and the other on the other side, holding up his hands, until the going down of the sun. And Moses' hands were steady.

Look carefully at this picture: one stone, two fellow workers, three men, one whole day. No flashing blades, no slogans — only a pair of held-up hands. The text says his hands were steady; the word used in the original is 'emunah ( אֱמוּנָה , H530), firmness, steadfastness, also faithfulness [4], from the same root as the "faith" in Habakkuk's famous sentence, "the just shall live by his faith." This is a beautiful wordplay in the original: Moses' hands being held steady rested precisely on God's faithfulness upholding human faithfulness. Human hands droop and human faith softens, but when a fellow worker's arms reach over, and when God's faithfulness upholds from beneath, those hands can be held up until sunset. Brothers and sisters, the elderly also need someone to support them. However great a figure Moses was, leading millions out of Egypt, his hands still grew heavy, and he still needed Aaron and Hur. Do not assume that those who have stood long do not need support; those who have served long have arms that ache even more — they are often just too embarrassed to say so. Those in the church who most need to be held up are often precisely those who have always held others up.

What was the outcome of the battle? Joshua defeated Amalek and his people with the edge of the sword. Notice the Bible's way of writing: the victory is recorded under Joshua's name, and the sword was swung by him in the valley; yet every reader of the chapter knows that without that pair of supported hands on the hill, the sword in the valley could not have prevailed either. Faithfulness on the ground and prayer on the hill were never an either-or. To pray only and not go out to battle is evasion; to go out to battle only and not pray is arrogance. The victory at Rephidim was a victory of hilltop and valley joined together, of old Moses, old fellow workers, and the new leader Joshua joined together. One generation's hands held up, the next generation's sword swinging, and fellow workers' arms supporting in between — this picture is what the church ought to look like.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Jehovah Nissi — Written in the Book, Raised on the Altar (17:14-16)

When the battle was over, Moses did two things: one concerned words, the other worship.

The first thing: record it in a book. The LORD said to Moses: Write this for a memorial in a book, and rehearse it in the ears of Joshua: for I will utterly put out the remembrance of Amalek from under heaven. "Blot out" in the original uses the emphatic form machoh emcheh ( מָחֹה אֶמְחֶה , H4229), an infinitive absolute placed before the verb, meaning to blot out utterly, to blot out without fail [6]. This sentence carries extreme weight, and we must handle it solemnly, not sensationalize it, and not preach it askew. First, the blotting out of Amalek's name was God's own declaration, God's judgment pronounced on the power that specialized in killing the weak and stood against His people from of old; it was not a license for the people to vent private vengeance. The Bible later records in 1 Samuel 15 the matter of Saul and Amalek, and records in Deuteronomy the command that Israel remember what Amalek did on the way, yet it keeps the timing and manner of execution within God's sovereignty. Second, there is another side of remembrance here, worth our slow reflection: Scripture often tells people to remember God's grace, lest they forget; this time God told them to remember the enemy's evil as well, lest they be naive. An evil name too must be recorded — not to feed hatred, but to warn later generations: what kind of power specializes in attacking the weak, and what kind of sin must not be indulged — later generations must know. Remembrance has two sides: one side remembers grace, the other remembers evil; remembering grace makes people grateful, remembering evil makes people watchful, and only when both are written in the book is remembrance complete.

The second thing: build an altar. Moses built an altar and called its name Jehovah Nissi. Nissi ( נִסִּי , H5251) means "my banner" [5]. A banner is the mark around which an army rallies, the allegiance that can be seen from far away: a soldier in the confusion of battle lifts his head, sees his own side's flag, and knows where to draw near and for whom he fights. After this battle Moses did not erect a monument to himself and did not boast of the swiftness of Joshua's sword; he built an altar and attributed the victory to one name: the LORD is my banner. The implication is that what truly rallied this company and truly gave this company victory was not Moses' hands and not Joshua's sword, but the LORD Himself. Moses also said that the LORD had sworn and would war against Amalek from generation to generation — in the original, milchamah laYHWH, the war against Amalek is the LORD's war [7]. This sentence places the battle of Rephidim within a much longer frame: this was not an encounter that ended when the fighting stopped; this is the war, across generations, between God and the power that resists Him, with God Himself in it, and His people merely standing under His flag.

Also notice the weight of the words "written in a book" in this chapter. Moses wrote God's word in a book as a memorial, and was to rehearse it in Joshua's ears, so that the next generation's leader would hear it personally and remember it personally. This is one of the Bible's very early records of "write it down and pass it on." The later Bible was written in exactly this way, scroll by scroll; the Book in our hands today grew out of just such remembrance. Brothers and sisters, the ministry of words has one of its roots right here: if victory stays only on the lips, it scatters when a generation passes; victory written in a book can be read, rehearsed, and believed by the next generation. Our Bible reading, note-taking, preaching, and writing today all stand on this line: write down what God has done, lest later generations forget what kind of God He is.

Gather the application of this chapter into three places. The first place falls on those who doubt His presence. Your question today may be exactly the people's: Is the LORD among us, or not? No job in sight, a problem found in the body, prayer that seems to strike empty air — you truly want to ask whether God is still here. This chapter's answer is: presence is not measured by whether the water jar is full; presence is measured by whether God's promise is true. On the road He leads there will be Rephidim, there will be thirst, but He will be standing upon the rock, and He will have a way to open the rock. Do not rush to conclude that He is not here; come before Him, speak out your thirst, and speak out your question too. He is not afraid of your question; He is only afraid that you will turn and walk away and not even ask anymore.

The second place falls on those whose hands are drooping. You have served in the church for years, held up your family for years, carried your post for years; your hands ached long ago, only no one ever asked. It is not that you do not believe; you are tired. This chapter tells you: drooping hands are no disgrace; Moses' hands drooped too. What is disgraceful is not drooping hands, but gritting through without saying anything until, aching on, you let go completely. Go and find your Aaron and your Hur: a brother to whom you can be honest, a sister to whom you can entrust yourself; let them bring a stone for you to sit on and reach out their hands to hold you up. Being held up is not a mark of weakness; it is the secret of standing until sunset.

And within this second place there is one more layer, falling on those who will hand things to the next generation. Moses wrote the words of this battle in a book and was to rehearse them in Joshua's ears; this was no superfluous procedure. Joshua had personally fought this battle in the valley and knew that day's flashing blades better than anyone; yet Moses still wanted him to hear about the hill, to hear God's words, to hear the declaration of blotting out and remembrance — because if a leader remembers only the sword he himself swung and not the banner God raised, when he later leads troops he will rely only on his own experience and temper. Pass on experience, yes, but pass on even more the One behind the experience: tell them at which Rephidim you once thirsted, at which moment your hands drooped and someone held them up, at which moment you saw with your own eyes water come out of the rock. When the next generation has heard these things, and their own Rephidim arrives, they will know where to go and to whom to lift their hands. The handing down of faith was never a slogan; it is fought battle by battle, told to them battle by battle, and written for them page by page.

The third place falls on those who care only about what is below the hill. You are very capable, you fight your battles very well, but you never go up the hill; you do not pray, or your prayer lasts only three minutes. Rephidim reminds you: half of victory is not on your sword. Make up the time on the hill, invite back the intercessors, and recognize clearly whose flag you are under: are you fighting under your own name, or under the banner of Jehovah Nissi? A different flag means a different ending. May every one of us have water to drink in the camp, battles to fight in the valley, hands raised on the hill, people supporting us at our side, and a banner flying overhead.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today we have walked through three places: the waterside at Rephidim, beside the rock at Horeb, and on the hilltop at Rephidim. The waterside showed us that doubting His presence is deeper than lacking water; beside the rock we saw God open living water from the driest and hardest place; on the hilltop we saw that the intercessor's hands droop and need a fellow worker's arms, and that victory is finally written in a book and raised on an altar, attributed to one name: Jehovah Nissi. The people asked: Is the LORD among us, or not? This chapter answered with the water of the rock and the banner of the battlefield: Yes, He has been here all along; and in victory, He Himself contends for us.

Let us pray together: Jehovah Nissi, our banner, we thank You. When we thirsted, You did not leave; when the enemy came while we were weary, You did not stand by. Forgive our hearts that tempted You and doubted Your presence, and teach us no longer to measure Your faithfulness by our circumstances. Hold up every drooping pair of hands among us; prepare an Aaron and a Hur for those whose arms ache, and prepare intercession on the hill for those who go out to battle. Let us stand firm under Your banner all our lives, until sunset, until victory, until we see with our own eyes Your name lifted high. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[6] machoh emcheh: Hebrew מָחֹה אֶמְחֶה (H4229), Exodus 17:14, "I will utterly put out the remembrance of Amalek from under heaven"; in the original the infinitive absolute machoh is placed before the cognate verb emcheh, forming an emphatic sense: to blot out utterly, to blot out without fail; the meanings and syntax are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's text emphasizes that this is God's declaration of judgment, not a license for the people's hatred, a homiletical characterization based on the whole chapter's context and God's sovereignty.

[7] milchamah laYHWH: Exodus 17:16 Moses said the LORD had sworn by His throne, and also said the LORD would war against Amalek from generation to generation; the original can be read as "the LORD's war is against Amalek, from generation to generation," with the subject belonging to the LORD; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT, with the punctuation following the renderings of the KJV and NIV. This is a self-chosen word anchor; its development, "God Himself contends, and the people stand under His banner," is homiletical exposition.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And he called the name of the place Massah, and Meribah, because of the chiding of the children of Israel, and because they tempted the LORD, saying, Is the LORD among us, or not?" (Exodus 17:7, KJV) "And it came to pass, when Moses held up his hand, that Israel prevailed: and when he let down his hand, Amalek prevailed. But Moses' hands were heavy; and they took a stone, and put it under him, and he sat thereon; and Aaron and Hur stayed up his hands ... until the going down of the sun." (Exodus 17:11-12, excerpt, KJV)

The lesson of Rephidim is not only about having no water. The people's deepest thirst was doubting whether God was still with them. If today you also ask, Is the LORD among us, or not? remember: God did not answer that question with rebuke; He answered it with water from the rock. A dry circumstance does not mean a dry presence, and an unanswered prayer does not mean He has walked away. Look also at Moses' hands: raised, Israel prevailed; lowered, Amalek prevailed — yet hands grow heavy. God's way was not to make Moses clench his teeth and hold on alone, but to set Aaron and Hur beside him and a stone beneath him. If your hands are drooping today, do not be ashamed, and do not carry it alone. Find a fellow worker, sit down, and let someone hold you up until sunset. Victory was never one person standing to the end with raised hands; it is God's faithfulness underneath, and a co-worker's arms beside you. With the banner of Jehovah Nissi overhead, helpers at your side, and water from the rock beneath your feet, you can finish today's battle.

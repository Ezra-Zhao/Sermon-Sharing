## Summary (about 100 words)

A son was born in a Levite household. His mother hid him for three months, then laid him in an ark of bulrushes among the reeds by the Nile. Pharaoh's daughter had compassion and adopted him, naming him Moses, meaning drawn out of the water. Grown up, Moses struck out for his own people yet was misunderstood, and fled to Midian to sojourn for forty years. At the chapter's end the king of Egypt died, and the people's groaning reached God: God heard, remembered, looked, and knew — the clock of deliverance matured amid the groaning.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter we read a very dark ending. Pharaoh's command had been issued: every son born to the Hebrews, you shall cast into the river. The river was originally Egypt's lifeline — the Nile fed the whole country — yet now it had become an execution ground for infants. A people enslaved in another's land could not even let a newborn's cry be heard without it becoming evidence of a crime. In just such a night, chapter 2 opens with an extremely ordinary sentence: a man of the house of Levi went and took a daughter of Levi as his wife. No fanfare, no vision — only a marriage, a pregnancy, and a birth. God's great deliverance often begins in just such an unremarkable way.

Today in this chapter we will walk a long stretch of road with one person: from the reeds by the river to the palace of Egypt; from the splendor of the palace to the well in the wilderness of Midian; and at last the camera pulls back, across forty years, and rests on the One in heaven. He heard the groaning, He remembered the covenant, He looked, and He knew. These four verbs are the key to the whole chapter, and also the words many people who are enduring in waiting most need to hear today.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And the woman conceived, and bare a son: and when she saw him that he was a goodly child, she hid him three months. And when she could not longer hide him, she took for him an ark of bulrushes, and daubed it with slime and with pitch, and put the child therein; and she laid it in the flags by the river's brink." (Exodus 2:2-3, excerpt, KJV)

"And Pharaoh's daughter said unto her, Take this child away, and nurse it for me, and I will give thee thy wages. ... And the child grew, and she brought him unto Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. And she called his name Moses: and she said, Because I drew him out of the water." (Exodus 2:9-10, excerpt, KJV)

"And it came to pass in process of time, that the king of Egypt died: and the children of Israel sighed by reason of the bondage, and they cried, and their cry came up unto God by reason of the bondage. And God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. And God looked upon the children of Israel, and God had respect unto them." (Exodus 2:23-25, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today we are going to hear the story of how an infant survived under a death decree, and also hear how You heard the groaning after a long silence. Comfort every one among us who is letting go, who is waiting, and who is weeping, and let them know that however deep the water, Your hand can still draw a person out of the water. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Ark of Bulrushes and the Nile — A Mother's Letting Go (2:1-10)

First look at this child's origin. Chapter 1 did not record his parents' names, and the opening of chapter 2 only says that a man and a woman of the tribe of Levi were married. Their names are supplied only later: his father Amram, his mother Jochebed. What tribe was Levi? At that time nothing special could be seen. In Genesis, because of the violence at Shechem, Levi was prophesied by the dying Jacob to be scattered in Israel. Who could have imagined then that this inconspicuous tribe, even carrying the shadow of a curse, would produce priests and the prophet Moses? God chooses people never according to the genealogies people count as glorious. In the most hopeless corner, He prepares the greatest vessel.

The mother's first comment upon giving birth to her son matters greatly: she saw that he was a goodly child. "Goodly," in the original tov (טוֹב, H2896), means good, beautiful [5]. We know this word very well: in Genesis chapter 1, God saw everything He had made and it was good; after making each thing, God saw that it was good — the same word is used. A slave mother held an infant who was on a death list, yet the Bible describes him with the word used at creation for the created world. This is not an ordinary mother praising her child; the narrator is telling us: this child was beautiful in God's eyes, and God's gaze rested on him first. In Acts, Stephen in his testimony also said that Moses was born a proper child. Hebrews goes further and makes clear that Moses' parents saw that he was a proper child, hid him for three months, and were not afraid of the king's commandment. "Goodly" here is not only appearance, but also a spiritual seeing: the parents saw God's mark upon the child, and so fear stepped back and faith stepped forward.

She hid him for three months. Three months sounds short, yet every night of it was long. The infant grew bigger and bigger, and his crying grew louder and louder. To hide a male infant in a slave's hut was for the whole family to stake their lives on the table every day. What if the neighbors heard, what if the inspectors came? The Bible does not write how the mother endured those three months; it writes only one sentence: she could no longer hide him. Brothers and sisters, in this sentence are the tears of many parents: I have done all I can; I can hide him no longer. When human strength reaches its end, that does not mean the story has reached its end; it is exactly where God takes over.

When she could no longer hide him, she took an ark of bulrushes. "Ark," in the original tevah (תֵּבָה, H8392) [1]. Please remember this word firmly: in the whole Hebrew Bible, this word is used in only two places. One is Noah's ark — the ark God commanded Noah to build uses this very word; the other is Moses' ark of bulrushes. In hundreds of chapters of the Bible it is used only twice; the author was by no means repeating it casually. Noah's ark carried a family of eight and the seed of living creatures through the flood; Moses' ark of bulrushes carried the future leader of a nation on the great river. The flood was the water of God's judgment, and the Nile was Pharaoh's water of slaughter; both waters took life, yet both arks became the means of life. The two narratives echo each other across the distance, pointing to the same God: He does not always remove His people from the water; He often prepares an ark so that His people pass through the water and come out alive. This correspondence is a fact of word and narrative; to say that in every desperate situation people think hopeless, God has already prepared the ark on the surface of the water, is a homiletical exposition this manuscript makes on that basis.

The details must not be passed over either. She daubed the ark with slime and pitch, that is, waterproof asphalt, and laid it in the flags by the river's brink. "Flags," in the original suph (סוּף, H5488), meaning reeds, water plants [2]. This word will appear again, and at the most crucial place: the sea Israel crossed when they left Egypt is in Hebrew yam suph, the Sea of Reeds. As an infant Moses lay beside the suph; eighty years later — forty years in the palace and forty years in Midian —, he would lead the whole nation to stand by the waters of the suph and watch God divide the sea. The little ark of bulrushes rested among the reeds like a seed, rehearsing the victory of the Red Sea in advance. This too is a fact at the level of the word; the exposition is: when God works, He often first leaves a mark in a small matter, so that later, when the great matter is accomplished, His people look back and see that the foreshadowing was there all along.

There is also a detail that decided life and death: she did not push the ark into the middle of the river to drift with the current; she laid it by the river's brink, and had the child's sister stand afar off, to know what would be done to him. This is not abandonment; this is entrusting and watching at the same time. Letting go does not mean washing one's hands of the matter. A God-fearing mother's hands released the ark, but her eyes did not leave the child, and her daughter still stood not far away, ready to rush forward the moment the door God opened appeared. Many people misunderstand faith, thinking that faith means closing one's eyes and tossing the child into the river. Jochebed's faith was not like that: the pitch that should be daubed was daubed fully, the place that should be chosen was chosen well, the person who should stand was standing firm — and what remained, only then, was left in God's hands. Do your human duty, and leave the outcome to God: this is called letting go without losing; it is placing the child in God's arrangement.

Who came to the river? Pharaoh's daughter came down to wash herself at the river. She saw the ark, sent her maid to fetch it, opened it, and behold, the child wept. The Bible specially writes the infant's crying and the princess's reaction: she had compassion on him. She recognized at a glance that this was one of the Hebrews' children. The death decree had been issued by her own father; she knew full well what keeping this child meant. Yet compassion prevailed over the royal command. Notice the order of God's arrangement: the death decree came from Pharaoh's house, and the hand of rescue also came from Pharaoh's house; the Nile was the execution ground Pharaoh designated, and Pharaoh's daughter, precisely on this river, became like a mother of deliverance. God does not need to remove the enemy in order to work; He can raise up a person of compassion inside the enemy's own household. Human power draws its enclosures, but God's compassion always finds a waterway.

At this critical moment, the sister standing afar off came forward. She said, shall I go and call to thee a nurse of the Hebrew women, that she may nurse the child for thee? A sentence spoken both humbly and wisely: she did not expose that she was the child's sister, but only offered exactly the help Pharaoh's daughter needed. The princess agreed, and the sister went and called the child's mother. Then appeared the most moving and most reversed scene of the whole chapter: the mother became her own son's nurse, nursing her own child with her own hands, and Pharaoh's daughter even paid her wages. Brothers and sisters, think carefully: three months earlier, this mother held her child hiding here and there, afraid of being discovered; now she held the same child, nursing him openly, with wages from the palace besides. When she laid the child in the river, what she laid down was possession; when God returned the child to her arms, what He gave her was a recognized place, peace, and provision. Those who let go do not necessarily lose; sometimes God returns the very same thing, only the standing is completely different: before, she nursed him in secret; now, she nursed him by commission. This is not to say that every letting go will be returned in the same way, and this manuscript makes no such promissory exposition; the text here records it plainly in this way to make us know that God has the sovereignty to dispose of what is entrusted to Him in His own way — either returning it, or leading it toward a distance we cannot see. The right of disposal is His, and peace is also in Him.

The child grew, and the mother brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. This sending was the true letting go: the first sending was into the river, with the sister still watching; this sending was into the palace, and from then on the child would grow up in the court of the enemy and learn all the wisdom of the Egyptians. But do not forget: in the years before he was sent to the palace — the years of nursing, of learning to speak, of hearing prayers — the mother had already planted the root of faith in the child's heart. That Moses later refused to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter, choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God, did not fall from the sky; it was fed into him sentence by sentence by his nurse-mother. Parents, please listen well: the years a child is in your hands may not be long, and the palace of the world will sooner or later take them away; but the words you plant in them when they are smallest, they will carry for a lifetime. The years of nursing are short, but if the root goes deep, the wind cannot pull it up.

Pharaoh's daughter named him Moses. The reason for the name is stated plainly in the text: because I drew him out of the water. "I drew out," in the original mashah (מָשָׁה, H4871) [3]. The name Moses, heard in Hebrew, is the one who was drawn out of the water. He would carry this name all his life: every time someone called him Moses, they restated his deliverance as an infant. Later he would become a deliverer, yet his name first declares that he was a delivered person. Brothers and sisters, this is one of the thematic landing points of the whole chapter: the deliverer was first delivered. One who does not know himself to be a person drawn up cannot bear the mission of drawing others up. A leader's true qualification is not that he never fell into the water, but that he knows deeply how deep the water is, how powerless his own hands are, and how steady the hand that drew him is. A seminary cannot teach this, and a palace cannot teach this; only the ark of bulrushes can teach it: you live, not because you are strong, but because someone drew you out of the water. Many years later, when Moses stood by the Red Sea and stretched out his staff, he should have known more clearly than anyone how one is saved from the water, because his own name is a short sermon: I was first drawn up; now God, through my hand, draws up a whole nation.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Man Who Grew Up in the Palace and the Well in Midian — Moses Strikes Out and Flees (2:11-22)

The camera turns, and Moses has grown up. Of the decades in between, the Bible says not a word. He was educated in the palace, ate Egyptian food, and wore Egyptian clothing, yet the matter of his bloodline could in the end not deceive his own heart. He went out to his brethren and looked on their burdens. "Looked" — note this word: he did not listen to reports in the palace; he went out himself and looked with his own eyes. What did he see? An Egyptian striking a Hebrew, one of his brethren. The word "brethren" recurs in this passage, showing that Moses in his heart had long since identified with his own people. Whom a person identifies with is not on a household register; it is in whose suffering makes his heart ache first when he sees it. Moses' heart ached first, and then his hand moved: he looked this way and that way, and when he saw that there was no man, he slew the Egyptian and hid him in the sand.

This passage must be preached honestly; Moses must not be beautified into a perfect hero. His righteous anger was real, but his method was of the flesh. He relied on his own fist to save one man and on sand to bury a corpse; this was the palace's way of solving problems, and the very thing the wilderness would later take forty years to grind away. That God would use Moses — of this there is no mistake — but the Moses God would use was a Moses shaped by Him, not a Moses who saved the people by private violence. Hebrews commends Moses because when he was come to years he refused to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter, refused the pleasures of sin, and chose to suffer affliction with God's people; that is an affirmation of his faith. Affirming his faith does not equal affirming his means of killing a man and burying the body. The two must be separated: the text records his zeal, and also records directly his recklessness; his flight afterward is the bill for that recklessness. If preaching only praises his courage, it misses God's forty-year lesson; if it only rebukes his recklessness, it misses his heart that refused to sever itself from his own people. God's choosing never waits until a person is complete before it begins; He uses and repairs at the same time.

The next day, he went out again and saw two Hebrews striving together, and he said to the one who did the wrong, why do you strike your fellow? He thought that having stood up for his people the day before, his words today should carry weight. The other man threw one sentence back at him: Who made thee a prince and a judge over us? intendest thou to kill me, as thou killedst the Egyptian? Moses heard it and feared, and said in his heart, surely this thing is known. What stings a person is often not the enemy's knife, but a compatriot's mouth. He had risked his life for his own people, and his own people did not appreciate it, but asked who he thought he was. Brothers and sisters, those who serve must be prepared for this lesson: the people you strike out for may not acknowledge your striking out; the matter for which you shed blood may not earn you even a word of thanks. Moses' grievance in that year is a required course for every leader afterward. Seen at a deeper level, the people's rejection also showed that the time had not yet come: God's deliverance cannot rely on one fleshly hero fighting alone; it must wait for God Himself to raise up the circumstances, prepare the leader, and awaken the people. Human zeal ran forty years ahead; God's timing was not disordered by a single step.

Pharaoh heard this thing and sought to slay Moses, and Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh and dwelt in the land of Midian. The one who had been drawn out of the river now fell again under pursuit. He sat down by a well in Midian. A well — again a well. Jacob met his wife at a well, and Moses at a well would also meet the home of the second half of his life. The seven daughters of the priest of Midian came to draw water for their father's flock, and the shepherds came and drove them away. Moses stood up and helped them and watered their flock. Note, his character had not changed: seeing oppression, he still had to act; what had changed was only the occasion: this time he did not kill anyone; he drew water and helped people. God's school — its first lesson is often not to teach him something new, but to grind the recklessness out of his old character and leave only righteous compassion. The seven daughters came home early, and their father Jethro was surprised; the daughters said, an Egyptian delivered us. In other people's eyes Moses was still an Egyptian: his clothing was Egyptian, his accent was Egyptian, his bearing was Egyptian. Yet this Egyptian carried in his heart the suffering of the Hebrews, walked under his feet the road of a fugitive, and his identity hung between two sides, neither of which fully accepted him. He tasted the flavor of being a sojourner from that time on.

Jethro kept him to dwell with him and gave him his daughter Zipporah as wife. Zipporah bore a son, and Moses called his name Gershom, for he said, I have been a stranger in a strange land. Gershom's name, like Moses' own name, is also a short sermon: one speaks of deliverance, the other of sojourning. However settled the foreign land, however sweet the well water, however good the wife and son, the sojourner's placard in his heart was never taken down. Brothers and sisters, this is exactly the reality of God's people in the world: they settle down and work on the earth, yet know in their hearts that their true home is not here. Moses made a home in Midian and lived by pasturing sheep, and in a flash forty years passed. Everything he had learned in the palace seemed voided, the affair of killing the Egyptian seemed past, the Hebrews seemed to have forgotten him, and God seemed to have forgotten the whole matter too. Had He really forgotten? In the next section, the Bible will answer us with four verbs. Before answering, let us first admit the value of the forty years in Midian: God placed an impatient man among sheep and taught him patience; placed a prince in the wilderness and taught him that ordinariness is a blessing; placed a man who had killed by a well and taught him to save people with water instead of killing people with fists. Later, when he led Israel through the wilderness for forty years, he relied precisely on the lessons learned in Midian. So do not despise that seemingly wasted stretch of years in your life. God rarely wastes a person's wilderness; He only sets the classroom where no grades can be seen. When He uses the person again, the person looks back and understands: every single year was on the syllabus.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Groaning Reached God — Four Verbs and God's Timing (2:23-25)

In process of time, the king of Egypt died. The title "king," which had pressed on the people for decades, finally had news of death. But the people's situation did not change at once: the children of Israel sighed by reason of the bondage, and they cried. Pharaohs die, but the hard labor remains; regimes change, but the oppressed are still in the brick pits and mud. The Bible never promises that circumstances will automatically improve; it places hope in another place: their cry came up unto God.

"Groaning" / the cry, in the original na'akhah (נְאָקָה, H5009), meaning sighing, moaning, groaning [6]. This is not an elegant prayer text; it is a sound squeezed out of the chest under hard labor. When people reach the point where they cannot even form complete sentences, what remains is only groaning. Brothers and sisters, God understands groaning. Prayer is not always a long discourse; sometimes a sigh in a hospital ward, a single "Lord!" at the end of one's road, is the truest prayer. The text says the cry came up unto God — "came up unto" is precious: the people could not reach heaven, but their cry could. Where human hands cannot reach, sound can reach; and where even sound cannot be formed, the Bible says elsewhere that the Spirit Himself intercedes for us with groanings which cannot be uttered. Today we stay with this chapter: the cry coming up is God's active condescension, not the people's attainment of a high level of practice. God's timing matured amid the groaning, not because the people cried a sufficient number of times and moved God, but because the moment of deliverance God had appointed and the cry the people had been prepared to make met, in His arrangement.

Please listen to the four verbs that close this chapter, each deeper than the one before.

First, God heard. God heard their groaning. Hearing is not as simple as sound reaching ears; in the Bible, God's hearing carries the sense of accepting a case and acting. Abraham heard Hagar, and God also heard the voice of the lad; when Leah named Simeon, she also said the LORD had heard. For one who is above, to be willing to listen is the beginning of being willing to take charge. Through the forty years of silence in Midian, God did not miss a single sentence of the groaning in Egypt. He did not doze over the calendar; His hearing accumulated, and in time it would become the action of the Exodus.

Second, God remembered. God heard their groaning, and God remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. "Remembered," in the original zakar (זָכַר, H2142), to remember, recall, and act because of remembering [7]. The fact must first be stated clearly: God's remembering is not like human remembering after forgetting; God never forgets. When the Bible describes God with "remember," it means that He now takes the corresponding action according to the covenant He made. Noah was in the ark, and God remembered Noah, and the wind passed over the earth and the waters subsided; now God remembered the covenant made with the patriarchs, and the train of the Exodus was about to depart. The covenant was made by God Himself, and the remembering is God keeping His own word. If the people's hope were built on Pharaoh's mood, the hope would collapse when Pharaoh died; the people's hope is built on God's covenant — the king dies and the covenant remains, the king changes and the covenant does not change. That is why the end of this chapter does not write who the new Pharaoh was, but only writes that God remembered the covenant: the main character of history is always the One who keeps the covenant.

Third, God looked. The KJV renders it "looked upon"; in the original it is likewise "saw," yet a seeing with examination and care. At the opening, the mother saw that the child was goodly, and the princess saw the ark; now God saw the children of Israel — all the seeings of the chapter converge here: human seeing saved one child, God's seeing saves a whole nation. God's looking is not spectating; where His gaze reaches, deliverance follows. People whom the world treated as slave numbers have names with God; faces covered by the dust of the brick kilns, God sees each one clearly.

Fourth, God knew. "And God had respect unto them" (KJV) — the verb rendered "had respect" is yada’, God knew them and their affliction by experience. These plain words could almost be slid past by a reader; in the original, yada' (יָדַע, H3045), to know, recognize, experience personally [8]. This knowing is not knowing a piece of information. Hebrew knowing carries personal experience and relational recognition: Adam knew Eve his wife — the word is "knew"; when God said, I know Abraham, it is also this word. Therefore this manuscript's exposition is: God's knowing is an experiential knowing; He did not merely master the data of the people's suffering from afar; He treated the people's suffering as a matter He Himself knew and bore. This short sentence, God knew, is heavier than any long comfort: your suffering — God has not merely heard of it; He knows it. To what extent He knows it, Exodus chapter 3 supplies God's own footnote: I know their sorrows, and I am come down to deliver them. Groaning, covenant, looking, and knowing — four verbs linked into one chain: God heard the sound, recalled the promise, opened His eyes in care, and knew it all in His heart. The deliverance had not yet begun, yet the deliverance was already certain.

This passage also pushes the application before us. First, to parents who are letting go. Entrusting a child does not mean it no longer hurts. After Jochebed let go she still stood by the river watching; your tears, your intercession, and the roots you planted in your child's youngest years — none of them will be in vain. Second, to those whose zeal has been misunderstood. Moses struck out for his compatriots and received in return, "Who made thee?" — the chill in your heart, God has recorded it all. Whether people acknowledge you or not is not the final judgment; when the time comes, God Himself will establish him. Third, to those in their Midian years. You have pastured sheep in the wilderness for many years and cannot see the continuation of your calling; please place yourself in that sentence, God knew: the wilderness is not abandonment; it may be equipment. Fourth, to those left with only a groan. If you cannot even organize the words of a prayer, then sigh, then cry out — this chapter states plainly that the groaning reached God. God heard, God remembered, God looked, God knew — please carve these four sentences in your heart; those who wait live through the night by them.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today, we walked from an ark of bulrushes to a well, and from a well to before God's throne. The infant Moses was first drawn out of the water, and only later was he sent to draw a whole nation up; the mother let go, and the child was not lost, but in God's arrangement was nursed, named, and prepared; Moses sojourned in Midian for forty years, seemingly forgotten, yet the groaning rose from Egypt to heaven, and God heard, remembered, looked, and knew. The deliverer's story tells us: God's timing is not lateness; it matures amid the groaning; human letting go is not the end; it is the beginning of entrusting.

Let us pray together: Covenant-keeping God, thank You. Thank You for preserving the infant under the death decree, for guarding the root of faith amid the palace, and for refining Your servant through forty years in the wilderness. Thank You for hearing the groaning, remembering Your covenant, looking upon Your people, and knowing all our suffering. Teach us to still have faith when we let go, to not lose hope when we wait, and to believe when we weep that the groaning can reach heaven. Draw us all up out of the waters of sin and bondage, just as You drew up Moses. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who truly saves people out of the dead waters, Amen.

The passage in Part One of this manuscript, "in desperate situations God prepares an ark," is a homiletical exposition made on the basis of this word's distribution, and is noted as such in the text.

[2] suph: Hebrew סוּף (H5488), reeds, water plants; Exodus 2:3 and 2:5 refer to the reeds by the Nile; the sea crossed at the Exodus is called yam suph (Sea of Reeds, Exodus 13:18, etc.); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the little ark of bulrushes rehearsed the victory of the Red Sea" is a homiletical exposition made on the basis of the same word, and is noted as such in the text.

[3] mashah: Hebrew מָשָׁה (H4871), to draw out of water, pull out; Exodus 2:10, Pharaoh's daughter said, "Because I drew him out of the water," and the name Moses (מֹשֶׁה, Mosheh) is directly associated with this verb in the narrative of the text; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Note: there also exists a scholarly view that this name may in Egyptian be related to a word for "born"; this manuscript treats the naming only according to the text's own explanation, and does not expand the debate among the views.

[4] Mosheh and the double note on the name: Moses, Hebrew מֹשֶׁה; the naming explanation of Exodus 2:10 is according to the original text. This manuscript's "the name is a short sermon; the deliverer was first delivered" is a thematic exposition, not an etymological conclusion.

[5] tov: Hebrew טוֹב (H2896), good, beautiful, goodly; Exodus 2:2, the mother "saw him that he was a goodly child," using the same root as Genesis chapter 1, where God saw what He had made as "good"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Acts 7:20, "exceeding fair," and Hebrews 11:23, where the parents "saw he was a proper child," as cross-evidence. This manuscript's "a spiritual seeing" is a homiletical exposition.

[6] na'akhah: Hebrew נְאָקָה (H5009), groaning, sighing, moaning; Exodus 2:24, "God heard their groaning"; see also Exodus 6:5; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[7] zakar: Hebrew זָכַר (H2142), to remember, recall, and act because of remembering; Exodus 2:24, "God remembered his covenant"; see Genesis 8:1, God remembered Noah; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "remembering is not recalling after forgetting, but acting according to the covenant" is an exegetical explanation, defined as such in the text.

[8] yada': Hebrew יָדַע (H3045), to know, recognize, know by experience; Exodus 2:25, "God had respect unto them" / God knew; see Exodus 3:7, "I know their sorrows"; this word is also used in Genesis 4:1 and elsewhere for knowing within an intimate relation; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "experiencing rather than information" is a homiletical exposition made on the basis of the word's range of meaning.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. And God looked upon the children of Israel, and God had respect unto them." (Exodus 2:24-25)

Moses' story begins with a little ark of bulrushes. When his mother could hide him no longer, she laid him in a basket daubed with pitch and set it among the reeds by the river. She let go, yet the child was not lost: Pharaoh's daughter drew him out of the water, and his own mother nursed him and even received wages. Later Moses fled to Midian and stayed forty years, as if forgotten by everyone. But at the end of this chapter the Bible answers us with four verbs: God heard, God remembered, God looked, and God knew. If today you too are letting go, waiting, and left with only a groan, remember these four verbs. Where human hands release, God's hand does not release; kings die, but the covenant does not die; however long the night, the groaning reaches heaven. The one who was drawn out of the water should remember that hand for a lifetime, and learn to reach out and draw others up as well. Today, honestly hand over to God the thing you can no longer hide and can no longer carry. You do not need to figure out all the answers first; like that mother, daub the pitch as you should, lay the child in the basket, then stand by the river and watch, waiting for the God who hears, remembers, looks, and knows to reach out His own hand in His own time.

# Was Budded, and Brought Forth Buds, and Bloomed Blossoms, and Yielded Almonds — A Sermon on Numbers 17

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Draft — awaiting cross-review (Sermon Production Batch 3, 23 October 2026, unpublished)
Text: Numbers chapter 17 (13 verses in full, in the numbering of the Chinese and English Bibles; the Hebrew canon divides the chapters differently — see Base-text notes and Endnote 1)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV); the companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (和合本新标点本, the "God" edition), verse by verse. The English text is not a back-translation of the Chinese: every quotation below is KJV wording, and where the two base texts differ in wording, the difference is registered, not harmonized.
Title verification: The title is taken from the moment of disclosure in verse 8, read as it stands in the base text: "the rod of Aaron for the house of Levi was budded, and brought forth buds, and bloomed blossoms, and yielded almonds" (17:8). Twelve rods lie together for one night; Aaron's rod alone runs the full course — budded, buds, blossoms, almonds — and the whole chapter turns on this verse: the command (17:1-3), the place of deposit (17:4), the criterion announced beforehand (17:5), the sign kept (17:10), and the people's fear (17:12-13). Candidates examined verse by verse and set aside: "the man's rod, whom I shall choose, shall blossom" (17:5) is only the criterion announced in advance and does not reach the bringing out and the keeping of verses 9-11; "to be kept for a token" (17:10) shares its theme with the brasen plates of chapter 16 and invites confusion between the two sermons, and in any case does not cover the budding itself; "shall we be consumed with dying?" (17:13) is only the closing question and does not cover the disclosure. The Chinese title is taken from the same verse in its own base text （发了芽，生了花苞，开了花，结了熟杏）; the two base texts word the verse differently (the Chinese has a word for "ripe" — 熟 — where the KJV has none), and each manuscript follows its own base text. Full record in Endnote 2.
Base-text notes (where the two base texts or common paraphrase differ, the base text governs): The total of the rods is stated twice in both base texts as twelve, with Aaron's rod counted inside the twelve: KJV 17:2 "twelve rods" and 17:6 "even twelve rods: and the rod of Aaron was among their rods"; the Chinese agrees at both places. Any paraphrase that adds Aaron's rod on top of twelve contradicts both statements of the text itself. KJV 17:5 reads "the man's rod, whom I shall choose, shall blossom," where the Chinese opens with a word the KJV lacks ("afterwards, the man whom I choose..."). At 17:5 the murmurings are "against you" (Moses and Aaron); at 17:10 they are "their murmurings from me" — the object differs between the two verses in both base texts, and the two must not be conflated. KJV 17:9 reads "took every man his rod," where the Chinese specifies "each prince took his own rod." KJV 17:10 reads "Bring Aaron's rod again before the testimony," where the Chinese reads "put Aaron's rod back before the testimony" — the same action, differently worded. KJV 17:8 has no word "ripe" before "almonds"; the Chinese reads 熟杏 ("ripe almonds"), a rendering of the sense of the verb (see the (Summary) paragraph of Section 3). KJV 17:12 reads "Behold, we die, we perish, we all perish" — three clauses, the third still carrying its subject; the Chinese third clause drops the subject ("all perish!"). Chapter division: vv. 1-13 of this chapter are 17:16-28 in the Hebrew canon; the Hebrew chapter 17 as a whole (28 verses) runs from 16:36 in the Chinese and English numbering to 17:13 here. Verse numbers in this sermon are those of the Chinese and English Bibles throughout. Details in Endnote 3.

## Summary

Chapter 16 ended with the plague stayed, but the question of the choosing did not die with the dead. At the opening of chapter 17 the LORD directs Moses to speak to the children of Israel: take a rod from every tribe, of all their princes according to the house of their fathers twelve rods, every man's name written upon his rod, and Aaron's name upon the rod of Levi (17:1-3). The rods are to be laid up in the tabernacle of the congregation before the testimony, where God meets with them; the criterion is fixed in advance — "the man's rod, whom I shall choose, shall blossom" — and the purpose is that the murmurings of the children of Israel shall cease (17:4-5). Moses speaks, the princes give him their rods, twelve in all, and the rod of Aaron among them; Moses lays them up before the LORD in the tabernacle of witness (17:6-7). On the morrow Moses goes in, "and, behold, the rod of Aaron for the house of Levi was budded, and brought forth buds, and bloomed blossoms, and yielded almonds" (17:8). Moses brings out all the rods unto all the children of Israel; they look, and every man takes his rod. Aaron's rod alone is commanded back before the testimony, "to be kept for a token against the rebels," that their murmurings be taken away, "that they die not" (17:9-11). Yet the chapter closes not with praise but with a cry of fear: "Behold, we die, we perish, we all perish. Whosoever cometh any thing near unto the tabernacle of the LORD shall die: shall we be consumed with dying?" (17:12-13). The account of the chapter reads like this: a sign may be given as clearly as a blossom on dead wood, and still not do the work of trust in the heart that receives it; the rod budded to end the murmuring, but the fear remained — and the very purpose for which the token was kept was that the people should not die.

## Main Text

### 1. A Rod for Every Tribe: The Command and the Names Written on the Rods (Numbers 17:1-3) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) The chapter opens with the introduction of a divine speech, as it stands in the base text: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying," (17:1). The speaker is the LORD, the hearer is Moses, and the content is a direction for all Israel. The direction is not a device Moses invents to settle the strife of chapter 16; the text places it in God's own initiative. In chapter 16 the trial was by censers and the criterion was shown by fire; in this chapter the trial is by rods and the criterion will be shown by budding. Neither trial is proposed by men and approved by God; in both, God sets the rule and men carry it out. Before a single rod is taken or a single name written, the nature of the event is fixed: this is not an election. It is a disclosure.

(Fact) The body of the command stands in verse 2, quoted in full: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and take of every one of them a rod according to the house of their fathers, of all their princes according to the house of their fathers twelve rods: write thou every man's name upon his rod." (17:2) Four items are registered in order. First, the source of the rods: they are taken of the children of Israel, from their princes — the rods are not newly made instruments but the staffs surrendered from the hands of the tribal heads. Second, the unit: a rod according to the house of their fathers — one rod per fathers' house, great tribes and small on the same terms, not by numbers and not by noise. Third, the total: twelve rods — the text's first statement of its own total; the second stands in verse 6, and both say twelve, a matter to which this sermon returns below. Fourth, the names: "write thou every man's name upon his rod" — twelve rods of like form, distinguished only by the names on them; once the name is written, the rod is no longer an anonymous piece of wood but one particular man's token before God. Verse 3 is the special case within the command: "And thou shalt write Aaron's name upon the rod of Levi: for one rod shall be for the head of the house of their fathers." (17:3) On the rod of Levi there is written not the name of Levi's prince — this chapter nowhere gives the name of a prince of Levi — but Aaron's name. The reason given is the general rule itself: one rod for the head of every fathers' house. Reading verses 2 and 3 together: of the twelve rods, eleven bear the names of the tribal princes, and one bears Aaron's name; the man to be disclosed stands in the row under his own name from the start, not sheltered behind the name of his tribe.

(Fact) The count of the rods is settled here, before the narrative proceeds, so that nothing later rests on a blur. The text states its total twice: in the command, "twelve rods" (17:2), and in the execution, "even twelve rods: and the rod of Aaron was among their rods" (17:6). The clause "was among their rods" is the text's own ruling on the question: Aaron's rod stands inside the twelve, not outside them. A reading that adds Aaron's rod on top of the twelve to make thirteen contradicts both of the text's own statements. It is true that in other reckonings in this book the tribe of Levi is often handled separately (the censuses of chapters 1-4, summary layer); that institutional fact from other chapters cannot be imported to alter this chapter's own total. The two reckonings are registered side by side, and within this chapter the text's own word governs: twelve, with Aaron's inside.

(Application) The first lesson of this section is the names. God's trial does not accept anonymous comment; it accepts only rods with names written on them. Church life knows the opposite habit well: everybody says, somebody said, it is being said — volume without a name. The rule of this chapter runs the other way: whoever would stand before God to be disclosed must first write his name. A name written is a man willing to stand behind what he represents and to accept the outcome, whether the outcome discloses him or another. Before we ask God to shew his will, his way, or who should stand in a given place, the first question is not "will God shew it?" but "is my name written?" — do I dare, under my own name, to stand inside the matter I am asking about and let the result fall on me? The man who will not write his name is fit only to watch; the man who writes it has entered the trial.

(Application) The second lesson stands on Aaron's name. Aaron does not enter the row by virtue of being Levi's prince; he enters under his own name. Office may borrow the honour of a tribe's name, but a man is disclosed before God under his own name. That is a warning to everyone who stands in an office: a title is no shelter against barrenness, and a tribe's name is no substitute for life. In chapter 16 the company spoke under a banner — all the congregation are holy; in this chapter the man to be disclosed has one name. God's disclosures fall on particular persons, never on banners. Those who serve should not hide behind "our church," "our team," "our generation": write the name, stand beside your own rod, and wait for the disclosure. That posture is at once the posture of being judged and the road to being chosen.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The word for "rod" is maṭṭeh (H4294), which in the Old Testament carries both senses, rod (staff) and tribe: one maṭṭeh stands for one maṭṭeh, the object and the group sharing a single word — a fact of the original language, registered here without further allegory built upon it in the application paragraphs. The word for "name" is šēm in form; its number was not separately verified for this manuscript and is therefore given without a number. The word for "princes" is nāśîʾ in form, likewise given without a number. Only numbers verified before writing are attached; the register is in Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) One alternative reckoning is recorded without adjudication: because Levi is often counted separately elsewhere in Numbers, some readers have supposed that the twelve rods of this chapter might be the twelve tribes with Levi's added, making thirteen. The proposal belongs to the discussion layer; the base text states its total twice as twelve and says expressly that Aaron's rod "was among their rods," and the sermon text follows the base text's own statement, keeping the alternative on record only. The material of the twelve rods is likewise nowhere stated in this chapter: that Aaron's rod bore almonds has led some to infer that the rods were almond wood, but the inference runs from fruit to material, and the text itself is silent; the blank is registered in Endnote 8.

### 2. Laid Up before the Testimony: The Place of Meeting and the Criterion Fixed in Advance (Numbers 17:4-5) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) With the rods taken and the names written, the next item of the command is the place of deposit, quoted in full: "And thou shalt lay them up in the tabernacle of the congregation before the testimony, where I will meet with you." (17:4) The place is defined in three strokes. First, in the tabernacle of the congregation — not at the camp gate, not where the congregation gathers, but inside the tent. Second, before the testimony — before the ark, before the sign of God's presence. Third, the characterising clause: "where I will meet with you" — the nature of the place is meeting: God meets with them there. The twelve rods are to spend the night not in a storehouse but in the place of meeting; the venue of the trial is not the crowd's observation but the presence of God. The order also deserves registering: first the place of meeting, then the disclosure; the rods are not displayed to the people and then taken in, but taken in before God first, and only afterwards brought out to be seen (17:9). The disclosure happens where there is no audience; the audience sees only the result.

(Fact) The criterion stands in verse 5, fixed beforehand, quoted in full: "And it shall come to pass, that the man's rod, whom I shall choose, shall blossom: and I will make to cease from me the murmurings of the children of Israel, whereby they murmur against you." (17:5) Four items. First, the content of the criterion: "the man's rod, whom I shall choose, shall blossom" — one criterion only, so plain that no negotiation can attach to it: whose rod blossoms, he is the chosen. Second, the direction of the criterion: blossoming is the sign of the choosing, not its cause; God does not wait to see which rod blossoms and then decide whom to choose — first stands "whom I shall choose," then "shall blossom"; the blossom discloses the choice, it does not produce it. Third, the one-sidedness of the criterion, registered exactly: verse 5 says only that the chosen man's rod shall blossom; it does not say that the other eleven shall not. What becomes of the others is not given in the command; it appears only in the narrative of verse 8, and this sermon registers it there, on the narrative's own terms, rather than turning verse 5 into a universal assertion it does not make. Fourth, the purpose clause: "I will make to cease from me the murmurings of the children of Israel, whereby they murmur against you" — the aim of the trial is not to shame the losers but to end the murmuring. The object of the murmuring is here "against you" — Moses and Aaron — while its ceasing is "from me": the words are spoken against men, but they rise to God, and God himself undertakes to stop them on their way.

(Application) The first lesson of this section is God's order of working: the criterion before the trial, the purpose before the result. God does not wait for the blossom and then explain what it means; before the rods are even gathered he has stated both the criterion — the blossom marks the chosen — and the purpose — the ending of murmuring. Men who seek a disclosure commonly reverse the order and rewrite the criterion afterwards: if the outcome pleases us, it was God's showing; if not, the criterion did not count. This chapter's order forbids it: the criterion is God's, stated in advance; men bring the rods in, wait, and accept. Churches handling disputes and the disclosure of callings should learn the same order — let the evidence be named openly beforehand, let the purpose be named openly beforehand (to end murmuring, not to crush the other side), and only then wait for the result; when the result comes, let each stand under his own name and return to his own place. A criterion that can be revised after the fact turns every trial into a search for props for a conclusion already reached.

(Application) The second lesson is the place of meeting. Not one of the twelve rods budded by performing before the crowd; they were laid up where there was no audience, before God, overnight. The disclosure of a ministry is usually decided the same way: not in the twenty minutes in front of people, but in the night in God's presence where nobody watches. Our habit is to put everything before the crowd — our grievances, our merits, our spiritual experiences. This chapter does the opposite: gather the rods, lay them before the testimony, let them spend the night quietly before God, and bring them out for men to see on the morrow. What is alive will bud of itself; what is dead will not blossom however large the audience. Leave the disclosing to the place of meeting and the result to the morrow — that is this chapter's medicine for everyone caught in contention.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Choose" is bāḥar (H977), in verse 5 in the first person, "whom I shall choose." "Blossom" is pāraḥ (H6524), in verse 5 in its future form ("shall blossom"), in verse 8 in its perfect form ("was budded") — one word running through both the criterion and the fulfilment. "Testimony" is ʾēdûṯ (H5715); the Chinese renders it by the ark （法柜）, the KJV by "testimony," and verse 7's "tabernacle of witness" carries the same word. "Murmurings" in verse 5 is the noun təlunnôṯ (H8519), while "they murmur" in the same verse is the participle of the verb lûn (H3885) — two words of one root, the thing and the act, with different numbers, not to be interchanged; the "murmurings" of verse 10 are the same noun, H8519. "Make to cease" rests on the verb šākak (H7918), to subside, to cease. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing; the register is in Endnote 5.

### 3. On the Morrow: The Rods Given, the Rods Laid Up, and the Four Stages of the Disclosure (Numbers 17:6-8) (24:00-42:00)

(Fact) The first half of the execution stands in verses 6-7, quoted in full: "And Moses spake unto the children of Israel, and every one of their princes gave him a rod apiece, for each prince one, according to their fathers' houses, even twelve rods: and the rod of Aaron was among their rods. And Moses laid up the rods before the LORD in the tabernacle of witness." (17:6-7) Execution and command correspond item by item: Moses speaks, answering his commission in verse 1; the princes give their rods, answering the taking of verse 2; the total "even twelve rods" answers "twelve rods" of the command, with the addition that "the rod of Aaron was among their rods"; Moses lays them up before the LORD, answering the place appointed in verse 4. The text records no dispute, no delay, and no added condition at this point: as the command was spoken, so the execution runs. In chapter 16 the men who were summoned said "We will not come up"; in this chapter the men who give their rods have not one word recorded — not because the text has lost their attitude, but because the narrative's weight is not on attitudes but on procedure: the rods are all in, laid up, and the night begins.

(Fact) Verse 8 is the disclosure itself, quoted in full: "And it came to pass, that on the morrow Moses went into the tabernacle of witness; and, behold, the rod of Aaron for the house of Levi was budded, and brought forth buds, and bloomed blossoms, and yielded almonds." (17:8) The items are registered in order. First, the time: "on the morrow" — the text specifies the day and no more; between the laying up (17:7) and the inspection (17:8) one night intervenes, as the sequence of the narrative gives it, and the text neither says "in one night" in so many words nor records anything of the night itself; this sermon registers the interval as the narrative gives it and supplies no hour. Second, the discoverer and the place: Moses goes into the tabernacle of witness; the disclosure is seen first by Moses alone, still inside the tent, not yet before the people. Third, the "behold": the narrator's own marker of astonishment — the astonishment is not that a rod budded at all (the criterion had been fixed beforehand) but the degree of it. Fourth, the four stages, read as they stand, none missing: "was budded," the start of life; "brought forth buds," the bud formed; "bloomed blossoms," the flower opened; "yielded almonds," the fruit brought to ripeness. In the ordinary course of a tree these stages unfold in sequence over a long season; this verse sets all four side by side on one rod on one morning — the text's own juxtaposition, quoted here as it stands; the judgements passed upon its nature belong to the discussion layer. Fifth, the identification of the rod: "the rod of Aaron for the house of Levi" — not "the rod of Levi" alone, nor "Aaron's rod" without its house, but Aaron's name and Levi's house together, exactly matching the special writing of verse 3: Aaron's name written, Levi's house owning.

(Fact) The state of the other eleven rods must be stated with the line between what the text says and what it does not say kept bright. What the text says is two things: the eleven were laid up together with Aaron's rod (17:6-7), and in verse 9 they were brought out together and taken back by their owners (17:9). What the text does not say must also be registered: verse 8 nowhere states rod by rod that the eleven had not budded — the narrative speaks positively of Aaron's rod alone and of the other eleven says not one word. Their stillness is read from the narrative's silence together with each man's taking back his own rod unchanged in verse 9; it is the narrative's way of presenting, not a sentence pronounced in so many words. This sermon keeps the line: at the fact layer it says "the text records the four stages of Aaron's rod only, and of the other eleven not a word," and does not say "the text says the other rods did not bud." The line is not pedantry: the force of the disclosure lies precisely in the silence — eleven rods need no sentence of death pronounced over them; when men have seen one rod alive, the rest are known to be dead.

(Application) The first lesson of this section is the degree of the disclosure. Had God caused Aaron's rod to put out a single bud, the criterion would have been satisfied and the contention could have ended; instead he carried it through all four stages to ripe almonds. God does not disclose the man he has chosen by a bare pass, a minimum of evidence scraped together; the mark of his choosing is life overflowing to fruitfulness. We defend ourselves by the opposite standard — a little evidence is enough for us: a little fruit, a little change, a little approval from men. This chapter's picture corrects the instinct: the true mark of life is not barely budding but the whole course run — bud, blossom, flower, fruit. Examine a ministry, a church, a believer's years not by "is there any movement at all?" but by "has it run the four stages?" — begun, yes, but blossomed? blossomed, yes, but borne ripe fruit that others can eat and be fed by? A life that buds and never blossoms stalls halfway; a life that blossoms and never bears is for viewing only. Aaron's rod ran all four, because what was put into it was not performance but life itself.

(Application) The second lesson is the night. Once the rods were laid up, Moses did not keep vigil at the door of the tent for the result; he went in on the morrow. Waiting is part of this trial: lay the matter before God, then leave it, and let God work in the night without a hand from us, without an audience, without urging. This is the night men find hardest: the prayer is prayed and the hand still reaches in; the matter is committed and the checking never stops, as though God's work needed our supervision. Moses in this chapter gives the pattern: speak what must be spoken, give what must be given, lay up what must be laid up — and then wait for the morrow. This is not passivity but a clear division of labour: budding is a thing no man can help with at all, and when a man has done all that is his, the remaining hours belong to God. Whoever cannot wait one night has most likely still got his rod clenched in his own hand, never truly laid up before the testimony.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The four stages of verse 8 rest on these originals in order: "was budded," pāraḥ (H6524) in the perfect; "buds," the noun peraḥ (H6525) — of the same root as pāraḥ but a different word, the verb naming the process and the noun the bud itself, not to be conflated; "bloomed," the verb ṣûṣ (H6692) with its noun ṣîṣ (H6731), "blossoms"; "yielded," the verb gāmal (H1580) with the plural noun šəqēdîm, from šāqēd (H8247), "almonds." One difference between the base texts is registered here: the Chinese reads "ripe almonds" （熟杏）, where the word for ripeness has no separate counterpart in the original — it renders the sense carried by gāmal, "to bring to maturity"; the KJV reads simply "yielded almonds," with no "ripe." Each manuscript follows its own base text. The almond word šāqēd (H8247) is the same word as the almond of Jeremiah 1:11; its play on sounds with the watching word belongs to the reading and discussion layers, not to this section's fact layer. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing; the register is in Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Several judgements about verse 8 are on record, set out here without adjudication. First, the material of the rods: the chapter never says of what wood the twelve rods were made; from the fact that Aaron's rod bore almonds, some readers infer that the rods were almond branches — an inference whose premise (fruit determines material) is not stated in the text, and it belongs to the discussion layer. Second, the nature of the "four stages in one night": one reading takes it as miracle — dead wood carrying bud, blossom, and ripe fruit by morning is precisely the disclosure that the choosing is God's; another reading seeks natural accounts, such as the ready sprouting of almond cuttings in early spring or the conditions inside the tent. The botany of šāqēd (the species intended, its flowering and fruiting cycle) and the staff customs of the ancient Near East are all discussion-layer materials; the sermon registers only the text's fact: after the laying up, at the inspection on the morrow, the four stages stood together on Aaron's rod. Third, the composition of the narrative: some scholarship reads this chapter as the priestly tradition's disclosure narrative for the house of Aaron, paired with the contention narrative of chapter 16. Which account is taken belongs to the discussion layer; the sermon reads the chapter as it stands — command, criterion, execution, disclosure — without first taking it apart.

### 4. Kept for a Token: Brought Out for All to See, Brought Back for All to Remember (Numbers 17:9-11) (42:00-56:00)

(Fact) The first step after the disclosure is public display, quoted in full: "And Moses brought out all the rods from before the LORD unto all the children of Israel: and they looked, and took every man his rod." (17:9) Three items of procedure: brought out — from before the LORD to before the people; the disclosure changes venue here: by night it was disclosed before God, in the morning before Moses in the tent, and now outside before the people. Shown to all — the recipients are all the children of Israel, not the twelve princes only; the disclosure is for the whole congregation. Taken back — every man took his rod; the eleven rods return to their owners on the spot, neither confiscated nor destroyed. One blank is registered here: verse 9 says every man took his rod; by whom Aaron's rod was taken, and whether at this moment, the verse does not say; the next verse opens directly with the LORD's command to bring Aaron's rod back before the testimony. The sermon registers the blank and supplies no handover detail of its own.

(Fact) Verse 10 is the institution of the token, the LORD's words to Moses quoted in full: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Bring Aaron's rod again before the testimony, to be kept for a token against the rebels; and thou shalt quite take away their murmurings from me, that they die not." (17:10) The items in order. First, the action: "bring Aaron's rod again before the testimony" — the rod returns to the place where it budded; it goes out once to be seen, and goes back to remain. Second, its standing: "to be kept for a token" — from this point the rod has an official identity: a token. It is no longer the rod of Aaron's daily use (the text never again shows Aaron using it); it is evidence kept in store. Third, the addressees: "against the rebels" — the token is for the rebels; the text does not soften the name. Fourth, the chain of purpose, its two links read together: "thou shalt quite take away their murmurings from me" — note that the murmurings here are taken away "from me," differing from verse 5's "against you": spoken against Moses and Aaron with the mouth, they reach God and are against him in substance, and it is these that the token is to end; and "that they die not" — the end of the chain is not humiliation but life: the token stands before the testimony so that rebels need not walk the road that ends in death again. Verse 11 is the formula of execution, quoted in full: "And Moses did so: as the LORD commanded him, so did he." (17:11) Command and execution fit exactly; the matter of the rods is closed with this, the chapter's second formula of completion (the first lying in the execution of verses 6-7).

(Fact) The theme of the token must be set beside chapter 16, a chain at the text's fact layer. In chapter 16, the censers of the men who had burned were beaten into broad plates for a covering of the altar, "a sign unto the children of Israel" (16:38), and "a memorial unto the children of Israel" (16:40), so that no stranger should come near to offer incense and repeat the ruin; in this chapter, the budded rod is brought back before the testimony, "to be kept for a token against the rebels" (17:10). Read together, three facts stand. First, in both cases the relic of a contention is kept as a sign: in one, brasen plates on the altar (outside, where all could see); in the other, a living rod before the testimony (inside, where only priests might draw near). Second, both aim at ending repetition: the plates mark "none may usurp," the rod marks "the choosing stands here" — one sign draws a boundary, the other identifies a man. Third, both arise from God's command and Moses' execution; neither is a memorial men devised afterwards. A token in Numbers is not decoration; it is God's own memorandum: because men's memory cannot be trusted, the evidence is put into storage, and the object remembers on men's behalf.

(Application) The lesson of this section is the purpose of a token. Hear how God states it: not that they should look at the rod and be ashamed for ever, but "that they die not." God keeps tokens out of mercy, not out of bookkeeping. Men keep tokens of the opposite kind: we archive one another's failures and preserve the evidence of falls, to be produced at need and used to press a man down. The token of this chapter runs the other way: it is kept so that rebels need not die; the direction of its reminding is forward into life, not backward into reckoning. When we keep the record of what God has done among us, the same purpose should govern: the testimony stored up is there to leave a road of life for those who come after, not a file of charges against those who fell. A church that uses its tokens rightly will find that the man who fell reads at the token, "do not walk that road again" — not "you are that kind of man for ever."

(New Testament Reading) This rod is mentioned once more in the New Testament, registered here at the reading layer only: Hebrews 9, describing the furniture of the covenant, places within the ark "Aaron's rod that budded" together with the golden pot of manna and the tables of the covenant (Heb 9:4, KJV: "wherein was the golden pot that had manna, and Aaron's rod that budded, and the tables of the covenant"). The two accounts word the placement differently: this chapter has the rod kept "before the testimony," Hebrews has it in the ark, listed with the pot and the tables. The two statements are registered side by side; this sermon does not harmonize them and does not adjudicate. This chapter is read by its own text — "before the testimony" — while the Hebrews account is the New Testament writer's reception and restatement of the tradition, belonging to the reading layer. At that layer one thing only is said: in the New Testament's field of vision, the rod that budded in that one night still stands among the evidences of the covenant, in one list with the manna and the tables — the disclosure of Aaron's office has become, in the later writer's hand, a link in the chain of the covenant's tokens. The fact layer of this chapter gains no detail from it.

### 5. Shall We Be Consumed with Dying?: The Closing Cry of Fear (Numbers 17:12-13) (58:00-60:00)

(Fact) The last two verses of the chapter are the words of the Israelites, quoted in full: "And the children of Israel spake unto Moses, saying, Behold, we die, we perish, we all perish. Whosoever cometh any thing near unto the tabernacle of the LORD shall die: shall we be consumed with dying?" (17:12-13) The items in order. First, the threefold cry — "we die," "we perish," "we all perish" — addressed to Moses, and spoken as settled verdict, not as question: as they say it, the case is already decided. (The Chinese base text's third clause drops even the subject — "all perish!" — a small difference between the base texts, registered in the notes to this sermon.) Second, the law they have drawn up for themselves: "Whosoever cometh any thing near unto the tabernacle of the LORD shall die" — the saying has facts behind it: in chapter 16 the two hundred and fifty who came near with incense died, the congregation was commanded to get up from about the tents of the rebels, and Aaron himself entered the congregation only with a censer in his hand; the people have gathered these facts into a universal law and laid it on their own heads. Third, the close is a question: "shall we be consumed with dying?" — the chapter ends on a question, and within this chapter there is no answer: Moses does not reply, and the LORD speaks no more in the chapter. The token is set up, the rod is stored, and the people's question still hangs in the air.

(Fact) This question stands in exact contrast with the chain of purpose in verse 10, and the two are set side by side as they stand: in verse 10 God says the token's purpose is to take away the murmurings, "that they die not"; in verses 12-13 the people's first word is "we die." The same event, read by God as a token for not dying, is read by the people as a proof of dying. The difference is not in the token but in where the reader stands: the law they have drawn up gathers in only the dead of chapter 16 and leaves out the purpose clause of chapter 17; they remember "whosoever cometh near shall die" and have missed that the rod was stored precisely "that they die not." The text does not correct them within this chapter, nor record any answer of Moses — the correction unfolds only in the next chapter, where God speaks to Aaron and re-establishes the priesthood and the Levites, that the priests bear the iniquity of the sanctuary (Num 18:1, cross-reference layer). The fact layer of this chapter stops here: a chapter that opens with the word of God (17:1) ends with man's question of fear (17:13), and between the two stands one rod with its four stages complete.

(Application) The closing lesson lies in this question. Nearest to God's token, furthest from God's purpose — that is the picture at the chapter's end. The people were not without evidence: the evidence had just been carried past them and stored again. Nor were they without the stated purpose: Moses had done as he was commanded (17:11), and the purpose clause surely came with the doing. Their trouble is that fear did their reading for them. Fear reads Scripture and finds only judgement; fear reads the tokens and finds only distance. Believers today read the same way more often than we admit: God's boundaries read as God's rejection of us, God's tokens read as proofs that we do not qualify. But the chain of purpose in verse 10 is written plainly: the token's end is "that they die not." Boundaries drawn, tokens kept, offices fixed — none of it was ever meant to shut men out to perish; it was meant to leave men a way to draw near and live, through the one God himself has chosen and disclosed. Do not end this chapter hearing only the trembling of "shall we be consumed with dying?" Hear the invitation behind the question: in the very next chapter God answers, and the priesthood itself turns out to be the arrangement by which the people are borne, and need not die. The cry of fear is not the end; it is the doorway into the blessing of the office.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The closing verses are read differently in scholarship, set out here without adjudication: some read the threefold cry as sincere fear, even as the threshold of repentance — at last they know that holiness is not to be trifled with; others read it as the continuation of panic — the murmuring has ceased, but trust has not been born; the mouth no longer complains, and the heart has only fear left. The text states neither; it records their words, and this sermon leaves the readings on record. One further fact of chapter division is registered: in the Hebrew canon these two verses are 17:27-28, the close of the Hebrew chapter 17, which begins at 16:36 in the Chinese and English numbering (the command about the brasen censers) — in that division, from the censers made a sign, through the budded rod made a token, to the cry of fear, forms one complete arc within a single chapter; the Chinese and English division sets the arc across two chapters. The verse-number conversion has been registered in the head of this sermon; here only the difference in reading effect between the divisions is noted, without adjudicating which division is to be preferred. Finally: the affair of Moses striking the rock in chapter 20, and his exclusion from Canaan, has nothing to do with the rod of this chapter: this chapter's rod is Aaron's, is almond, and buds and is stored as a token; the rock and Moses' own rod in chapter 20 are another narrative, and any reading that turns this chapter's disclosure into a foreshadowing of Moses' later failure belongs to the reading layer, which the fact layer of this sermon does not adopt.

## Devotional Excerpt

Twelve rods, every man's name written upon his rod, were laid up together before the testimony for one night. On the morrow, only Aaron's rod "was budded, and brought forth buds, and bloomed blossoms, and yielded almonds" (Num 17:8). God shewed the man whom he had chosen, not by the vote of the multitude, nor by the man's own defence, but by life: a rod cut off from its tree, without root, passed in one night before the LORD through bud, blossom, and fruit. The other rods were taken back by their owners, unnamed and unshamed; the light fell on one rod, and the rest need contend no more. This rod was not kept as an idol for worship; it was brought again before the testimony, "to be kept for a token against the rebels," with the purpose written plainly: "that they die not" (17:10). A token is not a charge sheet; it is a way to live. If thy heart murmur over thy place and thy portion today, do not hasten to plead thine own cause. Lay up thy rod — thy name, thy grievance, thy portion — before the LORD for the night. What can bud needs no urging from thee; what cannot bud cannot be won by contention. Leave the shewing to God, and the ceasing of murmurings to God; thy part is to write thy name, lay down thy rod, and wait for the morrow.

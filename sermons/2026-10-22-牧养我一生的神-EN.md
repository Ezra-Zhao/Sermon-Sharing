## Summary (about 100 words)

Jacob was gravely ill, and Joseph brought Ephraim and Manasseh to see him. He sat up in bed, recalled the appearance at Luz, and adopted his two Egypt-born grandsons as his own sons, reckoned alongside Reuben and Simeon; in blessing them he deliberately crossed his hands, placing his right hand on the younger son's head, and refused to be corrected. At the end of his life, he summed everything up in one word — shepherding: God has shepherded me, and the Angel has redeemed me.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

After a person has lived one hundred and forty-seven years in the world, what will he say in his final days? Will he take stock of his household property? Will he count the number of his sons? Will he tell of the ladder at Bethel in his youth, or of the wrestling at Peniel? Genesis chapter 48 tells us that the last things Jacob wanted to do on his sickbed were few. Someone came and told Joseph: your father is ill. Joseph took his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim, and went. When Jacob heard that his son had come, he sat up in bed. For a man who had to gather all his strength just to sit up, every sentence was one he had stored up over a whole lifetime before speaking it.

In this chapter there is no great battle and no miracle crashing loudly — only an old man's hands, and one confession. Yet the more I read, the more I feel that this is the quietest and weightiest chapter in the whole book of Genesis. Young Jacob grasped with his hands: he grasped a heel, grasped a blessing, grasped the red stew, grasped flocks. Old Jacob also stretched out his hands, but he no longer grasped; he laid his hands on, he blessed, he placed the conclusion of his whole life on the heads of two children. At the very end of life, everything he had contended for all his life went unmentioned; he mentioned only one thing: who it was that had shepherded him through this life.

Let us read together the most important passages in today's Scripture:

"And Jacob said unto Joseph, God Almighty appeared unto me at Luz in the land of Canaan, and blessed me, and said unto me, Behold, I will make thee fruitful, and multiply thee, and I will make of thee a multitude of people; and will give this land to thy seed after thee for an everlasting possession. And now thy two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, which were born unto thee in the land of Egypt before I came unto thee into Egypt, are mine; as Reuben and Simeon, they shall be mine." (Genesis 48:3-5, excerpt, KJV)

"And Israel stretched out his right hand, and laid it upon Ephraim's head, who was the younger, and his left hand upon Manasseh's head, guiding his hands wittingly; for Manasseh was the firstborn." (Genesis 48:14, excerpt, KJV)

"The God which fed me all my life long unto this day, the Angel which redeemed me from all evil, bless the lads; and let my name be named on them, and the name of my fathers Abraham and Isaac; and let them grow into a multitude in the midst of the earth." (Genesis 48:15-16, excerpt, KJV)

"Moreover I have given to thee one portion above thy brethren, which I took out of the hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow." (Genesis 48:22, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, teach us through Jacob's dying words to look back over our lives. Among us, some are still young and full of drive, some have already walked more than half the road, some are ill, and some are weary. Let us see that what upholds us is not luck and not connections, but a God who shepherds a person all his life. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Old Man Sitting Up by the Bed and the Declaration of Adoption (48:1-7)

First, look at the scene. Chapter 48 opens with someone coming to report to Joseph: behold, your father is sick. Joseph immediately took his two sons and went. These two children were born in Egypt and raised in Egypt; their mother was the daughter of an Egyptian priest. Within this Hebrew family, their status had always been somewhat awkward: half of their bloodline was Egyptian, their birthplace was not in the promised land, and even their names were given in the days when Joseph was at his most exalted and most homesick. Manasseh means causing to forget; Ephraim means fruitful. By human reckoning, at most they counted as Joseph's sons and Jacob's grandsons, ranked behind their twelve uncles, unable to enter the core of the family genealogy.

But the moment Jacob opened his mouth, he did not talk about the children first; he talked about God. He said to Joseph: God Almighty appeared to me at Luz in the land of Canaan. Luz is Bethel. That was more than seventy years earlier: a young man fleeing for his life, sleeping with a stone for a pillow, dreaming of a ladder, and God standing above it saying, I am the LORD God of Abraham thy father, and the God of Isaac: the land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed (Genesis 28:13). At the end of his life, what was clearest in the old man's mind was not the fourteen years of being deceived, not the wages Laban changed ten times, not the scene of his beloved wife Rachel dying on the road, but that first appearance. Brothers and sisters, this is well worth our learning: the anchor of a person's life is not in the storms in the middle, but in how God met you at the beginning. Storms pass; the promise stands firm. Jacob's dying recollection was like taking out the title deed of his whole life: this land was not earned by me; it was promised by God; this family was not calculated into being by me; it came out of God's blessing.

Having told of the appearance, he did an astonishing thing: he declared an adoption. "And now thy two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, which were born unto thee in the land of Egypt before I came unto thee into Egypt, are mine; as Reuben and Simeon, they shall be mine." Please note the weight of this sentence. Who were Reuben and Simeon? They were Jacob's firstborn and second son, Leah's own direct line. Now the old man said that these two Egypt-born grandsons stood exactly as Reuben and Simeon. In other words, they were no longer of the grandsons' generation; they were raised to the sons' generation. They were no longer attached under Joseph's name; they were reckoned directly under Jacob's name. Joseph thereby received a double portion: originally Joseph counted as one among the twelve tribes; after the adoption, Ephraim and Manasseh each counted as a tribe, and Joseph's house received the double inheritance of the firstborn. Later, when Israel came out of Egypt and entered Canaan to divide the land, on the map there truly were the land of Ephraim and the land of Manasseh, listed alongside Reuben and Simeon — and the source of it all was this one sentence spoken beside a sickbed.

Why did Joseph's sons in particular receive this grace? The text does not state the reason outright; it only records that the old man immediately went on to mention Rachel: "As for me, when I came from Padan, Rachel died by me in the land of Canaan in the way, when yet there was but a little way to come unto Ephrath: and I buried her there in the way of Ephrath; the same is Beth-lehem." When a dying man speaks of the person he loved most, he pauses. Joseph was Rachel's firstborn, and in adopting Joseph's two sons, in the old man's heart there may also have been a memorial to Rachel and a response to Joseph's many years of filial devotion. Here the text records only the facts, not the motives, and this manuscript likewise speaks only to the facts: the adoption truly happened, and the motives are left to the silence of the text. But one thing is certain: the adoption crossed the boundary of bloodline. By human calculation, born in Egypt, a foreign mother, and of the grandsons' generation — three counts of disqualification; by the order God set through the old man's mouth, they entered the standing of the firstborn. The Bible repeats this theme again and again afterward: God's acceptance of people does not rest entirely on where you come from. Genesis itself had already planted the foreshadowing: the one who receives the firstborn's standing is often not the one who ranks first in human eyes.

Jacob also added an arrangement: the sons Joseph would father afterward in Egypt would be Joseph's own and would not be reckoned under the names of their brothers. This sentence drew the boundary clearly: the adoption came from God's purpose and the old man's declaration; it was not an unlimited expansion. Grace has order, and declaration has boundaries. This is precisely the theological proportion of the adoption in this chapter: God's sovereignty decides who is reckoned under His name, and in this chapter that sovereignty is shown only in these two children; we preach according to the text and do not push one step further.

There is another detail in this passage that is easy to miss: Jacob said all this after sitting up in bed. A sick man, hearing that his son had come, gathered his strength and sat up. The Epistle to the Hebrews later says: by faith Jacob, when he was a dying, blessed both the sons of Joseph; and worshipped, leaning upon the top of his staff (Hebrews 11:21). Leaning on his staff, sitting up, worshipping, blessing: this is the posture of a person spending his last strength on the last thing that matters. Those among us who are grandparents and parents: when you are old and have the least strength left, what do you most want to leave to your children and grandchildren? Jacob's answer to us is this: the first word is not about dividing the family property; the first word is a testimony of how God has dealt graciously with me. Property, once divided, scatters; a testimony, once spoken, can nourish the next generation for a lifetime.

There is also a contrast the text places very quietly, yet it pierces the heart. The aged Jacob thought of Rachel, and his words stopped halfway: the place where he buried her is called Ephrath, which is Bethlehem. Why, at the very moment of adopting his grandsons, mention his late wife's grave? Because the origin of Joseph's line had always carried a wound: a mother who died early, a son who was sold, many years without any news at all. In adopting Ephraim and Manasseh, Jacob declared, as it were, before the whole family: this line has not only not been cut off — it is to receive a double portion; what was born of Rachel has not only not been swallowed by fate — it is to stand the firmest in this family. Brothers and sisters, God's way of restoring people is often not to wipe the wound away and pretend it never happened, but to grow double fruit in the very place where the wound was. If in your family there is a past that hurts whenever it is mentioned, do not hurry to hide it away. Bring it before God; in that same place He can write a sequel you never imagined. Jacob's tears were not shed in vain, and his weeping at Rachel's grave was not forgotten by God. The moment the words of adoption were spoken, the tears of decades past began to receive their echo.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Crossed Hands — Blessing Does Not Follow Human Ranking (48:8-20)

After the declaration of adoption comes the climax of the whole chapter. Jacob's eyes were dim with age, so that he could not see. He asked: who are these? Joseph said: they are my sons, whom God has given me here. The old man said: bring them, I pray thee, unto me, and I will bless them. Joseph brought the two children to his father's knees; the old man embraced them and kissed them, and spoke a sentence that aches the heart: "I had not thought to see thy face: and, lo, God hath shewed me also thy seed." In the old man's heart, Joseph himself was a person who had once died: on the day the bloodied coat was brought back, Jacob believed his beloved son had been torn apart by a wild beast, and he mourned for twenty-two years. Now not only was his son alive and standing before him, but his grandsons were also in his arms. Joseph's name in Hebrew carries the sense of increase, and what the old man tasted in this moment was precisely God's double increase.

Then comes the most famous action of the whole chapter. Joseph led his two sons forward and arranged their positions very clearly: with his right hand he led Ephraim, standing at his own left, facing his father's right hand; with his left hand he led Manasseh, standing at his own right, facing his father's left hand. According to the rule, the right hand is the hand of honor, given to the firstborn; the firstborn Manasseh facing the left hand and the younger Ephraim facing the right hand — everything was in perfect order. Who would have known that Israel would stretch out his right hand and lay it on Ephraim's head, and cross over his left hand to lay it on Manasseh's head? The KJV says Israel guided his hands "wittingly" (48:14) — a deliberate crossing, not an accident. The original is sikel yadav (שִׂכֵּל אֶת־יָדָיו, H7919), meaning he crossed his hands intentionally and wisely [1]. This was not an old man's hand trembling and landing in the wrong place; it was a clear-headed, deliberate act, reaching his right hand across his left to rest it on the younger son's head.

Joseph saw it, and it displeased him. He thought his father had made a mistake — advanced in years, eyes dim, and his hands had mistaken the children. He lifted up his father's right hand to move it onto Manasseh's head, correcting him as he did so: not so, my father: for this is the firstborn; put thy right hand upon his head. Joseph's heart here we can fully sympathize with: he was a filial son, afraid his father would leave behind a blessing with its places reversed at the end of his life; and he was a prime minister, a man who all his life prized order and procedure, who had managed even Pharaoh's storehouses in perfect arrangement. But this one time, the old father refused him. He said: "I know it, my son, I know it." Two "I know it"s lay bare the whole chapter's question of sight: Joseph thought his father did not know, precisely because his eyes were dim; the father said, I see more clearly than you. Manasseh also shall become a people, and he also shall be great: but truly his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his seed shall become a multitude of nations. When the words were finished, the hands were not withdrawn, and the blessing went out in the crossed position: Ephraim was set before Manasseh.

Brothers and sisters, please read this scene together with the earlier stories of Genesis. In Abraham's house, Ishmael was the firstborn, but the one who received the blessing was Isaac; in Isaac's house, Esau was the firstborn, but the one who received the firstborn's blessing was Jacob; and Jacob himself, back then, had stretched out his hands to receive, while his father's eyes were dim, the blessing that belonged to the firstborn. Now it was his turn to be the grandfather; his eyes were dim too, but his hands were clearer than anyone's. Again and again in Genesis God does the same thing: the order He establishes does not follow human ranking. It is not that birth order does not matter; it is that before God's choosing, human ranking is not the final court. Later the tribe of Ephraim indeed became so great that Joshua came from Ephraim, the tabernacle at Shiloh stood in Ephraim's territory, and in the prophetic books the northern kingdom of Israel is often simply called Ephraim. Nor was Manasseh shortchanged: of all the tribes his inheritance was the largest, with land on both the east and west banks. Crossed hands do not abolish the firstborn; they declare: both are blessed, but the order is set by God, not by rules men have laid down.

Here we must also guard against a misreading: the Bible is not teaching us to overturn rules on purpose just to be different. Jacob crossed his hands not out of willfulness, but because he had received God's way of seeing, and when he refused the correction he gave a clear prophetic explanation: the younger would become greater than the elder, by God's arrangement. Without sight given by God, crossing is just disorder; with God's order, clinging to human ranking becomes the hindrance. The sight we ask God for is not a wish to be different from everyone else; it is the courage, when everyone says impossible, not eligible, not his turn, to see that God has already laid His hand on that person's head. In the church today, and in families, how many people have been sentenced in advance by one sentence — he doesn't rank, her background is complicated, this child will amount to nothing? Jacob's crossed hands remind us: the one who ranks last in your eyes may be the one God means to use greatly. The old man with dim eyes saw farther than anyone, because what he looked at was not a résumé, but where God's hand was going.

The words of the blessing themselves are worth hearing carefully: "God, before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac did walk, the God which fed me all my life long unto this day, the Angel which redeemed me from all evil, bless the lads; and let my name be named on them, and the name of my fathers Abraham and Isaac; and let them grow into a multitude in the midst of the earth." In the wording of verses 15-16, the God who fed Jacob all his life long and the Angel who redeemed him from all evil stand side by side as the givers of the blessing. The word for blessing in this chapter, berakhah (בְּרָכָה, H1293), appears three times: the blessing at Luz, the blessing pronounced over the two boys, and Ephraim blessed above Manasseh [2]. Blessing in this chapter is no courtesy phrase; it is the old man taking everything God had given him in his lifetime and passing it on, hand to head, to the next generation. The most precious thing a person can leave his descendants in this life is not an inventory of assets, but a blessing with roots and grounds: the God I served, the God I experienced personally — may He treat you as He treated me.

Pause again and look at Joseph's lesson in this matter. Joseph was the person in the whole chapter most qualified to talk about order: he had managed a prison, managed storehouses, managed the famine policy of all Egypt; without his nod, no one in the whole country could buy grain. In a man who had carved order into his bones, at his father's bedside he met a matter that broke all order. His first reaction was to correct it, and his correction was entirely reasonable: the firstborn is the firstborn; the hand is in the wrong place, just move it. But the old man's one sentence, "I know it," gently set all his reasonableness down. There was no rebuke in it, only a lesson: some things, human regulations, calculated ever so clearly, cannot calculate the mind of God. Joseph did not argue further; he let his father finish speaking, finish laying on hands, finish blessing. The last lesson the prime minister learned at his father's bedside was the lesson of closing his mouth and withdrawing his hand. For those of us who have served in the church a long time, this is also the hardest lesson to learn: when God's hand rests on someone we did not think well of, will we reach out to straighten His hand, or withdraw our own hand, stand aside, and watch Him accomplish it? Joseph withdrew his hand. The greatness of Ephraim is reckoned from the moment Joseph withdrew his hand.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The God Who Has Shepherded Me All My Life — A Dying Confession in Outline (48:15-16, 21-22)

Now let us draw the camera in close on that confession of the old man's. This is the heart of the whole chapter, and the most moving summary of faith in the entire book of Genesis: "the God which fed me all my life long unto this day" (KJV) The original is ro'i (רֹעִי, H7462), the One who shepherds me, who is my shepherd [3]. Jacob did not sum up by saying, God is the God of my victories, or the God of my prosperity, or even, God is the God I met at Bethel. He used a shepherd's word and said that God had shepherded him. Why shepherding? Because Jacob himself was a shepherd; he knew the weight of this word. A shepherd is not a man who stands at a distance giving orders: where the sheep go, he follows; when a sheep strays, he goes to find it; when the nights are cold, he keeps watch; when a wild beast comes, he stands in front. Jacob looked back over his life: fleeing at twenty, God followed him to Bethel; in middle age, oppressed by his father-in-law in Haran, God defended him in secret; on the road home, afraid to meet Esau, God wrestled with him at Peniel and blessed him; in old age, bereavement, the pain of a lost son, famine and migration — God never walked away for a single step. In his youth at Bethel he had set conditions for God: if God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat, and raiment to put on, then shall the LORD be my God. Decades passed, and the dying man set no more conditions; there remained only a statement: He has shepherded me all my life. A conditional sentence had become a sentence of thanksgiving — this is what the maturity of a person's faith looks like.

Immediately after comes the second sentence: "The Angel who has redeemed me from all evil." hammale'akh haggo'el (הַמַּלְאָךְ הַגֹּאֵל, H4397 and H1350), the Angel who redeems me [4]. go'el is the word for a redeemer, a kinsman-redeemer, the one in the Law who redeems land and redeems persons on behalf of his kin. Jacob summed up his whole life in two things: being shepherded, and being redeemed. Shepherding speaks of the daily: led day by day; redemption speaks of the dangers: pulled back out of trouble again and again. Two words are enough for a lifetime. The Psalms later sang this confession into song: The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want (Psalm 23:1); and the psalmist prayed, Give ear, O Shepherd of Israel, thou that leadest Joseph like a flock (Psalm 80:1). Jacob was the first of the patriarchs to call God a shepherd, and that name traveled out from a sickbed, was sung for three thousand years, and arrived at the Lord Jesus saying with His own mouth: I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep. Brothers and sisters, if your whole life could be summed up in only two words, which two would you choose? Success and failure? Triumph and regret? Jacob's answer stands before us: shepherded all my life, redeemed all my life. This is not because his temperament was fine; it is because God truly never loosened His hand for one day.

Going one layer deeper, the word shepherding corrects a common misunderstanding we have of God. We easily picture God as a judge on the bench, or a referee at the awards platform: do well, and He nods; do badly, and He shakes His head. Jacob did plenty of wrong in his life: he deceived his father, cheated his brother, matched wits with his father-in-law, and played favorites among his children. Yet in his dying summary, the image of God is neither judge nor referee, but shepherd. The relation between a shepherd and his sheep is not reckoned by performance scores, but by belonging and responsibility: the sheep is mine, so I will care for it to the end. When it strays, I pull it back; when it is hurt, I carry it in my arms; when it is old and can barely walk, I walk slower and accompany it to the finish. Brothers and sisters, if today you are far from God and thinking, would He still want someone like me, please hear Jacob's words: the ones He shepherds were never the best-performing sheep; they are the sheep that belong to Him. When you come back before Him, you do not first have to repair yourself into acceptability; the Shepherd's hand reaches out first — and this is exactly what the word redemption means: He pays the price, buys back the lost one, and reckons it under His own name, with the same heart that adopted Ephraim and Manasseh.

At the end of the chapter, the old man spoke of one last concrete matter: "Moreover I have given to thee one portion above thy brethren, which I took out of the hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow." shekhem echad (שְׁכֶם אַחַד, H7926), one portion, one tract of land [5]. Shechem, in the original, is precisely Shekhem, and the wordplay is honestly noted here: it is both the place name Shechem and can mean shoulder or ridge, extended to mean one portion of inheritance. The old man gave that tract of hill country at Shechem to Joseph alone, above his brethren. This was no empty word. Later, when Israel came out of Egypt, Moses, following Joseph's charge, took Joseph's bones up with him (Exodus 13:19); and after they entered Canaan, Joseph's bones were buried at Shechem, in the very parcel of ground Jacob had once bought, and which here he gave to Joseph with his own mouth (Joshua 24:32). A place name spoken beside a sickbed became, more than four hundred years later, the address where bones came home to rest. The old man had not yet seen the exodus; even his grandchildren had not yet come out of Egypt; yet trusting God's promise, he divided the land first. This is the sight of faith: the people have not yet arrived, but the land is named first; the road has not yet been walked, but the homecoming is declared first. The farthest-reaching thing we can leave our children while we live is not money, but this kind of certainty: God's promise will travel the whole road, and our family will at last return to the land He has given.

Notice also the manner in which the old man spoke his prophecy. When he said these words, Canaan was still in the hands of the Amorites; his descendants were still sojourning in Egypt, and no one knew how many years they would stay. Yet as he divided the land, his tone was as calm as if he were dividing his own courtyard: this piece to Joseph, that piece above his brethren. Faith does not wait until everything has been settled before it speaks; faith, while nothing has yet been settled, fixes its words first according to God's promise. Later, history came up step by step behind it: the exodus, the crossing of the Red Sea, the entry into Canaan, the division of the inheritances — all of it honored exactly as spoken at the sickbed, down to Joseph's bones returning without the slightest deviation to Shechem. When we pray for our children today and bless our families, we should have this same calm certainty: we need not wait until we see the outcome before daring to speak; we speak first according to the promise, bless first, and place our children first within God's order. God's word walks ahead of history, and our blessing, if it comes from His word, will also walk ahead of our children and open the road for them.

Finally, the application; I will speak to only three kinds of people. The first kind: people who measure themselves by ranking. You always feel you do not rank: your education does not rank, your seniority does not rank, your origin does not rank, and even at home you feel your parents' hearts are with another child. Today the crossed hands are laid on your head: God's blessing does not follow human ranking. The hand He laid on Ephraim's head was not a sympathy vote; it was His sovereign choosing. You do not need first to squeeze into the front row before you are worthy to be blessed.

The second kind: people who are correcting God, like Joseph. With good intentions we often hold God's hand steady for Him: Lord, surely not him — his qualifications are too shallow; Lord, give it to me first — I am the firstborn; the rules say so. Joseph's correction came from filial devotion, and it was still refused. In prayer we may lay out all our reasoning, but at the end we must learn to set our hands down and say: my knowing is not enough, but You know. God's order is sometimes the opposite of all our experience, and obedience means admitting that His unseen hand is more accurate than the hand of ours that tries to steady it.

The third kind: the elderly, and parents. Learn from Jacob the order of a life's end: first speak of God's appearing, then of where the children belong, and last of the promised land. While your eyes can still see and your hands can still rest steadily, speak the words of blessing aloud to your children yourself. Do not let your children read your arrangements only in a will; let them hear you say while you live: the God who shepherded your forebears will also shepherd you. A blessing like that, spoken once, is remembered by a child for a lifetime.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have stood at Jacob's sickbed and seen three things. First, adoption: two children born in Egypt were reckoned as Jacob's sons and entered the standing of the firstborn — God's acceptance of people crosses the boundary of bloodline. Second, the crossing: the blessing deliberately did not follow ranking; the right hand rested on the younger son's head, and the old man said, I know it, my son, I know it — God's sight is higher than human order. Third, the confession: one hundred and forty-seven years of life summed up at the end in only two words: shepherding, redemption. The old man with dim eyes saw farther than anyone; he had not seen the exodus, yet he fixed the land at Shechem first, because he believed that the God who shepherded him would walk the whole road to its end.

Let us pray together: Lord who shepherds people, thank You. From our birth to this day, You have not loosened Your hand for one day; when we fell into trouble, You redeemed us again and again. Give us the sight of crossed hands, so that we do not look at people by ranking, nor look at ourselves by ranking; give us the peace of that dying confession, so that when we look back over our lives we can honestly say: it was You who shepherded me. As You shepherded Jacob, shepherd us and our children, until You lead us back to the land You have promised. In the name of the good Shepherd, the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

This note states the lexical and distributional facts.

[3] ro'i: Hebrew רֹעִי, from the verb ra'ah (רָעָה, H7462), to pasture, to shepherd, to be a shepherd; Genesis 48:15, Jacob says that God "fed me all my life long" (KJV: "the God which fed me all my life long unto this day"; the underlying Hebrew verb is the shepherd word ra'ah). This is the first instance in Scripture of a patriarch calling God his shepherd, later taken up by Psalm 23:1 and Psalm 80:1; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; "that name traveled out from a sickbed and was sung for three thousand years" is homiletical language, not a lexical fact, and is marked separately as such.

[4] hammale'akh haggo'el: Hebrew הַמַּלְאָךְ הַגֹּאֵל, Genesis 48:16, "the Angel which redeemed me from all evil"; male'akh (מַלְאָךְ, H4397) is messenger, angel; go'el is the participle of the verb ga'al (גָּאַל, H1350), redeemer, kinsman-redeemer; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Note: the relation of "the Angel" in this chapter to God, and whether its identity relates to discussions of the Angel of the LORD, a second person, and similar questions, is a matter of continuing scholarly discussion in the commentaries (both Wenham and Hamilton treat it at length); this manuscript gives only a brief note and reaches no conclusion. The main text's placing of the God who shepherds and the Angel who redeems side by side as givers of blessing merely restates the grammar of 48:15-16 and the rendering of the KJV, and draws no further doctrinal inference.

[5] shekhem echad: Hebrew שְׁכֶם אַחַד (H7926), Genesis 48:22, rendered in the KJV as "one portion above thy brethren," explainable as one portion, one tract of hill country; Shekhem is originally the place name Shechem, and its root also carries the sense of shoulder or ridge, so that "one portion / one tract" and the place name "Shechem" form a wordplay, as commentators across the generations have often pointed out; this manuscript honestly notes it as a possible wordplay and builds no further exposition on it. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Further, how Genesis 48:22's "which I took out of the hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow" relates narratively to the events of Simeon and Levi at Shechem in Genesis chapter 34 is a matter on which the commentaries differ; this manuscript quotes only the wording of the verse and draws no conclusion.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "The God which fed me all my life long unto this day, the Angel which redeemed me from all evil, bless the lads." (Genesis 48:15-16, KJV)

Jacob lived one hundred and forty-seven years. He had deceived, fled, wept, and buried his loved ones; yet at the end he did not sum up his life with victory, nor with success, but with one word: shepherding. To be shepherded is to be led day by day; to be redeemed is to be pulled out of trouble again and again. When you look back on your own road today, there may be many hardships you cannot put into words, and many grievances of not ranking and never having your turn. But notice those crossed hands: God's blessing does not follow human ranking. His right hand passes over human rules and rests on the head you never expected — and on your head. Stop measuring your life by someone else's rank. Learn Jacob's shortest confession today: from my birth to this day, You have shepherded me. Whoever can say that need not fear the road that remains, for the Shepherd is still here, and He will shepherd you to the very end of the journey. Before you leave home this morning, why not lay your hand on your own chest, the way the old man crossed his hands, and say this confession softly once, then think of that person in your family whom you feel does not rank and never gets a turn, and speak a word of blessing for him. As the God who shepherds us passed over ranking to lay His hand on our heads, so we too should hand blessing first to the one least thought well of. The moment the blessing is handed over, you will find that the Shepherd's heart has already begun its work within you.

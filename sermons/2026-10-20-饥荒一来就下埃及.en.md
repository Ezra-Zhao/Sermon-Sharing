## Executive Summary (Approx. 100 words)

Abram had just built two altars in Canaan when a severe famine struck the land, and he went down to sojourn in Egypt. Approaching Egypt's border, fear overwhelmed faith; he instructed his wife Sarai to claim she was his sister to save his life and gain advantage, but Sarai was taken into Pharaoh’s palace. The LORD plagued Pharaoh, who in turn rebuked Abram and commanded his men to send them away. This message examines three things: famine tests not whether an altar exists, but whether the words spoken beside the altar still hold true; fear drives the called back into the world to trade lies for security; when man cannot keep the covenant, God Himself keeps it—restraining Pharaoh and escorting Abram’s household out of Egypt intact.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Introduction · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace to all brothers and sisters!

The conclusion of the previous message presented a beautiful picture: Abram built an altar at Bethel, called on the name of the LORD, and then journeyed on toward the South (the Negev). The fire on the altar was burning, the man’s steps were moving forward, and it seemed the story should continue smoothly along this wonderful path.

Yet turning to the second half of chapter 12 today, the very opening sentence pours cold water on us: "Now there was a famine in the land." That very land where altars were built suffered famine; the one who called on the name of the Lord had to face days without food. Scripture does not immediately grant Abram an abundant harvest after building the altars; what He gives him is a famine. Brothers and sisters, this message is not easy to preach, because it speaks of how a called person grows weak, slides backward, lies, and loses face—speaking not of an outsider, but of Abram himself, who had just built those altars. Yet this message must be preached, because every one of us has our own Egypt.

Let us first read the key passages of Scripture:

"Now there was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to dwell there, for the famine was severe in the land." (Gen. 12:10)

"And it came to pass, when he was close to entering Egypt, that he said to Sarai his wife, 'Indeed I know that you are a woman of beautiful countenance. Therefore it will happen, when the Egyptians see you, that they will say, "This is his wife"; and they will kill me, but they will let you live. Please say you are my sister, that it may be well with me for your sake, and that I may live because of you.'" (Gen. 12:11–13, excerpted)

"But the LORD plagued Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sarai, Abram's wife." (Gen. 12:17)

"And Pharaoh called Abram and said, 'What is this you have done to me? Why did you not tell me that she was your wife? Why did you say, "She is my sister"? I might have taken her as my wife. Now therefore, here is your wife; take her and go your way.'" (Gen. 12:18–19)

"So Pharaoh commanded his men concerning him; and they sent him away, with his wife and all that he had." (Gen. 12:20)

Today we will examine this in three sections:

First, 12:10, The famine was severe, so he went down to Egypt—trials often come after building an altar, and the intention to sojourn temporarily is most liable to become the beginning of a downward slide;
Second, 12:11–16, Please say you are my sister—how fear squeezes out faith step by step, and how a man trades lies and his family for his own safety;
Third, 12:17–20, The LORD brings plagues, and Pharaoh sends them away—the covenant Abram could not keep, God comes to keep Himself; being sent away in disgrace is still grace.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. You recorded this episode of Abram's weakness in the Scriptures without concealing it for him, nor did You cast him off because of it. We pray that You would illuminate us today through this passage: help us see clearly our own Egypt, the words we have spoken out of fear, and the compromises we have made in exchange for temporary peace. We also thank You that Your covenant-keeping faithfulness is greater than our weakness; You are able to restrain Pharaoh, and You are able to bring us back. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: The Famine Was Severe, So He Went Down to Egypt (12:10)

### 1.1 Why Does Famine Come Precisely After Building Altars?

Verse 10 consists of just one sentence, yet it serves as the hinge for the entire passage: "Now there was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to dwell there, for the famine was severe in the land."

"Famine" in Hebrew is *ra'av* (H7458, famine, hunger) [1]. Notice the wording of Scripture: it was not that Abram took a wrong turn and therefore met with famine; rather, "the land" suffered famine—the very land God told him to go to, the very land where he built altars. The Promised Land can also experience famine. Brothers and sisters, we must first get this concept straight: obedience does not equal smooth sailing from then on, and being called does not grant immunity from trials. The faith of many people shatters right here: assuming that once they believe in the Lord and obey Him, life should proceed smoothly throughout; as soon as famine strikes, they doubt whether that initial step was a mistake.

The words of Psalms fit perfectly here: "Many are the afflictions of the righteous, But the LORD delivers him out of them all" (Ps. 34:19, excerpted). What Scripture promises is deliverance, not exemption. Abram's famine was not evidence that God had broken His covenant; it was a lesson through which his faith was to go deeper. Later, when Isaac faced a famine, God specifically restrained him: "Do not go down to Egypt" (Gen. 26:2, excerpted); Jacob’s family also later went down to Egypt because of a famine (Gen. 42–46). You see, along the very same path, God's instructions can differ for different people and different seasons—the crux is never whether one may go to Egypt, but whether, upon going, the heart still acknowledges God as Lord.

### 1.2 "Going Down to Egypt": A Directional Term Hiding a Spiritual Slope

Scripture says Abram "went down" to Egypt. This "going down" is first of all geographical: descending from the Canaanite highlands to the Nile River plain. But as you read through the entire Bible, you discover that "going down to Egypt" gradually becomes a spiritual directional term—leaving the Promised Land and returning to the old path of living by relying on the world. Egypt repeatedly represents in Scripture that entire system where one can survive without trusting God—relying instead on granaries, brute force, and human maneuvering. Throughout the entire Book of Exodus, God exerted such great effort to bring His people out of Egypt precisely to sever this path of turning back; in the prophetic books, Isaiah repeatedly warned: "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help" (Isa. 31:1, excerpted).

Regarding Abram's descent to Egypt this time, Scripture does not explicitly say that God rebuked him for going—seeking temporary shelter in a place with food during years of famine is in itself common sense for survival; in the Book of Ruth, Elimelech’s family also went to the country of Moab because of a famine (cf. Ruth 1:1). Where the real problem lay was not that his feet walked toward Egypt, but that his heart collapsed first at the border of Egypt. Therefore, when reading this passage, do not reduce it to a simplistic true-or-false question: going to Egypt to escape famine was not necessarily wrong; what was wrong was the entire sequence that followed once fear took over.

"To dwell," Hebrew *gur* (H1481, to sojourn, dwell temporarily) [2]—Abram’s intention is stated very clearly: to "dwell" there—temporarily, to ride out the storm and then return. Brothers and sisters, backsliding almost never appears in the guise of "I want to leave God." It always appears in the guise of "just for a little while": compromise just for a moment, use the world's methods just for a while, and return to the altar once the hardship has passed. Sojourning is not settling down—that is true. But beware: during the days of your sojourn, whose rules you live by is the real question. If a person sojourns in Egypt while their heart remains by the altar in Canaan, they will return when the famine has passed; but if the heart first makes its home in Egypt, it is hard for the feet to return.

Warren W. Wiersbe, in his commentary on Genesis, reminds readers when explaining Abram's descent into Egypt that famine is a test of faith; leaving the land of promise to rely on Egypt's resources reveals not a problem with the environment, but that the object of trust has quietly shifted. (cf. Warren W. Wiersbe, *The Bible Exposition Commentary: Genesis*)

Let us conclude the first part with an application: What is your famine? Is it an interrupted income, a failing body, or a door waited on for years that never opens? Famine itself is not shameful; it is the examination room for this lesson. Do not rush to ask, "Did I take a wrong turn?" First ask, "In the famine, whom am I prepared to live by?"—to rely on God and patiently endure in Canaan, or to go down to Egypt and use the old ways for a quick fix? This question is the doorway to the entire sermon today.

### 1.3 The Nile and the Jordan: The Waters of Two Ways of Living

Looking a layer deeper into the geography makes this lesson even clearer. The Egyptian way of life depended on the Nile: the waters flooded every year, the granaries had grain stored every year, and there was little need to look up to heaven. The Canaanite way of life was different; Deuteronomy 11 states it plainly: that land is "...not like the land of Egypt from which you have come, where you sowed your seed and watered it by foot, as a vegetable garden; but the land which you cross over to possess is a land of hills and valleys, which drinks water from the rain of heaven, a land for which the Lord your God cares..." (Deut. 11:10–12, excerpt). One depends on the river, the other on the rain; one depends on a human system striving to conquer nature, the other on lifting one's head to await God’s grace. God brought Abram all the way from Ur and Haran to Canaan precisely to teach him this very thing: change to a different way of living—live by promise, not by the granary.

So when famine came to Canaan, what was tested was not Abram's survival skills, but whether he was willing, when there was no rain and no food, still to remain in the land that depends on rain, acknowledging God as his provider. Isaiah later framed this as comfort: "Everyone who thirsts, / Come to the waters; / And you who have no money, / Come..." (Isa. 55:1, excerpt of general meaning). God's water and Egypt's water have different sources.

To sum up the first part in one sentence: **Famine is not God breaking His promise; it is the testing ground for faith to change its way of living. The greatest danger in going down to Egypt is not those physical steps, but that a heart intent on "sojourning" gradually begins to live by Egypt's rules, trading a life of relying on promise for a life of relying on the granary.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Please Say You Are My Sister (12:11-16)

### 2.1 Drawing Near to Egypt, the Words Change First (12:11-13)

In verse 11, there is a time marker we must catch: "when he was close to entering Egypt"—before the person even entered Egypt, the words had already changed.

Abram said to Sarai: "Indeed I know that you are a woman of beautiful countenance. Therefore it will happen, when the Egyptians see you, that they will say, 'This is his wife'; and they will kill me, but they will let you live. Please say you are my sister, that it may be well with me for your sake, and that I may live because of you."

Please read this passage slowly; it is full of "me" and "my": kill me, well with me, I may live. What place does Sarai occupy in this plan? A shield. Trading the wife's danger for the husband's safety. These words came from the mouth of a man who had just called on the name of the Lord at Bethel—the altar was still standing on the mountain, yet the tone of his speech had already changed. Brothers and sisters, this is what the sound of draining faith is like: not an open denial of God, but having the center of speech and action reduced to nothing other than "how do I protect myself."

To be honest, half of this lie was true: Sarai was indeed Abram's half-sister (cf. Gen. 20:12). Half-truths are the most dangerous, because the speaker can comfort himself with the half that is true. Later in Gerar, Abram used the very same line with Abimelech (Gen. 20)—you see, a tactic that succeeds once becomes a lifelong pattern. Methods prompted by fear are remembered for a lifetime; paths walked by faith, on the other hand, are easily forgotten.

"Beautiful," rendered "beautiful countenance" in the Chinese Union Version, is in the original Hebrew *yaphat mar'eh* (beautiful appearance), from the adjective *yapheh* (H3303, beautiful) [3]; "sister," *achot* (H269, sister) [4]. Sarai was by this time well along in years, yet she is still recorded in Scripture as being of beautiful countenance. This also shows that Abram's fear was not entirely unfounded given the customs of that era—pharaohs and kings forcibly taking beautiful women into their harems was a real danger in that world. It is precisely because the danger was real that this lesson must be clearly explained: danger being real does not mean a lie is right; fear having a basis does not give fear the right to take over.

Proverbs 29:25 is worth ten thousand words: "The fear of man brings a snare, / But whoever trusts in the Lord shall be safe." (excerpt of general meaning). Abram feared the Egyptians, and so fell into the snare woven by his own words. If he had first built an altar on the border, if he had first called upon the name of the Lord, this chapter might have been written entirely differently. Sadly, in the latter half of Genesis 12, there was no altar beside Egypt—the altars were all left behind in Canaan. The further a person is from the altar, the more their speech sounds like the world.

### 2.2 The Egyptians Saw, Pharaoh Was Commended, She Was Taken into the Palace (12:14-15)

Things unfolded exactly in the direction Abram feared, and concluded in a way he had never anticipated:

"So it was, when Abram came into Egypt, that the Egyptians saw the woman, that she was very beautiful. The princes of Pharaoh also saw her and commended her to Pharaoh. And the woman was taken to Pharaoh’s house." (Gen. 12:14–15, excerpt of general meaning)

"Was taken"—these words read lightly, but they fell upon Sarai with immense weight. The husband's plan succeeded halfway: no one killed him; but it also failed halfway: his wife entered another man's palace. Brothers and sisters, plans born of fear are always like this: they preserve the one thing you are most afraid of losing, and then take away the thing you failed to factor in. What Abram calculated was his life; what he failed to calculate was his wife, his marriage, and the promise. Do not forget that the promise said offspring would come from them; with Sarai entering Pharaoh's palace, humanly speaking, the line of promise was nearly severed in Egypt.

Let us ignite this truth here.

Brothers and sisters, among us today, who does not have their own "Please say you are my sister"? At work, knowing full well that an account is wrong, to protect your position, you first clear yourself of blame; at home, knowing full well you should admit fault, to save face, you first shift the blame onto your spouse; before God, having clearly heard His calling, to preserve your comfort, you first utter a half-truth to cover yourself. When fear speaks, every sentence sounds reasonable, every word seems to be for your good; yet in the end, it always uses the people dearest to you and the promises most vital to pay the bill. Today, not tomorrow, search out that excuse you repeatedly use: what have you preserved by it, and what have you forfeited for it? While Sarai has not yet entered the palace, while the words have not yet left your mouth, return to the altar—first call upon the name of the Lord, and only then speak.

### 2.3 Pharaoh Treated Abram Well: Profiting from a Lie Is the Deepest Trap (12:16)

Verse 16 is the most distressing: "He treated Abram well for her sake. He had sheep, oxen, male donkeys, male and female servants, female donkeys, and camels." (Gen. 12:16, excerpt of general meaning)

The lie actually yielded a profit. He not only survived; he grew rich. Here we must settle the spiritual accounts clearly: gaining profit is not the same as being blessed; making things work is not the same as doing what is right. In this world there is a kind of "success" that is merely the result of God allowing man to go his own way—it is not God's seal of approval. When Abram stood in Egypt with his herds and flocks, an outsider would have said he had struck it rich, but the readers of Scripture break into a cold sweat on his behalf. Among these livestock, there would later be an Egyptian maidservant named Hagar (cf. Gen. 16:1); the bill for this trip to Egypt would have to be paid back slowly over the next several chapters.

To sum up Part Two in a single sentence: **The moment fear takes over, man resorts to half-truths to save his own skin, makes the one closest to him foot the bill, and may even gain a momentary profit; profit is no evidence of blessing, and for the wrong road that seemed to work, the bill must sooner or later be paid.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The LORD Plagues, Pharaoh Sends Away (12:17-20)

### 3.1 Where Abram Is Silent, God Himself Steps in to Act (12:17)

Throughout the entire narrative, Abram utters not a single word while in Egypt. It is not he who speaks up for his wife; it is God:

"But the LORD plagued Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sarai, Abram's wife." (Gen. 12:17)

Notice the title Scripture uses here: she is not called "his sister," but "Sarai, Abram's wife." What man renames, God does not recognize. The wife Abram dared not claim, God claims Himself; the marital boundary Abram could not guard, God comes to guard Himself. "Plagued with great plagues"—in the original language, the verb is *naga* (H5060, to touch, strike, plague) [5], and the noun is *nega* (H5061, a plague, stroke, strike) [6]. God does not send plagues merely to punish; He strikes to intervene: to stop Pharaoh from sinning further, and to stop the promise from being cut off in Egypt. When Psalm 105 looks back and sings of this history, it says that God "rebuked kings for their sakes, saying, 'Do not touch My anointed ones, and do My prophets no harm'" (Ps. 105:14-15, paraphrased)—Abram looked hardly like a prophet in Egypt, yet God already treated him according to the covenant.

Brothers and sisters, this is the most tearful sentence in this entire message: when man fails to keep the covenant, God Himself keeps it. Abram's faith collapsed, but God's promise did not collapse; Abram's word was untrustworthy, but not one word of God fell to the ground. Romans 3:3-4 establishes this truth as an axiom: "For what if some did not believe? Will their unbelief make the faithfulness of God without effect? Certainly not!" (paraphrased). Our weakness can hurt ourselves and hurt our families, but it cannot nullify the faithfulness of God—this is not an excuse to indulge our weakness; this is the rope that pulls us back.

### 3.2 Pharaoh's Rebuke: The Called One Schooled by an Outsider (12:18-19)

Once Pharaoh discovered the truth, he summoned Abram and questioned him to his face with three inquiries: "What is this you have done to me? Why did you not tell me that she was your wife? Why did you say, 'She is my sister'?"

Reading this scene makes one blush. An idol-worshiping monarch turned around and gave a lesson in honesty to the man who was called. Scripture records this rebuke just as it was, without trying to save face for Abram—this is precisely the honesty of Scripture: it does not manufacture flawless heroes; it records how God uses flawed men. Faced with these three questions, Abram did not utter a single reply. Silence is sometimes the final shape of weakness: silent before God, silent before his wife, and still silent before Pharaoh.

Notice also what Pharaoh did after his rebuke: he returned Sarai and commanded his men to send Abram's whole household away with all their belongings in an orderly, courteous manner, without confiscating his wealth and without executing him for his crime. Given royal power at that time, Pharaoh had every right to severely punish someone guilty of deceiving the monarch. That Abram was able to march out of Egypt with his entire company, his wife restored, and his wealth preserved was not due to his own ability; it was because God restrained the king's heart through the plagues. Before that hard-hearted Pharaoh in the book of Exodus, there came first this Pharaoh who knew where to stop—God reigns supreme over different eras and different kings all the same.

### 3.3 Being Sent Away Is Still Grace: God Sends Man Back to the Road to Canaan (12:20)

Verse 20 concludes: "So Pharaoh commanded his men concerning him; and they sent him away, with his wife and all that he had."

The departure was not dignified: it was no triumphal march; he was sent away. It was not that he came to his senses and turned back; he was escorted out by outsiders. But brothers and sisters, being sent away is still grace. Had he not been sent away, Abram might have settled down permanently amid Egypt's abundance under the pretext of "sojourning," leaving the Promised Land as nothing more than a memory. God used Pharaoh's hand to give him a push, shoving him back onto the road to Canaan. The very first verse of the next chapter, Genesis 13, picks up right here: "Then Abram went up from Egypt... to the South"—notice, this time it is "up"; the direction is right, and the very first thing he did upon his return was to go back to Bethel, back to the place where he had built an altar at the beginning, and there he called once again on the name of the LORD (cf. Gen. 13:3-4).

Here is the second spark of today's message, and it is the call of the entire sermon.

Brothers and sisters, you know in your own heart what your Egypt is: it may be a relationship you know you shouldn't rely on, a set of methods you know are contrary to God's will, or a half-truth you have used for many years. The good news of this chapter today is this: God did not wait for you to walk out of Egypt with your dignity intact; when you were at your most humiliated, He still kept His covenant and was still leading you back. There is no shame in being sent away; the shame lies in staying in Egypt. There is no shame in admitting your fault; the shame lies in living a lifetime on lies. Today, while God is still restraining you and still pushing you, turn around. Go up from Egypt to the South, return to the place where you first built an altar, and speak that prayer calling upon the name of the Lord once again. God does not despise those who are sent back; He only fears that you will make Egypt your home.

To sum up Part Three in a single sentence: **Abram was silent throughout his time in Egypt, but God was not silent: He struck with plagues to intervene, restored Sarai through Pharaoh, and sent this household back intact onto the road to Canaan—when man cannot keep the covenant, God Himself keeps it; being sent away is also being brought back.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

Today, this chapter speaks to six categories of people:

**To seekers:** You might feel that believers in God ought to be better than others, and Abram's weakness disappoints you. Please observe the way Scripture is written: it does not cover up the flaws of its own people. What this chapter reveals is not how good Abram was, but how faithful God is. If you reject faith simply because you see believers being weak, it is like walking away from a hospital just because a patient coughs. What God seeks is not flawless people, but people who are willing to be brought back by Him.

**To new believers:** When you encounter a famine after believing in the Lord, do not panic, and by no means rush down to Egypt to take a shortcut. First sort out two things clearly: your circumstances may be temporarily adjusted, but the Object of your trust cannot be swapped. Before making any "temporary" decisions, pray first, return to the altar first; if you have already gone down to Egypt and spoken words you should not have said, do not hide—admit your fault and turn around, for God is able to receive those who return.

**To longtime believers in the Lord:** The most dangerous lies for seasoned believers are often half-truths: holding back a little before people, making excuses before God; the words are all technically correct, but the center has shrunk to nothing more than self-preservation. Use Pharaoh's three questions for an annual inspection: Is there anything in my life that even worldly people can see is wrong, yet I am still packaging it with spiritual language? If you discover it, confess it before God; do not wait for outsiders to come and interrogate you.

**To parents:** In Abram's plan, the one who footed the bill was Sarai. When parents make decisions out of fear, the bill is often charged to their spouse and children: the lies told to protect income, the wrongs covered up to save face—the children are watching it all, and they are learning it all. Establish a rule starting today: the difficulties of the home can be borne together, but lies cannot be told together. For parents to still speak the truth in the midst of hardship is the most priceless home education they can give their children.

**To those striving in the workplace:** The Egypt of the workplace is that way of living where "everybody does it": fudging the numbers a bit, holding back half the truth, deflecting responsibility—you can make a profit, and you can secure your position. Settle the account of verse 16 clearly: gaining profit is no evidence of blessing. A position bought with lies is uneasy to sit in; speaking the truth may bring temporary loss, but you won't have to spend a lifetime covering up lies. Life lived depending on the rain may be a bit harder, but the heart is at rest.

**To those currently in suffering:** You may right now be in the midst of a famine, or you may have already lost face in Egypt, feeling that you have completely ruined your called life. Listen to the words of verse 17: where you fell silent, God did not fall silent; where you could not hold the line, He intervened to hold it Himself. Do not condemn yourself into despair first; return first to the altar. Someone who has been escorted back is still someone God intends to use—after Abram came out of Egypt, there were still more than a dozen chapters of the journey left to walk, and your story has not yet reached its final chapter either.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Summary · Closing Prayer

Let us review today's three statements:

First, *the famine was severe, and he went down to Egypt*—famine is not God breaking His covenant; if the heart of a sojourner lives according to the rules of Egypt, the promised land drifts further and further away;
Second, *please say you are my sister*—fear uses half-truths to save one's own skin and makes the dearest person foot the bill; gaining profit is no proof of blessing;
Third, *the Lord plagued Pharaoh, and Pharaoh sent him away*—when man cannot keep the covenant, God keeps the covenant Himself; being sent away is also grace; when your direction turns around, you are heading home.

Now let us bow our heads and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. When Abram fell silent in Egypt, You did not fall silent; what he could not keep, You kept Yourself. We pray that You would shine Your light on our respective Egypts, the words we have spoken out of fear, and the compromises we have made in exchange for safety. As we lay them before You one by one, we confess our sins and will not try to cover up our lies. Bring us out of Egypt, back to the place where we first built an altar, to call upon Your name once more. In days of famine, teach us to live by Your word, not by the granaries. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

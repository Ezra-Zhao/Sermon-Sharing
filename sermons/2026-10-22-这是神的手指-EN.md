## Summary (about 100 words)

The plague of frogs climbed onto Pharaoh's bed and into his cooking pots, and for the first time Pharaoh opened his mouth to ask for intercession, yet set the day of his deliverance for tomorrow; the plague of lice came, the magicians could not reproduce it, and with their own mouths said, this is the finger of God; the plague of flies came, the land of Goshen was set apart, and Pharaoh instead began to offer prices and bargain. The magicians' "cannot" stands against Pharaoh's "will not": God stretched out only one finger, and it outweighed all Pharaoh's army; negotiated obedience has never been obedience.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

When the previous chapter ended, the waters of the Nile had been turned to blood, the fish had died, the river stank, and the Egyptians were digging for water everywhere. Seven days passed — and what was Pharaoh's heart like? The Scripture says his heart was hardened and he would not listen to Moses and Aaron, just as the LORD had said. People are easily like this: the disaster passes, life becomes bearable again, and the heart returns to its old place. Pharaoh was exactly such a person — not that he had never seen a sign, but that he had seen one and decided not to let the sign change him.

In today's chapter God raises three plagues in succession: frogs, lice, and flies. Within one chapter, He knocks on the palace door three times. Each plague comes closer to the person than the one before: the frogs entered the bedroom, got onto the bed, and got into the ovens; the lice came out of the dust of the ground and fell on people and on animals, with nowhere to hide; the flies came in swarms, filling the houses and the land. God's hand reached nearer and nearer, yet Pharaoh's heart bargained farther and farther away. First he asked for prayer, then he set conditions, and at last he went back on his word as soon as he had spoken it. What most deserves our sitting down and looking at closely in this chapter is not how strange the plagues were, but the several ways a person dodges before God: delay, imitation, bargaining, and breaking one's word — they are all here.

And the weightiest sentence in this chapter was spoken neither by Moses nor by Pharaoh, but by Egypt's magicians. They were Pharaoh's religious experts, people who lived by their skill. They tried and tried again, and at last spread out their hands and spoke one honest sentence: this is the finger of God. The smallest possible words — not God's arm, not God's great hand, only one finger — yet they measured the whole of Egypt's pride to the bottom.

Let us read several key passages together:

"Then Pharaoh called for Moses and Aaron, and said, Intreat the LORD, that he may take away the frogs from me, and from my people; and I will let the people go, that they may do sacrifice unto the LORD. And Moses said unto Pharaoh, Glory over me: when shall I intreat for thee, and for thy servants, and for thy people, to destroy the frogs from thee and thy houses, that they may remain in the river only? And he said, To morrow." (Exodus 8:8-10, excerpt, KJV)

"And the magicians did so with their enchantments to bring forth lice, but they could not: so there were lice upon man, and upon beast. Then the magicians said unto Pharaoh, This is the finger of God: and Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he hearkened not unto them." (Exodus 8:18-19, excerpt, KJV)

"And Pharaoh said, I will let you go, that ye may sacrifice to the LORD your God in the wilderness; only ye shall not go very far away: intreat for me." (Exodus 8:28, excerpt, KJV)

"And Pharaoh hardened his heart at this time also, neither would he let the people go." (Exodus 8:32, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, Your finger outweighs a thousand armies, and Your word allows no bargaining. Today, by this mirror of Pharaoh, shine on us: show us whether we too have set our repentance for tomorrow, and whether we too want to bargain with You for a faith that does not go very far. Give us a heart that does not delay, does not bargain, and is willing to obey. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Frogs on the Bed — The Second Plague and That "Tomorrow" (8:1-15)

First look at how the plague of frogs came. God commanded Moses to enter the palace again, with the same sentence: let My people go, that they may serve Me. If you will not, I will bring frogs upon all your territory. Notice: every time, God speaks first, warns first, gives an opportunity to turn first, and only then acts. God's judgment is never an ambush; He puts the word first and leaves the door open — it is Pharaoh himself who closes the door again and again.

Aaron, as commanded, stretched out his staff over the rivers and ponds, and the frogs came up and covered the land of Egypt. The Scripture counts in detail the places the frogs went, each more unbearable than the last: into the palace, into the bedroom, onto the bed, into the houses of the servants, onto the people, and even into the ovens and the kneading troughs. Brothers and sisters, when the Bible writes a plague, it does not write statistics; it writes humiliation. Pharaoh was a god in the hearts of the Egyptians, and his palace was the most majestic, most pure place in the whole land, a place no idle thing could enter. Now there were frogs on the bed where he slept, frogs beside the pot from which he ate; in the morning he reached out his hand and touched not a scepter but a slippery frog. What God wanted to dismantle was precisely Pharaoh's omnipotent dignity: you cannot even guard your own bed — what kind of god are you?

There is a background here, treated in detail in the endnotes and mentioned in only one sentence in the text: the Egyptians worshiped a frog-headed goddess named Heqet, who governed fertility and life. [1] Frogs in Egypt were originally objects of worship; now they multiplied until they became a disaster, until they had to be piled up and stank. A thing that is worshiped, once it leaves the measure and place God appointed, will turn around and rule the one who worships it. The Egyptians ordinarily offered incense to frogs; now they begged the frogs to leave — this is not something the Scripture states explicitly; it is a layer of irony that later interpreters have often read out of the text, and we keep the distinction clear: what the text records is the plague; the irony is a reading, not the reading treated as Scripture. Yet this reading truly fits: whatever a person worships will sooner or later climb onto his bed and occupy his most private, most dignified place, because an idol never consents to stay only on its shrine.

The magicians also used their secret arts to bring frogs up. This deserves a pause: they could add to the plague, but they could not remove the plague. They could make things worse, but they could not make things better. Counterfeit authority has one common mark: it can only copy a problem, it cannot solve a problem; it can only increase bondage, it cannot release people. Pharaoh's court did not lack experts; it lacked anyone who could drive the frogs away. All the skill of the full court added together only added one more land full of frogs.

Then came the first turning point of this chapter: Pharaoh called Moses and Aaron and opened his mouth to say, please entreat the LORD. Pharaoh said this sentence for the first time in his life. In chapter 5 he had still arrogantly asked: Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice to let Israel go? I know not the LORD. By chapter 8, he not only acknowledged this name, he also asked Moses to intercede with this God for him. Calamity has one use: it makes a person who never prayed know for the first time that he needs prayer. Frogs in the cooking pot can make Pharaoh lower his posture more effectively than a thousand sermons. People are often like this: in smooth times no one's words can get in; once frogs get into the pot, they remember to look for God's people.

Moses interceded for an enemy — this is a hidden line in this chapter, and it will appear once more later. [6] Notice Moses' attitude: Pharaoh was the man persecuting his people, a man who had just gone back on his word, yet Moses did not mock him, did not say, so you have come to this day too, and did not set the condition, release the people first and then I will pray. Moses said: the honor is yours — you set the time. An intercessor must first lay down his own desire to win before he can stand between God and people. Moses' heart was shaped by God in one intercession for an enemy after another: he was the one who delivered the words of judgment, and he was also the one who offered the prayers of mercy, and the two did not fight in him, because his heart was not for himself, but only for God's will and for people to turn back.

But Pharaoh's answer was only one word, and it exposed his whole person: Tomorrow. Moses asked him, when shall I entreat for you? The reasonable answer was now, immediately, the sooner the better — who would willingly sleep one more night with frogs? Pharaoh insisted on saying, tomorrow. Brothers and sisters, this one word deserves a whole section of preaching. This is the theology of delayed prayer: Pharaoh did not disbelieve that prayer works — he believed it, he came himself to ask for it; Pharaoh did not fail to want the frogs to leave — he wanted it, he had had enough. He only wanted to move the day of his deliverance one day later. Why? To say it plainly, he could not bear to pay the price of obedience tonight. He had promised to let the people go, but once that promise left his mouth, at daybreak it would have to be honored. By setting the prayer for tomorrow, he left himself a night's margin for going back on his word, and he left his face a night's step down: as if the coming and going of this plague were still arranged by his timetable, as if he were still the one who decided.

We laugh at Pharaoh's foolishness — passing up tonight's peace and insisting on enduring one more night of frogs. But are the "tomorrows" in our prayer lives any fewer than Pharaoh's? Lord, I will start reading the Bible next month; I will go and reconcile after I get through this busy stretch; I will follow seriously when the children are older; I will quit this addiction after the New Year. Every "tomorrow" sounds humble; every one of them is about praying, changing, obeying — only the date has been moved later. The most frightening thing about delay is that it wears the clothing of devotion: it does not refuse God, it only forever schedules God for tomorrow. Pharaoh was not an atheist; Pharaoh was a believer of the tomorrow school — he believed everything, he just did not act today.

How did Moses answer? He said: be it according to your word, that you may know that there is none like unto the LORD our God. Moses prayed according to the day Pharaoh set, so that Pharaoh would have no excuse: the frogs did not leave by themselves, the season did not change them; they left on the very day you named with your own mouth, in answer to the prayer you yourself requested — the one who did this was the LORD. God was even willing to accommodate the time set by an enemy, in order to lay the evidence out plainly. Moses went out and cried unto the LORD, and the LORD did according to the word of Moses: the frogs died, and people piled the dead frogs in heaps in the houses, the courtyards, and the fields, and the land stank. Notice this ending: the plague left, but the stench remained. Deliverance obtained by delay always carries a stink with it — the problem is solved, but the aftereffects are still in the house. The person who obeyed one day earlier has no such pile in his house to clean up.

Then comes the most sigh-worthy sentence of this chapter: when Pharaoh saw that there was relief, he hardened his heart. Notice the causality: it was not that the plague was too heavy and crushed him into hardness; it was that as soon as the plague eased, he hardened. Many people's devotion rises and falls with the disaster situation: in difficulty they can get down on their knees; once the difficulty passes, their knees straighten. When Pharaoh asked for prayer he truly could not bear it; when the frogs died he truly forgot the pain. If a person seeks God only when he cannot bear it, God is for him only an emergency tool, put back in its place after use. For such a person, prayer will be answered again and again — God truly heard Moses — yet his life will grow harder again and again, because every time he receives grace and then breaks his word, he adds another layer of callus to his heart.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Dust Turned to Lice — Where the Magicians Stopped, and "This Is the Finger of God" (8:16-19)

The third plague came with no warning at all. Before the plague of frogs, God warned first; before the plague of flies, God also warned first; only before the plague of lice did God command Moses to say to Aaron, "Stretch out thy rod, and smite the dust of the land, that it may become lice throughout all the land of Egypt" — and as soon as the word was spoken it was carried out, with no gap left in the middle for Pharaoh to bargain. When a person delays again and again, God withdraws the advance notice; a person who breaks his word again and again will find that later on God even omits the stage of consultation. This is not God losing patience; this is the order of righteousness: the door of grace was opened many times, and the person himself narrowed it shut.

The action of striking the dust is itself a sermon. Dust is the most worthless thing in Egypt; it is everywhere, and people tread it underfoot every day. God used precisely dust to produce living creatures. In Genesis, God formed man from the dust of the ground; here, God made the dust become lice, falling on people and on animals. Egypt's priests were extremely particular about purity, shaving their whole bodies and bathing diligently, in order to be fit to serve their gods; now tiny lice crawled all over their bodies, and pure people became impure people, unable even to stand before their own gods. God used only the lowliest material to dismantle the dignity of which Egypt's religion was most proud. He did not need to mobilize an army; dust was enough.

The word for lice, kinnim in the original, has always had divergent translations; the Septuagint uses a word for a kind of small fly or biting insect, and whether these were lice, gnats, or small biting insects, scholarship has reached no settled conclusion. This manuscript follows the KJV in speaking of lice, and does not treat the dispute over the name as settled. [2] What matters is not the species of insect; what matters is what happened next: the magicians also tried by their secret arts to bring forth lice, but they could not.

Connect the earlier plagues and look. The staff became a serpent, and the magicians could do the same — only Aaron's staff swallowed their staffs. The river water became blood, and the magicians could do the same. Frogs came up, and the magicians could also bring frogs up. The first several times, the counterfeiters kept up, at least on the surface. At the lice, the Scripture writes for the first time those words: but they could not. Why did they stop precisely here? The text does not explain, and when we preach we must be honest: this is exposition, not something the text states explicitly. It may be understood this way: serpent, blood, and frogs were all still a matter of changing and drawing out things that already existed; to produce living insects out of dust, however, carries the flavor of creation from nothing, and creation is God's unique work. Magic can imitate phenomena; it cannot imitate the source of life. Human hands can copy many things; the one who can give life out of nothing is only One. Where the counterfeiters stopped is exactly the boundary of true authority — the farthest place they could reach precisely measured out that they were not God.

What is even more awe-inspiring is their attitude. They did not hold on stubbornly, did not say let us try once more, and did not fabricate a set of excuses to fool Pharaoh. They said to Pharaoh: this is the finger of God. [3] This is the theme sentence of this chapter, and the most honest sentence in the whole chapter. The people who said this were the most knowledgeable about religion and the most skillful group in Pharaoh's camp; they were, in effect, admitting before their employer: this One is not someone our trade can deal with; this is not a contest at the level of magic; this is God Himself stretching out His hand. Admitting defeat takes courage, especially admitting defeat in a palace. The magicians' careers may well have ended on this one true sentence, yet they said it. In this one point they were stronger than Pharaoh: when they reached the boundary, they acknowledged the boundary; when Pharaoh reached the boundary, he pretended there was no boundary.

Savor this way of speaking again: the finger of God. Not God's arm, not God's mighty outstretched arm, not God's thousand armies and ten thousand horses — only one finger. The Scripture later used this word again: Jesus said, if I with the finger of God cast out devils — Luke's Gospel writes, by the finger of God — the kingdom of God has come upon you. [3] The Lord Jesus cast out a demon, and the expression He used was also a finger. One finger is the smallest unit of strength a person can use; the Scripture deliberately uses the smallest expression to state the greatest fact: for God to govern Egypt, He does not need to turn out in full force, He does not need a great mobilization; He stretches out one finger, and all the experts of the whole land of Egypt stop together. Brothers and sisters, what comfort this is to the oppressed. The Israelites in Egypt were slaves; they had no soldiers, no generals, and even their one staff was given by God. Opposite them stood the strongest empire in the whole world, with all Pharaoh's army, all his magicians, and all his temples. And God said, do not be afraid; one finger of Mine is enough. However great the difficulty you face today, in God's sight it is only a matter of one of His fingers — not that the difficulty is small, but that God is so great that even His smallest movement outweighs all the strength of the enemy put together.

But when Pharaoh heard this true sentence, what was his reaction? Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he would not listen to them. Notice, the "them" he would not listen to this time included his own magicians. He would not listen to the prophet's words, and now he would not listen even to his own people's words. When a person hardens to a certain point, he enters a state in which no one's words can get in: God spoke through Moses, and he did not listen; God spoke through calamity, and he did not listen; even the experts he paid to keep admitted defeat with their own mouths, and he still did not listen. This brings out the deepest contrast in this chapter: the magicians' problem was "cannot"; Pharaoh's problem was "will not." "Cannot" is a boundary of ability; at the boundary, a person can still turn back. "Will not" is a refusal of the will; if the will locks itself shut, no matter how great the sign, it cannot push that door open. The magicians lost their skill contest, yet kept their honesty; Pharaoh kept his throne, yet lost his opportunity to turn back. Brothers and sisters, God is not troubled by our "cannot" — we cannot, in any case. What grieves God is our "will not." A person who admits he cannot — God's finger goes to work for him; a person who clenches his teeth and says he will not — God could set His whole army before him, and he would still turn and return to his palace.

This passage is also for everyone who lives by skill, by profession, by experience. Your skill is real; the magicians' skill was also real — in the first several plagues they could indeed do it. But skill always has a day when it runs out: there is an illness your medical art cannot cure; there is a child your methods cannot manage; there is an emptiness your achievements cannot fill. On that day, may we learn to be as honest as the magicians, spread out our hands, and say: this is the finger of God; I cannot. And then hand the "cannot" over to the God who can. Admitting "cannot" is not failure; it is the sign that true authority is beginning to take over.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Flies Filled the Land, Goshen Was Set Apart — and Pharaoh's Prices (8:20-32)

The fourth plague was the plague of flies. God commanded Moses to rise early and stand before Pharaoh — Pharaoh was going out to the water, and Moses waited for him there. God's messenger always appears at the moment the king least wants to see him. The warning was the same as before: if you will not let My people go, I will send swarms of flies upon you, your servants, and your people, into your houses, and the houses of the Egyptians and the land on which they live shall be full of swarms of flies. The original word, 'arov, carries the sense of a mixed swarm, [4] not two or three flies, but mass upon mass, a swarm of insects covering the sky and the ground, until even the air belonged to them. The land of Egypt was corrupted because of the swarms — the land was disturbed until people could not live in it, and the days were disturbed until they could not be lived. The disturbance of sin is often like this too: it does not take your life all at once; first it makes you have no peace all day long, and lets your home, your work, and your heart be occupied by a buzzing sound around the clock.

But in the plague of flies there was something none of the earlier plagues had, and God stated it Himself: "And I will sever in that day the land of Goshen, in which my people dwell, that no swarms of flies shall be there; to the end thou mayest know that I am the LORD in the midst of the earth. And I will put a division between my people and thy people." [5] This is the first explicit mention of setting apart in the ten plagues. Of the earlier plagues the text does not say, as it says here, that Goshen was treated differently; from the plague of flies onward, a line was drawn: on this side Egypt, on that side Goshen; on this side swarms filling the houses, on that side not one. God wanted Pharaoh to see with his own eyes that God is not only a God who sends plagues; He is a God who sets apart; He knows who His people are, and He acknowledges them before the face of the enemy.

Brothers and sisters, this must be preached carefully, lest it be preached crooked. Goshen's exemption from the plague was not capital for the Israelites to boast about. In godliness, the Israelites at that time were not much better than the Egyptians; they had lived in Egypt for hundreds of years, had taken on Egyptian customs, and would later worship the golden calf. They were set apart not because they deserved it, but because God had chosen them and remembered the covenant He had made with their fathers. So exemption from disaster is not a point for boasting; it is a point for witness: it witnessed to Pharaoh that this God truly exists and that His people are distinct from the world; it also witnessed to the Israelites themselves that the reason you are different lies not in you, but in the God who chose you. It is the same today. If Christians experience preservation in calamity and receive peace in the storm, that is not capital for showing off to the world; it is a witness to be lived out: let people, from the distinction upon us, see God's reality, and see that His mercy is still open to them. To preach a point of witness as a sense of superiority is the fastest way to lose the witness.

This time Pharaoh held out for an even shorter time. As soon as the swarms arrived, he called Moses and Aaron and offered the first price of this chapter: "Go ye, sacrifice to your God in the land." Listen carefully to the cleverness of this sentence: he yielded, and he did not yield. "Go" was the concession; "in this land" was the bottom line. Your God may be worshiped on my territory, on the condition that you cannot leave Egypt. In other words, faith may exist, but it must be under Pharaoh's eyelids, inside Egypt's system, within a range that can be stopped at any time. Moses refused on the spot: it is not right to do so; what we sacrifice is an abomination to the Egyptians — if we sacrifice before their eyes, will they not stone us? We must go three days' journey into the wilderness and sacrifice to the LORD our God as He commands us. Moses refused with reason and evidence, and did not yield an inch: the place, the manner, and the journey of worship are all decided by God, not approved by Pharaoh.

Pharaoh stepped back half a step and offered a second price: I will let you go, that you may sacrifice to the LORD your God in the wilderness; only you shall not go very far away; entreat for me. This sentence is the second sentence of this chapter worth engraving on the heart. "Not very far away" — this is the most classic way compromise names its price. Pharaoh had grown clever: block them completely, and the plague of flies could not be blocked; release them completely, and he could not bear to lose the slaves. So he gave a middle price: you may go, just do not go far; you may worship, just do not cut the line to Egypt; you may follow God, just do not follow Him to the end. Go not far, and turning back is convenient; go not far, and the rope is still in his hand. Brothers and sisters, what the enemy fears has never been our believing; it fears our going far — thoroughly leaving the old life, thoroughly cutting off the retreat, thoroughly handing ourselves over to God. A faith that stops halfway is the faith Pharaoh can most accept: the person in the wilderness, the heart in Egypt; in church on Sunday, back to the old pattern on Monday; following with the mouth, and not more than three steps with the feet. Pharaoh is not afraid of a single such person, because the rope is still in his hand.

Negotiated obedience is not obedience. If obedience must first negotiate the place, negotiate the distance, negotiate the conditions, and negotiate what is reserved, then what is being negotiated is not obedience but business. God said to go three days' journey; Pharaoh said do not go very far — one spoke of God's sovereignty, the other calculated his own loss. The condition of many believers' whole lives is stuck precisely in this sentence, "not very far": willing to read the Bible, unwilling to confess sin to the end; willing to offer time, unwilling to offer that one thing they can least bear to part with; willing to change small faults, unwilling to let God touch that most central idol. It is not that we do not walk; it is that after two steps we turn back and measure how far we are from Egypt, confirming that the rope is still long enough. Pharaoh does not need to block us; he only needs us not to go far by ourselves — that is enough.

Moses accepted the request for intercession, but he did not accept that distance. He said: "Behold, I go out from thee, and I will intreat the LORD that the swarms of flies may depart from Pharaoh, from his servants, and from his people, to morrow: but let not Pharaoh deal deceitfully any more in not letting the people go to sacrifice to the LORD." [6] This was Moses' second intercession for Pharaoh. The first was for the frogs, the second for the flies; Pharaoh had broken his word, had bargained, had dealt deceitfully, and Moses still prayed for him. An intercessor's heart is shaped before judgment in exactly this way: he delivers the words of judgment, yet in his heart hopes that the other will turn back; he knows full well the other may break his word again, yet still offers the prayer without discount. We pray for family, for colleagues, even for people who have hurt us, and often after praying twice we grow cold and feel the other is not worth it. Moses tells us that whether intercession is worth it is not determined by the other person, but by the calling. God wants His servant to learn mercy first, and then deliver righteousness; a person who can only deliver righteousness and cannot show mercy will, by the end of his delivering, find that his own heart has become Pharaoh's.

Moses went out and entreated, and God did according to his word: the swarms of flies departed, and not one remained. God works cleanly and thoroughly, leaving no tail behind. But Pharaoh? This time Pharaoh hardened his heart again, and would not let the people go. He lived the word "again" into a habit: see the plague, ask for prayer, receive deliverance, break his word — one cycle completed, and back to the starting point. Everyone, breaking one's word the first time is weakness; the second time it is habit; and when a habit has been walked several times, it becomes character. Pharaoh's heart did not harden to the bottom in one day; it came out one "again" at a time. Every small broken word of ours today — a vow made to God and not paid, a promise made to a person and not kept, a boundary set for ourselves and not held — adds a layer of hardness to the heart. Turn back early, before the word "again" has been written all over the page.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In today's chapter we heard two human sentences, and one sentence God spoke through human beings. Pharaoh said, tomorrow — that was delay; Pharaoh said, do not go very far — that was bargaining; the magicians said, this is the finger of God — that was honesty at the boundary. The magicians' "cannot" could not save Egypt, yet it made clear where true authority begins; Pharaoh's "will not" could keep his throne for a time, yet it closed the door of turning back. God stretched out only one finger, and it outweighed all Pharaoh's army, all his magicians, and all his temples; and the line of Goshen that God drew for His people was not for us to boast about, but for us to bear witness.

Let us pray together: Lord who calmed the whole land by stretching out a finger, we hand our "tomorrows" to You. Among us are people who have delayed repentance year after year, people willing to walk only two or three steps and unable to bear going far, and people who admit they truly cannot and only wait for You to work. Have mercy on us: cut the rope between us and Egypt, remove from our hearts that sentence "not very far," teach us to obey today, and to obey thoroughly; and shape us, like Moses, to intercede sincerely for people who do not like us, and even for people who have hurt us. In the name of the Lord Jesus, who cast out devils by the finger of God and brought God's kingdom among us, Amen.

[1] tsepharde'im and the Heqet background: frogs, Hebrew צְפַרְדְּעִים (tsepharde'im, H6854), appearing repeatedly from Exodus 8:2 onward, referring to the frogs that came up in swarms onto the land; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT — this is the factual level. In Egyptian religion there was indeed a frog-headed goddess, Heqet, associated with fertility, childbirth, and life, attested in Egyptian temple and funerary literature — this too is a checkable background fact. As for "the plague of frogs was an irony and judgment against Heqet," this belongs to one of the common "ten plagues correspond to Egyptian deities" readings in scholarship and the interpretive tradition; the text does not explicitly state that a given plague targeted a given god, and the body of this manuscript observes this distinction, taking it only as a reading and not as a conclusion of the text. See also Exodus 12:12, "against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment," as the scriptural basis for the plagues carrying a meaning of judgment on Egypt's gods.

[2] kinnim: Hebrew כִּנִּים (kinnim, H3654), Exodus 8:16-18, referring to the small insects that came from the dust and fell on people and animals; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The Septuagint (LXX) here has σκνῖφες (skniphes), referring to a kind of small biting insect or fly. Translators through the ages have rendered this variously as lice, gnats, or small flies, and the species of insect remains unsettled to this day; this manuscript follows the KJV with "lice," and its exposition rests only on "the magicians could not bring them forth," not on any specific species — noted here expressly.

[3] 'etsba' elohim: the finger of God, Hebrew אֶצְבַּע אֱלֹהִים ('etsba' elohim; 'etsba' is H676, finger; elohim is H430, God), the magicians' words in Exodus 8:19; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Intertext: Luke 11:20, the Lord Jesus said, "But if I with the finger of God cast out devils, no doubt the kingdom of God is come upon you"; the Matthean parallel (Matthew 12:28) has "by the Spirit of God," and the two read together show that "finger" is a humble expression for the power of the Holy Spirit. See also Exodus 31:18 and Deuteronomy 9:10, where the tablets were "written with the finger of God," and Psalm 8:3, "the heavens, the work of Thy fingers," all uses of "finger" as a figure for God's personal action. This manuscript's "the smallest expression, the greatest fact" is homiletical exposition based on this intertext.

[4] 'arov: Hebrew עָרֹב ('arov, H6157), from Exodus 8:21 onward, referring to swarms of flies; its root is close to "mixture," hence one explanation as "a mixed swarm of insects"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; the specific species also has proposals such as swarms of flies or beetles, and the KJV likewise renders "swarms of flies." See Psalm 78:45, "He sent divers sorts of flies among them," and Psalm 105:31, as cross-texts in which the Psalms retell the plague of flies.

[5] pedut and setting apart: Exodus 8:23; the KJV has "And I will put a division between my people and thy people," in which the word rendered "division" is the Hebrew פְּדוּת (pedut, H6304), whose basic meaning is ransom, redemption, and by extension distinction, difference; the same section also contains, in 8:22, God's statement that He would set apart the land of Goshen, using a verbal form of separating and making distinct; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. English translations differ here in verse numbering and wording, some having "division" and some "redemption"; this manuscript presents both senses, "redemption" and "distinction," together, and the body's statement "exemption from disaster is not a point for boasting but a point for witness" is theological exposition, expressly marked separately from the lexical facts.

[6] he'tir: Hebrew הֶעְתִּיר (he'tir, the causative form of the verb 'athar, H6279), to entreat, to intercede, to plead with God on another's behalf; in Exodus 8:8 Pharaoh said "intreat the LORD," in 8:28-29 Pharaoh said again "intreat for me" and Moses said "I will go out and entreat the LORD" — all use this word family; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the intercessor's heart is shaped before the judgment" is narrative exposition based on Moses' two intercessions (8:12-13, 8:29-31), not the lexical meaning itself.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Then the magicians said unto Pharaoh, This is the finger of God: and Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he hearkened not unto them; as the LORD had said." (Exodus 8:19, KJV) "And Pharaoh said, I will let you go, that ye may sacrifice to the LORD your God in the wilderness; only ye shall not go very far away: intreat for me." (Exodus 8:28, KJV)

Egypt's magicians reached the end of their skill and honestly said one sentence: this is the finger of God; we cannot. Pharaoh heard it, yet said: I will not. "Cannot" and "will not" differ by very little, yet they are two roads. The one who admits he cannot opens his hands, and God's finger begins to work for him; the one who clenches his "will not" turns back to his palace even when signs fill the land. We too often set our obedience for tomorrow, and bargain with God with the words "not very far": willing to believe, unwilling to cut the rope that ties us to Egypt. Do not bargain today. Change that tomorrow, delayed year after year, into today; hand God the rope with which you measure the distance, and let Him cut it. Learn also, like Moses, to pray sincerely once more for that person in your heart who hardens again and again and breaks his word again and again, and do not withdraw your mercy because he is unworthy; the hand that intercedes and the feet that obey grow, after all, out of one heart. God does not need your great ability; He is only waiting for your willingness. One of His fingers is greater than all your enemies, and greater than all your excuses. However far the road out of Egypt may be, it begins to go far from this very step today.

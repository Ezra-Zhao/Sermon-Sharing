## Summary (About 100 Words)

Abraham married Keturah again, and after she bore sons he sent them eastward, giving all that he had to Isaac alone; at one hundred and seventy-five years old he breathed his last and died, and was buried with Sarah at Machpelah. Isaac prayed for twenty years for Rebekah, who could not bear children, and she conceived twins: the firstborn was hairy all over and was named Esau, skilled in hunting; the second grasped his brother's heel and was named Jacob, quiet and dwelling in tents. Once Esau came back from hunting exhausted and urgently asked to drink the red stew his brother was cooking, and Jacob named his price: sell me your birthright. Esau said: I am about to die; of what use is this birthright to me? He swore the oath and sold it, ate and drank his fill, rose and went his way. Scripture's verdict is only one sentence: Thus Esau despised his birthright. This sermon looks at three things: how weighty the birthright is, how lightly it was despised, and how the urgency of the belly steals away an everlasting blessing.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In today's chapter, we first bid farewell to an old man, then welcome two infants, and at the end we stop at a bowl of stew.

Abraham died at one hundred and seventy-five years old. In his lifetime he came out of Ur, sojourned in Canaan for a hundred years, waited twenty-five years for a son, went up Mount Moriah, and bought a burial plot. Scripture's closing words over his life carry great weight: "he died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years; and was gathered to his people." (Gen 25:8, excerpt, KJV) Full of years — in the original there is the sense of "satisfied with years": not that he was tired of living, but that he had lived to fullness. A man who was pursued by the promise all his life in the end departed satisfied.

Then Scripture's camera does not linger; it turns at once to the next generation, because the promise cannot stop. And the story of the next generation begins with the shadow of the older generation: Isaac's wife Rebekah could not bear children — just like Sarah. The household of the promise must in every generation learn to pray anew; blessing is never inherited automatically, and even childbearing must be sought back on one's knees.

And what this chapter leaves to the church as a warning for two thousand years is those last few verses: a pot of stew, an oath, a man who ate his fill and rose and went his way, and the verdict is "despised." Today let us stare at this bowl of stew — to see how a person, in the time it takes to eat a meal, sells with his own hands the greatest blessing of his life; and to see ourselves, whether we too, on some tired and hungry evening, are holding a bowl and reckoning the same price.

Let us first read several key passages of Scripture:

"And Abraham gave all that he had unto Isaac. But unto the sons of the concubines, which Abraham had, Abraham gave gifts, and sent them away from Isaac his son, while he yet lived, eastward, unto the east country." (Gen 25:5-6, excerpt, KJV)

"And Isaac intreated the LORD for his wife, because she was barren: and the LORD was intreated of him, and Rebekah his wife conceived. And the children struggled together within her." (Gen 25:21-22, excerpt, KJV)

"And the first came out red, all over like an hairy garment; and they called his name Esau. And after that came his brother out, and his hand took hold on Esau's heel; and his name was called Jacob." (Gen 25:25-26, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob sod pottage: and Esau came from the field, and he was faint: And Esau said to Jacob, Feed me, I pray thee, with that same red pottage; for I am faint: therefore was his name called Edom." (Gen 25:29-30, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob said, Sell me this day thy birthright. And Esau said, Behold, I am at the point to die: and what profit shall this birthright do to me?" (Gen 25:31-32, excerpt, KJV)

"Then Jacob gave Esau bread and pottage of lentiles; and he did eat and drink, and rose up, and went his way: thus Esau despised his birthright." (Gen 25:34, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, through this chapter, we pray, weigh the scales in our hearts: what is weighty and what is light. Save us from the valuation of that bowl of stew, so that when we are tired and hungry we may still remember who we are and how weighty the birthright is. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 22:00) Part One: The Old Man Died Satisfied, and the Promise Changed Shoulders (25:1-18)

The first half of this chapter is Abraham's old age and his passing. After Sarah died, he married Keturah again, and she bore six sons. Scripture records these things not to add branches to the promise, but precisely to draw a clear line: there may be many sons, but only one who inherits the promise. "And Abraham gave all that he had unto Isaac"; the other sons received gifts and were sent eastward — while their father was still alive everything was arranged in good order, so as to avoid disputes over the inheritance after his death and to keep the line of the promise from being diluted. For an elderly person to settle the affairs of the household clearly while still alive is a final kindness to those who come after.

After he died, Isaac and Ishmael — the brothers who had been separated in earlier years — returned together and buried their father in the cave of Machpelah, in the same place as Sarah. The text then records Ishmael's genealogy: he had many sons and grandsons, twelve princes, and he died at one hundred and thirty-seven years old — even to a child who was not on the main line of the promise, God did not break faith.

Then comes that sentence that joins what went before with what follows: "And after the death of Abraham, God blessed his son Isaac." The blessing changed shoulders. Immediately the genealogy of Isaac begins — Hebrew narrative uses the word "generations" as a section marker [5], telling us: the protagonist of the story has become Isaac's generation. But the first difficulty of this generation is exactly the same as that of the previous one: inability to bear children. Isaac married Rebekah at forty, prayed for a full twenty years, and only at sixty did he receive twins. Twenty years are counted year by year. Among us there are those who have prayed for a long time with no sign yet; please remember Isaac: he did not devise another method; he simply "intreated for her," and kept asking until God granted it. The lesson of the household of the promise must be relearned in every generation: the previous generation learned surrender on Moriah; this generation learned waiting in prayer.

Rebekah conceived, and the children struggled together within her. She went to inquire of the LORD and received those famous words: "Two nations are in thy womb, and two manner of people shall be separated from thy bowels; and the one people shall be stronger than the other people; and the elder shall serve the younger." (Gen 25:23, excerpt, KJV) At the birth, the first to come out was red and hairy all over, and was named Esau [3]; the second came out with his hand holding his brother's heel, and was named Jacob. The parents each had their own favorite: Isaac loved Esau, because he ate of his venison; but Rebekah loved Jacob. Scripture calmly records this favoritism without comment, but those who have read the later chapters know how great a breach this crack will later split into. Favoritism is the quietest kindling in a family, and it reminds the parents here: let your love be even; children keep accounts of the casual praise you give at the dinner table.

The temperaments of the two children also set the tone: Esau was skilled in hunting and was often in the field; Jacob was a quiet man, dwelling in tents. The struggle over the birthright was about to erupt between these two temperaments, by means of a bowl of stew.

## (22:00 - 40:00) Part Two: The Buying and Selling of a Bowl of Red Stew — The Birthright Sold in Exhaustion (25:29-33)

The scene is extremely simple: one day, Jacob was cooking stew in the tent, and Esau came back from the field "faint." First, let us have some sympathy for Esau: he was truly tired and truly hungry, a man who had returned empty-handed from hunting, his strength spent and his judgment at its lowest. The devil knows best how to choose his moment — it is always when we are faint that he brings the bowl before us. Many great mistakes are not decided in sober meetings; they are nodded through on weary evenings.

Esau spoke: "I am faint; feed me, I pray thee, with that same red pottage." He could not even name the stew; he only called it "that red thing" — the red stew [4]. In the original he speaks even more urgently, roughly "let me swallow down this red, this red thing": hunger had flattened even his language, leaving only a color. Scripture incidentally gave him a lifelong nickname: Edom [3], which is "Red." A man's name can be changed by a bowl of stew — he later became the ancestor of the nation of Edom; the name of a whole people commemorates this meal.

Jacob's asking price was frighteningly calm: "Sell me this day thy birthright." [1] Brothers and sisters, to be fair, this sentence of Jacob's was not noble: to press down the price while his brother was faint was calculation. Scripture did not let Jacob off later; he would have to repay this tuition with twenty years of exile, with his father-in-law changing his wages ten times, and with being deceived by his own sons. But the protagonist of today's sermon is Esau's answer, because the deal was only completed when he nodded: "Behold, I am at the point to die: and what profit shall this birthright do to me?"

First let us make clear what the birthright of the firstborn is [1]. bekhorah (H1062), the birthright of the firstborn [1]: in the family estate he receives a double portion; in the family he ranks first, inheriting the father's authority and blessing; and in Isaac's household it carried yet another incomparable weight — it was connected to the covenant God gave to Abraham: descendants, land, and the source of blessing for all nations. In other words, what Esau held in his hand was not an inheritance; it was the baton of the whole history of salvation. And the price he set on it was: less than a bowl of stew, because "I am at the point to die." Was this the talk of a man faint with hunger? He was still very far from death, and the household did not lack this one meal — he could well have gone into the tent and asked his mother for food. Exaggeration is the catchphrase of despising: when a person looks down on something, he always first magnifies the present difficulty to the utmost and shrinks that thing to the least. "What profit shall this birthright do to me?" — profit; he only counted the profit that could be used right away. The birthright could not be eaten at once, could not be drunk at once, could not relieve weariness at once, so on the scales of exhaustion it was worth nothing.

Note also what stew Jacob was cooking: only when the stew is divided does the text make clear that it was lentil stew [4] — a stew of lentils, the cheapest pot of farmhouse food at the time. Esau was not bought by a table of feasting; he was bought by a pot of bean stew. The price of temptation never needs to be very high, because in the heart of one who despises, that treasure was already marked at a cheap price. If in ordinary days you regard spiritual things lightly, on the day you are faint half a bowl of stew will be enough; if in ordinary days you regard the birthright as weighty, even a full table cannot buy it. The outcome of this transaction had already been decided in the daily valuation.

Jacob pressed one more sentence: "Swear to me this day." And Esau swore, and sold his birthright to Jacob. Swearing shows that this was no joke: an oral transaction in the ancient Near East, together with an oath, was an irrevocable transfer. Brothers and sisters, decisions that ruin a lifetime are often made extremely quickly, extremely formally, and with signatures made in full sobriety — Esau was not robbed; he sold it himself; he was not drunk, he was hungry; he was not deceived, he named the price himself. Jacob may be criticized; Esau has no excuse.

## (40:00 - 52:00) Part Three: He Ate His Fill, Rose, and Went His Way — Despising, and the Silent Tears Afterwards (25:34; Heb 12:16-17)

With the transaction completed, Scripture closes with a string of short verbs: "Then Jacob gave Esau bread and pottage of lentiles; and he did eat and drink, and rose up, and went his way." He ate, he drank, he rose, he went — four movements, clean and neat, as if he had eaten an ordinary meal. The verdict follows immediately, one sentence to settle the case: "thus Esau despised his birthright." [2]

Despised — in the original bazah (H959), to despise, to scorn, to regard as worthless [2]. Note that Scripture does not say Esau "lost" the birthright, nor that it was "taken away" from him; the charge is fixed on his attitude: he despised it. He was not a man who could not afford to lose; he was a man who never treated that thing as a matter of importance at all. Many people lose a blessing not because the blessing was too small, but because in their eyes it was never weighty. Despising is harder to undo than stealing: the one who steals knows it is a treasure; the one who despises treats the treasure as straw, and when you reason with him he asks you in return: what profit is there in this?

Look also at the closing judgment that the Book of Hebrews gives Esau: "Lest there be any fornicator, or profane person, as Esau, who for one morsel of meat sold his birthright. For ye know how that afterward, when he would have inherited the blessing, he was rejected: for he found no place of repentance, though he sought it carefully with tears." (Heb 12:16-17, excerpt, KJV) Hear two points clearly. First, the New Testament classifies this matter as "profane" — it was not hunger; it was placing momentary satisfaction above an everlasting birthright. Second, the word "afterward" cuts the deepest: Esau did not remain unrepentant forever. By chapter 27, after the blessing had been obtained by Jacob, he "cried with a great and exceeding bitter cry" and pleaded again and again: "Hast thou but one blessing, my father? bless me, even me also, O my father!" Only then did he know how weighty the birthright was — but the door had already closed. The moment of selling did not hurt, because the day when its weight would be revealed had not yet come. Scripture records his tears to leave a mirror for us who still have time.

Do not read this chapter as "Jacob's victory," either. That God chose Jacob was spoken before he was born — "the elder shall serve the younger" — and this election comes from God's sovereignty and mercy, not from Jacob's calculation pleasing God. In the story of the prodigal's brother and the father, God's grace never pays wages according to merit. That Jacob obtained the birthright was grace; that Esau sold the birthright was of his own doing. At the same pot, one man exposed his calculation and the other exposed his despising — God's judgment distinguishes them clearly, and when we read, we too should place ourselves in the mirror rather than hurrying to take sides.

One final mention: Esau's descendants later became the Edomites, who were repeatedly enemies of Israel. The despising of a bowl of stew grew into the temperament of a nation. A personal valuation never affects only the individual: how you value faith at home today, your children are watching and learning beside you; what you despise, they will find it hard to treasure.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

First, people in the moment of being tired and hungry right now. Business has failed, a child is ill, it is three in the morning and you still have not slept — decisions made when a person is faint will mostly be regretted. Establish one household rule: when you are faint, do not sign, do not refuse, do not make deals, and do not speak words of finality. First eat a proper meal, first sleep, and talk about the birthright when daylight comes.

Second, people who feel that faith has "no profit." If what you ask is "What profit is believing in the Lord for my promotion, and what use is this week's gathering to me?", you are already standing before Esau's scales. The birthright, the birthright of children, the inheritance in Christ — these were never priced by immediate usefulness. Those who price faith by usefulness will sooner or later meet a more fragrant bowl of stew.

Third, parents. How you talk about the church, prayer, and offering in front of your children is how you price the birthright. If you say "these things are of no use," your children will learn to despise them; if you regard the Lord's Day and God's word as weighty, your children may not believe at once, but they will know that on the scales of this household these things are heavy.

Fourth, people being pressed by "this day." Jacob's asking price contained a word: "Sell me this day thy birthright." The devil's bargains are always limited to today: if you do not sign today it will be gone; if you do not flare up today it will be too late. Whatever allows only today and does not allow a night of prayer before answering is most likely a bowl of red stew. God's leading can stand waiting; what is in a hurry to close the deal is often a trap.

Fifth, people skilled in calculation like Jacob. Obtaining the birthright is not the same as obtaining the peace of the blessing. Jacob won the transaction and lost twenty years of peace, sleeping in the wilderness with a stone for a pillow. What is seized by calculation must be repaid in fear. Leave the accounts to God; His promise does not need you to advance it by unrighteousness.

Sixth, people who have already sold their "birthright" and are now living in regret. Esau's tears could not obtain a reversal — that is a warning; but the gospel leaves us a road that Esau did not have: the Lord Jesus is the true Firstborn, and He freely shares His own birthright and His own blessing with us who deserved to weep. You cannot go back to that evening, but you can come before Christ and receive an inheritance that cannot be taken away — an inheritance that is not sold, not exchanged, and does not change hands for a bowl of stew.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In today's chapter: the old man died satisfied, and the promise took up new shoulders; twins struggled in the womb, and the birthright was set on the dining table; the firstborn ate his fill, rose, and went his way, and Scripture gives him its verdict in a single clause: thus Esau despised his birthright. May we remember these scales — the birthright is extremely weighty, the red stew is extremely light; the urgency of the belly is for a moment, and the birthright in Christ is forever.

Let us pray together: Lord, forgive us: we too, on weary evenings, have regarded You very lightly and a bowl of stew very heavily. Heal our valuation, we pray, and let our hearts know afresh what is precious. Preserve our children, so that they do not despise the birthright You have given; and remind us, when we are weary, hungry, and pressed, to come first before You and not hurry to reach out and take that bowl of stew. Thank You that the true Firstborn, Jesus Christ, has shared His birthright with us. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] bekhorah: Hebrew בְּכֹרָה (H1062), the birthright of the firstborn, the rights of the firstborn; in Gen 25:31-34 Jacob asks Esau to "sell me thy birthright"; this birthright includes a double portion of the family estate and the position of head of the family (cf. Deut 21:17), and in this narrative it is further connected with the inheritance of Abraham's covenant; for the meaning of the word, see BDB, HALOT.

[2] bazah: Hebrew בָּזָה (H959), to despise, to scorn, to regard as worthless; Gen 25:34, "Esau despised his birthright"; for the meaning of the word, see BDB, HALOT. This manuscript's observation that "the charge is fixed on the attitude" is a homiletical observation based on this word.

[3] Esau and Edom: Esau (עֵשָׂו, Esau, H6215); Edom (אֱדוֹם, Edom, H123), "Red"; Gen 25:30 states clearly, "therefore was his name called Edom," connecting "the red stew" with the name and with the later nation of Edom; for the meanings of the words, see BDB, HALOT.

[4] nazid: Hebrew נָזִיד (H5138), that which is boiled, soup, stewed food; both Gen 25:29, "Jacob sod pottage," and 25:34, "pottage of lentiles," use this word; for the meaning of the word, see BDB, HALOT. Esau calls it "that red thing" (הָאָדֹם, 25:30), and this manuscript speaks from this of the compression of language in hunger.

[5] The section marker of "generations": Gen 25:12 and 25:19 twice open a new section with "generations" (toledot, תּוֹלְדוֹת, H8435), recording the genealogies of Ishmael and Isaac respectively; it is a structural marker for the whole Book of Genesis; the section of this manuscript on "the promise changed shoulders" is told according to this division, and is noted here expressly.

[6] On the verdict of Heb 12:16-17: the New Testament lists Esau as a warning of a "profane person" and records that afterwards he sought it carefully with tears but found no place for reversal (Heb 12:16-17); Part Three of this manuscript develops from this, in mutual reference with the narrative in Genesis 27 of Esau crying aloud and seeking the blessing; the sources are marked in the main text.

## Devotional Excerpt

Genesis 25:34 (KJV): "Then Jacob gave Esau bread and pottage of lentiles; and he ate and drank and rose and went his way. Thus Esau despised his birthright." Esau came in from the field exhausted. He was not robbed and he was not deceived — he named the price himself: "I am about to die; of what use is a birthright to me?" He ate the stew, was satisfied, got up, and walked away, feeling no pain at all. The pain came years later, when the blessing had been given to another and he wept aloud — but the door had closed. The most frightening thing about despising a treasure is that the loss is painless at the moment it happens. Is there something in your life that God counts weighty but you have priced cheaply? Worship, prayer, your birthright as a child in Christ — none of these fills the stomach immediately, so on the scale of a tired day they seem worth little. Ask the Lord to remind you today: before reaching for the stew, remember who you are. Pray also that He would give us a heart that treasures the birthright in ordinary days — when the daily valuation is right, we will not sell it cheaply on the day of exhaustion. The birthright lasts forever; the red stew lasts only one meal; a moment's fullness cannot buy a lifetime of blessing, so do not let today's belly sign its name for your whole life.

## Summary (about 100 words)

The first sentence of the Ten Commandments is not a command but God's self-introduction: I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of Egypt, out of the house of bondage. Redemption first, commandments after; the commandments are the pattern of life for people already redeemed, not a ticket with which to buy salvation. The first four commandments, toward God, lay the root of worship; the six toward other people set the bounds of life together, and the last closes in the human heart, on coveting. The people fear and ask for a mediator, and Moses draws near to the thick darkness; and immediately after the Ten Words, what God gives is not a palace but an altar of atonement.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Today we come to Exodus chapter 20, one of the most famous chapters in the whole book — indeed, in the whole Old Testament. The Ten Commandments: everyone has heard of them. Some hang them on a wall, some treat them as a moral checklist, and some, the moment they hear the word "commandment," think first of rules, prohibitions, and judgment. But today I ask you first to set the checklist impression aside and go back to the first sentence with which the text opens. The first sentence of the Ten Commandments is, remarkably, not a command at all. The first thing God says is not "thou shalt not" this or that; it is a self-introduction, a declaration of salvation history: "I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage." The whole order is there: redemption first, commandments after; first God brings the people out, and only then does He tell them how those who have come out are to live. The commandments were never a ticket with which to purchase salvation, as if a person first had to qualify and only then would God consent to save; the commandments are the pattern of life received by people already redeemed — people led out of the house of bondage learning to live no longer in the way of slaves, and no longer in the way of Egypt. Reverse this order, and the Ten Commandments are read as a crushing burden; read the order rightly, and what comes out of them is the shape of grace.

Let us also first take in the whole chapter, lest by staring only at the ten commandments we miss the arrangement the text itself makes. Chapter 20 falls roughly into three parts. The first is the Ten Commandments themselves, verses 1 to 17, in which God speaks in person to all the people. The second is the people's response, verses 18 to 21: thunderings, lightnings, the noise of the trumpet, and the mountain smoking; the people tremble, remove and stand afar off, and ask Moses to be their mediator — do not let God speak with us directly. The third is the law of the altar, verses 22 to 26: God commands that no gods of silver or gold be made, that an altar of earth be built for sacrifice, that its stones not be hewn, and that no one go up the altar by steps. Within one chapter there are commandments, human fear, and an altar. The commandments show God's holiness and the human standard; the fear shows that man cannot stand by himself; the altar shows that God has prepared beforehand the way of atonement and approach. Read the three parts together, and the Ten Commandments do not stand alone: they are not an isolated slab of stone, but words given within the weave of redemption, reverence, and atonement.

There is also one matter of division that must be stated honestly before we begin preaching. This is a statement at the level of fact, not homiletical elaboration. The Ten Commandments are divided differently in different traditions. In the Catholic and Lutheran traditions, "thou shalt have no other gods" and "thou shalt not make a graven image" are usually combined as the first commandment, and "thou shalt not covet" is split into two, making up the ten. In the Reformed and Orthodox traditions, "no other gods" is counted as the first commandment, "no graven image" as the second, and "thou shalt not covet" is kept together as the tenth. The Jewish reckoning differs again, with a way of counting in which "I am the LORD thy God" itself is taken as the first of the words. Different divisions do not mean there are eleven versions of the text; the text itself does not number the words one by one — the numbering is a later traditional arrangement. This manuscript follows the Reformed division: four commandments toward God and six toward other people, and the exposition below follows that division. Adopting one division does not erase the differences of the other traditions; keep that in mind as you listen.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And God spake all these words, saying, I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage." (Exodus 20:1-2, KJV)

"Thou shalt have no other gods before me." (Exodus 20:3, KJV)

"Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy." (Exodus 20:8, KJV)

"Honour thy father and thy mother: that thy days may be long upon the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee." (Exodus 20:12, KJV)

"And Moses said unto the people, Fear not: for God is come to prove you, and that his fear may be before your faces, that ye sin not." (Exodus 20:20, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, thank You that You first save us, and then teach us how to live. Through the Ten Commandments, light up our hearts, so that we are first seized by Your grace and then willingly live according to Your heart — neither treating the commandments as a ticket, nor using grace as an excuse. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Prologue Before the Commandments — Redemption First, Commandments After; the First Four Commandments Lay the Root of Worship (20:1-11)

Look carefully first at the prologue of verses 1 and 2, as scholars often call the preface to the Ten Commandments. The text says: "And God spake all these words." This sentence settles who is speaking: the Ten Commandments are not Moses' invention and not the resolution of a council of elders; they are words God spoke in person. The very first sentence that follows is God's self-introduction: "I am the LORD thy God." The LORD is the name by which God revealed Himself to Moses, the name of the covenant God; and "thy God" fixes the relationship — He is not a vague ruler of the universe, but the God who has already entered into relation with this people and claimed them as His own. Then, instead of issuing a command at once, He first reports history: "which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage." The house of bondage reminds every hearer: your former identity was that of slaves, and the place you lived was a system of slavery; that you can stand here and hear the commandments is not because you were strong, clever, or devout, but because a God reached out His hand and brought you out. Brothers and sisters, the logic of the Ten Commandments is hidden in this prologue: God first declares who He is and what He has done, and only then commands what man is to do. The indicative comes first, the imperative after; the statement of grace first, the command of duty after. The ethical order of the whole Bible follows this order. The New Testament epistles do the same: first what Christ has accomplished, then how you ought to walk. If anyone preaches the Ten Commandments as ten steps to climb, one by one, in exchange for salvation, he has thrown away the prologue and kept only the slab. With the prologue in place, the commandments are the household rule of people who have received grace; with the prologue gone, they become a bill that crushes people to death. This is the order of the text at the level of fact; this manuscript sets it firm first, so that the application afterwards does not go astray.

With that lens, let us look at the first four commandments, toward God. The first commandment, verse 3: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me." This commandment governs the object of worship: God requires singleness. He had just brought the people out of Egypt — a land of many gods, where the river had a god, the sun had a god, and even livestock were treated as gods, and Pharaoh considered himself a god. The people had lived long in that environment, soaked in it through eye and ear, and were most prone to demote the LORD to one god among many, merely a stronger and more useful one. The first commandment is categorical: no other gods — not a chief seat reserved for the LORD in a pantheon, but no other god at all besides Him. Singleness of worship is the root of all the commandments that follow; if the object is wrong, however diligently the rest are kept, the direction of worship is wrong.

The second commandment follows immediately, verses 4 to 6: "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath, or that is in the water under the earth: thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them." The first commandment governs the object; the second governs the manner: even if your mouth confesses the LORD, you may not use an image you have carved to represent Him or to control Him. The problem of an idol is not only whether its material is gold or wood; it is that man wants to make God into something visible, touchable, portable, and usable — compressing the infinite God into the palm of man's hand. The text then gives the reason: "for I the LORD thy God am a jealous God" [5]. Jealousy here is not pettiness; the original speaks of this God's demand for single-hearted love from His people, as the faithfulness of marriage admits no third party; He is jealous precisely because He loves this people as His own possession. The text goes on: "visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me; and shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments." On one side, three and four generations; on the other side, thousands. The proportion itself is speaking: judgment is real, yet bounded; mercy is long, unto thousands of generations. In preaching this passage, one must not pick only half: to speak only of visiting iniquity is to preach God as a tyrant; to speak only of mercy is to preach the jealousy away. The text speaks both sides, and we preach both sides — that is honesty.

The third commandment, verse 7: "Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain; for the LORD will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain." God's name is God's own person and reputation as He has revealed them to man. To take it in vain carries in the original the sense of lifting up this name emptily, falsely, lightly: swearing falsely by God's name, using God's name to endorse one's own ambition, being pious in the mouth and empty in the heart — all fall within this commandment's range. It warns man: God's name is not to be used as a tool, not as decoration, and not as a talisman. This commandment is just as sharp today: whoever trades on God's banner for private gain or empty talk — God says He will not hold him guiltless. The weight of that sentence lies in this: the verdict rests not in the hand of human opinion, but in God's hand.

The fourth commandment, verses 8 to 11: "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy" [2]. Six days shalt thou labour and do all thy work, but the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God; in it thou shalt not do any work — thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, thy manservant, nor thy maidservant, nor thy cattle, nor thy stranger that is within thy gates. Rest is not the leisure of a privileged class; it falls impartially on the whole household — on servants, on cattle, and on the stranger. A people born as slaves should know the sweetness of this commandment best: in Egypt, the house of bondage knew no rest, and the brick kilns never stopped; now God Himself legislates, giving even the lowliest person the right to stop. The reason the commandment gives, in verse 11, is anchored in creation: "For in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that in them is, and rested the seventh day: wherefore the LORD blessed the sabbath day, and hallowed it." In keeping the sabbath, man imitates the rhythm of the Creator: work is not the whole of life, and stopping is a confession that the world does not hold up only because I never stop turning. Here an important point of likeness and difference must be noted side by side; this is a fact of the text. When Deuteronomy chapter 5 restates the Ten Commandments, both the word used for the sabbath and the reason given differ: Deuteronomy uses the word "keep" [2], and the reason it gives is not creation but the exodus — remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt, and that the LORD thy God brought thee out thence through a mighty hand, and therefore the LORD commanded thee to keep the sabbath day. The two reasons differ, yet they complement and do not contradict each other: one roots the sabbath in the order of creation, the other roots it in the history of redemption; the same commandment belongs both to man as created and to man as redeemed. This manuscript sets the two side by side precisely so that we see how Scripture itself lights up the same commandment from two angles, instead of setting them against each other.

Having walked through the four commandments, we may sum up in one sentence: the commandments toward God first fix the object, then the manner, then guard His name, then set the time of worship. Worship is not whatever man pleases to make it; God Himself teaches His people how to draw near to Him. And the root of all four is that prologue: because He is the LORD your God, and because He first brought you out, therefore you worship Him alone.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Six Commandments Toward Other People Set the Bounds of Life Together — From Honouring Parents to Coveting, the Commandments Close in the Human Heart (20:12-17)

After the sabbath, the camera of the commandments turns from man's relation to God to man's relations with man — what we usually call the six commandments toward other people. Their arrangement also has its order: family first, then society; life first, then marriage, property, and reputation, and at last closing in the heart. Let us walk through them one by one, neither hurrying nor padding.

The fifth commandment, verse 12: "Honour thy father and thy mother: that thy days may be long upon the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee." The original word for honouring [3] is not only obedience and compliance; it carries the sense of giving weight: treating one's parents as people of weight — respecting them, providing for them, caring for them tenderly. That this commandment stands first among the commandments toward other people is no accident: the family is the first school in which a person learns to respect authority and to treat others; how a person treats the parents who bore and raised him at home traces, in large part, how he will treat society outside the home. This commandment also carries a promise: "that thy days may be long." In Ephesians chapter 6 the apostle Paul points out expressly that this is the first commandment with promise; in other words, among the Ten Commandments it is the only one that carries a promise stated in so many words. The promise is not a transaction, as if one day of honouring were exchanged for one day of life; the promise is God's showing man a stable road: a community that honours parents and settles its families enjoys the blessing of long days. In preaching honouring parents, one must also not preach it into blind obedience or oppression: what the text commands is the respect owed from the children's side; it does not authorise wilfulness from the parents' side. Each side has its own duty, and neither replaces the other — that is the balance to keep in reading this commandment.

The sixth commandment, verse 13, is short — in English, four words: "Thou shalt not kill." Here a point of word meaning must be stated honestly, or the commandment is easily misread. The word used in the original is ratsach [4], which refers to unlawful killing — murder, the unlawful taking of a human life. Its range of meaning is not every form of killing whatsoever. Elsewhere, Scripture treats killing an enemy in war, punishment in judicial process, and manslaughter as distinct from intentional killing, with different words and different laws, and does not gather them all into this word. To stretch ratsach into a prohibition of taking any life at all is to inflate the word's meaning; to shrink it to premeditated murder only is to make it narrower than the text's usage. This manuscript marks it honestly according to the common view of the lexicons: what this commandment forbids is the unlawful killing of a human life. Its root is the sacredness of human life itself: man is made in the image of God, and to take a person's life unlawfully is to violate the dignity God placed in that person. The edge of this commandment today faces not only knives and guns, but every mindset that treats human life as grass; yet application is application and word meaning is word meaning — first distinguish them, then preach. That is this manuscript's rule.

The seventh commandment, verse 14: "Thou shalt not commit adultery." This commandment guards the boundary of marriage. God established marriage as the covenant union of one man and one woman; adultery is reaching out from within the covenant to take what does not belong to one. What it guards is not only an act, but the wholeness of a family, the trust of a spouse, and the security of children. When Scripture speaks of marriage, it never speaks only of personal feeling; it speaks of covenant faithfulness: if a person can be faithless in the most intimate relationship, there is no bottom line left in any other relationship. This commandment stands here to keep the gate for every family.

The eighth commandment, verse 15: "Thou shalt not steal." This commandment guards other people's property and labour. Theft takes many forms: taking openly is stealing; withholding in secret is stealing; using one's office, one's information, or another's ignorance of the rules to take advantage is, in nature, also taking what does not belong to one. That the Ten Commandments place this beside killing and adultery is no exaggeration: behind property stands a person. What is stolen is a thing; what is wounded is trust and livelihood. In the house of bondage, a slave's own body did not belong to him; people who have been brought out must learn to respect what belongs to others, because God has first given property and rest back to them.

The ninth commandment, verse 16: "Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour." The setting of this commandment is first of all the court: a witness's words can decide whether a person lives or dies, whether he is cleared or condemned. In a world without modern means of gathering evidence, false witness was the cheapest weapon — it could kill without a knife. The ninth commandment guards a neighbour's reputation and the justice of the process: the words of the mouth must match the facts; what is uncertain is not to be pronounced as settled; a lie that benefits oneself is still false witness. Today the cost of spreading a sentence is far lower than in the ancient world, and the harm far greater, so the lesson of this commandment is only more immediate: the mouth of a Christian should be a place where false witness cannot enter.

The tenth commandment, verse 17: "Thou shalt not covet." The text lists the objects in detail: "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's wife, nor his manservant, nor his maidservant, nor his ox, nor his ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour's." In this one verse the original uses the word for coveting twice [6] — first for the house, then for everything else. This commandment differs fundamentally from the ones before it: killing, adultery, stealing, and false witness all forbid outward acts; coveting forbids an inward state. No one is taken to court by his neighbour for mere coveting in the heart, because what is in the heart cannot be measured by a human court; yet in setting this commandment, God declares: His law governs not only the hand, but also the heart. That the Ten Commandments close with the heart is deeply meaningful: every outward evil act usually takes root in the heart first — first coveting another's house, and then the thought of stealing stirs; first coveting another's spouse, and then the step toward adultery; first coveting and not obtaining, and then hatred and murder are born. So the tenth commandment is like a key that turns back and unlocks the commandments before it: what God asks of His people is not only a bottom line of behaviour, but purity and contentment of heart. A person content in heart does not need prohibitions to keep his hand back; a person gnawed by coveting cannot be blocked by any number of articles. Let the homiletical point be stated here: the Ten Commandments begin with God and end with the human heart; the slab of stone outside is finally to be engraved on the tablet of the heart inside. That is the direction the later prophets and the New Testament repeatedly declare; this manuscript touches it here and does not expand it.

Gather the six commandments toward other people together: honouring parents establishes the family; the prohibition of killing guards life; of adultery, marriage; of stealing, property; of false witness, reputation and justice; and the prohibition of coveting closes into the heart. Six commandments, like six fences, enclose a life in which a community can dwell in peace. The fences are not the goal; that people within them can trust one another and live securely — that is the goal. And all of it still stands on the foundation of the prologue: precisely because God first brought this people out of the house of bondage, they are to learn to treat one another in the manner of people who have received grace, and no longer to live by Egypt's logic of plunder.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: A Mediator Amid the Thunder and an Altar of Earth — Fear, Reverence, and the Way of Atonement (20:18-26)

After the Ten Commandments are given, the text does not move straight on to the next set of laws; it first lets us watch the people's response, and this section must on no account be skipped. Verse 18: "And all the people saw the thunderings, and the lightnings, and the noise of the trumpet, and the mountain smoking: and when the people saw it, they removed, and stood afar off." Note that the people heard the Ten Commandments with their own ears; God's voice and the shaking of the mountain they experienced in person — and the result of the experience was not nearness, but trembling and withdrawal. In verse 19 they say to Moses: "Speak thou with us, and we will hear: but let not God speak with us, lest we die." This "lest we die" is man's truest self-knowledge before a holy God: those who heard God's voice discovered that their problem was not that they did not know the rules, but that they could not stand. The commandments lit up the standard, and also lit up how far man falls from the standard; the people's fear was not cowardly theatre, but the honest response under the presence of holiness.

Moses' answer, verse 20, is the pastoral hinge of the whole chapter: "Fear not: for God is come to prove you, and that his fear may be before your faces, that ye sin not." Distinguish the two kinds of fear. The people's fear is the fear that trembles, stands afar off, and wants to escape; the fear of God of which Moses speaks is the reverence that remains continually before Him, and therefore does not sin. Facing the same majesty of God, one kind of fear pushes a person away, and one kind of reverence keeps a person before God and changes his conduct. Moses' words bring out the connection: reverence and not sinning belong together. A person refrains from sin not necessarily because he fears being caught or fears disgrace; the road Moses gives is deeper: have His fear continually before your faces, and you will not sin. Reverence is not a one-time fright, but an abiding frame of heart: knowing that God is here, that He is holy, that His word is to be taken seriously — and so the hand draws back before it reaches out, and the heart is reminded at the very moment a thought moves. The word "prove" also deserves attention: God came not to crush the people with fright, but to reveal and to train their hearts, to see whether they take this voice seriously and take this God seriously. If fear stops at standing afar off, only panic remains; if fear is caught by Moses' words and turned into reverence, it becomes the starting point of obedience.

In verse 21 the picture divides into two sides: "And the people stood afar off, and Moses drew near unto the thick darkness where God was." On one side, distance; on the other, nearness. Between where the people withdrew to and where Moses walked to lies precisely the position of the mediator. The people asked Moses to hear for them and to speak for them, and Moses stood between God and man: toward God, he drew near to the thick darkness; toward the people, he brought the word back. The pattern of the mediator is set up in this verse: where most people cannot stand, one man stands for them; words most people cannot bear to hear, one man receives for them. This is still only the pattern, not the end of the story. Here a foreshadowing from a later reading may be mentioned, and it must be marked as a later reading: in Deuteronomy chapter 18, Moses looks back on this scene at Sinai and says that the people had asked not to hear God's voice again, that God granted it, and that He promised to raise up a Prophet like Moses, who would speak God's words to the people; the New Testament then connects this promise with the coming of Jesus Christ. This manuscript marks this only as the later reading and direction of fulfilment in Deuteronomy and the New Testament. What the text of Exodus 20 itself says at the time is the fact itself: the people ask for a mediator, and Moses draws near to the thick darkness. Foreshadowing is foreshadowing, and fact is fact; keep them labelled separately, so that your examination is clear.

The last section, verses 22 to 26, seems to turn suddenly to the ordinances of the altar, but in fact it fits the whole chapter seamlessly. God first reminds them: "Ye have seen that I have talked with you from heaven." Then He commands: "Ye shall not make with me gods of silver, neither shall ye make unto you gods of gold." Having just heard "no graven image," the people hear it restated at once: the first temptation of people who have received the commandments is to manufacture a visible substitute for God, and the more costly the gold and silver, the nearer the idol. Then comes the altar: "An altar of earth thou shalt make unto me, and shalt sacrifice thereon thy burnt offerings, and thy peace offerings, thy sheep, and thine oxen: in all places where I record my name I will come unto thee, and I will bless thee. And if thou wilt make me an altar of stone, thou shalt not build it of hewn stone: for if thou lift up thy tool upon it, thou hast polluted it. Neither shalt thou go up by steps unto mine altar, that thy nakedness be not discovered thereon." Notice the common temper of these rules: earth, unhewn stone, no steps. Earth is the most ordinary of materials; the stone is to keep its natural form; and for a man going up to the altar, even the dignity of steps is unnecessary — what matters first is covering and humility. Immediately after giving the Ten Commandments, what God gives next is not the blueprint of a palace and not the pageantry of kingship, but the ordinances of an altar: a plain altar, buildable anywhere, for burnt offerings and peace offerings. Why? Here it must be marked that what follows is exposition at the level of preaching, not the direct statement of the original text. What the original text directly states is the ordinances on the altar's materials and construction. What this sermon expounds, from the movement of the whole chapter, is this: people who have sinned, who have just trembled amid the thunder, need first of all not dignity but atonement; and God, immediately after giving commandments that expose sin, gives an altar at which sacrifice can be offered, approach can be made, and blessing received — setting the way out of grace right beside the law. The commandments make man know his sin; the altar gives man a road back. Without the commandments, man does not know where he is sick; without the altar, man knows he is sick but has nowhere to seek the cure. On that one day at Sinai, God gave both. The plainness of worship also reminds man: drawing near to God rests not on gold and silver, not on craftsmanship, and not on dignity built up by steps, but on coming to the altar humbly, with an offering, as He has commanded. To a generation fond of turning worship into performance, this lesson remains a quiet and severe word.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 20. Remember first the order: "I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage" — redemption first, commandments after; the Ten Commandments are the pattern of life for people who have received grace, not a ticket with which to buy salvation. Remember next the structure: four commandments toward God fix the object, the manner, the Name, and the sabbath of worship; six commandments toward other people, from honouring father and mother to not coveting, fence in life fence by fence, and close at last in the human heart. Keep also the two closing pictures of the chapter: the people standing afar off, and Moses drawing near to the thick darkness, with the mediator standing between God and man; and at the foot of the mountain an altar of earth — no gold or silver, no hewn stone, no steps — where people who have sinned come and find the road of atonement and blessing. May we both fear Him and not sin, and also, because of the altar prepared, draw near to Him with confidence: first living in grace, and then living out the commandments.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You that You first brought us out of the house of bondage, and then told us Your heart, commandment by commandment. Forgive the coveting in our hearts, the vanity in our mouths, and the unrighteousness in our hands; give us hearts that fear You continually, so that we do not sin. Thank You for preparing an altar beside the commandments, so that those who know their sin have a road back. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

One passage says remember, the other says keep: remembering is the root of keeping, and keeping is remembering in action. The difference between the two words is a fact of the text; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's judgment in the text — that the two sabbath reasons (creation and exodus) complement rather than contradict each other — is stated honestly side by side with this difference of wording.

[3] kabbed: Hebrew כַּבֵּד (H3513), the intensive imperative of kaved, whose basic sense relates to being heavy, weighty, and hence to honouring and glorifying; Exodus 20:12, "Honour thy father and thy mother," uses this word. Honouring is therefore not only a compliant attitude, but treating one's parents as people of weight — honouring them, providing for them, and caring for them tenderly; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's exposition in the text, that honouring includes providing and tender care, is a homiletical exposition drawn from this word meaning.

[4] lo tirtsach: Hebrew לֹא תִּרְצָח; lo (לֹא) is the negation, and tirtsach derives from ratsach, רָצַח (H7523), to murder, to slay — the unlawful taking of a human life; Exodus 20:13 is this sentence. The range of this word does not extend to every taking of life: for war, for judicial punishment, and for manslaughter, Scripture uses different words and different laws, as in the distinction Exodus 21 draws between intentional killing and manslaughter. The marking in this manuscript's text follows the common view of BDB and HALOT, stated honestly, neither enlarged nor narrowed.

[5] 'el qanna': Hebrew אֵל קַנָּא; 'el (אֵל, H410) is God, and qanna' (קַנָּא, H7067) is jealous, demanding singleness; Exodus 20:5, "I the LORD thy God am a jealous God," uses this expression, which also appears in passages such as Deuteronomy 4:24. The expression describes God's demand, within the covenant relation, for single-hearted faithfulness, as the covenant of marriage admits no third party; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's statement in the text, that this jealousy is not pettiness, is a homiletical exposition drawn from the covenant context, stated separately from the lexical facts.

[6] chamad: Hebrew חָמַד (H2530), to covet, to desire strongly, to lust after what belongs to another; within the single verse of Exodus 20:17 this word appears twice — once for coveting a neighbour's house, and once for coveting his wife, his manservant, his maidservant, his ox, his ass, and all that is his. The tenth commandment therefore forbids an inward coveting, in distinction from the outward acts forbidden by the preceding commandments; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's exposition in the text, that the Ten Commandments close with the heart, is a homiletical exposition drawn from this word meaning and from the arrangement of the commandments.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage." (Exodus 20:2, KJV) "Fear not: for God is come to prove you, and that his fear may be before your faces, that ye sin not." (Exodus 20:20, KJV)

When you read the Ten Commandments, read the first sentence first. God did not begin with "thou shalt not"; He began with "I am": I am the LORD your God, and I brought you out of the house of bondage. Grace walks ahead of the commandments, and the order must not be reversed. Much of our weariness in faith comes from reading the order backwards — as if God would love us only after we had kept everything well, as if He would be pleased only after we had reached the standard. The order at Sinai is clear: first led out, first loved, first made His people — and only then, commandment by commandment, learning to live according to His heart. Read the commandments slowly today, and before each one answer that opening word in your heart: You have already saved me, so this one I am willing to learn. The hardest may be coveting: seeing what another has, the heart feels a piece missing. At the end of the Ten Words God waits for us, to give contentment back to us. And may His fear be always before our faces: reverence is not hiding far away, but knowing that He is here, holy and loving, so the hand does not reach out wrongly and the heart is not moved astray. Where reverence stands, sin steps back; grace first, reverence following — this is the daily road of a redeemed person. And if today we have fallen again at one of the commandments, let us not hide far away like the people did; there is an altar at the foot of the mountain, and the altar points to the One who Himself stands between God and man — return before Him, and there is still atonement, forgiveness, and the grace of a new beginning waiting for us.

## Summary (about 100 words)

Moses kept sheep in Midian for forty years, and at Mount Horeb he saw a bush burning with fire yet not consumed. God called him from the flame, told him to remove his sandals and stand on holy ground, declared that He had seen, heard, and known the affliction of His people, and sent him to Pharaoh to bring the people out of Egypt. Moses asked who he was and asked God's name; God answered: I will be with thee, and revealed His name as "I AM THAT I AM."

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Today we come to Exodus chapter 3. This is one of the most important encounters in the whole Bible. An eighty-year-old shepherd, deep in the wilderness, saw a bush catch fire. A bush catching fire in the wilderness was in itself nothing rare; what was rare was that the fire burned, yet the bush was not consumed. Moses drew near to look, and God called him from the midst of the bush. After this chapter, the history of the Israelites, the life of Moses, and the name of the God we believe in today were all different.

First, keep Moses' forty years in your heart. In the previous chapter Moses was forty years old and thought himself a deliverer: he was a prince in Pharaoh's palace, trained in all the learning of Egypt; he struck down an Egyptian who was oppressing his fellow Hebrew, thinking his brethren would understand that God was delivering them by his hand. Instead his brethren did not accept him, Pharaoh sought to kill him, and he fled to Midian. At the well he again stood up against injustice and helped the seven daughters of the priest of Midian. It looked as if he were still the same Moses: full of zeal, capable, unable to watch people being bullied. But the Bible gives only one extremely quiet sentence about his years in Midian: Moses dwelt in Midian, and he kept the flock of Jethro his father-in-law. For forty years he went from prince to shepherd, from palace to wilderness, from a man mighty in words and deeds to one who would later call himself slow of speech and of a slow tongue. For forty years nothing great happened: no vision, no calling, and even his name was nearly forgotten. In Egypt the people were still groaning in the brick kilns; in Midian Moses every day drove another man's sheep along the paths another man's sheep had walked.

Many people's lives also have a Midian like this. The ambitions of youth have cooled, the zeal of former days has been worn smooth, the days repeat one after another, and you think God has set you aside and your story has already been written to its end. Exodus chapter 3 tells us: no. Midian is not the end of the story; Midian is preparation. God had not forgotten the people in Egypt, and He had not forgotten Moses in the wilderness. Do you still remember the four verbs at the end of the previous chapter? God heard their groaning, God remembered His covenant, God saw, and God knew. Today, in this chapter, God turns hearing, remembering, seeing, and knowing into action; and the way He acts is first to call a person.

Let us read together several key passages from today:

"Now Moses kept the flock of Jethro his father in law, the priest of Midian: and he led the flock to the backside of the desert, and came to the mountain of God, even to Horeb. And the angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush: and he looked, and, behold, the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed." (Exodus 3:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And when the LORD saw that he turned aside to see, God called unto him out of the midst of the bush, and said, Moses, Moses. And he said, Here am I. And he said, Draw not nigh hither: put off thy shoes from off thy feet, for the place whereon thou standest is holy ground." (Exodus 3:4-5, excerpt, KJV)

"And the LORD said, I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt, and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters; for I know their sorrows; And I am come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians." (Exodus 3:7-8, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses said unto God, Who am I, that I should go unto Pharaoh, and that I should bring forth the children of Israel out of Egypt? And he said, Certainly I will be with thee." (Exodus 3:11-12, excerpt, KJV)

"And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM: and he said, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you. ... The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, hath sent me unto you: this is my name for ever, and this is my memorial unto all generations." (Exodus 3:14-15, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, many among us are also in Midian, also thinking in repetitive days that You have forgotten us. Speak to us today through the flame of the bush, let us hear You calling our names, and let us also hear Your own name. Let us know that when You send a person, it is never because of what qualifications that person has, but because You Yourself are present. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Forty Years in Midian, and a Bush That Would Not Burn Up (3:1-6)

First look at the scene. Moses kept his father-in-law's flock, and "he led the flock to the backside of the desert, and came to the mountain of God, even to Horeb." Mount Horeb was later also called Mount Sinai, the very place where Israel would receive the law. On this day Moses walked farther than usual. He did not know how this day differed from any other day. A calling often comes like this: not at a carefully arranged retreat, not when you feel you are ready, but on an ordinary workday, while you are driving the sheep and doing the work you have repeated for forty years, God appears. So, brothers and sisters, do not despise your daily routine. If God is going to call you, He will call you within your daily routine. You do not need first to leave your post to find God; God will come to your post to find you.

The bush, in the original seneh (סְנֶה, H5572), is the most ordinary, most worthless thorny shrub of the wilderness [1]. Not a cedar, not an olive tree, but a thorn bush. The place God chose for His appearing was not a temple, not a palace, but in a bush; the person God chose for His appearing was not a priest, not a king, but a fugitive shepherd. Here already is the flavor of the gospel: God delights to appear in lowly places, and God delights to use lowly vessels. The bush later became a mark by which Israel remembered God's appearing; when Moses blessed Joseph at the end of his life, he still called the LORD "him that dwelt in the bush." A bush that would not burn up is also like a portrait of Israel itself: burned for hundreds of years in Egypt's fiery furnace, refined by suffering, yet not consumed, because God was in their midst.

"The angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush." Fire in the Bible is often a sign of God's presence: when God made a covenant with Abraham, a smoking furnace and a burning lamp passed between the pieces; when God led Israel out of Egypt, there was a pillar of fire; when God descended on Mount Sinai, the whole mountain smoked; when the Holy Spirit descended at Pentecost, there were tongues like fire. But what made Moses stop here was not the fire; it was "the bush was not consumed." Moses said: "I will now turn aside, and see this great sight, why the bush is not burnt." Note that God waited until Moses turned aside and drew near before He called. The Scripture says: "And when the LORD saw that he turned aside to see, God called unto him." Brothers and sisters, God's calling often waits for a person willing to stop, to turn aside, and to draw near to look. In forty years in Midian, how many times Moses drove sheep past this mountain we do not know; but on this day, he stopped. Busyness can make us miss a vision, and numbness can make us blind to what God is doing. If today you are willing, in worship and in reading the Scripture, to stop a moment and turn aside, God may, in the very moment you stop, open His mouth and call your name.

"Moses, Moses!" God called him by name, doubled — and every time this doubled calling occurs in the Bible, its weight is very great: God called Abraham, Abraham! Abraham! on the mountain of the offering of Isaac; God called Jacob in the night of his fear; the Lord called Samuel when he did not yet know the LORD. To be called by God with your name doubled means God knows you — not as one person in a crowd, but you, this person. Moses' answer was only one sentence: "Here am I." The original is hineni, the answer of a servant before his master and of a child before his father: here I am, I am listening, I am ready. Moses said this, Abraham said this, and Isaiah would later say it too. The story of a calling often begins with this simple answer. God does not need you first to deliver a speech; what God wants is this one sentence from you: here I am.

Next comes the command: "Draw not nigh hither: put off thy shoes from off thy feet, for the place whereon thou standest is holy ground." Removing one's shoes was the etiquette of entering a sanctuary or the home of an honored elder in the East, and also the posture of a servant: standing barefoot before the master. Moses had worn palace shoes in his life, had walked the road of flight, and now before God he must remove his shoes. Removing the shoes means: take off your past, take off your identity, take off your ability to walk by yourself; before God you are only a barefoot person. And why is this ground holy? Yesterday this ground was still grazing ground; today it is still the same ground, the soil the same soil, the stones the same stones. It became holy ground not because there was anything rare about the location, not because Mount Horeb had some special quality, but because God was present here. "Holy," in the original qadosh (קָדוֹשׁ, H6918), means set apart, consecrated [2]. Holy ground is not a rarity of location; it is God's presence that makes ground holy. This matters greatly for us today: what makes worship holy is not the building, not the lighting, not the atmosphere, but God's presence; what makes a person holy is not the environment, not the origin, but God dwelling in him. Wherever God is present, there is holy ground; your home, your workstation, your hospital ward can all, because of God's presence, become holy ground.

Then God introduced Himself: "I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." Please note: God did not first introduce His power; He first introduced His relationship. He was not a strange god; He was the God of Moses' forefathers, the One who called Abraham out of Ur, prepared a ram on Mount Moriah, and appeared to Jacob at Bethel. Moses may have thought, through forty years in Midian, that his line to the story of the forefathers had been cut; with one sentence God joined the line again: you are not a rootless person, your story is joined to the story of the forefathers, and My calling of you is also joined to the same covenant. When Moses heard this, he hid his face, for he was afraid to look upon God. In the Bible, no one who truly met God was casual about it. Reverence is the natural reaction to meeting the holy God. Today, as we draw near to God, we must have both the openness of children and the reverence of servants. Openness without reverence turns God into a tool; reverence without openness turns God into a distant judge. The first lesson Moses learned before the bush was not ability, but reverence: first remove the shoes, first hide the face, first confess that He is God and I am a man.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Seen, Heard, Known — God's Declaration and Moses' Two Questions (3:7-15)

The first thing God spoke of was not the sending, but His own heart. From verse 7 to verse 10, God personally repeated those verbs from the end of the previous chapter, and spoke them with even greater weight: "I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt, and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters; for I know their sorrows." Seen, heard, known. This is God's threefold assurance to sufferers. His seeing is not spectating, His hearing is not passing by, and His knowing is an intimate knowing, like a father who knows his children's crying in the night. Brothers and sisters, have you ever had such a night: you felt that no one saw your suffering, no one heard your prayer, no one knew your heart? Exodus chapter 3 is God's own answer to you: I have surely seen, I have also heard, I know. Each of these three verbs carries the weight of a completed action: it was not that He saw only today, but that He had seen all along; it was not that He heard by chance, but that as soon as the cry rose, He heard it. God remembers His covenant not because the people deserve it, but because He is a covenant-keeping God. In Genesis He had already told Abraham: thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs, and shall serve them four hundred years; and also that nation shall I judge, and afterward shall they come out with great substance. Today the time had come, and He announced from the bush: I am come down to deliver them.

Note this phrase: "I am come down." God is the omnipresent God, yet He says He came down. From the tower of Babel in Genesis to Exodus, the Bible again and again uses this phrase for God's stooping toward suffering people. God's salvation is never directing from a distance; He is the God who personally draws near, personally intervenes, and personally acts. He would deliver the people out of the hand of the Egyptians and bring them into a good land and a large, a land flowing with milk and honey. In God's hands, salvation always has two sides: what you are delivered from, and what you are brought into. To be delivered from Egypt is only half the journey; to enter the promised land is to complete it. When God saves us, He likewise never only causes us to escape the bondage of sin; He also leads us into the abundance He has prepared.

Then came the sending: "Come now therefore, and I will send thee unto Pharaoh, that thou mayest bring forth my people the children of Israel out of Egypt." Please read this sentence together with what came before. God said He had seen, He had heard, He knew, He had come down to deliver — and then He said: I will send thee. God's deliverance and God's sending are two sides of the same matter. God could fully have performed signs Himself and brought the people out of Egypt; He chose instead to use a Moses. God could fully have comforted sufferers Himself; He chose instead to send a person to stand before Pharaoh. It is the same today: God sees the suffering people of this city, and the ones God sends are we who have heard the calling. The church's mission does not come from human zeal; it comes from God's seeing: because He saw, therefore we go.

Moses' first question came quickly: "Who am I, that I should go unto Pharaoh, and that I should bring forth the children of Israel out of Egypt?" This question has two layers. On the surface it is humility; underneath it is forty years of wounds. The Moses of forty years earlier would not have asked this question: then he was a prince, he considered himself qualified, he went out on his own initiative to save people, and the result was that a fellow Hebrew threw him back to where he came from with one sentence — "Who made thee a prince and a judge over us?" — and Pharaoh sought to kill him. Forty years later he had learned one thing: I cannot. On his record was the case of a fugitive who had killed a man; his own self-assessment was that he was slow of speech; his identity was that of a son-in-law keeping another man's sheep; in Egypt's palace he had long been a wanted man, and among the Israelites he had long lost credibility. From a human standpoint, everything he said was fact: Who am I? I am nothing.

God's answer, however, was not on the line of qualifications at all. God did not encourage him by saying: Moses, you are actually very gifted, you were educated in Egypt's palace, you speak two languages, do not belittle yourself. God did not list his résumé; God gave only one sentence: "Certainly I will be with thee." This is the most important sentence about calling in this chapter, and in the whole Bible: the foundation of being sent is not a person's qualifications, but God's presence. God did not deny that Moses could not; God changed the question: the question is not who you are; the question is who is with you. You go to Pharaoh not because you are qualified, but because I go with you; you bring the people out not by your ability, but with My presence as the guarantee. Brothers and sisters, how much service in the church today is stuck exactly here: we first calculate our qualifications — education, eloquence, experience, what others think — and calculating and calculating, we calculate ourselves into retreat. God's arithmetic is different from ours. When God calls a person, He first gives the promise of His presence, and then gives the road to walk; if a person waits until the qualifications are complete before being willing to go, in most cases he will never go. Abraham went without knowing where he was going, Gideon considered himself the poorest and the least, Mary was only a virgin, Peter was a fisherman. When God chooses a person, He does not look at the register of qualifications; He looks at this: are you willing to let His presence become your only qualification?

God also gave a token: "When thou hast brought forth the people out of Egypt, ye shall serve God upon this mountain: this shall be a token unto thee, that I sent thee." This token is very special: the evidence is not before you go, but after you have gone. One day you will serve Me on this mountain — when that day comes, you will know that today's calling in the bush was real. The road of faith is often like this: obedience first, evidence afterward; take the step first, and see afterward. We always want to receive the full guarantee first and act afterward; God instead says, you go first, and when you reach that step and look back, you will understand it all.

Moses' second question went one layer deeper: "Behold, when I come unto the children of Israel, and shall say unto them, The God of your fathers hath sent me unto you; and they shall say to me, What is his name? what shall I say unto them?" This question was not looking for an excuse; it was a real question. A nation that had lived in Egypt for four hundred years was surrounded by the names of Egypt's gods: the sun god, the Nile god, the frog god, the cattle god, each with a name and a surname. The God of your fathers — what is His name? This question is also the question everyone who proclaims the gospel today will be asked: the God you believe in, who exactly is He? How is He different from other gods? Moses wanted a name, and God gave him the greatest revelation in the Bible.

"And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM." In the original it is 'ehyeh 'asher 'ehyeh (אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה, H1961, from the verb hayah, to be, to exist, to become) [3]. The renderings of this sentence, across the generations, have several readings among scholars, presented here together without asserting any single one as the definitive conclusion: it can be rendered I AM WHO I AM; it can also, according to the force of the verb's imperfect form, be rendered I will be what I will be, carrying the sense of His sure presence and sure accomplishment as a promise; it can also be understood as meaning that His existence depends on nothing else, that He Himself is the ground of His own existence — self-existent and eternal. Rendered in English, the name declares Him "self-existent and eternal," grasping exactly this layer: He is not created, not defined by others, not dependent on the people's offerings for His existence; all things exist by Him, and He exists by Himself. The gods of Egypt all had origin stories, all had territories, all had weaknesses; the God of Israel has no origin — He is the beginning, He is subject to no control, and His name is His very existence.

What is more important is that this name is joined to the promise just given. "I AM" and "I will be with thee" use the same verb root in Hebrew: the word He used when He said "Certainly I will be with thee" is precisely ehyeh — I will be, I will be present. In other words, God's name is His promise, and His promise is written into His name: I am the One who will surely be with thee. If they ask who He is, tell them: He is the One who is self-existent and eternal, and who is personally present and never absent. Other gods require people to serve them, provide for them, and guess at their intentions; this God's name guarantees to people: I will be there, and I will accomplish what I have promised. This was the answer Moses truly needed — not an academic definition, but a name he could carry into Pharaoh's palace, to the shore of the Red Sea, and through forty years in the wilderness.

Immediately in verse 15, God made this revelation concrete as a name that can be called upon: "Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, hath sent me unto you." The LORD is the traditional reading of the four-letter divine name YHWH (יְהוָה, H3068) [4]. This name is "my name for ever, and this is my memorial unto all generations." God bound together His eternal existence and His faithfulness in history in one revelation: He is both the self-existent One, and the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob — that is to say, He is the One who once called the forefathers, promised the forefathers, and never once broke faith. God's revelation goes before His action: He first told Moses who He is, and only then sent Moses to act; first knowing His name, one has the courage to walk His road. Our faith today is the same: it is not that we first accomplish something for God and then come to know Him; it is that we first know who He is, and only then can we accomplish what He sends us to do. Preaching, Bible study, and worship matter precisely because mission always begins with knowing God.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: They Will Hearken, Pharaoh Will Not Let Go, You Will Not Go Empty — God Previews the Whole Course (3:16-22)

Having received the promise of presence and the name of God, Moses was then told by God, in one telling, the whole course of what would happen after he went. Verses 16 to 22 are a complete preview, with three matters, each worth our slow reading.

The first matter: the elders will hearken. God told Moses to gather the elders of Israel and tell them, and He specifically instructed him to begin with these words: "The LORD God of your fathers ... appeared unto me, saying, I have surely visited you." Here "visited," in the original, is a doubled expression of great weight, paqod paqadti (פָּקֹד פָּקַדְתִּי, H6485, to visit, to care for, to muster) [5]. The older brothers and sisters will weep as soon as they hear this word, because it is an echo waited for for hundreds of years: in the last chapter of Genesis, Joseph on his deathbed said to his brethren: "I die: and God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land unto the land which he sware to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob." Joseph also made them swear to carry up his bones when the time came. Hundreds of years passed, and Joseph's words were handed down generation after generation in the huts of slaves: God will visit, God will surely visit. Today God said to Moses at the bush: go and tell them, that word has been fulfilled — I have surely visited you. Paqad is not merely a visit; it carries the sense of mustering, remembering, counting by name: God had not missed a single one. When the elders heard this sentence, they would surely bow and worship, because they recognized it: these were not words Moses had made up; this was the watchword handed down from the forefathers, the proof of God's covenant-keeping. Brothers and sisters, God's promises may be fulfilled only after hundreds of years, after days you cannot see within your lifetime, but not one word of His falls empty. If today you still hold in your hand a promise He gave you, even if you have held it for decades until your hand aches, do not let go: the God who said paqod paqadti will, in His time, personally come to muster His people, not one missing.

The second matter: Pharaoh will not let go. God did not coax Moses. He did not say: go, and everything will go smoothly. He said plainly: "And I am sure that the king of Egypt will not let you go, no, not by a mighty hand." The sending includes hardship, and God laid it out from the beginning. Pharaoh's heart would be hardened, the negotiations would repeat, the plagues would fall one after another, and there would be murmuring even among the people themselves. This is a very great comfort to us today: a true calling does not mean a smooth road, and God's presence with you does not mean Pharaoh immediately lets go. Sometimes, as soon as we meet obstruction, we doubt the calling: did I hear wrongly? Is God not here? Exodus chapter 3 says: the obstruction was in the preview from the start, the hardship was in the plan from the start. God's presence is not a charm that exempts us from hardship; God's presence is the guarantee of passing through hardship. He went on to say: "And I will stretch out my hand, and smite Egypt with all my wonders which I will do in the midst thereof: and after that he will let you go." Note the subject: the One who stretches out His hand and performs the wonders is God, not Moses. Moses' responsibility is to go and speak, to go and stand, to go and deliver the message; the One who makes Pharaoh's hand release is God's mighty hand. When we serve God, we must distinguish clearly which part is ours and which part is God's: faithfulness is ours, the outcome is God's. A person who carries the outcome on his own shoulders will sooner or later be crushed; a person who entrusts his faithfulness to God can walk into Pharaoh's palace again and again until the ten plagues are finished.

The third matter: when you leave Egypt, you will not go empty. The end of the preview is unexpectedly warm: "And I will give this people favour in the sight of the Egyptians: and it shall come to pass, that, when ye go, ye shall not go empty: But every woman shall borrow of her neighbour, and of her that sojourneth in her house, jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and raiment: and ye shall put them upon your sons, and upon your daughters; and ye shall spoil the Egyptians." God had already prophesied this sentence in Genesis chapter 15: He told Abraham that after four hundred years of sojourning, they would come out with great substance. Today He made the details clear: it was not robbery; the Egyptians would give willingly. It was not charity from the rich; it was several hundred years of slave wages settled in one payment. God is a righteous God; the sweat His people sweated in Egypt, the blows they took, the infants who died young — He kept account of them all; on the night of the exodus, He would make Egypt pay out the wages it owed, principal and interest together. Not going empty — this is also a word for those who suffer loss today: if God leads you out of your Egypt, He will not let you leave with both hands empty. What you lost in suffering, God will repay in His time and in His way; the years you endured will never be wasted in vain. The gold and silver spoiled from Egypt were later used by the people to build the tabernacle: what is recovered through suffering is finally used in the worship of God — this is the most beautiful economics Exodus gives us.

The three previewed matters joined together are exactly the outline of the whole Book of Exodus: the elders will hearken, Pharaoh will not let go, and in the end you will not go empty. Before sending him, God first told Moses the ending, so that Moses could stand firm amid the hardship in the middle. Brothers and sisters, God has also told us the ending today: He has written plainly in the Bible that His kingdom will surely come, His people will surely be redeemed, and our ending is not in Pharaoh's hand, but in the hand of the self-existent God. Only those who have seen the ending can endure the middle.

Applying this chapter to ourselves, I want to speak to four kinds of people. The first kind: those who feel they have no qualifications. You say my education is not high, my eloquence is not good, my past has stains, and my age is not small anymore. Moses' answer was even fuller than yours: he was eighty years old, he had a criminal record, and he was slow of speech. God did not use his qualifications; God used His presence. If today God has placed something in your heart, do not first convene a qualifications-review meeting; first hear that sentence, "Certainly I will be with thee." The second kind: those wearing away their days in Midian. Your forty years may still be continuing: repetitive days, an unclear future. Please believe this: the wilderness is not abandonment, and keeping sheep is not the end. God remembers His time; you simply keep the sheep faithfully, and the flame may kindle on the next ordinary day. The third kind: those who fear obstruction. You worry that as soon as you open your mouth you will be refused, and as soon as you act you will meet a Pharaoh. Pharaoh will not let go — God said so long ago; but God also said His mighty hand will stretch out. You need not fear the ending, because He has already written the ending. The fourth kind: those who do not know this God. Today you have heard His name: He is the self-existent and eternal One, the One who sees, hears, and knows your suffering. In the New Testament the Lord Jesus said with His own mouth: "Before Abraham was, I am" — using precisely this declaration, "I am." This is the New Testament's reading of this Lord, and according to the New Testament's witness we believe that He is this eternal "I AM" come among men. You do not need first to repair yourself before drawing near to Him; He called Moses at the bush, and today He is also calling you: remove your shoes, draw near, and He will be with you.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 3. Moses kept sheep in Midian for forty years and saw a bush that would not burn up; God called his name from the flame, told him to remove his shoes and stand on holy ground, and declared that He had seen, heard, and known the affliction of His people; Moses asked, Who am I? and God answered, I will be with thee; Moses asked, What is Thy name? and God answered, I AM THAT I AM. What this chapter gives us is not a set of success techniques; it is God's introduction of Himself: the foundation of being sent is not a person's qualifications, but God's presence and His name; holy ground is not a rarity of location, but God's presence making ground holy; and God's revelation goes before His action — He first lets us know who He is, and only then sends us to do what He wants done.

Let us pray together: Self-existent and eternal Lord, thank You: You are not a distant God; You are the God who sees, hears, and knows. Those in Midian You have not forgotten; those suffering in Egypt, not one have You missed. Forgive us for refusing Your calling with our qualifications and missing Your vision through our busyness. Teach us to answer You as Moses did: Here am I. And teach us, in every moment of fear, first to remember Your name: You are the "I AM" who will surely be with us. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who with His own mouth said "I am" and who is now with us, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] seneh: Hebrew סְנֶה (H5572), a thorn bush, a thorny shrub; Exodus 3:2-4 uses this word four times in the vision of the bush and the flame, and Deuteronomy 33:16 also uses this word when Moses, blessing Joseph, calls the LORD "him that dwelt in the bush." The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This word sounds close to "Sinai"; a derivation linking the two has been discussed among scholars — there are views that the name Sinai may be related to this bush, and there are also differing opinions. This manuscript records it only as a brief note and draws no conclusion; the text bases no exposition on it, and this marks the distinction between fact and the state of scholarly discussion.

[2] qadosh: Hebrew קָדוֹשׁ (H6918), holy, set apart, consecrated; Exodus 3:5, "the place whereon thou standest is holy ground," uses its adjectival form for the ground, meaning ground set apart as holy. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's statement in the text, "holy ground is not a rarity of location; it is God's presence that makes ground holy," is a homiletical exposition based on the context (the ground was originally grazing ground and was set apart because of God's appearing), and is noted as exposition.

[3] 'ehyeh 'asher 'ehyeh: Hebrew אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה (Exodus 3:14); 'ehyeh derives from the verb hayah (הָיָה, H1961, to be, to exist, to become, to be present), in the first-person imperfect form. The renderings of the whole sentence, across the generations, are presented together in their several forms: I AM WHO I AM (KJV: I AM THAT I AM); I will be what I will be (the reading of some modern translations and scholars, taking the future and present force of the imperfect); and readings emphasizing God's self-existence and faithfulness. The LXX renders it ego eimi ho on ("I am the Being"). The meanings and grammar are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript presents the several views together in the text and does not assert any single one as the definitive conclusion; "God's name is the promise of His presence" is a homiletical exposition linking verse 12, where the same root ehyeh appears ("I will be with thee"), and is noted as exposition.

[4] YHWH: the four-letter divine name יהוה (H3068), revealed in Exodus 3:15 as God's name forever and His memorial unto all generations. Out of reverence, the Jewish tradition avoided pronouncing it and read Adonai (Lord) in its place; English translations traditionally render it as "the LORD" (in full capitals), and it is commonly transliterated "Yahweh." Its original pronunciation is now commonly reconstructed as Yahweh; this is a scholarly reconstruction, and this manuscript records it only as a brief note. The meanings and usage are according to BDB and HALOT. See Exodus 6:2-3, where God tells Moses that He appeared to the forefathers as God Almighty but was not known to them by His name Jehovah, as cross-evidence for this manuscript's "revelation goes before action."

[5] paqod paqadti: Hebrew פָּקֹד פָּקַדְתִּי (H6485, paqad, to visit, to care for, to muster, to remember); Exodus 3:16 uses the emphatic construction of the infinitive with the perfect form to say "I have surely visited you." The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This word forms an echo with the promise of Genesis 50:24-25: Joseph on his deathbed said "God will surely visit (paqod yiphqod) you," and Exodus 3:16 announces the fulfillment of the promise with the same verb; this manuscript has made this echo explicit in the text.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Moses said unto God, Who am I, that I should go unto Pharaoh, and that I should bring forth the children of Israel out of Egypt? And he said, Certainly I will be with thee." (Exodus 3:11-12, KJV) "And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM." (Exodus 3:14, KJV)

For forty years in Midian, Moses had watched himself become smaller: older, stripped of ability, even unable to speak well. He asked God: Who am I? God did not answer his question about qualifications; God gave him a greater answer: I will be with thee. Brothers and sisters, when we pray, we too keep staring at our own insufficiency: not smart enough, not brave enough, not clean enough. But God's eyes on us are different. When He calls a person, He never looks first at the résumé; He first reveals His own name: I AM THAT I AM — I will be there, and I will accomplish what I promise. If today there is something in your heart that God has moved you to do, and you have kept refusing with "I cannot," take your eyes off yourself and put them on His name. You cannot, but He can; you change, but He does not; your presence may fail, but His presence never does. Answer Him first with that one sentence: Lord, here am I. The rest of the road, He will walk with you step by step to the end. Take the matter you have dragged on for so long before Him today; do not wait until you have become strong to begin. When you take the first step, you will discover that the bush that would not burn up has been waiting there for you all along, and the God who calls you has been waiting there for you all along too.

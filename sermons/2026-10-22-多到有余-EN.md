# More Than Enough

## A Sermon on Exodus 36:1-38

Preacher: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries

Note on the Text: This is an English translation of the Chinese original sermon manuscript; draft translation, to be proofread (初译待校). Scripture quotations are from the King James Version (KJV), aligned verse by verse with the Chinese text.

Text: Exodus 36:1-38
Connection: Exodus 35 records Moses assembling all the congregation, proclaiming the Sabbath command, and calling everyone whose heart stirred him and everyone willing to bring an offering, while naming Bezalel and Aholiab as filled with the Spirit of wisdom; Exodus 36 is the first scene in which that call lands — the workers have arrived, the materials have arrived, and the work has begun.

Abstract: This chapter records the formal start of the tabernacle's construction: the craftsmen set to work with the wisdom they have received, and the people's daily offerings become so abundant that Moses orders them stopped; then the ten curtains, the eleven curtains of goats' hair, the boards and bars, the veil of the Holy of Holies, and the screen for the door are made in turn. In the perfective style of completion, the text restates the pattern of chapter 26, showing a people who once cast a golden calf now bringing their gold, willingly and obediently, to the right place.

## Sermon Text

### I. 00:00-08:00 The Workers Have Arrived: The Call Becomes Work (Exod 36:1)

Brothers and sisters, let us first put this chapter back in its proper place. From chapter 25 to chapter 31, Moses is on the mountain, and the LORD shows him the pattern of the tabernacle piece by piece — from the ark to the lampstand, from the curtains to the altar — with every dimension, material, and method spelled out clearly. That is the blueprint, the command, the drawing not yet begun. In chapters 32 to 34, disaster strikes below the mountain: the people cast a golden calf, Moses breaks the tablets, intercedes for the people, and the LORD gives two new tablets, so that the covenant relationship is restored amid judgment and mercy. In chapter 35, Moses comes down and assembles all the congregation, first restating the Sabbath command, then laying the needs of the tabernacle before everyone, and announcing by name: the LORD has called Bezalel, filled him with the Spirit of God in wisdom, understanding, and knowledge in all manner of workmanship, and has given Aholiab and every wise-hearted person a share in the same gift.

At verse 1 of chapter 36, the text offers no further explanation; it goes straight to work. "Then wrought Bezaleel and Aholiab, and every wise hearted man, in whom the LORD put wisdom and understanding to know how to work all manner of work for the service of the sanctuary, according to all that the LORD had commanded" (Exod 36:1, KJV). Notice the tone of this verse: it is not a suggestion, not a discussion, and not a wait for a better moment. The call was issued in the previous chapter, the gifts were named in the previous chapter, and by this chapter the call must become work in the hands. If a person who is called remains forever in the realm of being moved, forever nodding agreement, yet never walks onto the site and never picks up a tool, the call hangs in the air over his life. When Bezalel's name was mentioned in chapter 35, he was still only a person being introduced; by chapter 36, he must stand among the materials and among the craftsmen and turn the drawing into a reality. Faith is often divided at exactly this line: some people listen to the pattern all their lives and never set to work; others hear it, roll up their sleeves, and begin. The tabernacle was not built in conversation on the mountain, nor in the admiration of the congregation; it was built in one concrete task after another — the work of weaving, of embroidery, of wood, of metal — and every task required someone willing to lay down his own name and put his hands to it.

There is another layer worth pausing over: those who work are not one person but a company. Verse 1 speaks of Bezalel, Aholiab, and every wise-hearted person. This is a team. Scripture never records the building of the tabernacle as the personal achievement of a single genius. Bezalel leads, but he does not walk alone; Aholiab works beside him, and many whose names are never recorded do their own work in their own places. The ministry of the church is the same: some stand in front, some stand behind; some are seen, and some are never mentioned from beginning to end. But what the LORD values is the whole team working together as commanded. A person who wants only his own share of glory will sooner or later become an obstacle on the site; a company of people willing to take their appointed places can bring a drawing to the ground. As we read this verse today, let us read not only Bezalel's talent but also the obedience of this company — the wisdom they received was not used to display themselves, but to complete something greater than any one of them. This is the doorway into chapter 36. The door is open, and the first thing we are about to see is not a curtain, not a board, but something extremely rare in the whole Old Testament: an offering so abundant that it has to be stopped.

### II. 08:00-24:00 More Than Enough: The Offering That Was Restrained (Exod 36:2-7)

In verse 2, Moses calls Bezalel, Aholiab, and every wise-hearted man in whom the LORD has put wisdom to do this work, and also everyone whose heart stirred him up to come to the work to do it, and they come together to work. Two expressions here are worth holding in our hearts: stirred up, and willing. The craftsmen of the tabernacle are not conscripted labourers or people saddled with an assigned burden; Scripture deliberately says they are people whose hearts were stirred, people who are willing. If the work of God is driven only by administrative command, it can be driven for a season but not for a lifetime; what truly gets the work done is a heart moved first, and then a hand stretched out. In verse 3, they receive from Moses' hand all the offering that the people have brought for the work of the sanctuary. Note the order of this transfer: the people bring the offering to Moses, and Moses hands the offering to the craftsmen. Giving is not a confusion of enthusiasm; it has order and hand-over — someone responsible for receiving, and someone responsible for making. When a church talks about giving, enthusiasm without order soon burns into disorder; order without willingness soon freezes into formality. This chapter sets the two side by side: the people bring willingly, Moses hands over faithfully, the craftsmen work diligently.

The second half of verse 3 contains a sentence that reads plainly yet is deeply moving: "and they brought yet unto him free offerings every morning." Not once, not in one burst of impulse, but every morning. The stirring of the first day is easy; the hard thing is to rise on the second day, the third day, the tenth day, and still prepare the offering and still carry it to the site. This shows that the people's hearts were not lit by a passing mood; they had truly turned. We must not forget that not long before, this same people had danced before the golden calf; their gold earrings had once been taken off and cast into an idol. Now the same people, with the same gold, silver, cloth, and skins, carry it piece by piece into the tabernacle's site. The materials have not changed; the direction has changed. The hands have not changed; the heart has changed. The truest evidence of a person's repentance often lies not in how much he says, but in the fact that he hands the same resources, the same talents, and the same days over to a different direction. In the golden calf incident, the gold was used in the wrong place — that is a record at the factual level, and Scripture itself says so plainly. Here, the gold is so abundant that a command must stop it — that too is a record at the factual level. Set side by side, the two records form a clear structural observation: the same community of people first gave everything to an idol, and later brought everything into the sanctuary. The text offers no sentence of commentary to sum this up for us; it simply places the two events next to each other and lets the reader see for himself what a turned heart looks like. That is the artistry of the narrative, and it is the Bible's way of speaking to us.

Then, in verses 4 and 5, comes the most unexpected scene in the whole chapter: all the wise men who are doing all the work of the sanctuary leave the work in their hands and come together before Moses to report. They say: "The people bring much more than enough for the service of the work, which the LORD commanded to make" (Exod 36:5, KJV). Put closer to the original, what the people have brought exceeds what the work requires, and there is still a surplus. Two Hebrew words are used here: one is marbeh, meaning a large, increased quantity; the other is hoter, meaning what is over, what remains. The craftsmen are not saying it is just barely enough, nor nearly enough; they are saying: it has exceeded the need, and there is still some left. Consider how rare this scene is: the people responsible for the work take the initiative to report that there is too much material. In ordinary projects we are used to hearing that there is not enough — not enough budget, not enough hands, not enough material, requiring top-ups, fresh appeals, further urging. But here the craftsmen stand before Moses and say: enough — too much. Why do the craftsmen come to report this? Because they are loyal and honest. They could perfectly well have kept the extra material, used it slowly, or even taken it for themselves; instead they chose to tell the plain truth at the first opportunity. This is a precious honesty: not exaggerating the need, not concealing the surplus, not exploiting the people's enthusiasm to stockpile for oneself. Without this honesty in leaders and craftsmen, no amount of giving would keep from leaking away; with it, the surplus can be properly stopped.

In verse 6, Moses gives a command, and they proclaim it throughout the camp: let neither man nor woman make any more work for the offering of the sanctuary. "So the people were restrained from bringing." The word rendered "restrained" is kala' in the original, meaning to hold back, to stop, to forbid. Moses is not making a vague gesture; he issues a command and has it proclaimed so that the whole camp knows: stop. Verse 7 gives the conclusion: "For the stuff they had was sufficient for all the work to make it, and too much" (KJV). The word of surplus echoes again and again in this chapter. Scripture rarely repeats a single idea so insistently unless it wants us to remember it. Note carefully: Moses stops the giving not because the people's hearts have grown cold, but precisely because their hearts are warmer than the work requires. Nor is the stopping a denial of giving; it is the fulfilment of giving — for the purpose of giving was never giving itself, but the completion of the work. Once the work has enough material, to bring more is to waste the people's willingness. Good leadership knows when to call people forward, and also when to tell them: it is enough; you may stop. Both take courage. To call for giving takes courage, for fear no one will respond; to stop giving takes even more courage, for it must resist the reflex that treats "the more the better" as spirituality. But Moses resists it. He does not treat the people's willingness as a resource to be drawn on without limit; he respects the real need of the work, and he respects the people's households. This passage is therefore that rare scene in the entire Old Testament of an offering being called to a halt: not people so stingy that God rebukes them, but people so willing that the leader restrains them.

There is one other place in the Old Testament that mirrors this one. In 2 Chronicles 31, Hezekiah restores the worship and commands the people to bring in their portions, and the people bring in abundance, heaping up tithes of grain and new oil. When Hezekiah asks the priests about it, Azariah the chief priest of the house of Zadok answers: "Since the people began to bring the offerings into the house of the LORD, we have had enough to eat, and have left plenty: for the LORD hath blessed his people; and that which is left is this great store" (2 Chr 31:10, KJV). That is centuries later — a different king, a different era — yet the same word appears again: plenty left over. Two surpluses, separated by the whole history of the kingdom, point to the faithfulness of the same God: when the people's hearts truly turn and they willingly hand over what they have, the house of God is never maintained by tight-fisted calculation. Here we must keep the levels distinct: setting these two records side by side is a comparison at the intertextual level, helping us see a theme that runs consistently through Scripture; it does not mean that every church and every believer who gives will necessarily abound materially — that inference goes beyond what these two texts themselves say. When we preach the surplus, we must preach it in the right place: the surplus first displays the meeting of the people's willing heart with God's accepting heart, and only secondarily the exceeding of material quantity. Reverse the order, and giving is preached as a transaction; keep the order, and the surplus is a sign of grace.

For people like us today, this passage carries a further quiet reminder. The craftsmen said it was enough, and Moses ordered the stop; the people heard the command and really did stop — no one sneaked in one more gift, no one considered his own portion so important that it simply had to be given. Willingness and obedience are joined together here: in willingness they brought with all their strength; in obedience they stopped cleanly. In the life of the church we often assume obedience shows itself only in doing; in fact it also shows itself in stopping. To shirk when it is time to work is disobedience; to push on when it is time to stop is also disobedience. The people did both well, and so the opening verses of chapter 36 read like a quiet song of triumph: a people who once worshipped an idol now bring in willingness and stop in obedience; their gold has entered the sanctuary, and their names have entered this book. That is the story of the offering that was more than enough. Next, the camera of the text moves from the camp to the site, from the hearts of the people to the hands of the craftsmen — the curtains are about to be woven.

### III. 24:00-42:00 The Curtain Work: Ten Curtains and Eleven (Exod 36:8-19)

From verse 8, the text enters the main body of the construction. Among the workers, the wise-hearted begin to make the tabernacle. The first thing begun is not a golden vessel, nor the altar, but the curtains. Ten curtains, woven of fine linen, with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, and cherubim worked into them by the skilled craftsman. Why weave the curtains first? In terms of the order of construction, the tabernacle is essentially a tent, and a tent has its covering before anything inside it; in terms of the order of narration, the text follows the sequence of chapter 26 — curtains first, then boards, then vessels, without disorder. As we read this passage, let us not rush to convert every curtain's dimensions into today's metres — those figures were already given in chapter 26 when the command was issued; here we have execution, the same figures made one by one into reality. The main line is not the numbers themselves, but two things: the beauty of the materials, and the skill of the work.

First, the materials. Fine linen is a refined fabric, and the blue, purple, and scarlet yarns were all costly dyes in the ancient world — purple especially, drawn from marine shellfish at great expense. The cherubim embroidered on the curtains are not a decorative flourish added at the weaver's whim; they follow the pattern: the cherubim are guardians of the sacred boundary — in Genesis, cherubim are stationed east of Eden to guard the way to the tree of life — and cherubim woven into the tabernacle curtains weave the same message into the cloth: this is the dwelling of the holy God, and it may not be entered casually. The word for curtain here is yeri'ah in the original, meaning basically a tent curtain, a length of cloth. The ten curtains, each twenty-eight cubits long and four cubits wide, are joined five and five, linked to one another with loops of blue and clasps of gold, becoming one great complete curtain spread over the tabernacle as its inner layer. Note how they are joined: not sewn dead into a single sheet, but linked with loops and clasps — they can be taken apart; they can travel. This tent is prepared for a wilderness journey; it must be beautiful, and it must be able to move. The dwelling of God in the midst of His people is not a monument of a settled age; it is a tent that journeys with them. Small as the clasps are, they remind us: this majesty is a majesty that goes with the people.

Next come the eleven curtains of goats' hair, as the tent over the tabernacle. Eleven — one more than the ten of the inner layer; and slightly larger in measure, thirty cubits long and four wide, five joined together and six joined together, the extra hanging down at the front and back of the tabernacle, joined with clasps of bronze and loops. Goats' hair is coarse — not as splendid as fine linen — but durable, wind-resistant, and rain-shedding. Placed outside the linen curtains, it forms a pattern of inner beauty and outer plainness: the most splendid layer lies nearest the sanctuary, the rough and sturdy layer faces the wilderness. Beyond that comes a covering of rams' skins dyed red, and outermost of all a covering of badgers' skins (sea-cow hides). That outer skin is inconspicuous, grey and dull, taking all the sun and rain upon itself. From the outside the whole tabernacle looks unremarkable, even somewhat shabby; only when you go in do you see the fine linen, the coloured yarns, and the cherubim. Readers through the generations have noticed this difference between outside and inside. Here we say only what the narrative itself supports: the text places the most precious layer innermost and the most hard-wearing layer outermost, which is both the engineering reality of the tent and a quiet shaping of the reader's eye — the glory of God is not flaunted on the surface; the true splendour is within the sanctuary, seen by those who come in.

Reading this passage, there is one unavoidable question: have we not just read all of this? Did chapter 26 not already say it word for word? Why does chapter 36 say almost exactly the same thing all over again? This is a stylistic feature of the Priestly literature, which scholarship calls ritual repetition: as the command is spoken, so the execution is recorded; every sentence commanded on the mountain must find its corresponding sentence of completion below the mountain. From a literary standpoint, this repetition is not redundancy but declaration: what was done matches what was commanded, seam to seam, with nothing out of place. From a theological standpoint, it declares the completeness of obedience — this time Israel added nothing, subtracted nothing, and did not act on its own initiative as it had in the golden calf incident. Of course, scholarly discussion raises a further question: in the literary formation of the text, do the commands of chapters 25-31 and the execution of chapters 35-40 come from the same hand and the same layer of tradition? Scholars have proposed different stratification schemes — some assign both parts to the Priestly source, others judge the dating of the latter section's redaction differently. We set these out honestly side by side: this is a question at the level of scholarly discussion, and what the text itself sets before us is a diptych in which command and execution answer each other; the stratification debate does not change what this diptych looks like before our eyes. A preacher should not present what scholarship has not settled as though it were settled, nor avoid the literary facts of the text because discussion exists. The fact is: the repetition is really there; and the effect of the repetition is that we hear the same sentence again and again — as it was commanded, so it was done.

Note also who does the work. Verse 8 says the wise-hearted made this work. Weaving curtains is a craft, the accumulated skill of weavers and women workers day after day; yet Scripture calls this wisdom. This reminds us that wisdom in the Bible is never only clarity in the head; it includes faithfulness in the hands. A weaver who aligns every thread and embroiders the cherubim's wings true is, in that very act, revering God. Every inch of the tabernacle demands this reverence: dimensions may not be approximate, clasps may not be makeshift, joins may not be slack. Our generation is used to "roughly right," used to "good enough," but the work of the sanctuary does not permit "good enough." Not because God is fastidious, but because the work itself teaches people: the objects of worship must follow the pattern, and those who work must bring their finest skill and their most upright heart. The curtains are finished; next to be raised is the skeleton of the tabernacle — the boards and the bars.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 Boards, Bars, Veil and Screen: According to the Pattern on the Mountain (Exod 36:20-38)

From verse 20, the craftsmen make the boards for the tabernacle of acacia wood — shittim wood, a tree that grows in the wilderness, hard in grain, resistant to decay and insects, the best timber the wilderness affords. Each board is ten cubits long and a cubit and a half wide, with two tenons by which they are joined, and silver sockets to hold them. The word for board in the original is qeresh, denoting an upright plank, a frame board; the word for the silver socket is 'eden, meaning a base or pedestal. Twenty boards on the north side, twenty on the south, six on the west plus two for the corners make the walls of three sides of the tabernacle. The boards are overlaid with gold, and gold rings are made to receive the bars; the bars too are acacia wood overlaid with gold, running through the boards and joining one independent board after another into a wall that does not shake. There is a plain engineering wisdom here: a single board, however good, cannot stand alone; tenon to tenon, socket under socket, bar through rings — joined together, they become a wall. So it is with the skeleton of the tabernacle, and so in a way with the company of the people — but we speak of this layer only as structural observation, without forcing every component to correspond to a spiritual meaning; that kind of item-by-item imposition goes beyond the text's own manner of speaking. What the text speaks of here is first of all real engineering: wood, silver, and gold, mortise and tenon and crossbar, made piece by piece and raised board by board.

The source of the silver for the sockets deserves attention. Chapter 38 will explain that the silver came from the ransom money paid by every numbered member of the congregation. That is to say, the foundation of the tabernacle rests on silver given in common by all the people. No one's silver was especially large, and no one's silver was disdained as too small; half a shekel each — the rich might not give more, and the poor might not give less. This foundation is therefore the foundation of the whole people. We say this on the basis of the explicit text that follows — a connection at the factual level, which may be preached with confidence: the sanctuary stands on a foundation given by all together, not on the donations of a few wealthy individuals.

Once the boards stand, verse 35 turns to the making of the veil of the Holy of Holies. This veil differs from the ten curtains of verse 8: those ten formed the inner covering of the tabernacle, while this one is the boundary dividing the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place, and the original gives it its own special name, parokhet. Its materials and workmanship match the inner curtains in splendour — fine linen, blue, purple, and scarlet, cherubim by the skilled craftsman — hung on four pillars of acacia wood overlaid with gold, with sockets of silver. The veil was already commanded in detail in chapter 26; this chapter simply records: as commanded, it was finished. Here we do not need to preach every layer of the veil's meaning again; standing where this chapter stands, one sentence suffices: the boundary has been set up. From now on the Most Holy Place, where the ark stands, is separated from the Holy Place outside it, and once a year the high priest enters with blood. This boundary will appear again and again in the later narrative of Scripture; and in the New Testament, Hebrews 10:20 reads the veil as Christ's body, saying that by the new and living way Jesus opened for us, we may enter the Most Holy Place with boldness. Note the level: this is the New Testament author's reading of the Old Testament veil, a reading at the level of New Testament interpretation and typology, cherished by the church through the ages; it is not to be read back as the plain historical sense of Exodus itself — when the craftsmen of Moses' day wove this veil, what the text records is boundary and holiness, not a prophetic sermon. Distinguishing the levels does not weaken the New Testament reading; it lets each level stand firm — the historical sense of the original stands firm, and only then does the New Testament's extension have something to rest on.

At the end of the chapter, verses 37 and 38 make the screen for the door of the tabernacle. The screen is woven with the same coloured yarns and fine linen, in embroidered work, hung on five pillars; the pillars are overlaid with gold, their hooks are gold, and their sockets are bronze — note that from the silver sockets of the Most Holy Place to the bronze sockets at the tabernacle door, the metals descend in value, exactly matching the spatial order of holiness decreasing from inside outward, and the craftsmen got even this order right. The screen is the face the people can see every day: they cannot enter the Most Holy Place, nor even the Holy Place, but they can lift their eyes and see this screen embroidered with coloured yarns, and know that God dwells in their midst. Here the main body of the construction comes to rest at the door — the covering is there, the skeleton is there, the boundary is there, and the door is there too. In the next chapter the text will turn to the making of the ark, the table, the lampstand, and the altar of incense — the world of the vessels — and today we offer only one sentence of indication without seizing that chapter's content. What this chapter wants us to carry away is the sentence that echoes through the whole record of execution: as the LORD commanded Moses, so they did it. In this chapter that sentence closes each section in various forms, until at Exodus 39:43 Moses looks upon all the work, sees that they have done it as the LORD commanded, and blesses them. The work of one chapter, the work of a whole scroll, ends finally in this one verdict: they did as commanded, and so they were blessed. Obedience is not necessarily dramatic; obedience is very often just this: aligning board to board at the tenons, threading bar after bar through the gold rings, and bringing every sentence of the command down onto the hands.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: The Gold Has Reached the Right Place (An Overview of Exod 36)

Brothers and sisters, in the last few minutes let us gather this chapter together. Exodus 36 contains no miracle, no thunder, no parting of the sea; it contains only a work site: offerings brought in, curtains woven, boards raised, bars threaded through, a screen hung up. Yet it is precisely this chapter that shows us a world turned upside down. A few months earlier, this people stood around the golden calf and said: these be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt. A few months later, the same people carry offerings to the site every morning, until the craftsmen must come out and call a stop. The gold has not changed, and the people's hands have not changed; what has changed is the direction of their kneeling, and where their willingness goes. The story of a person's life is very often written in this one small matter: where, in the end, are your gold, your time, your talents, and the best years of your life being carried? Sent to the wrong place, the finest materials are cast into an idol; sent to the right place, even ordinary goats' hair can cover the sanctuary.

This chapter also leaves us a sentence to carry home: more than enough. "More than enough" is not a word of boasting; it is a word of thanksgiving. It tells us that when a company of people truly turns, each bringing what he has willingly, with loyal craftsmen who report the truth and wise leadership that stops the giving decisively, the house of God will not lack. Today, on the work sites of our own churches, our own families, and our own lives, we may be anxious about insufficiency: not enough time, not enough strength, not enough hands. Chapter 36 gives us no cheap guarantee; it gives us an order: first set the heart right, first do the work at hand as commanded, first bring in willingness and stop in obedience. Those who reach that point often discover, some morning, with surprise: it is enough — and there is more than enough. May we all be people who bring offerings in the morning, and people who stop when they hear that it is enough; and may our gold all reach the right place. Amen.

## Endnotes

1. On Exod 36:1 and 35:30-35: chapter 35 names Bezalel's calling, his filling with the Spirit, and his ability to teach the work; 36:1 closes the calling section with "according to all that the LORD had commanded," shifting the narrative centre from election to execution. See Childs, *Exodus*, OTL, introductory discussion of the execution section in chapters 35-40; Durham, *Exodus*, WBC 3, note on the transitional sentence at 36:1.
2. marbeh (מַרְבֶּה, transliteration marbeh, Strong's number H7235): used in the craftsmen's report at Exod 36:5, from the root r-b-h, "to be many, great." According to BDB, H7235 is the Hiphil participle of that root used substantivally, meaning "a large quantity, an increased amount"; HALOT likewise lists the sense "abundance, much." Here it denotes that what the people brought exceeded what was needed in quantity.
3. hoter (הוֹתֵר, transliteration hôter / hoter, Strong's number H3498): found at Exod 36:5 and 36:7, from the root y-t-r, "to remain over, be left." BDB lists "remainder, surplus"; HALOT lists "rest, surplus." The "and too much" of 36:7 echoes 36:5 and forms the chapter's keyword refrain.
4. kala' (כָּלָא, transliteration kālā' / kala', Strong's number H3607): at Exod 36:6, "the people were restrained from bringing"; the verb means "to hold back, stop, forbid, shut up." BDB lists "restrain, withhold, shut up"; HALOT lists "zurückhalten, verbieten," i.e., to hold back, to forbid. Here it is used passively: the people were stopped and brought no more.
5. On the intertextual comparison between 2 Chr 31:10 and Exod 36: in Hezekiah's day the priest Azariah says of the offerings that they have "had enough to eat, and have left plenty," likewise taking the surplus as a sign of grace; the juxtaposition of the two passages is a thematic echo at the intertextual level, not typological prediction, as the sermon text marks.
6. yeri'ah (יְרִיעָה, transliteration yĕrî'â / yeri'ah, Strong's number H3407): the term for the ten curtains from Exod 36:8 onward, frequent also in chapter 26, meaning "a tent curtain, a length of cloth." BDB lists "curtain, tent-curtain"; HALOT lists "Zeltbahn, Vorhang." Its plural form denotes the whole set of cloth lengths forming the tabernacle covering.
7. On the double narration of command (chapters 25-31) and execution (chapters 35-40): scholarship either assigns both to the Priestly source (P) or proposes a different stratification for the execution section; Childs, with a canonical approach, stresses the responsive reading of the two sections as they now stand, while Durham compares command and execution verse by verse. The sermon text uses "ritual repetition" as a stylistic description and sets the stratification question side by side without settling it.
8. qeresh (קֶרֶשׁ, transliteration qereš / qeresh, Strong's number H7175): the term for the boards from Exod 36:20 onward, meaning "board, frame board." BDB lists "board, frame"; HALOT lists "Brett, Gestell." The material is acacia (shittim) wood ('ăṣê šiṭṭîm).
9. 'eden (אֶדֶן, transliteration 'eden, Strong's number H134): the term for the silver sockets at Exod 36:24 and elsewhere, meaning "base, pedestal, socket." BDB lists "base, pedestal, socket"; HALOT lists "Sockel, Basis." The sockets of the tabernacle boards are silver; for the source of the silver, see the congregation's ransom money recorded at Exod 38:25-28.
10. parokhet (פָּרֹכֶת, transliteration pārōket): the special term for the veil of the Holy of Holies at Exod 36:35; BDB and HALOT both list the sense "veil, curtain," denoting specifically the veil dividing the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place, distinct from the general yeri'ah. Hebrews 10:20 reads the veil as Christ's body, which belongs to the level of New Testament interpretation, as the sermon text marks.
11. Read together, the bronze sockets of the screen's five pillars at Exod 36:38 and the silver sockets of the veil's four pillars at 36:35 show the grades of metal descending from inside outward, consistent with the command in chapter 26 — the material presentation of the spatial order of holiness. See Durham's comparative notes on 26:36-37 and 36:38.
12. Exodus 39:43 — Moses looks upon all the work and blesses them — is the general conclusion of the whole execution section, chapters 35-40; the "as commanded" formulae closing the sections within chapter 36 echo it, and the sermon text offers this only as a structural indication without expanding on chapter 39.

## Devotional Excerpt

The quietest and yet loudest sentence in Exodus 36 is spoken by the craftsmen: what the people have brought is more than enough for the work, and there is a surplus. Not long before, this same people's gold had been cast into a calf; now their gold is carried into the sanctuary, so abundantly that Moses must order it stopped. The materials are the same, and the hands are the same; what has changed is the direction of the heart. Ask yourself today: where are my time, my gifts, and my possessions being carried? To bring willingly is evidence of a turned heart; to stop in obedience when told it is enough is evidence of the same. Bring your best to God each morning, and after faithful labour in His work, hear the comfort: it is enough, and there is a surplus. Receive the surplus as grace, not as boasting; entrust lack to order, not to anxiety. First the heart is set right, then the hand is stretched out — and so, inch by inch, the sanctuary rises. Pray today also for those who do the work: that God would give us the craftsmen's honesty, neither exaggerating the need nor concealing the surplus; the clear-headedness of Moses, knowing when to call and when to stop; and the gentleness of the people, wholehearted in bringing and at rest in stopping. Then the work in our hands, great or small, can be done piece by piece, just as He commanded.

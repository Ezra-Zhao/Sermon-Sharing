# Nothing New Under the Sun: Business

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese sermon manuscript «日光之下并无新事：生意».
> **Preacher:** Guangyi Zhao

- **Series:** Nothing New Under the Sun (Eccl. 1:9)
- **Preacher:** Guangyi Zhao (2019, compiled and edited)
- **Theme:** The patterns, pitfalls, and way out in the area of business

## Summary

In doing business and managing money, Christians most often struggle at two ends: at one end fearing that touching money makes one worldly, at the other fearing that a moment's inattention leads to greed. This chapter first finds the root of the illness — the problem is not money, but "greed"; then uses Proverbs to see clearly the several faces of wealth; finally it lands on concrete questions such as lending, stock trading, and discovering opportunities, whose answers are not in techniques, but in understanding, self-discipline, and execution.

## I. The Root of the Illness Is Not Money, but "Greed"

> "For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil, for which some have strayed from the faith in their greediness, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows." (1 Tim. 6:10)

The word "love of money" in Greek is **philargyria (φιλαργυρία)** (original text φιλαργυρία, transliterated philargyria; Strong G5365; according to *Thayer's Lexicon*, "love of money, greed for money"), literally "loving money." What Paul condemns is not money, but the heart that loves money. This difference of one word is the starting point of the whole chapter.

Many people read 1 Tim. 6:10 and read out a wrong understanding: Money is bad. This reading is wrong. What the Scripture says is "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil"; the key is in the definition of "greed," not in money itself.

The Bible's depiction of greed has several other places. The Psalms say: "For the wicked boasts of his heart's desire; he blesses the greedy and renounces the LORD." (Ps. 10:3) The greedy person renounces not only morality; he pushes the LORD aside. Peter said to shepherds, to shepherd the flock "not by compulsion but willingly, not for dishonest gain but eagerly" (1 Pet. 5:2) — even in people who serve God, greed can seep in, how much more in people who do business.

Then what is the right attitude toward money? Paul charged Timothy:

> "Command those who are rich in this present age not to be haughty, nor to trust in uncertain riches but in the living God, who gives us richly all things to enjoy. Let them do good, that they be rich in good works, ready to give, willing to share, storing up for themselves a good foundation for the time to come, that they may lay hold on eternal life." (1 Tim. 6:17-19)

These words give the measure in full: Money can be enjoyed — it is God who "gives us richly all things to enjoy"; but it cannot be trusted — riches are "uncertain"; the true riches of the rich are to be "rich in good works, ready to give." There is only one direction to lay hold of: that true life — eternal life.

The Lord Jesus' words are also often misread. He looked at people who have riches and said, "How hard it is for those who have riches to enter the kingdom of God!" The disciples were astonished, and He immediately made the words clear:

> "And Jesus, looking around, said to His disciples, 'How hard it is for those who have riches to enter the kingdom of God!' And the disciples were astonished at His words. But Jesus answered again and said to them, 'Children, how hard it is for those who trust in riches to enter the kingdom of God!'" (Mark 10:23-24)

One must distinguish two wrong understandings: "those who have riches" and "those who trust in riches" are not the same thing. What is hard has never been having money; it is trusting in money.

## II. How Proverbs Views Wealth: Four Faces

Proverbs' observation of wealth is very calm, neither beautifying it nor demonizing it. The original manuscript sums it up in four faces.

**The first face: wealth can bring a person a sense of security.**

> "The rich man's wealth is his strong city, and like a high wall in his own esteem." (Prov. 18:11)

Proverbs uses a strong city wall to describe the sense of security wealth brings: it can deal with certain sudden changes in life. Put realistically, when a person has ample savings, when he is sick and hospitalized he need not first fear expensive medical fees. The original manuscript here mentioned the example of Waterdrop fundraising as a contrast — people without savings, when they meet a serious illness, can only raise funds from the crowd. This face of wealth is real; there is no need to pretend to be lofty and deny it.

**The second face: wealth increases friends.**

> "Wealth makes many friends, but the poor is separated from his friend." (Prov. 19:4)

This is the Proverbs author's narration of general human nature. Chinese has the same saying: "When poor in the city, no one asks; when rich in the deep mountains, there are distant relatives." The author focuses on keen observation behind social phenomena: most people are unwilling to be friends with the poor, because the poor have needs and need to be cared for, and will become a burden to neighbors; the rich are welcomed, because by being friends with him one can obtain certain benefits. Proverbs writes these words down not to teach people to flatter the powerful, but to let people first see human nature clearly, then decide how to conduct themselves.

**The third face: wealth can bring a person power and influence.**

> "Many entreat the favor of the nobility, and every man is a friend to one who gives gifts." (Prov. 19:6)
> "The poor man uses entreaties, but the rich answers roughly." (Prov. 18:23)
> "The rich rules over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender." (Prov. 22:7)

The author of Proverbs saw that political and economic power are mostly concentrated in the hands of the rich; the weight of their words is much greater than that of the poor, almost "with money one has everything." Conversely, the poor must be careful with their words, especially when hoping for help from the rich, all the more to speak pleasing words. Some people live in luxury, some struggle in poverty all their lives — this is a fact of life. Proverbs does not whitewash this fact; it only places it before us, reminding every person whose hands have money: Influence is a trust; used in the wrong place it becomes oppression.

**The fourth face: wealth is not the whole of life.**

> "Treasures of wickedness profit nothing, but righteousness delivers from death." (Prov. 10:2)
> "He who trusts in his riches will fall, but the righteous will flourish like foliage." (Prov. 11:28)

The author of Proverbs did not deny the role of wealth: the sense of security is real, the ability to help people is real. But if a rich person regards money as the highest thing in life's value, and fixes his life's direction and reliance on money, he is doing an extremely foolish thing — because money has its limitations. It can block a moment's wind and rain, but cannot save a person from death; it can be a city wall, but cannot be a foundation.

There is another face concerning the speed of obtaining wealth:

> "A faithful man will abound with blessings, but he who hastens to be rich will not go unpunished." (Prov. 28:20)

The Scripture tells people to make money honestly. Those eager for quick success and hastening to be rich will be punished. Throughout the ages, money obtained by unlawful means has in the end become a stone smashing one's own foot. Here the original manuscript left only "Invest early; if you do not manage wealth, wealth will not manage you" and the titles of bosses' cases, without expansion; but the boundary of Proverbs is already clear enough: Slow is permissible; unrighteousness is not.

## III. Several Concrete Questions

Principles must land in concrete situations. The original manuscript left several Q&As.

**May I do a lending business?**

The Bible's boundary for lending to a brother is:

> "You shall not charge interest to your brother — interest on money or food or anything that is lent out at interest." (Deut. 23:19)

Charging interest to a brother — the Bible does not permit it. Before the lending business is done to a brother, it must first pass this line.

**May I trade stocks?**

The original manuscript did not give a simple "may" or "may not" for this question, but asked several questions in a row, requiring the asker to answer first:

- How much do we understand about stocks?
- How much do we understand about human nature?
- How much do we understand about history?
- How much do we understand about the industry?
- What are your investment rules? How is your execution?
- Do we rely on it in our hearts?

These several questions are in fact the method of the whole chapter: first understanding, then rules; first ask oneself what one relies on in the heart, then ask what opportunities the market gives. If you cannot answer these questions, do not be in a hurry to enter the field.

**How do I discover opportunities?**

The answer in the original manuscript is only eight concepts in Chinese, four pairs: understanding, cycles, patterns, execution. First do enough understanding — of the target, of human nature, of history, of the industry; then look at cycles and feel out patterns, including the life cycle of enterprises; finally, execute with self-discipline: set one's own rules well, and carry them out strictly. Opportunities are not bumped into by chance; they are recognized after understanding is in place.

## IV. How to Do the Work of Understanding

The two concepts "understanding" sound light, but doing them is clumsy work. The original manuscript left several concrete forms.

**Self-discipline questions about a stock.** Facing a company, one must be able to answer these questions: What industry is this enterprise in? How is this industry recently? In this company's prospectus, what is the focus? What are the key points in the most recent semi-annual report? How did you read and understand this enterprise? What are your investment rules? If you cannot answer, it shows understanding is not yet in place.

**Insight into human nature.** The original manuscript told a story of tomb robbers: Tomb robbers dug a hole into a tomb, using a rope to lower a person down to take treasure; the person pulling the rope often took the treasure and left, keeping it all, letting the person below the rope die in the tomb. Thus cooperative teams basically broke up; no one could trust anyone. Later a father-son combination appeared: the father went down, the son pulled the rope, but there were also unfilial sons who took the treasure and abandoned the father. Finally the trade rule was adjusted again: the son went down, the father pulled the rope — a tiger, though fierce, does not eat its cubs; a father, though greedy for money, would not abandon his son; this was most proper. The original manuscript's comment is: Institutional design that has insight into human nature — effective! In doing business and investing, first admit that there is greed in human nature, then use systems and rules to restrain it, rather than living by "he probably would not."

**Understanding of history.** The original manuscript used a long passage of history — the Sanwan Reorganization: On September 9, 1927, after the failure of the Autumn Harvest Uprising, the troops went up the mountain, being chased all the way; on September 29, arriving at Sanwan Village in Yongxin County, Jiangxi, the troops originally of more than five thousand were left with fewer than one thousand, thinking was confused, morale was floating, desertions were constant. That evening Mao Zedong convened a meeting of the Front Enemy Committee and decided to rectify and reorganize, initially conceiving the "Three Main Rules of Discipline," and several months later adding points of attention, later finalized as the Three Main Rules of Discipline and Eight Points for Attention. After the Sanwan Reorganization, this troop, with a brand-new appearance and clear principles, integrated various armed forces. Borrowing this history, the original manuscript asked three questions: What exactly happened in between? Do we truly understand what happened in the Bible? Have we ever thought, based on history, about our own problems today and the church's problems today? — The use of historical understanding is, in the low valley, to see clearly: a scattered troop stood up again by principles and discipline; this pattern counts the same in the business field.

**Traversing industries.** The method of the original manuscript's author in studying industries was to draw an "industry map": according to market, landscape, model, and path, traversing the companies in an industry one by one. In the education industry, they traversed about 470 enterprises — 130 higher-education informatization companies, 220 talent education and service companies, 120 college-student finance, life service, and social companies — and also did traversal research of more than a thousand companies in basic education and higher education; in the agricultural field, they traversed 178 agricultural Internet start-up enterprises, marking block by block which were fully competitive and which had no competition yet. After such a round, at least one knows what stage the market is in and where oneself is at present, and those several hundred enterprises also became a library for later screening targets. Understanding is not reading a few articles; it is walking through an entire industry.

## V. The Final Question: What Does My Heart Truly Rely On

The whole chapter circles to the end, and must return to one person. The original manuscript's close is very direct: Understanding is only a tool. What my heart truly relies on — is it God, or money?

> "Their sorrows shall be multiplied who hasten after another god; their drink offerings of blood I will not offer, nor take up their names on my lips." (Ps. 16:4)

Once money sits in God's position, sorrows only increase — this is precisely the piercing of "pierced themselves through with many sorrows" (1 Tim. 6:10). Conversely, putting wealth back in the position of a tool, it can be used to do proper things: to labor, to support the weak. Paul says:

> "In all things I have shown you an example, that by laboring like this you ought to support the weak, and to remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how He said, 'It is more blessed to give than to receive.'" (Acts 20:35)

Therefore the way out for doing business, the original manuscript sums up as several words side by side: the Bible, understanding, self-discipline — plus reliance on God. Jesus is the Lord of us who are in the workplace. Relying on Jesus (prayer), we face all kinds of challenges; we believe the promises He gives us (the Bible), landing the Bible's promises in this era; we are willing to trust Him and follow Him, leading us to live a life no longer the same, walking securely on the heights.

## Conclusion

There is nothing new under the sun. The temptation of lending at interest, the impulse to hasten to be rich, the fall of trusting in riches — the age of Proverbs had them, and our age has them too. The Bible does not tell people to avoid money; it tells people to see money clearly: it can be a strong city for a moment, but cannot be the foundation of life; it is a tool to serve people, not the reliance of the human heart. Do the work of understanding fully, establish the rules well and execute them to the end, and in the heart rely on the God who gives us richly all things to enjoy — a person who does business in this way can both make money honestly and give willingly.

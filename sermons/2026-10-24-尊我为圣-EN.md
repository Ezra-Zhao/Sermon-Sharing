# To Sanctify Me — A Sermon on Numbers 20

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Retranslated in full from the finalized Chinese manuscript, Rolling Batch 20, 25 October 2026; draft translation pending native-speaker proofreading
Text: Numbers chapter 20 (29 verses in full)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), fetched and checked verse by verse against Bible Gateway's KJV text for this retranslation; the companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (Simplified, "Shen" edition; hereafter "the Chinese base text"). Word meanings are checked against Strong's Concordance, Brown-Driver-Briggs (BDB), and the Blue Letter Bible (BLB) lexicon pages, as in the Chinese manuscript. The English text is not a back-translation of the Chinese quotations: every quotation below is KJV wording, and where the two base texts differ in wording, the difference is registered, not harmonized.
Title verification: The title is taken from the pivot word of verse 12 in the base text's own wording: "Because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel" (20:12). The chapter's three narrative movements — the strife over water, the striking of the rock, and Edom's refusal with Aaron's death — are all gathered into the judgment of this one sentence. Candidates set aside: "the water of Meribah" (20:13) names only the place; "speak ye unto the rock" (20:8) names only the one command; "to sanctify me" alone holds together God's command, man's failure, and the ground of the judgment, and is therefore the title. The Chinese title is taken from the same verse in its own base text; each manuscript follows its own base text. Full record in Endnote 2.
Base-text notes (where the two base texts or common paraphrase differ, each base text is recorded as it stands): At 20:6 the narrative runs "And Moses and Aaron went from the presence of the assembly unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation" — the subject stands at the head of the verse; the Chinese base text likewise has Moses and Aaron leaving the congregation for the door of the tabernacle. At 20:8 the command is "speak ye unto the rock"; the Chinese base text's wording likewise denotes the act of speaking to the rock, and the Chinese fact layer follows its own base text only. At 20:10 Moses addresses the people as "ye rebels" in both texts; but the second half of the verse differs: the Chinese base text reads in the first person singular ("Shall I fetch you water out of this rock?"), where the KJV reads the plural, "must we fetch you water out of this rock?" — versions that alter either reading to match the other are not followed; each manuscript keeps its own text, and the difference is registered here and in Endnote 1. At 20:13 the KJV reads "This is the water of Meribah" — a statement of identification, not a naming sentence as the Chinese base text has it ("this water is called the water of Meribah"); and where the Chinese reads that the LORD was sanctified before them, the KJV reads "he was sanctified in them" — each from its own text, not harmonized. At 20:16 the diplomatic letter says, in the KJV, "he heard our voice, and sent an angel, and hath brought us forth out of Egypt" — "an angel," where the Chinese base text reads "a messenger"; the clauses are recorded as each text stands and are not merged. At 20:19 the KJV reads "then I will pay for it" (the Chinese base text: "we will give you the value" — not "silver"). At 20:20 the king of Edom's second answer in the KJV is "Thou shalt not go through," followed by the narrative of his coming out in force. At 20:24 the KJV reads "Aaron shall be gathered unto his people"; the Chinese base text reads "gathered to his ancestors" and carries its own footnote, "the original reads 'his people'" — the sermon text follows its own base text and this note registers the Chinese footnote. At 20:29 the chapter closes, in the KJV, "they mourned for Aaron thirty days, even all the house of Israel" — the subject of the mourning is the whole congregation, the whole house of Israel. At Psalm 95:8 the KJV names no place ("as in the provocation, and as in the day of temptation in the wilderness"), where the Chinese base text names Meribah and Massah expressly; the quotation below follows the KJV.

## Summary

Numbers 20 is a chapter of generational handover: Miriam dies at Kadesh (20:1) and Aaron dies on Mount Hor (20:28-29), and between the two funerals stands the gravest failure of Moses' life. The congregation strives for want of water (20:2-5); the LORD commands Moses, "speak ye unto the rock before their eyes" (20:8); but Moses lifts up his hand and smites the rock twice with his rod (20:11). The water comes out all the same, and the congregation and their beasts drink (20:11) — and the judgment falls all the same: "Because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel, therefore ye shall not bring this congregation into the land which I have given them." (20:12) In the second half of the chapter the king of Edom refuses passage (20:14-21), and Israel turns away and does not provoke him; Aaron on Mount Hor is stripped of his garments, which are put upon Eleazar, and is gathered to his people in peace (20:22-29). The chapter's lesson is for leaders, and for everyone who acts in anger: God can give you the water, yet not lend His name to your anger; God can also block your road, yet not permit you to force your way through in anger.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, we have all done what Moses did: before the people, our anger spoke first and our hand moved first, and only after the words were out did we remember that You had not commanded it so. Teach us today, through Numbers 20, to see the weight of Your holiness, and, in the moment when the water still comes out, to learn to answer for that one blow of ours. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Main Text

### Part One: A Generation Dies, but the Strife Does Not Die (Numbers 20:1-6) (00:00-20:00)

(Fact) The chapter's first sentence is an obituary, quoted verbatim from the base text: "Then came the children of Israel, even the whole congregation, into the desert of Zin in the first month: and the people abode in Kadesh; and Miriam died there, and was buried there." (20:1) The items registered one by one: the time is the first month — the calendar of the exodus generation has turned back to its first page, yet the place is still the wilderness; those who arrive are "the whole congregation," and the one who dies is a single person; the eulogy Scripture gives Miriam is only this: "Miriam died there, and was buried there." No survey of her life, no number of days of mourning (Aaron's death has thirty days; see 20:29), not even her age is recorded. The silence of the text itself speaks: the generation of the leaders is leaving the stage, and leaving it without ceremony. But the silence is not contempt — set out Miriam's life by Scripture's own register, and you will know who stands behind these few words.

(Fact) The line of Miriam's life, each station with its layer marked. First station, Exodus 2: Pharaoh has commanded the Hebrew male children to be killed; the infant Moses is laid in an ark of bulrushes at the river's brink, and "his sister stood afar off, to wit what would be done to him" (Exod 2:4, fact-layer cross-reference); when Pharaoh's daughter comes down to wash herself, the sister steps forward and offers a nurse, and the child is given back into his own mother's arms to be nursed (Exod 2:7-9, key points). That chapter does not record the sister's name; that she is Miriam is fixed by the genealogy of Numbers 26, where Jochebed, Amram's wife and a daughter of Levi, bears to Amram Aaron, Moses, and their sister Miriam (Num 26:59, key points). Second station, Exodus 15: the waters of the Red Sea have gone back, Moses leads Israel in song, and Miriam enters with name and title: "And Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, took a timbrel in her hand; and all the women went out after her with timbrels and with dances." (Exod 15:20) Her antiphon, as the base text gives it: "Sing ye to the Lord, for he hath triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea." (Exod 15:21) She is the first woman in the Pentateuch to be called a prophetess, and the timbrel in her hand is the leading instrument of the whole camp's worship. Third station, Numbers 12: Miriam and Aaron speak against Moses because of the Ethiopian woman he has married; the cloud departs from the tabernacle, and Miriam is leprous; Aaron confesses, and Moses cries, "Heal her now, O God, I beseech thee." (Num 12:13) She is shut out of the camp seven days, and the whole camp does not journey until she is brought in again (Num 12:15, key points). Fourth station is this chapter: she dies at Kadesh and is buried at Kadesh. Miriam the prophetess, Miriam the leader of song, Miriam the disciplined, Miriam the healed — all are gathered at last into the one sentence, "Miriam died there, and was buried there." Micah later gives God's own verdict on her, setting her beside her two brothers: "and I sent before thee Moses, Aaron, and Miriam" (Mic 6:4). Three persons sent together are now taken back one by one — this chapter takes Miriam, the end of the chapter takes Aaron, and Deuteronomy will take Moses. A generation hands over the staff in just this way: not retiring in triumph, but dying on the road, buried on the road, and passing the staff to the next generation to walk on.

(Fact) Miriam is scarcely buried when the second sentence is strife, quoted verbatim: "And there was no water for the congregation: and they gathered themselves together against Moses and against Aaron." (20:2) "No water" is the trigger of the whole chapter. The people's accusation stands in verses 3-5, quoted sentence by sentence and registered sentence by sentence. The first sentence is a wish to be dead: "And the people chode with Moses, and spake, saying, Would God that we had died when our brethren died before the Lord!" (20:3) "Our brethren" are the generation that fell in the wilderness after Numbers 14 — the speakers would rather die with the dead than live as they now live. The second sentence is a question about the route: "And why have ye brought up the congregation of the Lord into this wilderness, that we and our cattle should die there?" (20:4) Note their name for themselves: "the congregation of the Lord" — God's title, used to accuse God's servants. The third sentence is the longest, and sets Egypt and the present side by side as an account: "And wherefore have ye made us to come up out of Egypt, to bring us in unto this evil place? it is no place of seed, or of figs, or of vines, or of pomegranates; neither is there any water to drink." (20:5) One phrase — "have ye made us to come up" — rewrites the great grace of the exodus as compulsion; and the list — no place of seed, no figs, no vines, no pomegranates, no water — reads the produce-catalogue of the promised land backwards as an indictment. This indictment's cadences have been heard many times on the wilderness road; set them side by side (cross-reference layer): at Rephidim in Exodus 17 it was likewise "no water," the people likewise strove, and likewise spoke of being made to die of thirst, themselves and their cattle, and God there commanded Moses to smite the rock, and water came out (Exod 17:1-7, key points); in Numbers 14, after the spies' evil report, the whole congregation lifted up their voice and cried, saying likewise that they wished they had died in Egypt or in the wilderness (Num 14:1-3, key points); in Numbers 16 Korah's company, in Numbers 17 the whole congregation — again and again. The content of the strife has not changed; what has changed is only who speaks it — the older generation has nearly all died, yet the accusation has lived on and been handed to the next. What sin passes down is not blood but syntax: one generation teaches the next its words of complaint, and the next generation, facing the same want of water, replays the sentences verbatim.

(Fact) The reaction of Moses and Aaron is as before, quoted verbatim: "And Moses and Aaron went from the presence of the assembly unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, and they fell upon their faces: and the glory of the Lord appeared unto them." (20:6) This step is not wrong: before strife, first fall on your face, then speak — for forty years this has been their steadiest posture. God does not at once rebuke the people, nor first comfort Moses — after the glory appears, verse 7 has only a speech introduction: "And the Lord spake unto Moses, saying," (20:7). He gives the command directly. Verse 8 is the most pivotal sentence of the chapter, quoted verbatim: "Take the rod, and gather thou the assembly together, thou, and Aaron thy brother, and speak ye unto the rock before their eyes; and it shall give forth his water, and thou shalt bring forth to them water out of the rock: so thou shalt give the congregation and their beasts drink." (20:8) Take this command apart into four items: first, take the rod — the rod is to be carried, but the rod is a token, not a tool; second, gather the assembly together with Aaron — the thing is to be done before all eyes; third, speak ye unto the rock before their eyes — the commanded action is "speak," speech, not striking; fourth, the outcome: water comes out of the rock, for the congregation and their beasts to drink. The centre of the command is not the rod and not the water but the speaking — God wants the people to see that at His word the water comes out, with no need for any man to lay a hand to it for Him. This verse is set here first as it stands and will be taken apart word by word in Part Two; remember first the order of the command as given: the first thing God names is taking the rod, but the core action is speaking — the rod in the hand, the word in the mouth, and the hand must not go before the mouth.

(Argument) This section's first lesson for today's hearers is to recognise the syntax in your own mouth. What wounds most in the people's accusation is not the want of water — the want is real, and the thirst is real — but the way they explain the want: they call the men who led them out of Egypt men who "made us to come up," call the road of forty years "this evil place," and call their dead brethren luckier than their living selves. When a man suffers, his memory is revised: the slavery of Egypt is forgotten, only Egypt's food and drink remembered; the water-walls of the Red Sea forgotten, only the present thirst remembered. Men today revise their own histories the same way: when a marriage is bitter, the vows of the wedding day are rewritten as "I was deceived"; when service is bitter, the first touch of God is rewritten as "I was used"; when work is bitter, everyone who ever led them is rewritten as an enemy. Scripture records the accusation verbatim not to shame the people but to give us a mirror: that sentence in your heart, "Would God that we had died," and that sentence, "why have ye brought us" here — God has heard them both. He did not turn and walk away for those sentences — His servants were still fallen on their faces, His glory still appeared, and His command was still to give water. Complaint can be heard, but complaint cannot become your only way of knowing God; want of water is a circumstance, but turning circumstance into accusation is a choice.

(Argument) The second lesson is in the falling on the face. Having heard the accusation, Moses and Aaron's first reaction is not self-defence and not answering abuse with abuse, but leaving the congregation, going to the door of the tabernacle, and falling on their faces. This order they kept for forty years: they fell on their faces in Numbers 14, in Numbers 16, in Numbers 17, and in this chapter still. Falling on the face means: before you open your mouth to answer men, let God open His. How many conflicts go wrong exactly at this inversion of order — the man is answered first, and prayer remembered afterwards; the words are sent out first, and what God says remembered afterwards. If today you are surrounded by a crowd accusing you — at home, at work, in the church — this chapter's first action for you is not explanation but falling on your face: leave the scene, come back before God, let the glory appear first and the command come down first. Moses' tragedy in this chapter is not at this step — this step he did right; the tragedy lies in the few steps after he rose from his face and walked to the rock. An order kept halfway must still be kept to the last step — that is Part Two's business.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The verb behind "strove/chode" is rîb (H7378), to strive, accuse, contend at law; in this chapter verse 3 (the people "chode with Moses") and verse 13 (the children of Israel "strove with the Lord") are both of this root, and its noun form mərîbâ (H4809) is the place-name "Meribah" of verse 13; the details are registered in Parts Two and Three. The word for "congregation" is ʿēdâ (H5712), the formally assembled whole; it occurs repeatedly in this chapter, and the count is in Endnote 3. The sentence "Would God that we had died" rests on a wish-construction, which the base text renders as the people's wish to die with their brethren; this sermon registers the rendering only and builds no original-language argument on it here. "Prophetess" is Miriam's title-word in Exodus 15:20; this sermon registers it as the base text gives it and does not enlarge the scope of her office.

(New Testament Reading) Hebrews reads Meribah as a warning sign still in force, its pivot sentence quoted from the base text: "Harden not your hearts, as in the provocation, in the day of temptation in the wilderness:" (Heb 3:8). It says further, in key points, that if today we hear His voice we are not to harden our hearts as in the day of provocation (Heb 3:15, key points). The New Testament writer cites Psalm 95 in reading back this chapter and Exodus 17, and his landing point is "today": Meribah is not an archaeological site but every present moment in which a man hears God's word and hardens his heart. At this chapter's fact layer only this chapter's own events are registered: the people strive, Moses falls on his face, God gives the command; how the matter is taken up in the New Testament belongs to the reading layer, and this sermon does not lay it down in the fact layer beforehand. The original sentence of Psalm 95 is likewise registered at this layer, as the base text gives it: "Harden not your heart, as in the provocation, and as in the day of temptation in the wilderness:" (Ps 95:8) — note that the KJV at this verse names no place; Meribah and Massah are named expressly in the Chinese base text, and each manuscript registers its own text.

### Part Two: Speak to the Rock — but He Struck the Rock (Numbers 20:7-13) (20:00-42:00)

(Fact) After the command is given, verse 9 records Moses' first action, quoted verbatim: "And Moses took the rod from before the Lord, as he commanded him." (20:9) Two items registered: the rod is taken "from before the Lord" — where this rod ordinarily rests the text says only "from before the Lord," and this sermon records it as it stands without determining which rod it is (for the register of blanks see Endnote 7); and Moses' start is according to the command — taking the rod and gathering the assembly are not yet wrong. The wrong begins when he opens his mouth. In verse 10 Moses and Aaron gather the congregation together before the rock; the text records no prayer of Moses, no pause of Moses, only the first sentence out of his mouth, quoted verbatim: "And Moses and Aaron gathered the congregation together before the rock, and he said unto them, Hear now, ye rebels; must we fetch you water out of this rock?" (20:10) The problems of this sentence, registered item by item. First, the address: "Hear now, ye rebels" — God's command gave Moses no warrant to fasten this label on the people before them all; that the people are striving is fact, but the title of judgment ought not to come out of a leader's anger. Second, the pronoun: "must we fetch you water" — the authority to speak to the rock is God's command, yet what comes out of Moses' mouth makes it his own achievement; note the base-text difference registered in the head of this sermon — the KJV reads the plural "we," where the Chinese base text reads the first person singular "I," sharper still; each manuscript argues from its own text, and on either text the fetching of the water is being entered under the speakers' own name. Third, the order: God commanded him to speak to the rock, but he spoke first to the people — and in anger. What God wanted was Himself sanctified before the people's eyes; what Moses gave was a venting of temper before the people's eyes; God's glory had already appeared in verse 6, and Moses' words in verse 10 drag the eyes of the crowd from God's glory onto his own anger.

(Fact) Then comes the blow, quoted verbatim: "And Moses lifted up his hand, and with his rod he smote the rock twice: and the water came out abundantly, and the congregation drank, and their beasts also." (20:11) "Twice" is no slip of the pen; it is a number the text deliberately records: one blow is the old command repeated, the second blow is anger's surcharge. The water came out all the same — God did not, because Moses' method was wrong, withhold the people's water. Grace is supplied as usual: this is God's faithfulness to the people. Judgment falls as usual: this is God's holiness toward the leader. The two run side by side in one chapter without contradiction; do not conclude, because the water came out, that the blow was no problem; and do not conclude, because judgment fell, that the people did not drink. God's supply does not endorse man's method, and God's judgment does not withdraw His supply.

(Fact) The words of judgment are in verse 12, quoted verbatim: "And the Lord spake unto Moses and Aaron, Because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel, therefore ye shall not bring this congregation into the land which I have given them." (20:12) Two charges, each answering to its act. First, "ye believed me not" — what Moses disbelieved was not "whether God can bring out water"; he plainly believed the water would come, or he would not have dared lift the rod; what he disbelieved was "whether God's word is enough": God said "speak," and he insisted on adding a striking — as if to say, God's word plus one blow of the rod is the safer course. Second, "to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel" was not done — what God wanted was that the people should see, before the rock, a God who brings out water by speaking one sentence; what Moses gave the people was a God who brings out water only when the leader loses his temper and lays on his hand. The people drank the water, but they mistook God — that is the whole weight of "not sanctifying Him in their eyes." Note also that the verdict is addressed to two men, Moses and Aaron: Aaron in this chapter has not spoken one sentence and has not lifted the rod once; he only gathered the congregation together with Moses and stood beside the rock — and when the verdict is restated in verse 24 it still says "ye rebelled against my word." Silent presence is still presence; a leader's account is never entered only under the name of the man whose hand moved.

(Fact) Verse 13 is the chapter's seal, quoted verbatim: "This is the water of Meribah; because the children of Israel strove with the Lord, and he was sanctified in them." (20:13) The items registered: this water bears a name, the water of Meribah; and the ground of the naming is two-sided — the children of Israel strove with the LORD, and the LORD was sanctified in them. At the same place, in the same event, what man records is strife, and what God displays is holiness. The place-name itself is the written verdict: this place is henceforth called "Strife," because man strove here and God was sanctified here. Psalm 106 supplies the other face of the judgment: "They angered him also at the waters of strife, so that it went ill with Moses for their sakes: Because they provoked his spirit, so that he spake unadvisedly with his lips." (Ps 106:32-33) The psalm states the responsibility more completely: the people provoked Moses' spirit, and Moses spoke unadvisedly with his lips — the people have their account and Moses has his, and the two accounts do not cancel each other. This chapter records the mouth first (the words of verse 10) and the hand after (the rod of verse 11): on God's account, the unadvised words and the striking rod are two faces of one offence — out of the mouth first, then out of the hand.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only; only verified items are registered, set out in a list. "Speak" in the command is dābar (H1696), to speak, say, command — the action of the command in 20:8 is this word. "Hear" is šāmaʿ (H8085): the "Hear" of Moses' first sentence in 20:10, "Hear now, ye rebels," is this word, and it is not the same verb as dābar — God commanded him to "speak" (dābar) to the rock, and he first cried "Hear" (šāmaʿ) to the people; the contrast of the two verbs is the text's own, not the preacher's invention. "Rock" is selaʿ (H5553), a crag, a cliff-rock; verses 8, 10, and 11 of this chapter are all this word. The rock at Rephidim in Exodus 17 is ṣûr (H6697) in the original — not the same word as selaʿ; the distinction of the two words is registered as the original gives it. "Rod" is maṭṭeh (H4294), in verses 8, 9, and 11 of this chapter. "Smote" is nākāh (H5221), the verb of the striking of the rock in 20:11; "twice" rests on the plural form of pāʿam (H6471), a time, a step — this verse expressly records two times. "Rebels" is mōrîm, from the root mārâ (H4784), to rebel, to be refractory; the number is supplied by this round's supplementary check: H4784. "Believed not" rests on the verb ʾāman (H539), to believe, trust, be established; in 20:12 it stands in the negative, "ye believed me not" — and from this same root come "amen" and "faithfulness/truth": the sin laid to Moses' charge is precisely the reverse of this word. The verb of "to sanctify" is qādaš (H6942), to set apart as holy, to show oneself holy; verses 12 and 13 are both of this root. "Meribah" is mərîbâ (H4809), its root rîb (H7378), to strive, accuse; it occurs twice in this chapter, at verses 13 and 24. "Congregation" is ʿēdâ (H5712), already registered in Part One. The numbers above were cross-checked against the BLB lexicon pages and the Strong's entries before writing.

(Fact) Here is a contrast that must be registered. In Exodus 17 at Rephidim, God commanded Moses to smite the rock, its pivot sentence in the base text: "thou shalt smite the rock, and there shall come water out of it, that the people may drink" (Exod 17:6, key-point quotation). Moses obeyed, the water came out, and that striking was obedience. In this chapter God commands Moses to speak to the rock, and Moses, following the old method of forty years before, strikes again — and this striking is unbelief. Two places (Rephidim and Kadesh), two words (ṣûr and selaʿ), two commands (smite and speak) — the text keeps them perfectly distinct. Old experience fails before a new command — this is the chapter's sharpest lesson for all who have served long: that God told you to strike last time does not mean He tells you to strike this time; to take last time's successful experience as this time's command is itself unbelief.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The nature of Moses' sin has been held in several accounts through the ages, set out here without adjudication. First, the unbelief account: resting on the verdict's own text in verse 12 — the sin lay in not believing God's word sufficient, the striking being added to make it up; the verdict says this expressly, and the sermon text builds its argument chiefly on this. Second, the anger account: resting on Psalm 106:33, "he spake unadvisedly with his lips" — the sin lay in anger and unadvised words, the striking being anger's extension. Third, the overreach account: the sin lay in presumption — putting himself forward in the first person as the fetcher of the water (20:10) and turning God's command into his own performance. Fourth, the representative-bearing account: resting on Moses' own words in Deuteronomy, that the LORD was angry with him for the people's sake (Deut 1:37, key points) — read as the leader bearing the consequences on the people's behalf. Each of the four accounts has its textual ground; the weighing of their relative weight belongs to the discussion layer. At this chapter's fact layer the verdict itself alone stands: "believed me not" and "to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel" not done — these two are the charges God names with His own mouth, and no other account may step over the verdict and set up another charge. Whether selaʿ and ṣûr are the same rock likewise belongs to the discussion layer: the fact layer registers only that the two words differ, the two places differ (Rephidim and Kadesh), and the two commands differ; whether they are geographically one rock formation the text does not say, and this sermon makes no factual statement on it.

(New Testament Reading) Paul in 1 Corinthians 10 reads back the wilderness rock, its pivot sentence quoted from the base text: "And did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ." (1 Cor 10:4) He says further, in key points, that these things happened as examples for us (1 Cor 10:11, key points). Paul reads the rock as Christ and Meribah as a warning — at this reading layer, the rock is struck once (Exodus 17) and thereafter is only to be spoken to (Numbers 20), because Christ dies once and thereafter is only to be prayed to. Moses' error, in the New Testament's reading, becomes a footnote to the gospel: the rock that has been struck is not to be struck again. Proverbs, for its part, lays bare the mechanism of anger; two places are registered at this layer from the base text: "A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger." (Prov 15:1) "He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city." (Prov 16:32) Moses' words in verse 10 are precisely what Proverbs calls "grievous words," caught in the act.

(Argument) The kindling point of this section — carve it into your heart: God can give you water, yet not lend His name to your anger. Moses was faithful for forty years, and one failure cost him his qualification to enter Canaan — not because God is petty, but because holiness has weight. God's name is not there to endorse your temper. Today, not tomorrow: if you are even now losing your temper with your family, with your fellow workers, with the congregation, and saying as you do it, "This is God's righteous anger," stop. God's command is "speak ye unto the rock," not "smite the rock"; He wants you to sanctify Him before the people's eyes, not to prove before the people's eyes that you are right. Confess now, lay the rod down now, change your words now — speak, do not strike. And a further lesson for the results-driven: that the water came out does not mean you did right. Israel drank their fill, and on the scoreboard Moses was still the loser. How many servants absolve themselves by results — the people multiplied, the money arrived, the thing succeeded, so the method need not be asked about; this chapter nails results and evaluation apart: the result is evidence of God's faithfulness, not evidence of your obedience. That God has mercy on the people does not mean God approves the leader. Do not take the grace God gave to others as the warrant of your own acquittal.

### Part Three: Edom Bars the Way, and Aaron Ascends Mount Hor (Numbers 20:14-29) (42:00-54:00)

(Fact) The chapter's third movement is often read as an appendix; in fact it is the scene where the judgment is carried out. Verse 14, the opening of the diplomatic letter, quoted verbatim: "And Moses sent messengers from Kadesh unto the king of Edom, Thus saith thy brother Israel, Thou knowest all the travail that hath befallen us:" (20:14) The letter's content stands in verses 15-17, registered verse by verse. Verse 15 tells the family's suffering, in key points: the fathers went down into Egypt and dwelt there a long time, and the Egyptians vexed the fathers and them. Verse 16 tells the grace of the exodus, quoted in full: "And when we cried unto the Lord, he heard our voice, and sent an angel, and hath brought us forth out of Egypt: and, behold, we are in Kadesh, a city in the uttermost of thy border:" (20:16) Verse 17 is the request itself, quoted verbatim: "Let us pass, I pray thee, through thy country: we will not pass through the fields, or through the vineyards, neither will we drink of the water of the wells: we will go by the king's high way, we will not turn to the right hand nor to the left, until we have passed thy borders." (20:17) This is a letter written with the utmost measure: it claims brotherhood, states its origin plainly, marks its route clearly, and promises not to take one drop of water and not to turn to right or left. The king of Edom's answer is one sentence, quoted verbatim: "And Edom said unto him, Thou shalt not pass by me, lest I come out against thee with the sword." (20:18) Israel yields one step further, quoted verbatim: "And the children of Israel said unto him, We will go by the high way: and if I and my cattle drink of thy water, then I will pay for it: I will only, without doing anything else, go through on my feet." (20:19) The king of Edom answers once more, quoted verbatim: "And he said, Thou shalt not go through. And Edom came out against him with much people, and with a strong hand." (20:20) Verse 21 closes the matter, in key points: Edom refused to give Israel passage through his border, and Israel turned away from him. Israel turned aside and did not provoke him.

(Fact) Who is Edom? The descendants of Esau, Jacob's brother. The standing that the opening sentence of Genesis 36 fixes is, in key points, that Esau is Edom (Gen 36:1, key points). Further back, in Genesis 25, Esau sells his birthright to Jacob for a bowl of red pottage, and is therefore also called Edom (Gen 25:30, key points) — Edom means "red": a name taken from a pot of red stew, and kept for four hundred years. That family affair has become, by this chapter, a roadblock between two nations: the brother will not let the way through — not because the route is wrong, but because the old account in the blood has never been settled. Scripture passes no moral comment on Edom's refusal; it records one fact only: Israel turned away and did not provoke him. Deuteronomy later states the rule of this road plainly, its pivot sentence quoted from the base text: "Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite; for he is thy brother:" (Deut 23:7). Obadiah, for its part, records Edom's account from the other end: in the day when his brother Jacob suffered calamity, Edom stood afar off and gloated (Obad 1:10-12, cross-reference layer) — Edom's "come out with the sword" in this chapter has a sequel in the prophets, but the one who judges Edom is God, not this chapter's Israel. Israel, which has just learned at Meribah "not to act in anger," this time does not force its way through in anger — they turned aside. This one turning aside is the first lesson after the judgment of verse 12: not every blocked road has to be forced open, and not every refusal has to be answered in kind. Psalm 133 writes the right form of the brotherly relation there: "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!" (Ps 133:1) Edom and Israel are both sons of Isaac, yet one blocks the road and the other goes around it — dwelling together in unity did not happen; restraint and yielding did. Proverbs' rule fits this turning exactly: "Say not thou, I will recompense evil; but wait on the Lord, and he shall save thee." (Prov 20:22) Turning aside is not weakness; it is handing the sovereignty of vengeance back to the LORD.

(Fact) Then comes the death of Aaron. Verses 22-23 are the journey and the utterance, registered in key points: the whole congregation journeys from Kadesh and comes to Mount Hor, and the LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron at Mount Hor, by the coast of the land of Edom. Verse 24 is God's verdict, quoted verbatim: "Aaron shall be gathered unto his people: for he shall not enter into the land which I have given unto the children of Israel, because ye rebelled against my word at the water of Meribah." (20:24) Two items in the verdict are to be registered: first, at "gathered unto his people" the Chinese base text reads "gathered to his ancestors" and carries the footnote "the original reads 'his people'" — it is a word of comfort: death is not disappearance but rejoining the ranks; second, the verdict enters the Meribah account under both Moses' and Aaron's names together: "ye rebelled against my word." Verses 25-26 are the procedure of execution, quoted verbatim: "Take Aaron and Eleazar his son, and bring them up unto mount Hor: And strip Aaron of his garments, and put them upon Eleazar his son: and Aaron shall be gathered unto his people, and shall die there." (20:25-26) Verses 27-28 are the completion of the procedure, quoted verbatim: "And Moses did as the Lord commanded: and they went up into mount Hor in the sight of all the congregation. And Moses stripped Aaron of his garments, and put them upon Eleazar his son; and Aaron died there in the top of the mount: and Moses and Eleazar came down from the mount." (20:27-28) The procedure registered item by item: three men go up the mountain — the priest who is to die, the brother who sees him off, the son who succeeds; only two come down. The garments are stripped off and put on at the top of the mount, in the sight of all the congregation: the handover of the office is done in public, leaving no vacuum and allowing no contest. The garments are not buried on the mountain with Aaron; the garments are passed on — the office is longer-lived than the man, and the priest's office does not die with one man's death. Verse 29 closes the whole chapter, quoted verbatim: "And when all the congregation saw that Aaron was dead, they mourned for Aaron thirty days, even all the house of Israel." (20:29) At Miriam's death the text records no number of days of mourning; at Aaron's death the whole camp mourns thirty days — answering, before and after, to the thirty days for which Israel mourned Moses at the end of Deuteronomy (Deut 34:8, cross-reference layer). Within one chapter two leaders are buried: the sister at Kadesh, without sound or show; the brother on Mount Hor, with mourning for a full month. The text gives the two funerals different measures of ink, but the same weight: neither of them may enter the land, and both are buried by God Himself or by His own arrangement — within the verdict there is still dignity, and within the judgment there is still grace.

(Argument) The connection between Aaron's death and the preceding chapter is this chapter's most deeply hidden lesson. Chapter 19 has just laid down the law of cleansing from a corpse: whoever touches a dead body is unclean seven days and must be sprinkled with the water of separation on the third day and the seventh day (Num 19:11-12, key points). Immediately in chapter 20 the whole camp loses two of its nearest kin in succession — Miriam and Aaron. The statute was not written in peaceful years: God lays down the procedure of cleansing, and the very next chapter puts it to use. See also the order clearly: Aaron dies on the mountaintop; where his body rests and who gathers it in, this chapter does not record (for the register of blanks see Endnote 7); the camp's mourning is recorded at thirty days, yet the procedure of cleansing this chapter does not mention in a single word — the text sets the mourning in the open and leaves the cleansing to chapter 19's statute to speak for itself: those who mourn act in cleansing according to the statute, and those who are cleansed return to the camp and walk on. Death can be mourned and death can be cleansed, but death cannot be bypassed. The church's procedure for sending off its own dead today is the same: the tears of the memorial service and the recovery of daily life are not two opposed things; mourn in the order God has laid down, and set out again in the grace God has laid down — when the thirty days are full, the people below Mount Hor must still march on toward the plains of Moab, and this chapter's next chapter (Numbers 21) is marching and war. Mourning has a term; that is not coldness but mercy: God does not permit His people to stop forever before a grave.

(New Testament Reading) Hebrews draws this line up more cleanly still, three sentences quoted from the base text: "And they truly were many priests, because they were not suffered to continue by reason of death:" (Heb 7:23) "But this man, because he continueth ever, hath an unchangeable priesthood." (Heb 7:24) And, in key points, that He is able to save them to the uttermost who come unto God by Him, seeing He ever liveth to make intercession for them (Heb 7:25, key points). Aaron's death proves one thing: priests made of men all die, and the office must be carried on by replacement; Christ, made priest, does not die — He ever liveth to make intercession for men. Numbers 20 ends with thirty days of mourning; the New Testament continues with a High Priest who liveth ever — this is no negation of the Old Testament, but the gap the Old Testament itself left, waiting for the New Testament to fill it. The scene of Eleazar putting on the garments at the mountaintop also prepares this hope at the reading layer: God's people never lack a priest; one falls, another puts on the garments — until that One who continueth ever comes, and the garments need never be stripped off again.

## Application and Response (54:00-60:00)

Application to six groups of hearers. First, to seekers: you may suppose that believing in Jesus is just exchanging one set of rules for a stricter set. This chapter tells you the opposite: God's requirement of the leader is stricter than His requirement of the people, because the rod in the leader's hand has weight. The God you are to know is not a God who indulges anger — He is holy — yet He still brings out water for the people: His holiness and His supply are never at two opposite ends. Second, to new believers: do not take "God led me this way last time" as "God leads me this way every time." The striking in Exodus was right; the striking in Numbers was wrong; the only difference is what God said this time. Reading the Bible, praying, listening for God's voice are not formalities; they are how you keep yourself from putting old experience in the place of a new command. Third, to those easily angered at home and at work: Proverbs says, "He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city" (Prov 16:32). Moses' lesson is that words spoken in anger, even when the outcome is right (the water did come out), are still wrong in process. Next time the anger rises, first leave the scene for three minutes, then open your mouth — three minutes are enough for you to exchange "striking" for "speaking." Fourth, to parents: your children learn most readily the way you lose your temper. Moses said one sentence before the congregation — "Hear now, ye rebels" — and the whole camp heard how a leader treats the people. Your children are also listening to how you treat your family: the words you speak at the table today are the words your child will speak at school tomorrow. Fifth, to church leaders and servants: the greater your rod, the heavier your Meribah account. God's judgment on Moses was not aimed at the small in faith but at the most faithful and the most senior. The leader's lesson is: when the people provoke you, first fall on your face (20:6), then receive the command, then speak; once the order is disordered, judgment begins with the leader. Sixth, to those now grieving and sending off loved ones: Miriam died, Aaron died, and Moses will die too — this chapter expressly records two funerals, and the third waits in Deuteronomy. Thirty days of mourning is not weakness; it is a procedure Scripture permits; and at the same time the garments are passed to Eleazar, telling you this: the office you love, the church you love, the family you love, God will carry on with new people; they will not be cut off by one person's death.

## Closing Prayer

Let us pray together. Heavenly Father, we thank You: Your water still came out, and Your holiness still took no discount. We confess that we have all, in anger, struck the rock — at home, in the church, at work, with our own hand adding one blow of the rod to Your command. Lord, forgive us. Give us the courage of a decision: from today on, where You say speak, we will speak; where You say wait, we will wait; where You say turn aside, we will turn aside, and not force our way through in anger.

(Call) If you have heard today and know in your heart that you are striking a rock God told you to speak to — toward your spouse, your child, your fellow worker, your church — I invite you to stand up now and come forward. Not when your anger has passed, not when the other party has confessed first. Lay the rod down, and change your words: Lord, I was wrong; I did not sanctify You before the people's eyes. Forgive me now, and entrust Your name to my mouth again, so that from today I speak only the words You command. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

God said "speak ye unto the rock"; Moses "smote the rock twice." The water came out all the same, and the judgment fell all the same — this is the hardest chapter in Numbers to read, and the most honest. God's supply does not endorse your method, and God's judgment does not withdraw His supply. The most dangerous thing about old experience is that it once succeeded: the striking in Exodus was right, the striking in Numbers was wrong, and the only difference is what God said this time. Today, examine the rod in your hand: is there a rock God told you to speak to, that you have been striking all along? Is there a person God told you to address softly, that you have been hammering with anger all along? Lay the rod down and change your speech to speaking. What He wants is that you sanctify Him before the people's eyes, not that you prove before the people's eyes that you are right. And there is the chapter's second half: when the road was blocked by Edom, Israel turned aside and did not provoke him; when a loved one died on the mountain, the whole camp mourned thirty days and then marched on. Turning aside and mourning are both forms of obedience — not every blocked road must be forced open, and not every funeral must be turned over in haste. Walk in His order, and He will bring you into the land.

English (KJV-based):

"Take the rod, and gather thou the assembly together... and speak ye unto the rock before their eyes" (Num 20:8, KJV). Moses spoke, but he also struck — twice. "And the water came out abundantly, and the congregation drank" (20:11, KJV). Yet the verdict followed: "Because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel, therefore ye shall not bring this congregation into the land" (20:12, KJV). God will give water to His people, but He will not lend His name to our anger. The rock was struck once; now we are to speak to it. Put down the rod. Speak, do not strike — and let Him be holy before their eyes. And when the way is blocked, turn aside as Israel turned from Edom; when a loved one dies, weep your thirty days as Israel wept for Aaron — then rise and go on, for He is still leading.

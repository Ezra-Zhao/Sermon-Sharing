# All the Earth Shall Be Filled with the Glory of the LORD — A Sermon on Numbers 14

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Draft — awaiting cross-review (Sermon Production Batch 3, 23 October 2026, unpublished); first translation awaiting proofreading
Text: Numbers 14 (the whole chapter, 45 verses)
Base texts: Scripture quotations in this English manuscript are embedded from the King James Version (KJV) verbatim; the companion Chinese manuscript follows the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation edition (和合本新标点本), checked verse by verse on 23 October 2026. The two manuscripts each follow their own base text and are not translations of each other's quotations.
Title verification: The title is taken from the continuous wording of God's oath in 14:21 KJV — "all the earth shall be filled with the glory of the LORD". The Chinese title 遍地要被我的荣耀充满 is taken independently from the continuous wording of the same verse in the Chinese base text. Candidates examined verse by verse and set aside: "I have pardoned according to thy word" (14:20, pardon without the consequence), Caleb's "another spirit" (14:24, one man's exception only), and "fear them not" (14:9, Joshua and Caleb's counsel, rejected on the spot). Only 14:21 stands between the pardon (14:20) and the verdict (14:22-23) as the chapter's hinge, and fixes its direction: this generation may fail, but the filling of the earth with God's glory will not. ## Summary

This chapter follows straight on from the spies' report. The report is barely finished when the whole congregation weeps all night and proposes making a captain and returning to Egypt (14:1-4). Moses and Aaron fall on their faces; Joshua and Caleb rent their clothes and plead with the people, ending, "fear them not" (14:5-9); the congregation answers that they should be stoned, and the glory of the LORD appears in the tabernacle (14:10). God proposes to smite the people with pestilence and make of Moses a greater nation; Moses intercedes on the ground of what the Egyptians will hear, what the nations will say, and the long self-description of God's character (14:11-19). God's answer comes in two halves: "I have pardoned according to thy word" (14:20), and then, under oath, "all the earth shall be filled with the glory of the LORD" (14:21) — while this generation shall not see the land, Caleb alone excepted by name (14:22-24), and the camp turns back toward the wilderness by the way of the Red sea (14:25). The verdict is then spelled out: carcases in the wilderness, the twenty-years-and-upward bar, the little ones brought in instead, the children wandering forty years, each day for a year (14:26-35); the ten spies die by the plague, Joshua and Caleb live still (14:36-38). After mourning, the people rise early and presume to go up, and are beaten back as far as Hormah (14:39-45). The pardon is real, and the consequence is real — the chapter sets the two side by side and lets the reader keep neither half alone.

## Main Text

### 1. The Night of Weeping and "Fear Them Not" (Numbers 14:1-10) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) The chapter's first sentence gives no setting beyond the hour; its first stroke is the sound of the whole camp: "And all the congregation lifted up their voice, and cried; and the people wept that night" (14:1). The previous chapter has only just ended with the ten saying, "we are not able to go up against the people; for they are stronger than we" (13:31), and the narrator does not let the report cool: the weeping follows in the same breath. The subjects of 14:1 should be read as the Chinese base text has them: it is "all the congregation" （全会众） that lifts its voice and "the people" （百姓） that weep — two terms set side by side for the same crowd. Verse 2 then gives the words: "And all the children of Israel murmured against Moses and against Aaron: and the whole congregation said unto them, Would God that we had died in the land of Egypt! or would God we had died in this wilderness!" (14:2). In the base text the murmuring is directed at Moses and Aaron and spoken by "all the children of Israel" / "the whole congregation"; this manuscript records it so, and does not narrow the speakers to the ten spies or enlarge the address — though God will presently rule that the murmuring was against himself (14:27), that is his verdict at its own level, not to be moved forward into this verse.

(Fact) Verse 3 is the congregation's argument, quoted in full: "And wherefore hath the LORD brought us unto this land, to fall by the sword, that our wives and our children should be a prey? were it not better for us to return into Egypt?" (14:3). The three clauses form one chain of reasoning: first God's motive is fixed — he brought them out in order for them to fall by the sword; then the family's fate — wives and children shall be a prey; then the conclusion — better to return into Egypt. Every step is stated in the future-certain mood: the prey clause, in the Chinese, carries the word 必， "shall surely", though no battle has been fought. In verse 4 the proposal takes shape: "And they said one to another, Let us make a captain, and let us return into Egypt" (14:4). In the base text the speakers are "the people" （众人） and the verb is simply "make/set up" a captain — not the "appoint another leader" of some other versions. And the content matters: returning to Egypt requires a new captain, because the one who led them out of Egypt is precisely the one leading them into Canaan. This is no longer only fear; it is a change of leadership, and with the leader, a reversal of direction.

(Fact) The response comes in two groups, and action precedes speech. First: "Then Moses and Aaron fell on their faces before all the assembly of the congregation of the children of Israel" (14:5). No rebuttal, no self-defence — two men on their faces before the whole assembly, the chapter's first prostration and the leaders' only recorded posture before the crowd's anger. Second, two of the spies: "And Joshua the son of Nun, and Caleb the son of Jephunneh, which were of them that searched the land, rent their clothes: and they spake unto all the company of the children of Israel, saying, The land, which we passed through to search it, is an exceeding good land" (14:6-7). Rent clothes are a sign of grief, and in the base text the act belongs to Joshua and Caleb. Their first sentence speaks of the land, not the enemy — "an exceeding good land" （极美之地）, agreeing with the whole team's "milk and honey" while being these two men's own judgment spoken in person. Verse 8 gives the condition: "If the LORD delight in us, then he will bring us into this land, and give it us; a land which floweth with milk and honey" (14:8). The condition is not their own strength but the LORD's delight, and the subject of "will bring us in" is the LORD throughout.

(Fact) Verse 9 is the two men's conclusion, quoted entire: "Only rebel not ye against the LORD, neither fear ye the people of the land; for they are bread for us: their defence is departed from them, and the LORD is with us: fear them not" (14:9). Its sense has three layers, and their order must not be disturbed: first the prohibition — rebel not against the LORD, which names the Egypt proposal by its nature: not a change of route but rebellion; then the judgment — the people of the land are bread for us, and their defence is departed from them; last the ground — the LORD is with us, therefore fear them not. "Fear not" occurs twice in the verse: first "neither fear ye the people of the land", then the closing "fear them not". One divergence from secondary summaries must be registered here: the Chinese base text reads 荫庇他们的已经离开他们 — "that which shaded/sheltered them has already departed from them", with both the word 荫 (shade) and 已经 (already) present — not the shortened 他们的庇护离开他们 of the commissioning summary; and those addressed are warned against fearing 那地的居民， "the inhabitants of the land", not "the people" in general. Read word for word, the edge of the two men's plea is not dulled in transmission.

(Fact) The outcome of the plea is verse 10: "But all the congregation bade stone them with stones. And the glory of the LORD appeared in the tabernacle of the congregation before all the children of Israel" (14:10). The "them" to be stoned is, by context, the two who had just spoken — Joshua and Caleb; Moses and Aaron, still on their faces, are not named in this sentence, and this manuscript follows the base text and does not enlarge the list. At the very moment the congregation speaks of stoning, the glory appears. The narrator sets the two side by side: the stones are not yet thrown, and God's glory has already arrived. The place is the tabernacle, the witnesses "all the children of Israel" — the whole camp sees it; this is no private comfort for Moses but a public intervention. Here the section stops: what God says after the appearing belongs to the next section.

(Application) The order of that night is the chapter's first lesson. Weeping first, reasoning second, proposal last — the emotion leads, the reasons are supplied afterwards, and the conclusion was in fact settled before the argument began: it only waited for a set of words to dress it. Verse 3 sounds as though every clause were for the family's sake, wives and children laid on the table; but its first step fixed God's motive as murderous intent, and once the motive is wrong, every later step is "reasonable" — persuasively, confidently wrong. Major decisions are still made in this order today: the heart first takes its step backward, then assembles its reasons, and the fuller the reasons, the less visible the crooked first step. The two men differ not in what they saw — they walked the same forty days and saw the same tall men — but in whom they put first. The congregation's first sentence was "wherefore hath the LORD brought us unto this land"; the two men's first sentences were the land's goodness, God's delight, and his presence. Who is looked at first decides what is seen afterwards.

(Summary) Words at the philological level only. The verb rendered "murmured", lûn (H3885), recurs across the chapter (14:2, 27, 29): it is the term that defines the congregation's relation to God and its leaders, not a mere measure of noise. The verb rendered "rebel", mārad (H4775, 14:9), is a political word for revolt: Joshua and Caleb use it to name the Egypt proposal, showing they read the proposal not as timidity but as mutiny. The noun rendered "defence", ṣēl (H6738, 14:9), properly means shadow or shade; the KJV's "defence" catches its function — shade departed is a man left uncovered at noon. And leḥem (H3899), "bread", makes "they are bread for us" a metaphor of eating, to be read as the metaphor it is, not as literal devouring.


### 2. Moses Intercedes: The Egyptians Will Hear of It (Numbers 14:11-19) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) After the glory appears, the first word is God's to Moses, and its two questions are quoted as the base text has them: "How long will this people provoke me? and how long will it be ere they believe me, for all the signs which I have shewed among them?" (14:11). Two "how long"s enclose an inventory: the signs have already been shown — "all the signs which I have shewed" is past tense — while the unbelief continues. God's characterization is therefore not "they have not yet learned" but provoking and not believing: they have seen, and still do not believe. Verse 12 is the proposal, and it is the exact counterpart of the congregation's proposal in verse 4: "I will smite them with the pestilence, and disinherit them, and will make of thee a greater nation and mightier than they" (14:12). The congregation wanted to replace its leader; God says to the present leader: I will destroy this batch, and from you raise a nation greater and mightier. If Moses had a private interest, this was his greatest personal opportunity — the people had just talked of stoning the spokesmen and of making another captain, and God offers him a ready nation, stronger than the present one. Moses' answer, from verse 13, does not mention himself once.

(Fact) Moses' intercession is here counted item by item, and registered as follows. (1) The Egyptians will hear (14:13): "And Moses said unto the LORD, Then the Egyptians shall hear it, (for thou broughtest up this people in thy might from among them;)". The premise is that the exodus is already public: the One who worked the mighty acts cannot destroy, mid-journey, the people he brought out, without the news travelling back to the place of departure. (2) The Egyptians will tell the inhabitants of Canaan — and those inhabitants have already heard four things (14:14): "And they will tell it to the inhabitants of this land: for they have heard that thou LORD art among this people, that thou LORD art seen face to face, and that thy cloud standeth over them, and that thou goest before them, by day time in a pillar of a cloud, and in a pillar of fire by night." The four, in the verse's own order: that the LORD is among this people; that he is seen face to face; that his cloud stands over them; that he goes before them in a pillar of cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night. The people of the destination already know the manner of God's presence with this people; if the people perish entire in the wilderness, that "already heard" becomes evidence on the other side. (3) The nations will speak (14:15): "Now if thou shalt kill all this people as one man, then the nations which have heard the fame of thee will speak, saying," (4) And Moses writes their speech for them in advance (14:16): "Because the LORD was not able to bring this people into the land which he sware unto them, therefore he hath slain them in the wilderness." The operative word of the speech is "not able": the nations will not say God would not, but that God could not; destruction, in the nations' mouths, will show not the righteousness of judgment but the failure of the promise. These four items form one line — the line of the name: from Egypt, to the inhabitants of Canaan, to the nations that heard the fame, the circle widens at each step, and Moses' argument grows heavier at each step.

(Fact) After the line of the name, Moses turns to the line of character, in two items only. (5) According to God's own spoken word (14:17-18): "And now, I beseech thee, let the power of my Lord be great, according as thou hast spoken, saying, The LORD is longsuffering, and of great mercy, forgiving iniquity and transgression, and by no means clearing the guilty, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation" (14:17-18). Moses' method is singular: he does not ask God to make an exception; he asks God to act according to his own word. The greatness of power, as Moses frames it, is not one more sign larger than the last, but the self-description coming due at this very moment. And the long self-description has two halves: the first — longsuffering, of great mercy, forgiving iniquity and transgression; the second — by no means clearing the guilty, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation. Moses quotes both halves and cuts neither — his intercession leaves room for consequence from the very start, a fact whose weight only appears after verse 20. (6) According to the greatness of mercy and the record of past pardons (14:19): "Pardon, I beseech thee, the iniquity of this people according unto the greatness of thy mercy, and as thou hast forgiven this people, from Egypt even until now" (14:19). The argument is the record: from Egypt until now, pardoning is not a novelty but a habit; Moses asks that this instance be joined to God's consistent course. The intercession ends there: six items in all — four on the line of the name, two on the line of character — and the counting is registered in the Endnotes.

(Application) Moses' intercession is the chapter's heaviest lesson for anyone who leads. He stands between two proposals: the congregation will replace him with another captain, God will keep him and raise a greater nation. Both proposals profit him personally — one relieves him of the burden, the other promotes him — and he takes neither. For a people who had just spoken of stoning its spokesmen and wished it had died in the wilderness, he intercedes, and the capital he lays out is none of it human: not that the people are pitiable, not that they will improve — only God's name, God's word, God's mercy, and God's record. This is what intercession is, once laid bare: not finding excuses for people, but standing in the breach for God's name and presenting God's own word back before God. When you pray today for family, for a church, for a person in whom you can see no hope, Moses' six items give the order: first that God's name be honoured, then that God's word be fulfilled, last that God's mercy be exercised as before — never beginning the reckoning from human merit.

(Summary) Two word-level observations only. First, the "forgiving" of 14:18 and the "pardon" of 14:19-20 are not the same Hebrew verb. Verse 18 uses the participle of nāśāʾ (H5375), "to lift, bear away"; verses 19 and 20 use sālaḥ (H5545) — in verse 19 in the imperative, as Moses prays, and in verse 20 in the perfect, sālaḥtî, "I have pardoned", as God answers. The word of the request and the word of the answer share one root: God's answer, at the verbal level, meets Moses' prayer head-on. This is stated from the text and not pressed further. Second, the instrument of destruction in God's proposal in verse 12 is deber (H1698), "pestilence", whereas the plague that actually falls on the ten spies in verse 37 is maggēp̄â (H4046). The two words stand side by side in one chapter: verse 12 is the penalty named in a proposal; verse 37 is an executed event. The reader should distinguish them, not merge them into one term.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The correspondence between the self-description in 14:17-18 and Exodus 34:6-7 is registered here as fact, at this level only. Both passages contain "longsuffering" / "slow to anger" （不轻易发怒）, "great mercy" / abundance of kindness （丰盛的慈爱） — Exodus 34:6 has the longer self-description, "a God merciful and gracious" among its clauses — "forgiving iniquity and transgression" (Exodus 34:7 lists three nouns, iniquity, transgression, and sin), "by no means clearing the guilty", and "visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation". The Numbers passage is Moses quoting within his plea, and it is an excerpt, not the whole: the "keeping mercy for thousands" clause of Exodus 34:7 is not quoted here, and the order is adjusted to the plea's need. What redactional account explains the relation — Moses citing a formed tradition of the same revelation, or a later editor aligning the two texts — belongs to this discussion level; this sermon registers the correspondence and the differences themselves and pronounces no verdict. Likewise, the "greater nation" offer of 14:12 closely parallels the structure of God's word to Moses at the golden calf in Exodus 32:10, where again a proposal is made to Moses and again Moses answers with intercession; the parallel is registered in the Endnotes as a cross-reference summary only, and no detail of Exodus 32 is read into this chapter.

### 3. Pardon and Oath: This Generation Shall Not See the Land (Numbers 14:20-25) (24:00-42:00)

(Fact) God's answer opens with the pardon itself, quoted exactly: "And the LORD said, I have pardoned according to thy word" (14:20). Note both its weight and its limit. The pardon is spoken, and spoken as complete — not a future hope but a present fact; and its ground is written into the sentence: "according to thy word" — God publicly credits it to Moses' intercession. But the story after the full stop is not so simple, because verse 21 follows immediately with "But": "But as truly as I live, all the earth shall be filled with the glory of the LORD" (14:21). The pardon does not withdraw the judgment, and the oath goes first: swearing by his own life, God swears that all the earth shall be filled with his glory — the verse that is this chapter's hinge and this sermon's title. It is spoken before every sentence of verdict, and its sense is plain: this generation's failure will not make the filling of the earth with God's glory fail; the filling of the earth rests not on this generation's success but on the One who swears.

(Fact) Only after the oath comes the main clause of the verdict, spanning verses 22-23 and read continuously: "Because all those men which have seen my glory, and my miracles, which I did in Egypt and in the wilderness, and have tempted me now these ten times, and have not hearkened to my voice; surely they shall not see the land which I sware unto their fathers, neither shall any of them that provoked me see it" (14:22-23). The verdict's grounds are three, counted one by one: first, they have seen — the glory and the miracles, in Egypt and in the wilderness; second, they have tempted — "now these ten times": ten is the base text's own number, the chapter gives the total and does not itemize the occasions, and this manuscript records the number as given without supplying a list of ten; third, they have not hearkened to my voice. The verdict's content is "shall not see": not an immediate execution, but the land closed to this generation for good; and the reinforcing clause is the second sentence — "neither shall any of them that provoked me see it" — not one exception. Looking back to verse 11, God's characterization was provoking （藐视， nāʾaṣ) and unbelief; here it becomes the verdict's own word: as they treated God, so the sentence answers them in the same term — those who provoked him shall not see.

(Fact) After the general rule comes the exception, and only one man is named: "But my servant Caleb, because he had another spirit with him, and hath followed me fully, him will I bring into the land whereinto he went; and his seed shall possess it" (14:24). Read in the base text, the sentence has four layers: first, the address — "my servant Caleb": of all the people in this chapter, he alone receives this title; second, the reason — "he had another spirit with him, and hath followed me fully": "another" is a difference from the congregation, not a superiority of strength; third, the promise — "him will I bring into the land whereinto he went": the verb is still God's bringing in, and the land is the very land he had walked and rent his clothes for; fourth, the promise extends to his seed — "his seed shall possess it". Note that Joshua is not mentioned in this verse: his exception appears only later, in verse 30, set alongside Caleb's. This section reads the two places separately as the text gives them and does not merge verse 30 forward into verse 24. After the exception, the turn (14:25): "(Now the Amalekites and the Canaanites dwelt in the valley.) To morrow turn you, and get you into the wilderness by the way of the Red sea" (14:25). The reason for turning is written into the sentence: the Amalekites and the Canaanites dwell in the valley — the very two peoples the congregation feared now serve as the terrain-reason in God's command to turn back; the time is "to morrow", the route "into the wilderness by the way of the Red sea". Yesterday they were still saying "let us go up"; today's command is to turn back into the wilderness — the direction is formally reversed overnight, and the reversal is the echo, in God's mouth, of the congregation's own proposal in verse 4: they wanted to return, and God lets them turn back — only the destination is not Egypt, but the wilderness.

(Application) Pardon and consequence held together is this chapter's hardest lesson, and the one that may not be edited. We commonly take pardon to mean the consequence is cancelled, as though, once God has said the word, yesterday had never happened. This chapter dismantles that idea to our faces: the pardon is spoken, and spoken at once, according to the intercessor's word; and "shall not see the land" is spoken too, and spoken under oath. Both sentences come from the same God in the same speech. The pardon deals with relationship — this people is still his people, Moses may still intercede for them, and the pillar of cloud and fire is nowhere withdrawn in this chapter; the consequence deals with history — the door this generation shut with its own hands is not reopened by the pardon. Preach pardon as though there were no consequence, and people will make light of sin; preach consequence as though there were no pardon, and people will despair of God. This chapter permits neither: the pardoned must still walk out the wilderness road of their former choices; and those walking the wilderness road are not a cast-off people — they walk it under pardon. That line is one every person living today with the consequences of old choices needs to hear exactly.

(New Testament Reading) The New Testament's reading of this chapter is registered at summary level only and changes none of the chapter's facts. Hebrews 3 quotes Psalm 95 looking back at this generation — "To day if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts, as in the provocation, in the day of temptation in the wilderness" (Heb 3:7-8, in summary) — and binds "temptation" and "shall not enter into my rest" to this chapter's verdict; 1 Corinthians 10 writes the wilderness generation's history as examples, saying plainly that "these things were our examples" and "written for our admonition" (1 Cor 10:6, 11, in summary). The New Testament reading alters not one number or name in this chapter; it only reads the chapter onward — from one generation's old story into a warning for later readers: having heard the word, answer it today, and do not let the answer decay into tomorrow's presumption. This level is registered separately from the fact level: in its own setting, the chapter speaks of the first exodus generation's entering and not entering; the New Testament's admonition-reading is a later application layer.


### 4. As Ye Have Spoken in Mine Ears: Forty Years and the Ten Spies (Numbers 14:26-38) (42:00-56:00)

(Fact) In verse 26 God speaks a second time, now commissioning Moses and Aaron to deliver the sentence: "And the LORD spake unto Moses and unto Aaron, saying, How long shall I bear with this evil congregation, which murmur against me? I have heard the murmurings of the children of Israel, which they murmur against me" (14:26-27). "This evil congregation" is God's own name for the group, set against the people's self-understanding in verse 2: they supposed their murmurings were spoken to Moses; God says, "I have heard", and in his ledger the murmurings were "against me". The first sentence of the message is the verdict's heading, quoted exactly: "Say unto them, As truly as I live, saith the LORD, as ye have spoken in mine ears, so will I do to you" (14:28). This is the chapter's second "as truly as I live" oath — the first, in verse 21, for the filling of the earth with glory; this one, for dealing with the people according to their own words. In verse 2 the people had said, "Would God that we had died … in this wilderness"; in verse 3, that their wives and children "should be a prey". God now takes both sentences up verbatim: you shall die in the wilderness, as you said; the matter of the children, however, I shall not settle as you said. What follows is the unfolding of those two sentences.

(Fact) The verdict's items are counted one by one and registered. (1) Carcases and the threshold (14:29): "Your carcases shall fall in this wilderness; and all that were numbered of you, according to your whole number, from twenty years old and upward, which have murmured against me" — the range is drawn by the census list of chapter 1, the threshold is twenty years old and upward, the condition is having murmured against God. The word "carcases" occurs three times in this passage (14:29, 32, 33); it is the verdict's key noun and is not to be read as a general word for death. (2) The two exceptions (14:30): "Doubtless ye shall not come into the land, concerning which I sware to make you dwell therein, save Caleb the son of Jephunneh, and Joshua the son of Nun." Only here does Joshua's name enter the exception list alongside Caleb's — verse 24 names Caleb alone, verse 30 names the two together; the division of labour between the verses is as the text gives it. (3) The little ones reversed (14:31): "But your little ones, which ye said should be a prey, them will I bring in, and they shall know the land which ye have despised." The people had used the children as their reason for retreat; God uses the children as the verdict's reversal: those pronounced certain prey shall certainly be brought in, and the land their parents despised, the children shall know. (4) The speakers' own end (14:32): "But as for you, your carcases, they shall fall in this wilderness" — answering verse 29 across the passage and nailing "you" and "them" apart.

(Fact) (5) The children's forty years (14:33): "And your children shall wander in the wilderness forty years, and bear your whoredoms, until your carcases be wasted in the wilderness." In the base text, the subject of the forty years is "your children", their state "wander in the wilderness", and what they bear is "your whoredoms" — "whoredoms" is the base text's own characterization of this generation's unfaithfulness, in the marriage metaphor, and this manuscript records it as written, not in a lighter word; the forty years have their end written in as well: until the fathers' carcases are wasted in the wilderness. (6) The arithmetic of the years (14:34): "After the number of the days in which ye searched the land, even forty days, each day for a year, shall ye bear your iniquities, even forty years, and ye shall know my breach of promise." The rule is one line: forty days of searching, each day for a year, gives forty years; and what is borne for forty years is their iniquities. A divergence from the commissioning summary is registered here: the Chinese base text reads 一年顶一日， "a year standing for one day", in the character 日， not 一年顶一天 with 天； and the days clause likewise reads 四十日. (7) The closing summary (14:35): "I the LORD have said, I will surely do it unto all this evil congregation, that are gathered together against me: in this wilderness they shall be consumed, and there they shall die." The verdict opens with God naming himself and closes with "there they shall die"; the place-words "in this wilderness" and "there" cash verse 2's own wish verbatim.

(Fact) Beyond the verdict, the passage's end records one thing executed immediately (14:36-38): "And the men, which Moses sent to search the land, who returned, and made all the congregation to murmur against him, by bringing up a slander upon the land, even those men that did bring up the evil report upon the land, died by the plague before the LORD. But Joshua the son of Nun, and Caleb the son of Jephunneh, which were of the men that went to search the land, lived still" (14:36-38). Three points, as the base text gives them. First, the charge stands in the narrative sentence itself: they are the men who "did bring up the evil report upon the land", and the slander "made all the congregation to murmur" — source and spread are written together. Second, the manner of death is "by the plague", with the place-phrase "before the LORD". Third, the survivors' list has two names only, and this verse's wording is "lived still", its range "of the men that went to search the land". The number is registered as arithmetic: twelve men went (the roster of 13:4-15), two lived, therefore ten died — "ten" is the result of twelve minus two; verses 37-38 do not themselves write the numeral "ten", and this manuscript marks the derivation instead of dressing arithmetic as a quotation. This event differs from the sentence of verse 29: the falling of that generation's carcases in the wilderness is a forty-year matter; these ten men die on the spot, before the LORD — the one is the general sentence, the other its first executed instance.

(Summary) Three word-notes only. First, "carcases", peḡer (H6297), occurs three times in this passage (14:29, 32, 33): it is the concrete word for a corpse, and the verdict uses it rather than any general word for dying — its register is bodies lying in the wilderness, not rest. Second, "your little ones" is ṭaph (H2945), a collective noun for the household's small children and dependants, which the Chinese base text renders 妇人孩子 ("women and children"); the "wives and children" of the people's own speech in verse 3 and the "little ones" of God's speech here refer to the same group under differing renderings, each recorded from its own verse. Third, the children's state in verse 33 is a participle of rāʿâ (H7462), whose proper sense is pasturing or tending flocks; the KJV renders it "wander", and the Chinese follows with 飘流. The shepherd image of the original and the wandering sense of the renderings are registered together: the next generation's forty years in the wilderness are an unsettled, flock-tending sojourn — registered at the philological level only.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The arithmetic relation between the forty-year total and the exodus chronology is kept at this level, as reference, with no harmonizing assertion in the body of the sermon. This chapter's own numbers are three: forty days of searching, each day for a year, forty years of bearing (14:34); the children wandering forty years (14:33). Other chronological materials within the book stand elsewhere: the census of Numbers 1 is dated by its own superscription to the second year after the exodus (Num 1:1), and Deuteronomy 2:14 states thirty-eight years from Kadesh-barnea to the brook Zered. How this chapter's forty years are to be fitted together with the second year and the thirty-eight, if the forty are counted from the searching, commentators have never computed uniformly: some take the forty as a total counted from the exodus itself, the searching falling about two years in, so that the remaining thirty-eight agree with Deuteronomy; others take it as a round generational number. Both computations are registered here as discussion; this sermon states only the chapter's own numbers — forty days, forty years, twenty years old — and announces no scheme as settled. Likewise, "these ten times" (14:22) has carried two readings from antiquity: a literal count, in which commentators have tallied the murmuring incidents from Exodus to this chapter item by item — their lists do not agree with one another — or "ten" as a number of completeness meaning again and again. Both readings are registered side by side; the body of this sermon records "these ten times" as the base text gives it and asserts neither reading.

### 5. Up Early, to the Top of the Hill: A Confession That Came Too Late (Numbers 14:39-45) (58:00-60:00)

(Fact) When the sentence reaches the people, the first response is mourning: "And Moses told these sayings unto all the children of Israel: and the people mourned greatly" (14:39). "These sayings" is the whole verdict just delivered. Early next morning, mourning becomes action, quoted entire: "And they rose up early in the morning, and gat them up into the top of the mountain, saying, Lo, we be here, and will go up unto the place which the LORD hath promised: for we have sinned" (14:40). The sentence contains a confession — "we have sinned", the first confession of sin from the people's mouth in the whole chapter — and a volunteering — "will go up unto the place which the LORD hath promised". Yesterday it was they who said "we are not able to go up" (13:31, the ten's words, which the people adopted); today it is they who volunteer to go up. The place has not changed, the promise has not changed; what has changed is the command: in verse 25 God has just said, "To morrow turn you", and on that very morrow, early, they go the opposite way. The confession is real, and the ascent contradicts the command just given — the text sets the two side by side in one verse and does not reconcile them for them.

(Fact) Moses' restraint has three layers, read continuously in the base text (14:41-43). First, the characterization: "And Moses said, Wherefore now do ye transgress the commandment of the LORD? but it shall not prosper" (14:41). Transgress again: yesterday's retreat was transgression, and today's advance is transgression too, because the command has changed. Second, the warning: "Go not up, for the LORD is not among you; that ye be not smitten before your enemies" (14:42). The key sentence is "the LORD is not among you": in verse 9 Joshua and Caleb's ground was "the LORD is with us", and that ground was valid yesterday, when the people refused it; today, when the people want to go up on their own, the ground is withdrawn. Third, the prediction of defeat: "For the Amalekites and the Canaanites are there before you, and ye shall fall by the sword: because ye are turned away from the LORD, therefore the LORD will not be with you" (14:43). Again the Amalekites and the Canaanites — the two peoples of the valley in verse 25, given there as the terrain-reason for God's command to turn back, now become, in Moses' prediction, the two peoples who execute the defeat. Moses' words end there; he does not go with them, and he does not change his mouth to a blessing.

(Fact) The people's choice and its outcome stand in the last two verses, quoted exactly: "But they presumed to go up unto the hill top: nevertheless the ark of the covenant of the LORD, and Moses, departed not out of the camp. Then the Amalekites came down, and the Canaanites which dwelt in that hill, and smote them, and discomfited them, even unto Hormah" (14:44-45). Verse 44 carries two of the chapter's heaviest strokes. One is "presumed" — in the Chinese, 擅敢， "dared on their own authority", not the milder 擅自： presumption is knowing it may not be done, and daring it anyway. The other is the stillness of the ark and of Moses — "departed not out of the camp": the people went up the hill; the ark stayed in the camp, Moses stayed in the camp, and not one token of the Presence moved. In verse 45 the enemy comes down from the hill — the people attack upward, the enemy strikes downward, and slope and outcome reverse together; "discomfited them, even unto Hormah" is the chapter's last line: the pursuit ends at a place-name. Of Hormah this chapter records only the name, and only that it was the limit of the rout; it does not record the name's origin or bestowal — the bestowal of that name is recorded in another chapter (Num 21:3 records a Hormah naming), which belongs to the discussion level and is not read back into this one. And the chapter ends on this defeat: no word of comfort, no turn of fortune; the next chapter's first sentence is already another matter. By leaving the ending here, the narrator lets the reader weigh "rose up early in the morning" for himself: the same early morning, the same hill top, the same promised place — yesterday obedience, today presumption; once the hour has passed, the nature of the act has changed.

(Application) What this chapter finally leaves with its hearers is heavier than "do not be late". The people's fault was not that they would not confess — they confessed. It was not that they would not act — they rose early, more zealous than anyone. Their fault was that they would only obey on their own timetable: when God said go up, they said we are not able; when God said turn back, they said we will go up. What they wanted, all along, was never God's command but God's promise — the promise without the command's timing. If confession does not include accepting the consequence and accepting the new command, it is still the old man bargaining. People do the same today: when the opportunity is lost, zeal suddenly arrives, and doubled activity is meant to chase the consequence back, as though energy could substitute for obedience. This chapter blocks that road: the ark did not leave the camp. God's presence does not endorse our remedial campaigns; true repentance is to turn back onto the wilderness road and walk out the forty years under the new command — or, in words from beyond this chapter, to accept the pardon, and to accept also the stretch of road the pardon did not recall. Caleb's "another spirit" and the people's "rose up early" stand as the chapter's final contrast: one follows fully, all the way; the other is zealous for a morning and transgresses by noon. The land, in the end, is left to the first kind of person.

(Summary) The place-name is registered as fact only: Hormah （何珥玛， Ḥormâ) occurs in this chapter once, at 14:45, as the limit of the rout. That its form shares a root with ḥērem, "devoted to destruction", is a philological observation many readers have made, but this chapter's text gives no explanation of the name; this sermon records the form Ḥormâ without a Strong's number — the number was not separately verified before writing, and by the governing rule it is therefore not affixed — and the naming layer is held in the discussion level throughout. The verb rendered "presumed", ʿāp̄al (H6075), stands in 14:44 in the Hiphil, wayyaʿpəlû, whose base sense is to swell or rise up presumptuously; it occurs elsewhere in the Old Testament only at Habakkuk 2:4, where its sense is disputed — registered as standing alongside, and not developed here.


## Devotional Excerpt

“Pardon, I beseech thee, the iniquity of this people according unto the greatness of thy mercy, and as thou hast forgiven this people, from Egypt even until now” (Num 14:19, KJV). Moses stood between the people and the judgment with no merit of his own to plead; he pleaded God’s own word. And the LORD answered, “I have pardoned according to thy word” (14:20) — yet the pardon did not recall the consequence: that generation would not see the land. We often imagine pardon as yesterday wiped clean, as though nothing had happened. This chapter teaches us to look steadily: the pardon is real, and the consequence is real. The pardoned need not fear being cast off, yet they must still learn obedience day by day in the wilderness. If you are living today with the consequences of old choices, do not misread them as proof that God has not pardoned you; and do not treat pardon as a shortcut around them. Take Caleb’s way instead — another spirit, following the LORD fully — for the pardon has been spoken, the road is still ahead, and all the earth shall be filled with the glory of the LORD.

2. Title verification. Four candidates were examined verse by verse: (i) "I have pardoned according to thy word" (14:20) — God's first word in answer to the intercession; it contains the pardon only, and not the consequence, and so cannot carry the chapter's second half. (ii) "All the earth shall be filled with the glory of the LORD" (14:21) — God's oath, sworn by his own life, set between the pardon and the verdict; it is the chapter's hinge and fixes its direction. (iii) Caleb's "another spirit" (14:24) — covers one man's exception only, not the congregation, Moses, or the verdict as a whole. (iv) "Fear them not" (14:9) — the closing words of Joshua and Caleb's plea, which the congregation rejected on the spot, with the verdict still to unfold; it cannot carry the whole chapter. Candidate (ii) was therefore adopted. The English title is set independently from the KJV wording of 14:21; the Chinese title 遍地要被我的荣耀充满 is set independently from the Chinese base text's wording of the same verse; the two are not literal translations of each other.
3. Divergences between the commissioning orientation and the base texts, recorded as the base texts read. (i) The orientation rendered 14:9 as 他们的庇护离开他们； the base text reads 荫庇他们的已经离开他们， with both 荫 and 已经 present; the same verse addresses the fear of 那地的居民 (the inhabitants of the land), not 百姓， and opens 但你们不可背叛耶和华. (ii) The orientation rendered 14:7 as 那地极其美好； the base text reads 我们所窥探、经过之地是极美之地； renderings of the "that land is a very good place" type belong to other versions. (iii) The orientation rendered 14:4 as 另立一个首领 ("appoint another captain"); the base text reads 众人彼此说：『我们不如立一个首领回埃及去吧！』 — no 另 and no 元首； the "another" wording belongs to a modern version. (iv) The orientation rendered 14:34 as 一年顶一天； the base text reads 一年顶一日， and the preceding clause 四十日 — 日 throughout, never 天. (v) The orientation rendered 14:44 as 约柜与摩西没有离开营中； the base text reads 他们却擅敢上山顶去，然而耶和华的约柜和摩西没有出营 — 擅敢， not 擅自， and 没有出营. (vi) The orientation spoke of the children wandering in 14:33 in general terms; the base text's subject is 你们的儿女， and the sin borne is 你们淫行的罪. The character formula (14:18) itself has five self-descriptions: longsuffering; of great mercy; forgiving iniquity and transgression; by no means clearing the guilty; visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation. The oath "as truly as I live" occurs twice (14:21, 28). "Carcases" occurs three times (14:29, 32, 33). The Amalekites and Canaanites are paired three times (14:25, 43, 45). Glory (kābôd) occurs in three verses (14:10 the appearing, 14:21 the filling, 14:22 "have seen my glory"). 7. The twenty-years threshold and the census connection, stated as fact only. The "numbered … from twenty years old and upward" of 14:29 refers back to the census standard of Numbers 1 (from twenty years old and upward, all able to go forth to war — Num 1:3, 45, in summary). This chapter sets up no new roster; the verdict's range is defined by the chapter 1 census. The connection is registered here and chapter 1's contents are not developed.
8. The naming of Hormah, stated as fact only. This chapter, at 14:45, records only the rout "even unto Hormah" and gives no origin for the name. Numbers 21:3 records that after another battle "the name of the place" was called Hormah, and Judges 1:17 records Zephath renamed Hormah. The place-name is the same in the three passages and the events are different; their relation — successive names of one place, or the same name borne by different places — belongs to the scholarly discussion level. This sermon registers the three side by side and announces no conclusion.
9. The boundary of the "ten times" count. This chapter gives the total "these ten times" at 14:22 only and does not itemize the occasions. Lists compiled by commentators, tallying murmuring incidents from Exodus to this chapter, differ from one another in the events they include and in how the total is reached; neither the body nor the summary of this sermon attaches a homemade list.

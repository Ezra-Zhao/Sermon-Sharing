Summary: The ordinances of Leviticus chapters 1–7 and the ordination rite of chapter 8 converge on the eighth day in the first complete operation of the sacrificial system: Aaron first offers for himself, then presents the four offerings for the people; Moses and Aaron enter the tabernacle and come out to bless; the glory of the LORD appears to all the people, and fire comes out from before the LORD and consumes the offering on the altar, and the people shout and fall on their faces. This sermon expounds the liturgical logic and the theme of theophany of that day.

---

## I. The Eighth Day: The First Day After Long Preparation 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, the first sentence of Leviticus chapter 9 is very short, yet it has been long awaited: "And it came to pass on the eighth day" (Lev 9:1, KJV). Which day is the eighth day? Look back and it becomes clear. Leviticus chapter 8 records the ordination of Aaron and his sons: Moses washes them, clothes them, anoints them, and offers sacrifice, and commands them to abide seven days and seven nights at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, keeping the charge of the LORD, and not to go out of the door of the tabernacle, lest they die. For seven full days the newly ordained priests do not go home and do not attend to other business; they remain at the door of the tabernacle and complete the ordination rite. When the eighth day arrives, the seven days of waiting are ended, and Moses calls Aaron and his sons, and the elders of Israel. This is an account at the level of narrative fact: chapter 9 is not an isolated day; it follows directly upon the seven days of chapter 8 and is the exit and the goal of that waiting.

Look further back. Leviticus chapters 1–7 are the ordinances of sacrifice — the burnt offering, the meat offering, the peace offering, the sin offering, and the trespass offering — explaining offering by offering the animal, the blood, the fat, and the priest's portion. Chapter 8 prepares the men, so that Aaron's household actually puts on the holy garments, is anointed, and stands in the priest's place. The ordinances are given and the men are ordained, yet one thing is still lacking: this liturgy has not yet operated once before all the people. Chapter 9 is that day. The ordinances become an event for the first time, the priest ministers formally for the first time, and the whole congregation gathers to watch for the first time. In other words, chapters 1–8 are preparation; chapter 9 is the opening. The earlier chapters tell how to offer; this chapter tells what happened when it was offered. Read chapter 9 apart from the first eight chapters and one sees only a lively day; read it in sequence and one sees how weighty that day is.

The expression "the eighth day" itself deserves a pause. The Hebrew wording will be given in the notes; here we note only a structural observation, drawing no final conclusion: the creation in Genesis takes seven days as a complete cycle, and on the seventh day God rested from His work; the ordination rite in Leviticus chapter 8 is also seven days, during which the priests keep watch at the door of the tabernacle for seven days. The day that comes after the seven, in the rhythm of the narrative, is like the beginning of something new after the completion of an old cycle. Scholars and preachers often associate a new beginning here, and that association has a basis in the rhythm of the text, but the text itself does not expressly say that the eighth day is a new creation. We keep it at the level of structural observation: the seven days of preparation are full; on the eighth day the preparation is to bear fruit, and the priest is to stand for the first time before all the people and offer the first complete round of sacrifices according to all the ordinances that have gone before.

Before the day has even begun, a promise is hung over it. Moses says to Aaron that he is to offer the sin offering and the burnt offering for himself and for the people, and says, "for to day the LORD will appear unto you" (Lev 9:4, KJV). Verse 6 says it again through Moses: this is the thing which the LORD commanded that ye should do, "and the glory of the LORD shall appear unto you" (Lev 9:6, KJV). The same word is spoken three times within one chapter. That is not verbose repetition; it means that the liturgy of this day moves from beginning to end in one direction: appearing. Sacrifice is not an end in itself; sacrifice is the road that prepares the appearing. The animals, the blood, the washing, and the lifting up of hands in blessing are all the road; at the end of the road God appears to His people and shows Himself. Brothers and sisters, this sentence sets the tone of chapter 9: we have not come today to watch an ancient rite as spectators; we have come to pursue one question — how does a holy God show His glory to an ordinary people, a people even bearing sin, and how should the people prepare themselves to meet this appearing?

Notice to whom the appearing is promised. The text says the glory of the LORD will appear unto you — and this "you" does not mean the priests alone, nor Moses and Aaron alone, but includes the gathered elders and all the congregation. Verse 5 says they brought what Moses commanded before the tabernacle of the congregation, "and all the congregation drew near and stood before the LORD" (Lev 9:5, KJV). Verse 6: "this is the thing which the LORD commanded that ye should do." The preparation for worship belongs to all the people, not only to the few priests on duty. The priest has his irreplaceable office; the people have their gathering, watching, obeying, and waiting, which they may not push away. God's appearing comes to all who are gathered, not to a crowd of onlookers, but to a company prepared according to the command. This still instructs the church today, at the level of homiletical exposition: we often imagine worship as a few ministering and the many watching; the picture of Leviticus 9 is exactly the reverse — the priests are busy at the altar and the congregation stands before the altar; both are before the LORD, and both are within the liturgy of this day. The appearing has not yet happened, and already the people are standing there. Waiting itself is part of worship.

There is a further weight: the appearing of this day is not God's first appearing to Israel. The ten plagues in Egypt, the parting of the Red Sea, and the thunder and lightning on Mount Sinai were all appearings. But those appearings were mostly on the road, on the mountain, in the journey through the wilderness. The appearing of chapter 9 is different: it takes place after the tabernacle has been set up, the altar built, and the priests installed, within the ordinary liturgy of daily sacrifice. God does not appear only in great upheavals; He wills also to appear in the ordinary worship in which His people offer, gather, and bless according to His word. This must be stated clearly: the theology of Leviticus neither shuts God up inside miracles nor shrinks Him into a rite; it speaks of a God who wills to dwell in the midst of His people and to appear to them in the worship He has appointed. The eighth day is the first day on which this life of dwelling together formally begins.

So we enter what follows with three words: preparation, order, and appearing. Preparation is the waiting of seven days plus one; order is the sacrifice first for oneself and then for others; appearing is the end of it all. If the order is disordered, the road to appearing is blocked; if preparation is lacking, the people can only stand far off and guess. Moses has called the company together, and Aaron is about to act. Let us first look at the sacrifice Aaron offers for himself, for the most surprising arrangement of chapter 9 is precisely this: on the first day of his ministry, the first thing the priest must deal with is not the people's sin, but his own.

## II. First for Himself, Then for the People: The Priest Too Must First Be Dealt With 08:00-24:00

Moses' command to Aaron falls into two halves, with a clear order — the narrative main line of this chapter, at the factual level. The first half is for himself: Aaron is to take a young calf for a sin offering and a ram for a burnt offering, both without blemish, and offer them before the LORD (Lev 9:2, KJV). The second half is for the people: he is to take a kid of the goats for a sin offering, and a calf and a lamb, both of the first year and without blemish, for a burnt offering, and also a bullock and a ram for peace offerings, and a meat offering mingled with oil, to sacrifice before the LORD (Lev 9:3-4, KJV). For himself there are two offerings; for the people there are four. His own come first; the people's come after. Aaron does exactly so: first he slaughters the calf of the sin offering for himself; his sons present the blood to him, and he dips his finger in the blood and puts it upon the horns of the altar and pours out the blood at the bottom of the altar; the fat, the kidneys, and the caul above the liver he burns upon the altar, and the flesh and the hide he burns with fire without the camp. Then he slaughters the ram of the burnt offering, sprinkles the blood, washes, and burns it wholly. Only when his own offerings are finished does he turn to the people's: he slaughters the goat of the people's sin offering, the calf and the lamb of the people's burnt offering, the bullock and the ram of the people's peace offerings, offers the meat offering mingled with oil, burns the fat upon the breasts, and waves the breasts and the right shoulder for a wave offering. Finally Aaron lifts up his hand toward the people and blesses them. The order is not disturbed at a single step: in the order Moses commanded, in that order Aaron acts.

Why must the priest first offer for himself? The reason is written into the liturgy itself; we need invent nothing more. The priest too is a man; the priest too has sin; if the priest is not first dealt with by the sin offering, he has no qualification to stand between the people and God and deal with the people's sin. The sin offering Aaron offers for himself is a calf, and this is the same grade as the sacrifice prescribed in Leviticus chapter 4 when an anointed priest sins: chapter 4 says that if the priest that is anointed do sin according to the sin of the people, he shall bring for his sin a young bullock without blemish unto the LORD for a sin offering. The cost of a priest's sin is the highest, because the priest's place is nearest to the sanctuary. On the first day of Aaron's ministry in chapter 9, he first offers for himself at this heaviest grade. This is not going through the motions; it is a confession before all the people: the man standing at this altar is first a forgiven man, and only then a man who makes atonement for others. At the level of homiletical exposition we apply it thus: whoever stands before men to serve God must first be dealt with before God; reverse the order, and ministry becomes performance, and even danger.

The list of the offerings for the people must likewise be examined closely. The people's sin offering is a kid of the goats. Compare chapter 4: the sin offering prescribed there for the congregation's sin requires the elders to bring a young bullock; but the sin offering offered for the people in chapter 9 is a goat, not entirely the same as the bullock appointed for the congregation in chapter 4, and scholars explain the difference in different ways. Some hold that chapter 9 is the special liturgy for the completion of the ordination and need not correspond item by item with the ordinary ordinances; others hold that the goat here bears the function of atonement for the congregation, corresponding to the use of a goat for the congregation's sin offering at the feasts in Numbers. We set the views side by side and draw no final conclusion; we hold only to what can be established as fact: the offerings presented for the people on this day are a complete set of four — sin offering, burnt offering, peace offering, and meat offering — the sample of the sacrificial system's first complete operation. Each of the four has its own work: the sin offering deals with sin; the burnt offering expresses a whole giving to God; the peace offering points to peace and sharing between man and God and between man and man; the meat offering is the presenting and memorial of grain. Offered together on one day, the four together set the ordinances of chapters 1–7 in motion and demonstrate them within a single day. What the people stand and watch is not one action, but the landing of a whole order of drawing near to God.

The elders' place in this liturgy must not be missed. Moses calls Aaron and his sons and the elders of Israel, and the elders are the representatives of all the people. When the offerings are presented for the people, the elders' presence shows that this sacrifice is offered for the whole congregation, not for one household or one tribe. In the congregation's sin offering in chapter 4, it is precisely the elders who lay their hands on the animal's head, representing the congregation in identifying with the offering. Chapter 9 does not restate the detail of the laying on of hands word for word, but the gathering itself of the elders and all the congregation says the same thing: the sacrifice of this day concerns the whole body. Priests, elders, and congregation — three companies of people — are all present, each in his place. The public character of the liturgy is part of the worship-view of Leviticus, not decoration. God appears to all the people, and all the people are therefore to gather together, watch together, and receive together; it is not enough for one or two to receive light.

There is one more layer, whose level must be marked with care. The sin offering offered for Aaron himself is a calf, and the original word will be given in the notes. A preaching tradition connects this calf with the event of Exodus chapter 32, in which Aaron fashioned the golden calf: the Aaron who once cast a calf before the people must now, on taking office, first offer a calf as a sin offering for himself. Read so, the correspondence carries great power, and many preachers have preached it in this way. But we must be honest: the text of chapter 9 itself does not mention the golden calf, nor does it expressly say that this calf atones for that event. To join the two belongs to the level of homiletical exposition and intertextual association; it may be preached, but it must be marked as exposition, not as the express wording of this chapter. Distinguishing this does not weaken preaching; it protects preaching: we do not pass off a powerful association as the original words of Scripture, and the hearers know on which level of ground every weighty sentence they hear is standing. What can be established at the factual level is this: Aaron, bearing all the weakness of his life before he was a priest and after he became one, first offered a sin offering for himself, and only then lifted up his hand to bless the people. The hand of a man first forgiven does not tremble when it blesses others.

The climactic act of the offering is blessing. When Aaron has finished the peace offerings and the meat offering, he lifts up his hand toward the people and blesses them, and comes down. The lifting up of the hand in blessing is a most beautiful moment in the priest's office: the high priest who has finished handling blood and offerings turns round, faces the people, and brings God's favour upon men in words. Sacrifice is directed toward God; blessing is directed toward men; the two directions meet in the priest. But the blessing of chapter 9 is not yet complete. After Aaron comes down, there is still the scene in which Moses and Aaron together enter the tabernacle, together come out, and again bless the people — and it is right after this second blessing that the glory appears to all the people. Why is the blessing divided into two? Why is an account of entering the tabernacle inserted in between? This is precisely what the next section will examine closely — the quietest and most pivotal verses of the chapter.
## III. In and Out Again: The Glory Appears to All the People 24:00-42:00

Verse 23 of chapter 9 is only a short sentence, yet it is the pivot of the whole chapter: "And Moses and Aaron went into the tabernacle of the congregation, and came out, and blessed the people: and the glory of the LORD appeared unto all the people" (Lev 9:23, KJV). First state the narrative facts clearly. First, those who enter the tabernacle are two men, Moses and Aaron, not Aaron alone. Second, having entered, they come out again. Third, after coming out, they bless the people. Fourth, right after this blessing, the glory appears to all the people. Aaron has already lifted up his hand and blessed once before; this time it is the blessing given by Moses and Aaron together after coming out, and the appearing follows immediately upon this blessing. The order is given by the text and may not be rearranged: offering, entering the tabernacle, coming out to bless, the glory appearing — link joined to link.

Why do Moses and Aaron enter the tabernacle together? The text does not directly state the purpose — that is the fact. In discussing its meaning, the views must be set side by side and no final conclusion drawn. One reading stresses Moses' role: Moses is the man who promulgated the ordinances and presided over the ordination rite; he accompanies Aaron on his first entry into the tabernacle, as though formally bringing the priest into the sanctuary where he will minister, completing the handover, so that from now on Aaron can serve within the tabernacle. Another reading stresses prayer and intercession: the two enter together, after the offering and before the blessing, first to intercede for the people before God, so that the blessing after they come out carries a weight received from before God. A third reading stresses confirmation and witness: Moses' companionship witnesses that Aaron's ministry accords with the commanded pattern, so that the congregation knows that everything done at the altar is not Aaron's invention, but according to the rule the LORD appointed through Moses. Each of the three has its basis, and each its incompleteness; preaching sets the three side by side, which is more honest than forcing a choice of one. What can be established is the narrative effect: the people wait outside, see their leaders go in, and see them come out again — and when they come out they do not come empty-handed, but come out bearing blessing. This rhythm of in and out itself becomes teaching: leaders first go before God, then return into the midst of men; first receive, then distribute. Reverse it, and blessing becomes words without a source.

The word "glory" is the theological centre of this section. The original Hebrew word, its transliteration, and its number are given in the notes; here we first grasp its use in this chapter. The glory appears to all the people — this appearing is not an atmosphere imagined by the people, nor the gleam of sunset on the gold face of the tabernacle, but the visible manifestation of the LORD's presence. In the Exodus narrative, the glory once filled the tabernacle so that Moses could not enter; on Mount Sinai the glory rested on the mountaintop, in appearance like devouring fire. Now in chapter 9 the glory appears to all the people, and the object of the appearing is extended from Moses alone to the whole people. This is a key step in the theology of dwelling together: God's presence is no longer only the leader's experience, but a fact the whole congregation sees together and receives together. The purpose of the priestly system was never to hide God away for the few, but, according to the order of holiness, to let all the people, each in his own place, draw near to this present God.

When the original says the glory appeared, the verb used is connected with the root meaning to see and to appear; the notes will give the original form and its number. What verses 4 and 6 foretell is "will appear"; what verse 23 records is "appeared" — promise and fulfilment face each other within the same chapter. When Moses said, "to day the LORD will appear unto you," the people could only hear; by verse 23 the people have seen with their own eyes. The narrative arc of this chapter is therefore very complete: promise, preparation, offering, blessing, appearing. Without the preparation of offering and blessing, the appearing would become a rootless spectacle; without the appearing, offering and blessing would become actions without an answer. Leviticus chapter 9 gives us both sides: man prepares according to God's word, and God comes according to His own promise. This is precisely the deepest logic of worship — not that man summons and manipulates God by a rite, but that God first establishes the promise, man responds in obedience, and God manifests Himself in His sovereignty. What man can do is to offer as commanded and wait as commanded; whether and when the appearing comes has never been in man's hand.

Notice also the scope of the appearing and the character of its witness. The text says the glory appeared unto all the people — to all the people, that is, to every person present, not to the elders only, nor to the priestly household only. The worship of this day therefore bears the character of public witness: what happened has the whole congregation as its witnesses. This matters for what follows, because in the next verse, verse 24, the fire that comes out from before the LORD is likewise something all the people see with their own eyes. The author of Leviticus writes both the appearing and the fire as public events, not as a private vision or a leader's report. The people afterward shout and fall on their faces not because they have heard another's report, but because they have seen with their own eyes. We who read the text today stand before the words in a different position, but the lesson is connected, at the level of homiletical exposition: God delights to let His works be seen, witnessed, and remembered; faith has never been a matter of talking to oneself in a hidden corner.

Brothers and sisters, at this point let us set this passage beside our own life of worship — but let the comparison rest at the level of exposition and not pretend to be the original meaning of the text. We come into worship often longing to experience God's presence, yet impatient with the order of preparation: unwilling first to deal with our own sin, unwilling to adjust our lives according to the word, wanting only to jump directly to the moment of being moved. The order of chapter 9 does not allow us to jump: first the seven days of waiting, then the offerings for oneself and for the people, then the quiet of entering the tabernacle, and only then the appearing of the glory. God's appearing is grace, not technique; but grace often comes to a place already prepared according to the word. May we learn not the heart that hunts for spectacles, but the obedience that is willing to prepare. After the glory appears, there is one last scene, and the most shaking scene of the chapter: fire — fire comes out from before the LORD.

## IV. Fire Came Out from Before the LORD: Shouting and Falling on Their Faces 42:00-56:00

Verse 24 is the peak of the chapter: "And there came a fire out from before the LORD, and consumed upon the altar the burnt offering and the fat: which when all the people saw, they shouted, and fell on their faces" (Lev 9:24, KJV). First see the facts clearly. First, the source of the fire is before the LORD; it is not a firebrand the priests fetched from elsewhere, nor the woodpile catching fire by itself. Second, what the fire consumes is the burnt offering and the fat on the altar — the core portion of the offerings presented this day. Third, the people's reaction has two sides: on one side shouting, on the other falling on their faces. The lexical sense of the shouting and the posture of falling will both be given in the notes. Together these three points form a complete theological picture: God answers man's offering with His own fire, showing acceptance; the people answer acceptance with shouting, showing joy; and they answer holiness with falling on their faces, showing reverence. Joy and reverence appear at the same moment in the same company of people, without cancelling each other out.

Fire coming out from before the LORD is not an isolated picture in Scripture. Set side by side at the intertextual level, at least three similar appearings deserve to be viewed together, but each has a different setting and they must not be conflated. In Judges chapter 6, Gideon puts flesh and unleavened cakes on a rock, the angel of the LORD reaches out the end of his staff, and fire rises out of the rock and consumes the flesh and the cakes, confirming Gideon's call — a sign in the setting of a personal calling. In 1 Kings chapter 18, on Mount Carmel, where Elijah confronts the prophets of Baal, the LORD sends down fire, which consumes the burnt sacrifice, the wood, the stones, and the dust, and licks up the water in the trench, and the people fall on their faces and say, "The LORD, he is the God" — a judgment and a turning back in the setting of a contest with idolatry before the nations. In 2 Chronicles chapter 7, when Solomon has finished praying at the dedication of the temple, fire comes down from heaven and consumes the burnt offering and the sacrifices, and the glory fills the house, and the people bow and worship — an acceptance in the setting of a temple's dedication. Add Leviticus chapter 9, and there are four descents of fire, each with its own occasion: the confirming of a call, a contest, a dedication, and the first ministry of the priests. What is the same is that in each the fire proceeds from God's initiative; what differs is what each fire confirms. Preaching the fire of chapter 9, one must hold to its own setting: this fire confirms that the first complete operation of the sacrificial system has been accepted, that Aaron's first formal ministry has received God's confirmation. To preach it as another passage's story is misplacement in every case.

For this very reason, the acceptance signified by this fire must be preached through. As the people offered sacrifice, there must have been in their hearts an unspoken question: we offer in this way — will God receive it? The priest, on the first day of his office, may well have had the same question himself. A completed rite is not the same as acceptance; complete actions are not the same as God's confirmation. The fire coming out from before the LORD is precisely the answer God Himself gives: received. It is not the priest who announces that it is received, nor Moses who announces it; God, with fire from before Himself, consumes the offering on the altar and lets all the people see the sign of acceptance with their own eyes. The deepest comfort in worship lies right here: acceptance is decided not by man's feeling about himself, but by God's response. When we worship God today we no longer wait for fire on an altar, but the principle stands at the level of exposition: when we come before God, our peace is built not on the intensity of our own emotions, but on the acceptance God has already manifested through His word and His works. Man offers, God answers — this structure of dialogue is the heart of the worship-view of Leviticus.

The people's reaction likewise deserves slow preaching. Shouting comes first, falling on their faces after — and neither of the two actions may be lacking. Shouting without falling easily turns joy frivolous, as though God's acceptance meant God is easy to bargain with; falling without shouting easily turns reverence into dread, as though God's holiness meant God cannot be approached. The people of chapter 9 have both: they shout for acceptance and fall for holiness. Shouting is the voice going upward; falling is the body going downward — one expresses the joy of release, the other expresses a reverence that has been conquered. Mature worship is precisely the union of these two: truly joyful before God, and truly trembling; truly near, and truly in awe. This picture is the most concrete lesson this chapter leaves to every worshipper; it needs no addition and no exaggeration — the text stands here, and it is enough for us to learn for a lifetime.

Finally, one layer of tension must be noted — noted only, without preaching ahead into the next chapter. Leviticus chapter 9 ends with shouting and falling on faces; the opening of the immediately following chapter 10 is the event of Nadab and Abihu offering strange fire and being consumed by fire. In the same book, in adjacent chapters, on one side fire comes out from before the LORD and consumes the offering and the people shout; on the other side fire comes out from before the LORD and consumes priests and men's hearts are stricken. This is an observation at the level of narrative structure: the author places the fire of acceptance and the fire of judgment immediately beside each other, so that the reader learns trembling while the joy has not yet dispersed. Chapter 10's content has its own preaching; this sermon does not unfold it, but reminds us of one sentence only: the fire of chapter 9 tells us how gladly God accepts worship offered according to His word, and that gladness never lowers His holiness. The shouting crowd and the fallen bodies are the most fitting place to stand between the two chapters. In the next section we gather up the chapter and see how the New Testament looks back on the fire and the sacrifice of this day, and where it leads us.
## V. Consuming Fire and the Once-for-All Offering: Looking from the Eighth Day Toward the New Testament 58:00-60:00

Before gathering up the chapter, let us distinguish the levels clearly, and then look toward the New Testament. What follows belongs to the level of New Testament reading and homiletical use, and must not be read back as the plain original meaning of Leviticus chapter 9. Chapter 9 of Hebrews looks back on the whole sacrificial system and points out that these sacrifices are patterns of the cleansing of heavenly things, and says that Christ entered once into the holy place not with the blood of goats and calves, but with His own blood, having obtained eternal redemption; that He appeared once to put away sin by the sacrifice of Himself, and will appear the second time to save those who look for Him. Many sacrifices were offered within the single day of chapter 9, yet Hebrews gathers the gaze upon the once: not that the many sacrifices were not lively enough, but that the many sacrifices all pointed to that one offering which truly puts away sin. The picture of Aaron first offering for himself becomes especially clear in this contrast: even the high priest himself must first be atoned for, which shows that within this system no priest can stand by himself; but the One witnessed to in the New Testament needed no offering for Himself, and first offered Himself for others. Homiletical use of this contrast aims for the hearers to see both the honesty of the old order and the surpassing fulfilment of the New — neither side may be blurred.

Chapter 12 of Hebrews says, "for our God is a consuming fire" (Heb 12:29, KJV). Preachers often connect this sentence with the fire of chapter 9, and its use must be marked with measure: the consuming fire of chapter 12 appears in a passage exhorting reverent service, speaking of serving God acceptably with reverence and godly fear, for our God is a consuming fire. Reading it together with the fire that came out from before the LORD in chapter 9 is homiletical use: both places remind man that the God who accepts man is at the same time a God holy as fire, and that nearness and trembling cannot be divided. But the fire of Leviticus chapter 9 itself is the fire that accepts the offering; it is not the original sense of chapter 12's teaching on the attitude of service, and the two must not be merged into one sentence of Scripture. Once they are distinguished, the use becomes more powerful: when we come before God today, we have both the boldness to enter through Christ's once-for-all offering, and, because God is still a consuming fire, we learn the manner of the people of chapter 9 — falling on our faces within our shouting, and reverence within our nearness.

Let us close with chapter 9 itself. What this chapter teaches us is not an outdated knowledge of rites, but a complete picture of worship: God first establishes the promise — to day He will appear unto you; man prepares according to the order — first for himself, then for others, the four offerings complete; the leaders first go before God, then come out and bless the company; God appears in glory according to His promise and confirms acceptance with His own fire; and the people's answer is shouting and falling on their faces. May our worship today be corrected by this picture: not skipping confession of sin, not reversing the order, not preaching blessing as words without a source, not living joy into frivolity, nor living reverence into dread. The fire of the eighth day has long gone out on the altar, but the lesson of the eighth day is not finished: the man who comes before God according to God's word will, in His acceptance, be joyful and trembling, falling down and shouting, all at once.

## Notes

1. yom hashemini: Hebrew יוֹם הַשְּׁמִינִי, transliteration yom hashemini, the eighth day, at Leviticus 9:1. Of its parts, yom is יוֹם, day, Strong number H3117, BDB: day; HALOT: day; hashemini is the ordinal "eighth," from שְׁמֹנֶה, eight, Strong number H8083. This sermon places the correspondence between the eighth day and the seven days of keeping the tabernacle in Leviticus 8, and the seven-day cycle of creation, at the level of structural observation, setting side by side scholarly associations of a new beginning, and draws no conclusion supposedly stated in the text; see Wenham's discussion on 9:1 and Milgrom's analysis of the ordination cycle.

2. kavod: Hebrew כָּבוֹד, transliteration kavod, glory, honour, weight, Strong number H3519. Meaning according to BDB: glory, honour, glorious abundance; HALOT: glory, honour, in its origin connected with being heavy and weighty. In this chapter, at 9:6 and 9:23, the glory of the LORD appears — the visible manifestation of God's presence in the theology of dwelling together; Hartley and Wenham both treat the glory as pivotal in the tabernacle narrative of Leviticus.

3. neraah: Hebrew נִרְאָה, transliteration neraah, appeared, was seen, the passive form of the verb ראה. The root רָאָה, transliteration raah, to see, Strong number H7200, BDB: to see, look at, inspect; in the passive it bears the sense of appearing and showing oneself. Leviticus 9:4 says the LORD will appear unto you, and 9:6 and 9:23 say the glory appeared — all three using the passive expression of this root, promise and fulfilment corresponding in the same word; see Milgrom's note on 9:4.

4. 'egel: Hebrew עֵגֶל, transliteration 'egel, calf, young bull, Strong number H5695. Meaning according to BDB: calf; HALOT: calf, young bull. The sin offering Aaron offers for himself at Leviticus 9:2 and 9:8 is this animal. This sermon explicitly marks the correspondence between this calf and the golden calf event of Exodus 32 as belonging to the level of homiletical exposition and intertextual association; the text of this chapter makes no express mention of the golden calf, and readers must distinguish the levels.

5. The grade of Aaron's sin offering for himself, compared with Leviticus 4:3: when the anointed priest sins, he must offer a young bullock without blemish as a sin offering. In Leviticus 9 Aaron first offers a calf as a sin offering for himself, and then offers for the people; the order appears in Moses' command and Aaron's performance at 9:7–8. Wenham treats the liturgical logic by which the priest must first be dealt with before he can make atonement for the people; Hartley likewise notes the theology of order in this passage.

6. The four offerings for the people and the elders: what is prepared for the people at Leviticus 9:3–4 is a kid of the goats for a sin offering, a calf and a lamb for a burnt offering, a bullock and a ram for peace offerings, and a meat offering mingled with oil — the four offerings complete. This differs in the animal from the ordinance of Leviticus 4, where the congregation's sin offering is a young bullock; scholars either explain it as the special arrangement of a dedication liturgy, or compare it with the use of a goat for the congregation's sin offering at the feasts; this sermon sets the views side by side and draws no final conclusion. The elders, called at 9:1 and gathered with all the congregation at 9:5, stand as representatives of all the people; see Milgrom's discussion on 9:1 and 9:5.

7. Moses and Aaron entering the tabernacle: see Leviticus 9:23. The text does not expressly state the purpose of the entering. Discussion of its meaning is set side by side: the handover and formal bringing-in view, the intercession view, and the confirmation-and-witness view; all three appear in the commentary tradition, and both Milgrom and Hartley discuss them; this sermon draws no final conclusion, noting only, on the basis of the narrative order, that entering, coming out, blessing, and appearing are joined together as a matter of textual fact.

8. esh me'et YHWH: Leviticus 9:24, "there came a fire out from before the LORD." The original structure corresponding to this rendering is fire going out from before the LORD; the word אֵשׁ, transliteration esh, fire, Strong number H784, BDB: fire; HALOT: fire. This fire consumes the burnt offering and the fat on the altar — the original verb meaning to eat up, to consume — and is joined with the sign of God's acceptance; Wenham notes this as the theophany event in which God Himself confirms the ministry.

9. Intertexts of descending fire: Judges 6:21, fire rises out of the rock and consumes Gideon's offering; 1 Kings 18:38, the fire of the LORD falls and consumes the burnt sacrifice on Carmel; 2 Chronicles 7:1, fire comes down from heaven and consumes the sacrifices at Solomon's dedication of the temple. The four settings differ: confirmation of a call, a contest with idolatry, acceptance at a dedication, and confirmation of the priests' first ministry. This sermon sets them side by side at the intertextual level without conflating their settings; for the comparative framework see Hartley's intertextual discussion on Leviticus 9:24.

10. ranan: Hebrew רָנַן, transliteration ranan, to shout for joy, to sing joyfully, Strong number H7442. Meaning according to BDB: to give a ringing cry, shout for joy; HALOT: to shout with joy, jubilate. At Leviticus 9:24, when all the people saw the fire they shouted; the word in this chapter denotes the collective joyful cry raised because of acceptance, appearing in the same verse as the falling on faces of the next note — joy and reverence together.

11. naphal 'al-panav: a Hebrew phrase, to fall on one's face, to fall face-down. Of its parts, naphal is נָפַל, to fall, to fall prostrate, Strong number H5307, BDB: to fall, lie; panav is פָּנָיו, his face. At Leviticus 9:24 the people fell on their faces — in the original, the posture of the face falling down — a common bodily language in Scripture for worship and for meeting a theophany; see BDB's collection of uses of this collocation.

12. Sources at the level of New Testament reading: Hebrews 9:23–28 on the sacrifices as patterns of the heavenly things, Christ entering once into the holy place and obtaining eternal redemption, offering Himself once to put away sin and appearing the second time; Hebrews 12:29, "our God is a consuming fire," whose original context exhorts serving God with reverence and godly fear. Both are New Testament interpretations and homiletical uses of the Old Testament sacrifices and of God's holiness; when cited, the level must be marked, and they must not be read back as the plain original meaning of Leviticus 9. In addition: the adjacency and tension between Leviticus 9 and the event of Nadab and Abihu in Leviticus 10 belong to observation of narrative structure; this sermon notes the point only and does not unfold the next chapter.

## Devotional Excerpt

On the eighth day, Aaron first offered the sin offering for himself, then offered for the people; he entered the tabernacle with Moses, came out and blessed the people, the glory appeared, and fire came out from before the LORD and consumed the offering on the altar. Reading this, my heart grows quiet: to come before God, I need not pretend I have no problem. First be dealt with, first be forgiven, and only then speak of service and blessing. I learn also from the people: shout for joy when God accepts, fall on my face when God is holy — joy and reverence are not a choice between two. Lord, teach me today to come before You in order, without skipping steps and without reversing them: lay my sin and weakness before You first, then offer this day's life wholly to You, like the sacrifice on the altar. May my shouting today carry a heart that falls on its face, and my falling carry the hope of shouting; let me rest in the acceptance You have already shown, not by feeling, but by Your word and Your works, standing steadily before You through this day. And let me remember that the people waited gathered together: drawing near to God has never been a matter for me alone; teach me to come before You together with brothers and sisters, to confess together, to receive blessing together, to shout together for Your acceptance, and together to fall on our faces before Your holiness, carrying this day's worship from the church into the home and into work.

> Reading reminders: Five things must be nailed down before teaching this chapter. First, David became king of all Israel by waiting, not by seizing: before this chapter he had already been anointed twice — privately by Samuel at Bethlehem (see 1 Samuel 16) and publicly by the men of Judah at Hebron (see 2 Samuel 2); this chapter records the third anointing. This time the tribes themselves came to Hebron, made a covenant first and anointed him afterward — the order itself is the theology. The preacher must not turn this chapter into a success story about finally making it: between the anointings David never once reached out to take anything, and that refusal to reach is the foundation of this chapter's legitimate kingship. Second, on the blind and the lame, the pulpit must set a guard in advance: the line comes from the Jebusites' taunt and the echo of David's battle order; commentators remain divided on its exact attribution and meaning to this day (one reading: the Jebusites trusted their strong city and mocked that even the blind and lame could defend it; another: David ordered his men to strike those he hated). The details are registered here, not adjudicated. On either reading, this chapter must never be used to mock or despise anyone with a physical disability — later this same David brought Mephibosheth, lame in both feet, to eat at the king's table for life (see 2 Samuel 9, registered in the same series); that is David's settled attitude toward the lame. Third, only what the text states may be preached about the capture of Jerusalem: the word for the water shaft, צִנּוֹר (ṣinnôr), occurs only twice in the whole Old Testament, and whether it denotes the city's vertical water shaft or another installation, commentators have reached no consensus; the parallel account in Chronicles adds that Joab went up first and so became chief (see 1 Chronicles 11:6, registered in the same series), while 2 Samuel does not name who went up first — the two passages are read together and only registered; the preacher must not present any reconstruction as fact, nor turn the siege into material that incites violence. Fourth, on David taking more wives and concubines in Jerusalem and the sons born to him (vv. 13-16), Scripture records but does not commend: the law of the king in Deuteronomy expressly says the king must not multiply wives for himself (see Deuteronomy 17:17, registered in the same series); the list of six sons at Hebron in chapter 3 already contains the cast of the coming family tragedies, and the list of eleven sons here must likewise not be read as a simple list of blessings — Nathan and Solomon later stand, one in the line of promise in the Lord's genealogy (see Luke 3, same series), the other as the temple builder who failed in old age (see 1 Kings 11, same series). The preacher should register the tension as it stands, neither developing it prematurely nor whitewashing it. Fifth, two inquiries and two different battle plans are not a success formula: the first time David was told to go straight up; the second time to circle around and wait for the sound in the treetops — the two instructions differ, which proves instructions cannot be copied. The sound of marching in the balsam treetops was a sign specific to that day and that battle, not a method for seeking omens to imitate; hearers today must not use this chapter to learn to listen for sounds and divine fortune. What this chapter teaches is to inquire afresh for every battle, and not to treat yesterday's victory as today's talisman. ## Summary

2 Samuel 5 is the total harvest of David's lifelong waiting: from the horn of oil at Bethlehem to the palace at Hebron — more than ten years, three anointings — and the kingship finally comes in full. The chapter does not open with David marching north to demand the kingdom; the tribes of Israel themselves travel south to Hebron, and the first words through the door are, "We are your own bone and flesh," followed by the public recital of God's promise: "You shall shepherd my people." Elders and king make a covenant before the LORD, and the anointing follows: covenant first, crown afterward. In the middle of the chapter David takes Jerusalem: the Jebusites boast from their natural fortress that even the blind and the lame can hold it, yet David goes up by the water shaft, captures the stronghold of Zion and renames it the City of David. The timetable — anointed at thirty, forty years on the throne — is nailed down in this chapter; the king of Tyre sends cedar and craftsmen, a palace rises, and the conclusion David draws is not "I deserved it," but that God exalted this kingdom for the sake of His people. In the last part of the chapter the Philistines twice spread out in the Valley of Rephaim and David twice inquires: in the first battle he goes straight up, and God breaks through the enemy before him like bursting water; in the second he is told to circle behind them, lie opposite the balsam trees, and not move out until he hears the sound of marching in the treetops — the same God, the same enemy, the same man, two battles, two different plans. This sermon treats three things: unity is not beaten out of people but recognized out of kinship — bone and flesh first, covenant after; a city that cannot be entered — God has His own inconspicuous channel, and the louder the mockery, the more it shows the defenders have nothing left but mouths; and victory cannot be copied — yesterday's straight charge, carried into today, becomes today's cause of defeat; every battle must begin with a fresh inquiry.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, today we stand before the covenant at Hebron, beneath the walls of Zion, and beside the balsam trees in the Valley of Rephaim. Teach us first to recognize kinship: how many families, teams and churches, divided for years, lack not a win or a loss but someone willing to say first, "We are your own bone and flesh." Teach those who have waited many years, on the day the promise comes in full, not to reach out or seize, but still to make covenant first and receive afterward. Restrain our contempt for the broken, and let us remember how You treasure every person; open for those facing a city they cannot enter that inconspicuous way, like the water shaft. Finally, cure our disease of living off experience: yesterday's battle was asked for yesterday; for today's battle we kneel again and ask again. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: The Third Anointing, and a New Capital

Read the first four chapters together with this one. The first four chapters are a downward road with an upward thread hidden in it: a lament, a divided kingdom, blood at a city gate, a knife in a bedchamber — the house of Saul exits one by one, yet David never once reached out to push anyone. Then this chapter opens and the situation turns itself over: it is not David who goes looking for the tribes; the tribes come to Hebron looking for David. When Scripture writes a change of kingship, its settled pattern here is this: what is meant to come will come on its own, and when it comes it brings a covenant, a recital of the promise, and the anointing of the whole people. A seat seized must be guarded every day; only a seat established by covenant can be sat on for forty years.

The chapter has three sections. First, the tribes and the covenant (vv. 1-5): kinship recognized, the promise recited, the covenant at Hebron, the third anointing, the forty-year timetable. Second, Zion and the palace (vv. 6-12): the Jebusites' boast, the attack through the water shaft, the City of David, Hiram's cedar and David's knowing. Third, the sons and the two battles (vv. 13-25): the list of eleven sons born in Jerusalem, the Philistines' two invasions, David's two inquiries, one battle straight up and one by circling around. The chapter has three inner threads. The thread of recognition: the tribes recognize David as bone and flesh, David recognizes that the LORD established him, Hiram recognizes the new king and sends craftsmen — everyone in this chapter is recognizing someone, and when recognition is right, the order is right. The thread of the city: from Hebron to Jerusalem, the kingdom's center moves from a city beside the patriarchs' tomb into a city that had never belonged to Israel; when God chooses a site for His kingdom He does not pick a ready-made holy place — He renames an enemy city the City of David, which later becomes the dwelling where He set His own name (see 2 Chronicles 6, registered). The thread of inquiry: in chapter 2 David asks whether to go up to Hebron; at the end of chapter 4 he hands judgment over; in this chapter he asks about two battles in a row — having become king of all Israel, his habit of inquiring has not changed by one degree; the bigger the seat, the more one must ask. This chapter is a mirror left for everyone in authority.

Set three measures before reading. First, do not preach the tribes' submission as opportunists piling onto a winning side: the text gives them three layers of reasons — kinship, the proven record of leading Israel out and in, and the promise of the LORD — with the promise as the climax; they did not come to place a bet but to claim words God had spoken years before. Second, do not miss where the covenant in verse 3 was made: before the LORD — the covenant is not a political document signed for the people to see but a covenant made in God's presence; in the covenant the king is first a man under restraint, and only then a man in power. Third, do not read "became greater and greater" in verse 10 as David's personal rise: the second half of the sentence gives the reason — for the LORD, the God of hosts, was with him; and verse 12 nails it down: the kingdom was exalted for the sake of His people. Every attribution of greatness in this chapter rests on God and the people; the preacher must not reverse it.

## Part One: The Tribes Come to Hebron (2 Samuel 5:1-5)

### 1. "We are your own bone and flesh" (2 Samuel 5:1-2)

All the tribes of Israel came to David at Hebron and said, "We are your own bone and flesh. In the past, while Saul was king over us, it was you who led Israel out and brought them in; and the LORD said to you, 'You shall shepherd my people Israel, and you shall be prince over Israel'" (see 2 Samuel 5:1-2, condensed rendering in this series).

"Bone" is עֶצֶם (ʿeṣem, H6106), bone. See BDB, s.v. עצם.[1] "Flesh" is בָּשָׂר (bāśār, H1320), flesh, flesh and blood. See BDB, s.v. בשר.[2] This expression "bone and flesh" was spoken in Genesis by Laban to Jacob: "You are my very bone and flesh" (see Genesis 29:14, condensed rendering in this series) — words of recognizing kin, not courtesy. The tribes' first sentence through the door is not about terms or spoils; first they recognize kin: "We are your own" — "are" meaning have always been, only unacknowledged for years. The first step of unity has never been the negotiating table but the family table: first admit that we were one family all along; division is the accident, unity is coming home. Look at their second layer of reasons: "It was you who led Israel out and brought them in." "Out and in" in the original is מוֹצִיא וּמֵבִיא, causative participles used together, "leading out and bringing in" — they were recognizing a proven record: in the years Saul held the throne, the one who actually led the army out and brought the people home was David; title and record had been out of alignment for years, and this chapter aligns them in public. The third layer is the weightiest: "The LORD said to you." The whole people publicly reciting God's promise amounts to a declaration: today's anointing is not our invention; we have finally caught up with what God said long ago. Preach the order of these three layers to everyone seeking unity: recognize kin first, then the record, and finally the promise — unity that skips kinship and goes straight to interests is a merger, not unity.

### 2. "You shall shepherd my people" (2 Samuel 5:2)

The LORD had promised, "You shall shepherd my people Israel, and you shall be prince over Israel" (see 2 Samuel 5:2, gist in condensed rendering).

"Shepherd" is רָעָה (rāʿâ, H7462), to pasture, to shepherd. See BDB, s.v. רעה.[3] "Prince" is נָגִיד (nāgîd, H5057), leader, ruler. See BDB, s.v. נגיד.[4] Notice the wording of the promise: God does not say "be king" first; He says "shepherd" first. Shepherding comes before being prince — in God's ledger the throne is a tool of shepherding, not its reward. As a boy David shepherded sheep in the fields of Bethlehem; as a man he shepherded a band of fugitives in the wilderness; in this chapter God makes it plain: Israel is My people, and your assignment is to shepherd. The three words "my people" deserve circling most of all: the people are not the king's people but God's people, and the king is only an entrusted shepherd. This verse is one of the anchors of the whole Bible's theology of kingship; when the prophets later rebuke Israel's shepherds for feeding themselves instead of the flock (see Ezekiel 34, same series), the measuring rod is taken from here; and when the Lord Jesus calls Himself the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (see John 10, same series), He takes up this same line. Preach this to everyone today who leads a team, a church or a family: if your seat makes you think of power before shepherding, the order is already reversed; when God places people in your hands, His first word of entrustment is always shepherding, never domination.

### 3. A covenant made before the LORD (2 Samuel 5:3-5)

All the elders of Israel came to Hebron, and King David made a covenant with them at Hebron before the LORD, and they anointed David king over Israel. David was thirty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned forty years (see 2 Samuel 5:3-4, condensed rendering in this series).

"Make a covenant" joins כָּרַת (kārath, H3772, to cut; the cutting of covenant-making) with בְּרִית (bərîth, H1285, covenant). See BDB, s.v. כרת, ברית.[5][6] "Anoint" is מָשַׁח (māšaḥ, H4886), to anoint. See BDB, s.v. משח.[7] Look at the order of covenant and anointing once more: covenant first, anointing afterward; restraint first, crown afterward. And the covenant was made before the LORD — king and elders covenanted with each other, but they did so in God's presence, so the first witness to any breach is God Himself. David made covenants all his life: a life-and-death covenant with Jonathan (see 1 Samuel 18 and 20, same series), and in this chapter a national covenant with all the people; he is the Bible's most covenant-willing king, because he knew the human heart cannot be relied on and was willing to place relationships inside the frame of a covenant and keep them there. Then the text nails down the timetable: anointed at thirty, forty years on the throne, seven years and six months at Hebron, thirty-three years at Jerusalem. The figure of seven years and six months already appeared in chapter 2 — the years of the divided kingdom are entered into the total account to the last fraction: God did not delete those seven and a half years of partial rule from the record; on the day the promise came in full, even the years of waiting counted. Preach this to those now settled in a small corner: your one city, one tribe, seven and a half years of small circumstances are not a stain on your record but the first line of the forty-year account.

## Part Two: Zion Captured, a City Gained (2 Samuel 5:6-12)

### 1. The Jebusites say, "You shall not come in here" (2 Samuel 5:6-8)

David and his men went to Jerusalem against the Jebusites who lived in the land; the Jebusites said to David, "You will not come in here; unless you first drive away the blind and the lame, you cannot enter this place," thinking David could never enter. Yet David captured the stronghold of Zion (see 2 Samuel 5:6-7, condensed rendering in this series; on the attribution and reading of the blind-and-lame line, see the second reading reminder at the head of this manuscript — registered, not adjudicated).

"Jebusite" is יְבוּסִי (Yəḇûsî, H2983). See BDB, s.v. יבוסי.[8] "Zion" is צִיּוֹן (Ṣîyôn, H6726). See BDB, s.v. ציון.[9] "Stronghold" is מְצֻדָה (məṣudâ, H4686), fortress, stronghold. See BDB, s.v. מצדה.[10] Jerusalem had been in Jebusite hands for centuries: in Joshua's day Israel could not drive them out (see Joshua 15:63, condensed rendering in this series), and in the days of the judges the Benjaminites lived alongside them (see Judges 1:21, condensed rendering in this series) — a city in the very middle of the promised land that had never been on Israel's map. The Jebusites' confidence was their terrain: the city sits on a ridge ringed by valleys, and they boasted to the point of saying the defense was so strong that even the blind and the lame could hold it. The essence of mockery is contempt: they despised this newly made king and his newly united army. This chapter's answer is not verbal: that day David ordered his men to go up by the water shaft and attack. "Water shaft" is צִנּוֹר (ṣinnôr, H6794), water channel, shaft; the word occurs elsewhere in the Old Testament only in Psalm 42, and what exactly it denotes, commentators have not settled — registered only. See BDB, s.v. צנור.[11] Whatever exactly that channel was, one theological layer is clear: the city was not taken by the most respectable frontal assault but by a route nobody could see and nobody thought much of. A city judged impossible to enter was finally entered through something like a drain — inconspicuous. Preach this to those facing a hard situation today: your city that cannot be entered — a post, a discipline, a person's heart — may not wait for you to be strong enough head-on before it opens; first find the water shaft: the step so small it draws no attention, yet leads straight into the city. One more measure must be stated in public: David's capture of this city is a record within the Old Testament's particular history of God's judgment on the Canaanite peoples and the fulfillment of His promise (see the registration of the Amorites' iniquity being full, Genesis 15, same series); it is not a license for anyone today to use violence. What hearers today may imitate is only the wisdom of trusting God to find a way — never the sword.

### 2. David became greater and greater, because God was with him (2 Samuel 5:9-10)

David lived in the stronghold and called it the City of David; and David built all around from the Millo inward. And David became greater and greater, for the LORD, the God of hosts, was with him (see 2 Samuel 5:9-10, condensed rendering in this series).

"Millo" is מִלּוֹא (Millôʾ, H4407). See BDB, s.v. מלא.[12] For "became greater and greater," the central word of the original's doubled construction is גָּדַל (gādal, H1431), to grow great, to become strong. See BDB, s.v. גדל.[13] The stronghold is renamed the City of David — within one chapter a centuries-old enemy city changes its name. In Scripture a renaming is never merely administrative: it declares belonging. Then comes building: from the Millo all around — first secure the defense, then talk of other things; David's first projects after gaining the city were not a palace but walls. Then read the attribution sentence word by word: "David became greater and greater, for the LORD, the God of hosts, was with him." Greatness is the fruit; God's presence is the cause. The narrator nails cause and effect here precisely to prevent later generations from reading this as a biography of David's ability. Earlier in this book the same kind of sentence was written: when Saul saw that the LORD was with David, he was afraid of him (see 1 Samuel 18, same series). God's presence is David's only core asset in his whole life: it was there when he shepherded, there when he fled, and still there in this chapter when his throne was secure. Preach this verse to those who panic just as things begin to improve: what you must guard is not greatness but presence; when greatness cannot be held and presence is still there, the account is not yet lost.

### 3. A palace of cedar, and David knew (2 Samuel 5:11-12)

Hiram king of Tyre sent messengers to David, and cedar trees, carpenters and masons, and they built David a house. And David knew that the LORD had established him king over Israel and had exalted his kingdom for the sake of his people Israel (see 2 Samuel 5:11-12, condensed rendering in this series).

"Hiram" is חִירָם (Ḥîrām, H2438), a personal name. See BDB, s.v. חירם.[14] "Cedar" is אֶרֶז (ʾerez, H730). See BDB, s.v. ארז.[15] "Knew" is יָדַע (yādaʿ, H3045), to know, to know for certain. See BDB, s.v. ידע.[16] Tyre was the maritime trading power of the day, and what Hiram sent was not a letter of congratulations but building materials and craftsmen — international recognition built into a palace. The conclusion David drew standing in his new palace is worth memorizing by everyone who is ever exalted: he knew that the one who established him was the LORD, and that the kingdom was exalted for the sake of His people Israel. He credited the exaltation upward to God and its purpose downward to the people, leaving not a fraction for himself. Later, sitting in that palace, he was restless because the ark still dwelt in a tent (see 2 Samuel 7, same series); the root of that restlessness is this chapter's word "knew." Register the measure here too: the palace itself is not sin — David later prayed and composed psalms in it at ease; the sin is not in the palace but in the person who reads the palace as proof he deserved it. Preach this section to those today whose income has risen, whose seat has been promoted, who have been recognized: on the day you are exalted by others, say two sentences aloud — "This was established by God" and "This is for the people I serve." The part you cannot say aloud is the doorway of future corruption.

### Ignition Point One: The city you said could never be entered — God comes in by the water shaft

Brothers and sisters, hear the Jebusites' "never." How many people hear those words echoing all their lives: you will never. You will never enter that school, never do that work, never climb out of that pit, never be accepted again. Those who say it usually stand on high ground, trusting their terrain and your past record. Today, on the basis of this chapter, I say to you: terrain can stop people; it cannot stop the way God finds for you. God did not make David's voice louder than the Jebusites'; He made his men bend down, go through the water shaft, and enter from the city's least defended place. Today, not tomorrow — go and find your water shaft: that thing so small nobody thinks anything of it, yet leading straight into the city; do that one thing first. The mockers are still shouting on the wall, and you are already inside the city.

## Part Three: The Sons, and the Two Battles in the Valley of Rephaim (2 Samuel 5:13-25)

### 1. Eleven sons in Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5:13-16)

After David left Hebron he took more concubines and wives in Jerusalem, and more sons and daughters were born to him. The sons born in Jerusalem who are named are Shammua, Shobab, Nathan, Solomon, Ibhar, Elishua, Nepheg, Japhia, Elishama, Eliada and Eliphalet (see 2 Samuel 5:13-16, condensed rendering in this series).

"Solomon" is שְׁלֹמֹה (Šəlōmô, H8010), a personal name. See BDB, s.v. שלמה.[17] "Nathan" is נָתָן (Nāthān, H5416), a personal name. See BDB, s.v. נתן.[18] Read this list by the same rule as the list of six sons in chapter 3: it must not be read as a list of blessings and left there. In the middle of the list two names stand side by side: Nathan and Solomon. In Luke's genealogy the Lord Jesus' line ascends to David through Nathan's branch (see Luke 3, same series); in Matthew's genealogy it ascends through Solomon (see Matthew 1, same series) — in the disordered genealogy of a polygamous king, God still threaded the line of promise through without tangling a single strand. A man's house can be in disorder; God's covenant is not. That is the hope this list gives desperate families. At the same time the warning is registered as it stands: the taking of many concubines clashes head-on with the law of the king in Deuteronomy (see Deuteronomy 17:17, condensed rendering in this series), and the later Bathsheba affair and Absalom's revolt have half their root buried in this list and these wives (see 2 Samuel 11-18, same series, registered). This chapter places the list before the two battles, like driving a nail into the edge of the victory ledger first: the city has been taken and the battles not yet fought — remember first that the kingdom's greatest crack would one day appear not in its walls but in the king's house.

### 2. The first battle: "Go up; I will deliver them into your hand" (2 Samuel 5:17-21)

When the Philistines heard that David had been anointed king over Israel, they all came up to the Valley of Rephaim to seek David. David inquired of the LORD, and the LORD said, "Go up, for I will certainly deliver the Philistines into your hand." David came to Baal-Perazim and struck them there, and said, "The LORD has broken through my enemies before me like a bursting flood" (see 2 Samuel 5:17-20, condensed rendering in this series).

"Philistines" is פְּלִשְׁתִּים (Pəlištîm, H6430). See BDB, s.v. פלשתי.[19] "Valley of Rephaim" is עֵמֶק רְפָאִים (ʿēmeq Rəpāʾîm): "valley" is עֵמֶק (H6010) and "Rephaim" is רְפָאִים (H7497). See BDB, s.v. עמק, רפאים.[20][21] "Break through" is פָּרַץ (pāraṣ, H6555), to break through, to burst out. See BDB, s.v. פרץ.[22] "Baal-Perazim" is בַּעַל פְּרָצִים (Baʿal Pərāṣîm, H1188), a place name meaning "lord of breakthroughs." See BDB, s.v. בעל פרצים.[23] Look first at the Philistines' timing: they did not come when Saul died, nor when David became king of Judah; the moment David was anointed king over all Israel, their whole army came up. The enemy's mobilization follows your anointing: however large you are established, that is how much pressure is adjusted on the other side — this is not a curse but the basic market condition of the spiritual world; do not, the moment you are targeted, suspect something went wrong with the anointing. Then look at David's order: he did not rush down into the valley on the sharpness of a newly won world; he inquired. "Inquire" is שָׁאַל (šāʾal, H7592). See BDB, s.v. שאל.[24] Only with permission did he go up. After the victory he named the place Baal-Perazim, "lord of breakthroughs" — naming as crediting: the one who broke through was the LORD; I merely arrived. The Philistines abandoned their idols as they fled, and David and his men took them away (2 Samuel 5:21 reads "took away"; the parallel account in 1 Chronicles 14:12 reads that he ordered them burned with fire — the two are read together and registered in the same series; destroying idols accords with the command in Deuteronomy 7, same series): the close of a victorious battle is not displaying the captured gods as souvenirs but removing them. Preach this to the victorious: if your first act after victory is displaying trophies to boast, the next battle is already at the door.

### 3. The second battle: "Do not go straight up; wait opposite the balsam trees" (2 Samuel 5:22-25)

The Philistines came up again and spread out in the Valley of Rephaim. David inquired of the LORD, and the LORD said, "Do not go straight up; circle around behind them and come at them opposite the balsam trees. And when you hear the sound of marching in the tops of the balsam trees, bestir yourself, for then the LORD has gone out before you to strike the army of the Philistines." David did as the LORD commanded, and struck the Philistines from Geba as far as Gezer (see 2 Samuel 5:22-25, condensed rendering in this series; for the name Geba, the parallel account in Chronicles reads Gibeon, same series — registered, not adjudicated).

"Circle around" is סָבַב (sāḇaḇ, H5437), to turn around, to circle. See BDB, s.v. סבב.[25] "Balsam tree" is בָּכָא (bākhāʾ, H1057), a kind of balsam tree; which species exactly, commentators have not settled — registered only. See BDB, s.v. בכא.[26] "Marching" is צְעָדָה (ṣəʿāḏâ, H6807), marching, stepping. See BDB, s.v. צעדה.[27] The most memorizable thing in the second battle is God's first sentence: "Do not go straight up." In the first battle going straight up was right; in the second, going straight up is wrong — the same valley, the same enemy, the same man, and God forbids him to copy. How many victors die of copying: last time I prayed like this and was heard, so this time I fill the same prescription; last time I invested like this and gained, so this time I double the stake; last time I handled my child this way and it worked, so this time I do it again without asking why. God's instructions are asked fresh for every battle; they are not a replay of the last battle's recording. This battle's method is circling and waiting: go around opposite the balsam trees and lie down, ears against the treetops — when you hear the sound of marching, it means the vanguard is not you but Him: the LORD has already gone out before you. The human lesson in this battle is only two words: keep up. Not charging ahead of God to win merit, but hastening after Him. Preach this to everyone eager to prove themselves: the hardest obedience is not charging but lying among the balsam trees listening; when no sound has come, you must not manufacture one, and when it comes, you must not delay a single second.

### Ignition Point Two: When you hear the sound of marching in the balsam treetops, bestir yourself

Today I appeal to those who can no longer sit still in their waiting. You have lain opposite the balsam trees for a very long time: prayers without echo, doors that do not open, not a breath of wind in the treetops. You are beginning to wonder whether you are lying in the wrong place. On the basis of this chapter, let me say it plainly: when you have not heard, waiting is not failure; it is part of the orders. Had David charged out before the sound came, history would have written this battle differently. But once you hear it — the marching rising in the treetops, God already moving ahead of you — you must not keep lying there! How many people's problem is not failing to wait but waiting turned into habit: God has moved and they are still seeking confirmation in place; the door has opened and they are still counting risks at the threshold. Today: if you hear His footsteps already ahead of you, get up and bestir yourself. None of the years of waiting is wasted; they are all counted in the strength of this one charge. Do not let yesterday's straight charge block you, and do not let long lying low disable you — when you hear it, move!

## Cross-Exegesis: Five Passages Read Together

This chapter's events echo elsewhere in Scripture; five readings nail it down. Old Testament, first: the two faces of the words "bone and flesh" — Laban says to Jacob, "You are my very bone and flesh" (Genesis 29:14, Chinese Union Version): kinship recognized. But Gideon's son Abimelech says to the men of Shechem, "I too am your bone and flesh" (see Judges 9:2, condensed rendering in this series), borrowing kinship to stir them into making him king, then slaughtering seventy brothers. The same root: spoken from sincerity it is the door of unity; spoken from ambition it is the fuse of a bloodbath. Read both alongside this chapter: the tribes put bone and flesh first, yet immediately set out the LORD's promise as their warrant and bound themselves by a covenant before God — kinship must be recognized inside promise and covenant, or it degenerates into faction. Old Testament, second: the law of the king in Deuteronomy — "You shall surely set over you a king whom the LORD your God chooses, one from among your brothers… The king must not multiply horses for himself, nor multiply wives, nor greatly multiply silver and gold" (see Deuteronomy 17:15-17, condensed rendering in this series). Read together with this chapter, the tension faces us directly: David is precisely the one chosen from among the brothers and anointed by all the people — he matches the first half of the king's law entirely; but the second half, on multiplying wives, collides exactly with verse 13 of this chapter — the law written five centuries earlier had already placed the warning there, and this chapter registers it as it stands, deleting nothing, decorating nothing. If the shepherd-king loses restraint first in his own house, the second half of a forty-year reign will pay for it; the record comes later in the text, and the pulpit here shows the measuring rod without rushing the verdict. Psalms, one passage: the shepherd-king who chose Zion — "For He chose the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion which He loves… He also chose David His servant and took him from the sheepfolds… to shepherd Jacob His people and Israel His inheritance. So he shepherded them with integrity of heart and guided them with skillful hands" (see Psalm 78:68-72, condensed rendering in this series). The psalm merges this chapter's two great events into one sentence: choosing Zion and choosing David are the same act; city and king are chosen together. And the words of assessment for the king fall not on conquest but on integrity of heart and shepherding — Psalm 78 is this chapter's most fitting footnote. Proverbs, one passage: the righteous in power and the exaltation of a city — "When the righteous increase, the people rejoice… A city is exalted by the blessing of the upright" (see Proverbs 29:2 and 11:10-11, condensed rendering in this series). David gained the city, yet Proverbs reminds us: a city is exalted not by higher walls but by the blessing of the upright within it and by righteous governance; Jerusalem later became the holy city not because David captured it but because God set His name there and did righteousness (see Psalm 122, praying for the peace of Jerusalem, same series, registered). New Testament, one passage: David's throne and the everlasting kingdom — the angel says to Mary, "He will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High, and the Lord God will give Him the throne of His father David, and He will reign over the house of Jacob forever, and of His kingdom there will be no end" (see Luke 1:32-33, condensed rendering in this series). This chapter's forty-year timetable, seen in New Testament light, is only a prelude: David's city later saw the birth of David's descendant, and David's throne points to a King whose kingdom has no end. Every covenant, anointing and captured city in this chapter finally prepares the stage for that One; a pulpit preaching David's glory must land its endpoint on his Descendant's kingdom and not stop at David himself.

## Application: Six Groups of People

First, to those who have waited many years and seen the promise fulfilled only in a corner. David waited at Hebron seven years and six months before the tribes came. Concrete exercise: this week, write the thing you are waiting for in three lines following the three anointings — God's first stirring, the small confirmations along the way, and the full picture not yet fulfilled; having written it, neither press nor seize — do one duty you can do today, and tell one companion, asking him to be the witness of your not reaching out.

Second, to those living in division and longing for unity. The tribes' order is your exercise: recognize kin first, covenant afterward. Concrete exercise: choose one relationship broken for years, and within this month send first one sentence of kinship — no old accounts, no conditions, only the admission that we were one family all along; if the other side responds, then talk about a concrete covenant: how you will treat each other from now on, written as two or three articles both sides can keep — never make covenants you cannot keep at an emotional peak.

Third, to those newly exalted, beginning to hold power. David's "he knew" is your mirror. Concrete exercise: for this promotion or new entrustment, say the two attributions aloud, in public or at least before your family — "This was established by God" and "This is for the people I serve"; and set one household rule: for every level power rises, add one layer of checkable transparency at home and in the accounts — never let anyone, your spouse included, be unable to ask about your money and your time.

Fourth, to those facing a city they cannot enter. The Jebusites' city was not taken by volume of shouting but by finding the water shaft. Concrete exercise: write down your city, then list three inconspicuous small channels — a letter, sitting in on a class, a small project, an internship; choose one and take the first step within this week. Do not answer the mockery on the wall; spend the time you would spend answering it on finding the way.

Fifth, to those who have been mocked and despised, who think themselves disqualified by some defect. Read this chapter together with chapter 9 and find your place: the men on the wall made the blind and the lame a joke, but at the king's table there is a lifelong seat for a lame man. Concrete exercise: this week, find an elder or pastor who respects you, and say aloud to him the "you will never" that has pressed on you for years; ask him to pray for you once from this chapter. Words of God's acceptance must be heard by your ears once for real, not merely argued into yourself in your own heart.

Sixth, to those winning battle after battle and now living off experience. "Do not go straight up" in the second battle is your order. Concrete exercise: for the matter you know best and most want to handle the old way, stop for twenty-four hours first, pray and inquire afresh, and write down three ways this time differs from last time; do not start until the differences are clear, and once you hear the new instruction, do not delay a second — bestir yourself.

## Closing Response Prayer

Lord, we gather 2 Samuel 5 into a response. Heal the divided bone and flesh among us, and let words of kinship be spoken before words of blame. Establish those who have waited many years, and keep us from seizing with our own hands what You mean to give with Yours. Open the water-shaft way for those facing a high city, and keep us from using Your word to despise anyone who is broken. Give those lying opposite the balsam trees a pair of ears: able to wait when it is time to wait, and able to rise and keep up when they hear Your footsteps. May our unity stand in covenant, our greatness be credited to Your presence, and all our victories finally be gathered into the unending kingdom of David's Descendant. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotion use, 300-500 words)

In 2 Samuel 5 the tribes come to Hebron and say to David, "We are your own bone and flesh" (see 2 Samuel 5:1, condensed rendering in this series); before the LORD they make a covenant with the king and anoint him king over all Israel. David moves against Jerusalem; the Jebusites say he can never enter, yet God leads him to take the city of Zion by the water shaft (see 2 Samuel 5:6-8, condensed rendering in this series). The Philistines invade twice and David inquires twice: in the first battle he is told to go straight up, and God breaks through the enemy before him like bursting water; in the second he is told to circle around, lie opposite the balsam trees, and only bestir himself when he hears the sound of marching in the treetops, for the LORD has already gone out before him (see 2 Samuel 5:19-24, condensed rendering in this series). Three questions for meditation today: in seeking unity, do I recognize kin first, or negotiate terms first? Where is the water shaft of the city I cannot enter? In this battle, am I copying my last victory, or inquiring afresh? Prayer: Lord, teach me to recognize kin, to make covenant, and to inquire afresh; if Your footsteps are already ahead of me, give me feet that hasten to keep up.

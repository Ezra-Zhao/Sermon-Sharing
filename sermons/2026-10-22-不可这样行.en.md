- Source: Sermon mass-production batch 2, late night of 2026-10-22 (continuing from the preceding text: Jacob's household had settled peacefully at Shechem, bought land and built an altar, calling it El-elohe-Israel; it seemed all the storms had passed, yet disaster came upon the whole family through a daughter who went out alone)

## Summary (about 100 words)

Dinah, Leah's daughter, went out to see the women of the land and was seized and defiled by Shechem; a marriage proposal and an offer of intermarriage followed, Jacob's sons answered with deceit, and on the third day Simeon and Levi slaughtered the city and plundered it. One sentence of Scripture — such a thing ought not to be done in Israel — measures out a chain of sins: the sin of the violator, the sin of the avengers, and the sin of the silent one. Justice is real, and anger is real, but justice must never be pursued through a greater injustice, or God's name is first dishonored among the nations.

---

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all.

This chapter is one of the heaviest chapters in Genesis. As we read it, please lower your voices and put away any curious heart. There are no heroes in this chapter — only a woman who was harmed, a group of men burned out of their senses by anger, a father who was silent from beginning to end, and an altar that had only just been built. The altar was built at Shechem, and disaster also came to Shechem. Today we have not come to watch a spectacle; we have come to hear God, through a tragedy, speak one extremely solemn sentence to us: in Israel, such a thing ought not to be done.

First, the connection with what came before. In the previous chapter Jacob was reconciled with his brother Esau, pitched his tent at Succoth, and came in peace to Shechem, where he bought a parcel of land and erected an altar, calling God El-elohe-Israel — God, the God of Israel. A man who had wandered for twenty years finally had land, an altar, and a settled home. By human reckoning, the story should have moved toward stability. But the Bible's pen turns cold: settling down is not the same as peace, and building an altar is not the same as a whole household fearing God. The enemy outside had been reconciled, but the sin inside had not yet been dealt with; and precisely at the most settled moment, disaster opened its mouth through the least guarded person in the family.

Let us read several key passages together, noting as we read how the Bible itself characterizes this matter:

"And Dinah the daughter of Leah, which she bare unto Jacob, went out to see the daughters of the land. And when Shechem the son of Hamor the Hivite, prince of the country, saw her, he took her, and lay with her, and defiled her." (Genesis 34:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And the sons of Jacob came out of the field when they heard it: and the men were grieved, and they were very wroth, because he had wrought folly in Israel in lying with Jacob's daughter; which thing ought not to be done." (Genesis 34:7, excerpt, KJV)

"Simeon and Levi are brethren; instruments of cruelty are in their habitations. ... in their anger they slew a man, and in their selfwill they digged down a wall." (Genesis 49:5-6, excerpt, KJV, Jacob's dying words)

Let us pray: Lord, today's word is not pleasant to hear, yet it is Your word. Guard our hearts first on the side of the victim; teach us to hate sin without letting anger drag us into sin as well; teach us to long for justice, yet to return the hand of vengeance to You. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Dinah Went Out — What One Woman Suffered, and the Calculation Behind a Marriage Proposal (34:1-12)

The first sentence of the text is very plain: "Dinah went out to see the daughters of the land." A young woman who wanted to go and see women of her own age, and how they lived — that in itself was not a sin. The Bible does not have one word of blame for Dinah. She went out with a young girl's normal curiosity about the world; she came back carrying a wound. The first boundary we must keep in preaching this chapter is this: the wrong was never in the woman who went out; the wrong was in the man who stretched out his hand to harm. If the church, in such matters, blames the victim first, it wounds her a second time and stands on the wrong side before God.

The text's pen then becomes extremely heavy, yet extremely restrained: "Shechem saw her, he took her, and lay with her, and defiled her." Three actions in one breath, without a single sentence of embellishment. That verb "defiled," in the original 'anah (עָנָה, H6031), carries the sense of bringing low, afflicting, and humiliating [2]. The Bible uses this word so that we understand what happened: this was not mutual affection, not a romantic episode, but a man of power — the son of the ruler of that land — treating the daughter of a sojourner as a thing that could be seized. Shechem was the son of "the prince of the country" — in the city of Shechem, his family's word was law. When power reaches the point of having no restraint, a person begins to regard other people's personhood, bodies, and dignity as his own territory. The Bible's characterization of this deed, from beginning to end, is one word: folly — a disgraceful deed. Verse 7 says he had "wrought folly in Israel"; the original is nevelah (נְבָלָה, H5039), a foolish, despicable deed that violates the sacred order [1]. The text does not call it love and does not call it a mistake; it calls it a disgraceful deed — that is God's view, and when we preach we can only say it as it is, without finding it any respectable excuse.

Even more chilling is the turn that follows. Afterward Shechem "loved" Dinah deeply, his soul "clave unto" her, he spoke kindly to her, and went home to ask his father to get him this girl as his wife. Brothers and sisters, please do not be deceived by this word "love." To violate first and speak of love afterward is not the order of love. Love never begins with seizure; love's first lesson is respect, boundaries, and treating the other as a bearer of God's image. What Shechem wanted from start to finish was "to have her": first by force, then by marriage — the means changed, but the center was still himself. However tenderly he spoke his words of comfort, they cannot erase the verdict the text has already pronounced — defiled. A truly repentant person does first of all confess his sin, submit himself to judgment, and make restitution to the victim, rather than hurrying to marry the victim into his house, packaging a disgraceful deed as a marriage and rewriting a crime as a wedding. It is the same today: using affection, benefits, or marriage to "wrap up" a wrong after harming someone is not repentance; it is only possession continued by another means.

Hamor came to make the proposal, and his words were even more respectable — and revealed even more. He said: "Make ye marriages with us, and give your daughters unto us, and take our daughters unto you. And ye shall dwell with us: and the land shall be before you; dwell and trade ye therein, and get you possessions therein." There are two layers here. One layer is assimilation: let us no longer distinguish you and us; with marriages coming and going, after a few generations your descendants will be our people, and your God, your covenant, and your set-apart identity will naturally fade away and vanish. In his day Abraham had repeatedly charged that Isaac must not take a wife from the daughters of the Canaanites, guarding precisely against this step — not out of arrogance of bloodline, but against the dissolving of faith. A family that an enemy cannot overthrow head-on often melts away slowly through intermarriage and living together. The other layer is profit: the land shall be before you. What Hamor also had his eye on was Jacob's family's livestock, property, and numbers. Underneath the marriage-proposal table lay a ledger. Shechem added one more sentence: "Ask me never so much dowry and gift, and I will give according as ye shall say unto me: but give me the damsel to wife." The dowry, in the original mohar (מֹהַר, H4119), was the property a bridegroom paid to the bride's household when taking a wife [3]. He was signing a blank check: name your price; the person I must have. The mistake people with money and power most often make is to think everything in the world has a price, and that the harm a person has suffered can be leveled out with gifts. Dinah's tears and the rewriting of Dinah's whole life were, in the eyes of Shechem and his father, merely a question of the size of a dowry figure. Once a person is treated as a price, the sin has already spread from one man onto a negotiating table.

There is another layer we easily slide past in reading this chapter: who was Dinah? The text introduces her in only half a sentence — "the daughter of Leah, which she bare unto Jacob." In the lists of Jacob's children, the sons appear one by one with names, tribes, and descendants; she is the only daughter whose name is recorded. She was the one daughter of this large household, the sister among the brothers. Of the sons' affairs the Bible records dozens of chapters — blessings, rivalries, the selling of a brother, the search for grain, one matter larger than the next; of this daughter, the Bible records only that she went out, that she was harmed, and that she was brought back. Human history is always written this way: the sons' affairs are the great matters, and the daughter's wound is an interlude. But God's pen is not like that: He used an entire chapter to record a woman whom human eyes treated as an "interlude." With God there is no such thing as an interlude person. Every victim who cannot speak has a whole chapter's place in His book. If, preaching this chapter in the church today, we talk from start to finish about the men's anger, calculations, and safety, without one minute stopping to remember Dinah, we have not yet understood why God left this chapter to us. Protecting the victim is not an application sentence tacked on at the end of a sermon; it is the very heart of this chapter: God looks first at the wounded person, and every other judgment of right and wrong must be measured in that light.

At the end of this part, notice one glaring silence. When Jacob heard the news that his daughter had been defiled, "Jacob held his peace until his sons came from the field." A father, hearing such a thing, reacted first by waiting. Waiting for what? For his sons to decide. He had his difficulties: sojourning on another people's land, few in number and weak, a fight could endanger the whole family. All of that is true. But the Bible sets those words — "held his peace" — there by themselves, and not without intent. As we shall see, the heaviest disaster of this household grew precisely out of the father's silence: if he would not step forward to uphold justice, justice fell into the hands of sons burning with rage; if he would not stand up at the first step, every step after that would be taken by others in his place, and taken farther and farther. Silence is sometimes wisdom, and sometimes it is handing one's responsibility over to loss of control. When someone in the family is harmed and the father, the leader, does not speak, the harmed person will remember it for a lifetime, and the one who acts will be emboldened all the more.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Deceitful Answer and the Sword on the Third Day (34:13-29)

The sons came home. The text first characterizes their hearts: when they heard it, "the men were grieved, and they were very wroth." The grief was real, and the anger was real — when a sister has suffered such harm, brothers who feel nothing would be the beasts. Anger itself is not sin: God also is angry, and righteous anger is evidence that the conscience is still alive. If, preaching this chapter today, we made everyone indifferent to Shechem's evil, we would have preached it wrong. The problem was never that they were angry, but how they used that anger.

"The sons of Jacob answered Shechem and Hamor his father deceitfully, and said, because he had defiled Dinah their sister." Please fix your eyes on that word "deceitfully." This was precisely Jacob's signature word for a lifetime: he deceived his brother out of the blessing and deceived his father's recognition; now his sons had learned the trick and used it on outsiders. Sin is inherited — not in the blood, but absorbed in the home by watching and listening. Whatever means a father has won by, his sons will do business by. The condition the sons set was this: if you will be circumcised as we are, every male among you, then we will give our daughters to you and take yours, and dwell with you; otherwise we will take our sister and go. It was spoken in a dignified manner, and even the reason of faith was brought out: we cannot give our sister to an uncircumcised man, for that would be a reproach to us. What is circumcision? It is the sign of God's covenant with Abraham, the seal of holiness, the outward mark of being set apart. To use it as bait for a trap, as a means of making men lose their power to resist, is to turn the sign of the holy covenant into an instrument of killing. This is the most piercing layer of sin in the whole chapter: not unbelievers doing evil, but men who bore God's covenant and flew faith's banner, using deceit to do evil. Once the name of religion is conscripted by anger, evil puts on the garment of holiness — more frightening than naked evil, because it deceives others and deceives itself.

Hamor and Shechem actually agreed. Every male of the city was circumcised — the prince in order to marry, the old king for the profit of intermarriage, and the men of the city for the prospect that "shall not their cattle and their substance and every beast of theirs be ours?" When the human heart is led by desire and greed, its judgment sinks astonishingly low: they saw only the benefit to be gained, and could not see what waited at the gate on the third day.

"And it came to pass on the third day, when they were sore," Simeon and Levi each took his sword, "came upon the city boldly, and slew all the males," and they slew Hamor and Shechem, and took Dinah out of Shechem's house, and went out. The Bible records this passage in a tone cold as stone: no laying out of details, no depiction of satisfaction, only the facts. Brothers and sisters, we should read it the same way: this was not the pleasure of revenge; this was the slaughter of a city. As for Shechem who committed the violence, he deserved his punishment, and no one cries injustice for him; but the place where the sword fell was not Shechem alone: a whole city of unarmed men, utterly unable to resist in their pain, fell in blood. Then the brothers entered the city and plundered it: the livestock, the wealth, the little ones, the wives, and everything in the houses — they took it all. Notice this stroke about plundering — if it were only to obtain justice for their sister, why empty a man's city? Anger, by this step, had changed in quality: at first it sought justice for a family member, then it killed until the eyes were red, and at last it coveted what it saw. This is how the chain of sin works: link locked into link, each link borrowing legitimacy from the one before. The violation was the first link, deceit the second, slaughter the third, plundering the fourth. The final catastrophe of this city was larger by far — a hundredfold — than the original disgraceful deed. Dinah's wound was not healed; she was only brought out of one house, walked through a city of blood, and returned to a home that from then on bore a stench. In this entire chapter she never spoke a single word — victims are often exactly like this: everyone speaks in her name, acts in her name, and grows rich in her name, while her own voice is asked by no one. Reading Scripture today, let us supply that question on her behalf: Dinah, how are you? If justice forgets to ask this one question, all that remains is men's anger and men's accounts.

Look carefully also at this link of plundering, for it most clearly shows the process by which anger changed in quality. The brothers entered the city and "took their sheep, and their oxen, and their asses, and that which was in the city, and that which was in the field; all their wealth, and all their little ones, and their wives took they captive." Notice that the text expressly writes "little ones" and "wives" into the list of the plunder: men who only yesterday were burning with rage over their sister's disgrace today carried off other families' wives and children as spoils of war. As Shechem had treated Dinah as a thing that could be seized, so they treated the wives and children of Shechem's city as things that could be seized — the form of the victimhood changed to a new set of people who bore it, but the pattern of the sin was exactly the same. This is precisely the most insidious place of sin: it can wear the garment of "recovering justice," so that the wronged side, without noticing, becomes a new wrongdoer. How many families and how many communities spend a generation avenging the wounds the previous generation suffered, and in avenging them lay the same wounds on people who had nothing to do with it, link locked into link, unable to stop for decades. The Bible records this stroke about plundering without the slightest approval, only calm reporting — God lets later generations see clearly: once anger crosses the boundary of justice, its endpoint is never justice; it is greed and new tears. To restrain anger is not weakness; it is refusing to become the kind of person one hated at the beginning.

Here the theological word must be spoken through. The Bible never denies that Shechem deserved judgment; the Law later expressly treated the seizing of a woman with the utmost seriousness — death for the man who forced a betrothed woman in the field (Deuteronomy 22:25-27), and, where she was not betrothed, marriage with no right ever to divorce her (22:28-29). Justice must be upheld — that is certain. The questions are: who upholds it, by what means, and to what extent. The sons were wrong in all three: those who executed judgment were private men filled with rage, not impartial judges; the means used were deceit and indiscriminate killing, not punishment measured to the crime; and the extent was the destruction and plundering of a city, not one man bearing his own crime. Once personal anger crowns itself as God's judgment, the sword no longer has a sheath. The New Testament draws the boundary with complete clarity: "Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord." (Romans 12:19) And again: "For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God." (James 1:20) Please nail these two sentences onto this chapter: it is not that God does not want justice; it is that God does not permit us to carry out His justice by injustice. However right the motive, once the means are dirtied, the whole matter turns from justice into another sin.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Stench, the Rebuke, and That One Question in Return (34:30-31)

Afterward, Jacob finally spoke. But the first sentence he spoke was not about right and wrong; it was about safety: "Ye have troubled me to make me to stink among the inhabitants of the land, among the Canaanites and the Perizzites." "Stink," in the original ba'ash (בָּאַשׁ, H887), literally to emit a foul odor, to become loathsome [4]. He said: I am few in number; if they gather themselves together against me and slay me, I and my house shall be destroyed. How much of this was factual? All of it. Shechem occupied a strategic place; a family sojourning there, having incurred such a blood feud, truly lived from morning to night in uncertainty. But notice: after his daughter's disgrace and his sons' slaughter of a city, the first thing a father reckoned was his own family's name and safety — not Dinah, and not right and wrong. In his rebuke there is not one sentence of "you should not have killed"; there is only "ye have troubled me." Advantage had replaced right and wrong — and this was exactly the old ailment of his whole life: at Bethel he had bargained with God; fleeing Esau, he calculated; facing Laban, he endured; and now, facing his sons' grave crime, he still reckoned risks first. It was not that Jacob did not believe in God; it was that Jacob believed in God too shrewdly, so shrewdly that right and wrong always ranked behind safety.

The sons' answer is the last sentence of the whole chapter, and the hardest to answer: "Should he deal with our sister as with an harlot?" This question is right. A sister is not a harlot, not merchandise, not something Shechem and his father could settle by naming a price; she is a person, a sister, a bearer of God's image. In this stand the brothers took for her there is a genuine light of human dignity. But brothers and sisters, the text deliberately places this question after the slaughter of the city, letting it come to us with hands full of blood: if the proposition is right, is the conclusion thereby right? "Should he deal with our sister as with an harlot?" — no, absolutely not; and yet "may a whole city of innocent men be slaughtered, and their wives and children carried away?" — equally not. The Bible sets the two "ought nots" face to face: Shechem's sin ought not to be, and the sons' sin also ought not to be. This question in return does not excuse the slaughter by one fraction; instead it measures out a deeper pain: when, in a family, the father is silent and justice is absent, the children can only answer — in a bloody way of their own — a question that ought to have been answered by the father and by public justice. The more rightly the question is asked, the more inexcusable the father's absence becomes.

The stench Jacob worried about has another layer, deeper than safety, which we must not miss: God's name was dishonored among the nations. How would the people around Shechem view this matter? They would say: that foreigner who built an altar at Shechem — what kind of God is his God? His sons used the sign of their covenant as bait, killed every man of a city, and robbed a city bare — so this is how the household of a God-fearing man behaves. Brothers and sisters, when believers and unbelievers weigh our God, the first thing they look at is not our doctrine but our conduct. Jacob's household ought to have been a blessing to that land: the altar had been built and God's name proclaimed; but after one blood case, the altar still stood and the witness had collapsed. From then on, what the nations remembered was not El-elohe-Israel, but slaughter and plunder. This is exactly the rebuke Paul later gave: "For the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles through you." (Romans 2:24) The price of our anger, deceit, and excessive revenge is never paid by our own household alone — God's name must pay it for us. A person who calls himself God-fearing yet acts unrighteously harms not only the victim, but also a whole company of people who might have come to know God through him. So the account of this chapter must be reckoned in full: Shechem wronged Dinah, the sons wronged the people of a city, and Jacob's household wronged God's name. The first two accounts people can see; the third is the one God cares about most.

Decades later, on his deathbed, Jacob blessed his sons and came to this page. His words were no longer shrewd; sentence by sentence they were verdicts: "Simeon and Levi are brethren; instruments of cruelty are in their habitations. ... in their anger they slew a man... Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce; and their wrath, for it was cruel: I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel." (Genesis 49:5-7, excerpt) Please note: the aged Jacob finally placed right and wrong ahead of safety. He did not praise his sons for standing up for their sister, nor count the slaughter of the city as the family's honor; what he cursed was not the cause of the anger, but anger out of control. That Levi was later set apart by God for service is another page, begun by God's grace; it cannot be turned back to whitewash the sword at Shechem. God's grace can overturn a person's outcome, but it never rewrites the nature of sin.

This chapter records no ending for Dinah; that is the Bible's honest silence. Her name appears only once more, in a family list. What we can do for her is this: let everyone who reads Scripture today remember that God placed this chapter in the Bible not so that we could watch a blood case, but so that we could see that He remembers the one who never spoke a word. In a world where everyone talked about her, used her, swung the sword for her, and made bargains over her, God's pen calls that deed only a disgraceful thing and her experience only a defilement, standing on her side from beginning to end. When someone in the household of the church suffers such harm, whom do we learn from? Not from Shechem's seizure, not from Hamor's calculation, not from the sons' deceit and sword, and not from Jacob's silence and shrewdness. We learn from the Bible itself: name sin as sin, stand with the victim, hand judgment to God, and carry out protection in concrete action. In the family and in the church, the moment a weak person is being harmed — stop it at the first moment, seek help and report it at the first moment, walk with her at the first moment through the road that must be walked — this is what "it ought not to be done" looks like concretely today. Anger must be restrained within the day, justice must travel the proper road, and the place of the father and the leader must never stand empty — an empty place will sooner or later be occupied by anger.

Gather up the application of this chapter, and there are only four things, every one of them landing on ourselves. First, concerning justice: sin must be called sin, and the victim must be protected, believed, and accompanied along the proper road of redress; the church must not smooth things over, nor use "keeping the peace" to press down on the harmed person. Second, concerning anger: righteous anger may exist, but private vengeance may not; the one inch by which anger crosses its boundary is the inch by which we pass from victim to wrongdoer, and it must be dealt with before God that very day, not allowed to stay overnight and take root. Third, concerning the vigilance of parents and leaders: when something happens to a child, where the father is and where the leader is will decide where the whole family goes next; silence and shrewdness may seem to preserve safety, but in fact they push the family toward loss of control — fathers must learn early to stand up at the first moment, on the side of the weak and on the side of right and wrong, even if it costs. Fourth, concerning God's name: the nations are watching our every word and deed, and anger and deceit wrong not only people but God's name even more; for the sake of God's name, we practice justice, and we also restrain ourselves from practicing injustice. When these four things are done, "it ought not to be done" is no longer only a verdict spoken at Shechem three thousand years ago, but the rule of our household and our church today.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

From beginning to end, this chapter has only one sentence of Scripture fit to be its title — the very verdict the sons themselves heard, and the verdict the Bible pronounces: in Israel, such a thing ought not to be done. Shechem ought not to have done this — no seizing, no defiling. The sons ought not to have done this — no deceit, no indiscriminate killing, no plundering. Jacob ought not to have done this — no silence, and no replacing right and wrong with advantage. If one person's sin is not handled by anyone with justice, it will snowball into the whole family's sin; if anger is restrained by no one, it will snowball from seeking justice into a new injustice. May we remember: justice is God's, and vengeance is God's; our duty is to stand on the victim's side, to act in the light, and to entrust our anger to Him.

Let us pray together: Righteous Lord, You saw Dinah, and You also see those today who are still oppressed, harmed, and unable to speak. Make us hate sin as You hate sin, yet not let anger take root and reign in us; make parents and leaders silent no longer, guarding the weak at the first moment; make our hands clean, unstained by deceit and unstained by excessive revenge; let Your name suffer no loss on us, but rather be glorified. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] nevelah: Hebrew נְבָלָה (H5039), a disgraceful deed, a shameful thing, an outrage, denoting an act that gravely violates the sacred and moral order; Genesis 34:7, "he had wrought folly (nevelah) in Israel." Compare Deuteronomy 22:21, Joshua 7:15, and 2 Samuel 13:12 (the matter of Amnon and Tamar, where Tamar likewise says such a thing ought not to be done), which use the same word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the Bible's characterization of the violation is a disgraceful deed, not love and not a mistake" is a scriptural fact based on this word; "God's pen stands on the victim's side" is homiletical exposition, stated separately from the fact.

[2] 'anah: Hebrew עָנָה (H6031), to bring low, afflict, humiliate, defile; Genesis 34:2, "and lay with her, and defiled ('anah) her." This word is also used in the Old Testament for the Egyptians' affliction of the Israelites (Genesis 15:13) and for Sarai's harsh treatment of Hagar (Genesis 16:6), in contexts of oppression; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's observation that the word carries the sense of compulsion and humiliation, and is not a word of mutual consent, is a fact of the word's meaning.

[3] mohar: Hebrew מֹהַר (H4119), a bride price or dowry, the property a bridegroom gave to the bride's father's household when taking a wife; Genesis 34:12, Shechem said, "Ask me never so much dowry (mohar) and gift, and I will give according as ye shall say unto me." Compare Exodus 22:16-17 and 1 Samuel 18:25 (Saul demanding a dowry of David); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "Shechem signed a blank check, seeking to level out the harm with property" is narrative analysis based on the dialogue in the text, and is homiletical exposition.

[4] ba'ash: Hebrew בָּאַשׁ (H887), to stink, to become loathsome, to make oneself odious; Genesis 34:30, Jacob said, "Ye have troubled me to make me to stink among the inhabitants of the land," the original sense being "you have made me stink among..." Compare Exodus 5:21, where Moses and Aaron were made odious before Pharaoh, using the same word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "Jacob reckoned safety and name first, and right and wrong afterward" is a homiletical judgment based on the original of Genesis 34:30 read against the whole chapter, and is identified as exposition.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Dinah the daughter of Leah, which she bare unto Jacob, went out to see the daughters of the land. And when Shechem the son of Hamor the Hivite, prince of the country, saw her, he took her, and lay with her, and defiled her. ... And Shechem spake unto his father Hamor, saying, Get me this damsel to wife." (Genesis 34:1-2, 4, excerpts, KJV)

Dinah does not speak a single word in this chapter. One man seizes her, others bargain over her, others take up the sword in her name, and others reckon their own safety by her story — everyone talks about her, and no one asks her. Reading this chapter calls for a quiet heart first: God recorded it because He remembers the silent one. If today you carry a hurt you cannot put into words — wronged, used, treated as a condition and a price — hear first how God names that thing: a disgraceful deed, a defilement. He does not whitewash it, and He does not shift the blame onto the victim. The anger is real, and the grievance is real, but leave the hand of vengeance to God: "Vengeance is mine; I will repay." Justice seized through a greater injustice only adds another wound in the end. Bring your wound before God. He sees, He does not despise it, and in His righteousness and His time He will speak for the oppressed. May those who read Scripture today also learn this lesson: anger may be poured out to God, and grievance may be laid plainly before God, but do not draw the sword yourself. To restrain anger is not injustice; it is leaving justice to the Lord who truly judges righteously. And if you are one who accompanies a victim, stand firm beside her, walk with her through the road that must be walked, and wait with her for God — what the silent one needs most is not blame, and not to be used, but someone at her side who is, on God's behalf, a pair of caring eyes.

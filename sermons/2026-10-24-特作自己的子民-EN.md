# A Special People unto Himself — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 7

> Status: First translation, pending proofreading (初译待校). English version translated in full from the finalized Chinese manuscript "特作自己的子民" (Deuteronomy 7; Chinese text reviewed and finalized, ZH strong re-check, third Deuteronomy batch, 2026-10-25). Scripture quotations in this English version are taken verbatim from the King James Version (KJV) anchor text of the whole chapter, not translated from the Chinese. See the Production Record at the end of this manuscript.
>
> Title verification: Five candidates were considered — (1) "A Special People unto Himself," from verse 6, "hath chosen thee to be a special people unto himself"; (2) "Not Because Ye Were More in Number," from the negative sentence giving the reason for the election in verse 7, which covers only the election section; (3) "But Because the Lord Loved You," from the positive sentence giving the reason for the election in verse 8, which covers only the election section; (4) "By Little and Little," from the word of process in verse 22, which covers only the comfort section; and (5) "Neither Shalt Thou Bring into Thine House," from the warning within the house at the chapter's end in verse 26, which covers only the closing section. Candidate (1) is adopted: the chapter's twenty-six verses fall into two halves — the first half is the command of separation (vv. 1–16), the second half the comfort for the time of fear (vv. 17–26) — and the commands of the one half and the comforts of the other both hang on the same sentence: thou art an holy people unto the Lord thy God; he hath chosen thee to be a special people unto himself. The command stands because of this identity; the comfort is issued because of this identity. English title as settled: A Special People unto Himself (from KJV 7:6, "a special people unto himself").

## Main Body

Introduction (00:00–05:00). [Fact layer] Deuteronomy 6 lays down the general outline of the faith — hearken, love, keep it in the heart; chapter 7 at once carries that outline forward to the first scene after the entry into the land: seven nations stand before them. The chapter has twenty-six verses; its structure is registered first: the command of separation, vv. 1–5 (5 verses); the ground of the election, vv. 6–11 (6 verses); the blessing of hearkening, vv. 12–16 (5 verses); the comfort for the time of fear, vv. 17–24 (8 verses); the warning within the house, vv. 25–26 (2 verses) — twenty-six verses in all (Endnote 1). In the base text the discourse is divided into three quoted blocks: vv. 1–11, vv. 12–16, and vv. 17–26 (Endnote 2) — command, promise, comfort: precisely three blocks. [Theological-inference layer] The relation of chapter 6 to chapter 7 is the relation of identity to action: chapter 6 says "the Lord our God is one Lord"; chapter 7 says how the people of the one Lord conduct themselves among all peoples. Before every severe command of this chapter there stands a sentence of identity (v. 6); after every sentence of comfort there follows an act of remembering (v. 18). First there is "a special people unto himself"; only then does the rest come into question. [Summary layer] The governing sentence of this chapter can be put this way: east of the Jordan Moses speaks two things to a people fewest in number — you are chosen, therefore be separate; you are loved, therefore do not be afraid. The separation is not because you are strong; the election is not because you are many; the whole chapter's strength flows out of the one sentence, "But because the Lord loved you."

### I. Seven Nations Before You: The Command of Separation (7:1–5; 05:00–16:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 1, quoted in full: "When the Lord thy God shall bring thee into the land whither thou goest to possess it, and hath cast out many nations before thee, the Hittites, and the Girgashites, and the Amorites, and the Canaanites, and the Perizzites, and the Hivites, and the Jebusites, seven nations greater and mightier than thou;" Items registered one by one: first, the subject of the action of entering the land is God — both "shall bring thee into" and "hath cast out" (nāšal) have God as subject; Israel is the one who is brought in. Second, the list of the seven peoples is given in full: the Hittites, and the Girgashites, and the Amorites, and the Canaanites, and the Perizzites, and the Hivites, and the Jebusites — a full count of seven peoples (Endnote 3). Third, the base text's whole verdict on the seven peoples is one sentence: "greater and mightier than thou" — the original has two words (many, mighty) combined in the verdict, and the chapter's comparison of strength is set from its first sentence: the other side has the advantage in both number and power. [Fact layer] Verse 2, quoted in full: "And when the Lord thy God shall deliver them before thee; thou shalt smite them, and utterly destroy them; thou shalt make no covenant with them, nor shew mercy unto them:" Items registered one by one: first, "shall deliver them before thee" — the subject of the delivering (nātan) is still God; the smiting (nākâ) is the people's action; the order is registered. Second, the original of "utterly destroy them" is an intensified construction (see headnote, item 2); its tone is the heaviest among this chapter's commands. Third, two prohibitions: make no covenant (bərît) with them, and shew no mercy (ḥānan) unto them — the former forbids a relation of political and religious alliance; the latter forbids a sparing that goes under the name of mercy. [Fact layer] Verses 3–4, quoted in full: "Neither shalt thou make marriages with them; thy daughter thou shalt not give unto his son, nor his daughter shalt thou take unto thy son. / For they will turn away thy son from following me, that they may serve other gods: so will the anger of the Lord be kindled against you, and destroy thee suddenly." Items registered one by one: first, the prohibition falls on marriage, and it is registered in both directions — giving a daughter and taking a daughter; both directions are forbidden. Second, the reason is stated expressly: the danger lies not in blood but in direction — "they will turn away thy son": marriage is here guarded against as a channel of a turning of the faith. Third, the verb of the consequence is "destroy": the word prepared for the seven peoples turns back and falls on a covenant-breaking Israel (Endnote 4) — the command of separation applies one and the same standard within and without; this is the coldest registration of this verse. [Fact layer] Verse 5, quoted in full: "But thus shall ye deal with them; ye shall destroy their altars, and break down their images, and cut down their groves, and burn their graven images with fire." Items registered one by one: first, four verbs answer to four objects: destroy — their altars; break down — their images; cut down — their groves (ʾăšērîm); burn with fire — their graven images (pesel); a one-to-one correspondence, with nothing left vague. Second, this verse is a near-verbatim restatement of this chapter's parallel text, Exodus 34:13 (that verse was checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:2: "But ye shall destroy their altars, break their images, and cut down their groves:"); the sameness of form of the command in the two places is registered on the record. Third, note where the command points: the severity of vv. 1–5 closes, at the end of this section, upon "things" — altars, images, idols: the material carriers of a turning of the faith. [Fact layer] This section's parallel text stands in the closing stretch of Exodus: Exodus 34:12 warns against making a covenant with the inhabitants of the land whither thou goest, lest it become a snare in the midst (its verse position and purport checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:2), and 34:15–16 writes making a covenant, eating of their sacrifice, and taking of their daughters for one's sons as one connected chain (registered as reported paraphrase) — in this section Moses is not inventing a new command; he is reciting, word for word, the old covenant clauses from below Mount Sinai, east of the Jordan on the eve of the entry; registered on the record. [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: first, the logic of this section's commands is not hatred but quarantine — the three prohibitions (covenant, sparing, intermarriage) seal three channels by which other gods enter: the alliance enters through politics, marriage enters through the family, idols enter through worship; what Moses guards against is not people but channels. Second, the same-word loop between verse 2 and verse 4 deserves a separate note: the command of utter destruction is, outwardly, judgement; inwardly, warning — the same God, the same standard; the elect identity is a bond of responsibility, not a certificate of exemption (in the same line as the logic of Amos 3:2, registered separately at the reading layer and the discussion layer). [New Testament reading layer] This section's echo in the New Testament is registered at the reading layer only: the stretch of 2 Corinthians 6, "Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers" (registered as reported paraphrase), shares vocabulary with this section's motifs of separation (be separate, be not unequally yoked, a holy people); its chain of citation runs mainly through Exodus 19 and Leviticus 26 and does not cite this chapter directly; this sermon registers a motif echo only and does not assert a relation of quotation. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] For the ethical evaluation of the extermination command (ḥērem), this sermon registers here only the outline of the several readings; the items are set side by side in the discussion layer of Section V. Three factual boundaries are set down first: first, the command's textual scope is the seven peoples, in their own land, in that period (the list of v. 1 and the "by little and little" of v. 22 are both within the text); second, within the same covenant, texts make other provisions of peace for peoples beyond the seven (Deut 20:10–15, registered as reported paraphrase); third, the motive this section's reason sentence (v. 4) gives for the command is a turning of the faith, not racial superiority — so much for the textual registration of the motive; the evaluating readings are put down below. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the seven nations are all greater and mightier than thou, yet the command is not vague at all: no covenant, no intermarriage, every idol broken down — the first lesson of separation is to see the channels clearly first.

### II. Chosen, Not Because You Were Many (7:6–11; 16:00–27:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 6 is the pivot of the whole chapter, quoted in full: "For thou art an holy people unto the Lord thy God: the Lord thy God hath chosen thee to be a special people unto himself, above all people that are upon the face of the earth." Items registered one by one: first, this verse opens with "For" (kî) — the reason for all the commands of vv. 1–5 is accounted for here: it is not first separation and then election; it is first election and therefore separation; the order is registered. Second, "an holy people" (ʿam qāḏôš): holiness here is first of all a word of belonging — what belongs to the Lord is called holy. Third, "hath chosen" (bāḥar, twice in this chapter, vv. 6, 7) together with "a special people unto himself" (səgullâ) — this form of words is of the same form as Exodus 19:5's being a peculiar treasure unto him above all people (that verse was checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:6); the identity sentence of the Sinai covenant is restated here. [Fact layer] Verses 7–8, quoted in full: "The Lord did not set his love upon you, nor choose you, because ye were more in number than any people; for ye were the fewest of all people: / But because the Lord loved you, and because he would keep the oath which he had sworn unto your fathers, hath the Lord brought you out with a mighty hand, and redeemed you out of the house of bondmen, from the hand of Pharaoh king of Egypt." Items registered one by one: first, the reason for the election is first negated and then affirmed: the negative sentence — "did not … because ye were more in number than any people," with a self-deprecating fact added, "for ye were the fewest of all people" (məʿaṭ); the affirmative sentence has only two items — "But because the Lord loved you" and "because he would keep the oath which he had sworn unto your fathers": love and oath are the whole of the reason this chapter gives, and there is no third item (such as righteousness, such as strength, such as number). Second, the two words for love are different words: verse 7 has the verb ḥāšaq (set his love upon, clinging affection), verse 8 has love in a noun form ("because the Lord loved you") — the former speaks of the singleness of God's clinging affection, the latter sets that love up as the title under which all that follows is done; the two are accounted separately in Endnote 5. Third, the election is at once proved by the exodus: a mighty hand, the house of bondmen, redeemed (pāḏâ, to redeem, H6299), the hand of Pharaoh — the election is no abstract proposition; within eight verses it lands inside a stretch of history. [Fact layer] Verses 9–10, quoted in full: "Know therefore that the Lord thy God, he is God, the faithful God, which keepeth covenant and mercy with them that love him and keep his commandments to a thousand generations; / And repayeth them that hate him to their face, to destroy them: he will not be slack to him that hateth him, he will repay him to his face." Items registered one by one: first, this chapter's way of naming God stands here: the faithful God (hāʾēl hanneʾĕmān) — faithfulness is this chapter's theological anchor. Second, the range of keeping covenant and mercy (šāmar together with ḥeseḏ) is "to a thousand generations," and its object is limited to "them that love him and keep his commandments." Third, the repaying side is worded with extreme weight: "to their face," "he will not be slack" — the two sides stand side by side: mercy is reckoned in thousand generations, repaying arrives face to face; the asymmetry of the numbers is itself a textual fact on record (of the same form as the three and four generations against a thousand at Exod 20:5–6 and Deut 5:9–10, verified in those sermons and registered here as cross-reference). [Fact layer] Verse 11, quoted in full: "Thou shalt therefore keep the commandments, and the statutes, and the judgments, which I command thee this day, to do them." Items registered one by one: first, this section closes with a second "therefore" (vv. 9 and 11 both open with the "therefore" form) — from knowing to doing is this section's inner line of movement. Second, the three names in a row, "the commandments, and the statutes, and the judgments," are of the same form as at 6:1 (the differences among the three forms are registered in Endnote 3 of that sermon). Third, the word "this day" draws the ancient history of the election back to the scene of the discourse: the election belongs to the fathers' age; the doing belongs to today. [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: first, this section is the whole book of Deuteronomy's only positive answer to "why Israel," and the structure of its answer deserves to be recorded word by word: not because of you (the negation of v. 7); only because he loved, only because he keeps his oath (the affirmation of v. 8) — the ground of the election lies wholly on God's side: his love is a love without a why, his oath a covenant by which he binds himself; on Israel's side the only thing that can be produced is "the fewest." Second, read the first section again from here: the severity of the command of separation matches precisely the thickness of the grace of the election — a people made a special possession (səgullâ, a treasure in a king's private store) cannot consort casually with the altars of the seven peoples; the more precious the identity, the more thorough the separation. Third, the "Know" of verse 9 is an imperative: faithfulness is not a feeling; it is a fact to be known and acknowledged; this chapter's way of comforting fear (Section IV) is precisely to turn this "knowing" into a discipline of remembering. [New Testament reading layer] This section's citations and echoes in the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only; it is the section of the chapter with the densest echoes: 1 Peter 2:9, "But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people" (checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:6) — the three words "a chosen generation," "holy," and "a peculiar people" stand in one chain with verse 6 of this section and Exodus 19:5–6, and the New Testament extends the application of the Sinai identity sentence to the church; registered on the record. Titus 2:14, "and purify unto himself a peculiar people, zealous of good works" (checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:6) — "a peculiar people" is of exactly the same form as the base text of verse 6, and is the most direct single echo of this chapter's vocabulary in the New Testament. Romans 9's argument on election, "not of works, but of him that calleth" (Rom 9:11; its verse position checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:7), shares the motif of this section's "not because of you" and is registered as a motif echo. All three are registered only, without reading them back as this chapter's original meaning. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters set side by side. First, the dispute over election and condition: verses 6–8 of this chapter rest the election wholly on God's love and oath, while vv. 9–11 immediately follow with keeping the commandments as the conditional sentence for obtaining mercy — the readings of the two stretches placed side by side are set side by side: (a) the unconditional reading: the election comes first and is irrevocable, and the condition of v. 9 concerns the enjoyment of blessing within the covenant, not the gaining or losing of the identity; (b) the covenant-conditional reading: identity and blessing alike are maintained within the obedience of the covenant, and the covenant-breaker brings upon himself the repaying of vv. 4 and 10; (c) the narrative reading: the two stretches belong to two moments of the discourse (recalling grace, and the parting charge), and the tension is the rhetoric of the discourse form itself and need not be systematized. The three readings stand side by side; this sermon gives no verdict. Second, the registration of səgullâ's context: in ancient Near Eastern treaty and royal documents this word denotes a king's private treasured property (registered from scholarship); the same word is used in five places — Exod 19:5, v. 6 of this chapter, Deut 14:2, 26:18, and Mal 3:17 (the verse positions and near-verbatim forms of 14:2 and 26:19 were checked directly in the cross-reference column) — the word's derivation is a textual fact; its theological extension belongs to the inference and reading layers. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the weightiest words of the whole chapter are not on the battlefield but in this section: he chose you not because you were many, but because he loved you; he keeps covenant to a thousand generations, therefore you shall keep and do — grace first, responsibility hard behind it: this is Moses' definition of election.

### III. The Blessing of Hearkening, and the Eye That Must Have No Pity (7:12–16; 27:00–36:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 12, quoted in full: "Wherefore it shall come to pass, if ye hearken to these judgments, and keep, and do them, that the Lord thy God shall keep unto thee the covenant and the mercy which he sware unto thy fathers:" Items registered one by one: first, this verse opens the quotation of the discourse's second block (vv. 12–16 are the second quoted block). Second, the sentence form is a positive conditional–promise pair: on man's side three verbs, "hearken, and keep, and do them"; on God's side two verbs, "shall keep … the covenant and the mercy" — of the same form as v. 9 (keeping covenant and mercy, twice in this chapter, Endnote 6); man's verbs are more than God's; the asymmetry is registered. Third, the promise's warrant is still "which he sware unto thy fathers" (the wording of the oath to the fathers occurs three times in this chapter: vv. 8, 12, 13). [Fact layer] The "these judgments" of verse 12 have a concrete referent: what is hearkened to is not a general loyalty but judgments (mišpāṭîm) that can be carried out and examined — the list of blessings is tied to concrete doing; Deuteronomy 28:1 opens the whole chapter of blessing and cursing with "hearken diligently … to observe and to do" (its verse position checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:12) and is of the same form as this verse; registered as cross-reference. [Fact layer] Verses 13–14, quoted in full: "And he will love thee, and bless thee, and multiply thee: he will also bless the fruit of thy womb, and the fruit of thy land, thy corn, and thy wine, and thine oil, the increase of thy kine, and the flocks of thy sheep, in the land which he sware unto thy fathers to give thee. / Thou shalt be blessed above all people: there shall not be male or female barren among you, or among your cattle." Items registered one by one: first, the promise falls into three faces: number (multiply thee), land (corn, wine, and oil — the threefold form of Canaan's produce, appearing as a set for the first time in this chapter, registered on the record), and livestock (the increase of thy kine, and the flocks of thy sheep — in the original, what the herd and the flock produce; see headnote, item 8). Second, "Thou shalt be blessed above all people" — the seven nations of the first section, of whom it was said "greater and mightier than thou," become here the "all people" above whom the blessed one stands; the comparison of strength and weakness completes its reversal within this chapter, and the pivot of the reversal is the hearkening of v. 12. Third, the blessing of v. 14 is concrete to the point of fertility: male and female, and cattle, are named together — in the ancient world fertility is continuance, and this verse enters continuance in the covenant's list of blessings. [Fact layer] Verse 15, quoted in full: "And the Lord will take away from thee all sickness, and will put none of the evil diseases of Egypt, which thou knowest, upon thee; but will lay them upon all them that hate thee." Items registered one by one: first, "the evil diseases of Egypt, which thou knowest" — sickness is here named by the historical memory of the exodus: Egypt's hardships were experienced by the people themselves and need no explanation. Second, the direction of the transfer is registered: away from thee, laid upon them that hate thee — read against the "them that hate him" of v. 10: in this chapter's sentences of repaying, those who hate God and those who hate his people stand in one list (śānēʾ is the same word in both verses; the headnote's list is on record). Third, this verse is this chapter's only promise concerning the body and sickness; its scope is a corporate promise within the covenant; the readings are put down to the discussion layer and it is not read here as a guarantee of personal health. [Fact layer] Verse 16, quoted in full: "And thou shalt consume all the people which the Lord thy God shall deliver thee; thine eye shall have no pity upon them: neither shalt thou serve their gods; for that will be a snare unto thee." Items registered one by one: first, this verse is the last verse of the discourse's second block (the quotation opened at v. 12); in the base text no closing quotation mark is set at the end of this verse (the opening and closing of the quotation follow the base text's punctuation as recorded; Endnote 2). Second, the first section's two prohibitions are taken up again here: consume (taking up v. 2) and snare (announcing in advance the snare account of vv. 25–26). Third, "thine eye shall have no pity upon them" — the construction of eye (ʿên) plus have pity (ḥûs) is the only psychological description among this chapter's commands: the danger lies not only in the hand (covenant, intermarriage) but also in the eye (pity, lingering attachment). Fourth, this verse gives the general reason sentence for the whole chapter's commands: "for that will be a snare unto thee" — the end of serving other gods is a snare (môqēš); the severity of the command is, at this point, written out in plain causal form together with the reality of the trap. [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: first, read v. 12's conditional sentence side by side with the unconditional election of Section II: the election is unconditional (vv. 7–8), the covenant blessing is conditional (v. 12) — the identity is freely given; the blessing is experienced within obedience; Moses does not permit anyone to use v. 6 as a substitute for v. 12. Second, v. 16 moves the battlefield of separation from the altar into the eye: in this chapter idolatry never begins with kneeling; it begins with "having pity" — first one cannot bear to part with it, then one leaves it standing, and at last one serves it; the snare is not snapped shut suddenly; the eye softens first. [New Testament reading layer] This section's echoes in the New Testament are registered briefly: John's teaching that the one who loves him keeps his word and the order of hearkening and being loved of this section's v. 12 belong to a motif echo (registered as reported paraphrase); how the material covenant blessings of vv. 13–15 are taken up and transformed in the New Testament (as in the distinction of 1 Tim 4:8, "bodily exercise profiteth little," registered as reported paraphrase) is a long topic in the church's history of interpretation; this sermon registers its existence only and does not develop the reading here. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters set side by side. First, the readings of the materiality of the covenant blessings set side by side: (a) the literal-historical reading: the list of blessings is the Moses covenant's corporate promise to that generation and that land, and its efficacy is tied to the covenant's continuance; (b) the creation-order reading: fertility, land, and health are a list of God's common grace, which the covenant only brings into the open; (c) the eschatological-type reading: the list of blessings points to the abundance of the new creation, of which this age tastes only the firstfruits. The three readings stand side by side, without verdict; the one thing that can be fixed is the textual fact: the list of blessings is corporate, landed, and conditional (v. 12). Second, the readings of v. 15's sentence of the transfer of evil diseases set side by side: (a) the covenant-requital reading: it belongs, with v. 10, to the covenant's structure of curse and blessing set in contrast; (b) the rhetorical-contrast reading: the emphasis lies on the protective face of "will take away from thee," and the transfer wording is the foil. The two readings stand side by side. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the one who hearkens has a list of blessings: number, land, livestock, body — everything is in it; but this section's edge is in its last sentence: thine eye shall have no pity — between blessing and snare there stands exactly whether or not you can bear to part with it.

### IV. If Thou Shalt Say in Thine Heart … Be Not Afraid (7:17–24; 36:00–49:00)

[Fact layer] Verses 17–18, quoted in full: "If thou shalt say in thine heart, These nations are more than I; how can I dispossess them? / Thou shalt not be afraid of them: but shalt well remember what the Lord thy God did unto Pharaoh, and unto all Egypt;" Items registered one by one: first, this section opens the discourse's third quoted block (vv. 17–26). Second, Moses here rehearses in advance the monologue in his hearers' hearts and quotes it word for word — "These nations are more than I" answers from afar to v. 1's "greater and mightier than thou": v. 1 is God's settled verdict on the enemy's state; v. 17 is the human heart's restatement of the enemy's state; the same fact, once it has entered the heart, becomes fear. Third, the prescription is two commands: be not afraid (yārēʾ) and well remember (zākar) — remembering is the antidote to fear, and this pairing is this section's structural axis (Endnote 8). [Fact layer] Verse 19, quoted in full: "The great temptations which thine eyes saw, and the signs, and the wonders, and the mighty hand, and the stretched out arm, whereby the Lord thy God brought thee out: so shall the Lord thy God do unto all the people of whom thou art afraid." Items registered one by one: first, the contents of the remembering are given in full: the great temptations, the signs (ʾōt), the wonders (môpēṯ), the mighty hand (ḥăzāqâ, of the same form as in v. 8), and the stretched out arm (zərôaʿ) — all five from the exodus store, in one chain with the sign lists registered at Deut 4:34 and 6:22 (verified in those sermons). Second, "which thine eyes saw" — the warrant of the remembering is eyewitness, not hearsay; this generation are witnesses. Third, the promise's inference sentence is written expressly: "so shall the Lord thy God do unto all the people of whom thou art afraid" — as it was in the past, so shall it be in the future; the logic of this chapter's comfort is a line of analogy: from verified history, infer the unseen future. [Fact layer] Verse 20, quoted in full: "Moreover the Lord thy God will send the hornet among them, until they that are left, and hide themselves from thee, be destroyed." Items registered one by one: first, the subject of "will send" is still God. Second, the hornet (ṣirʿâ) is sent before and amid the people's own action, and its object is "they that are left, and hide themselves from thee" — before the battle is even finished, those who have hidden have nowhere to hide. Third, this verse, together with the hornet announcement of Exod 23:28 (that verse checked directly in the cross-reference column: "And I will send hornets before thee, which shall drive out the Hivite, the Canaanite, and the Hittite, from before thee.") and the retrospect of Josh 24:12 ("And I sent the hornet before you, which drave them out from before you, even the two kings of the Amorites," checked directly in the cross-reference column), forms a three-stretch chain of announcement — promise — retrospect; registered on the record; the dispute over what the hornet denotes is put down to the discussion layer. [Fact layer] Verse 21, quoted in full: "Thou shalt not be affrighted at them: for the Lord thy God is among you, a mighty God and terrible." Items registered one by one: first, this is the second command of the be-not-afraid class (be affrighted, ʿāraṣ). Second, the reason sentence is a sentence of space: "is among you" — the comfort does not rest on denying that the enemy is strong, but on identifying the presence: the one among the people is that mighty and terrible God (gāḏôl says both "great/mighty" and "terrible"; the base text renders both). Third, this verse and the question and answer of vv. 17–18 form a closed loop: the heart says the enemy is strong; the answer does not rebut the enemy's strength; it only states God's position clearly. [Fact layer] Verse 22, quoted in full: "And the Lord thy God will put out those nations before thee by little and little: thou mayest not consume them at once, lest the beasts of the field increase upon thee." Items registered one by one: first, "by little and little" (məʿaṭ məʿaṭ, a little, a little) is the most unexpected word of the whole chapter's comfort section — the speed of the driving out is set by God, and it is slow with a reason. Second, the reason sentence is unexpectedly ecological: "lest the beasts of the field increase upon thee" — if the land empties too fast, the people cannot hold it. Third, this verse and the parallel of Exod 23:29–30 (registered as reported paraphrase: "I will not drive them out … in one year," "lest the land become desolate," "by little and little I will drive them out") are of the same form: the slowness of the process has an express precedent in the Torah; it is not indulgence but design. [Fact layer] Verses 23–24, quoted in full: "But the Lord thy God shall deliver them unto thee, and shall destroy them with a mighty destruction, until they be destroyed. / And he shall deliver their kings into thine hand, and thou shalt destroy their name from under heaven: there shall no man be able to stand before thee, until thou have destroyed them." Items registered one by one: first, "deliver" occurs twice in this stretch (in this chapter nātan's delivering of the enemy to you occurs four times in all: vv. 2, 16, 23, 24; Endnote 9). Second, "shall destroy them with a mighty destruction" (məhûmâ gəḏōlâ) — the destruction goes before, inside the enemy's camp, and the outcome is divided before the engagement; and this word has a precedent registered in the Torah: Exodus 23:27 already announced that God would discomfit all the people to whom Israel would come (its verse position checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:2; registered as reported paraphrase); this chapter's destruction is the due restatement of an old promise. Third, the object of v. 24 rises to kings (melek, plural): what is destroyed is their "name" (šēm) — a name destroyed from under heaven means that their rule and their memory end together. Fourth, "there shall no man be able to stand before thee" is the peak of this section's comfort: from the heart's saying in v. 17, "how can I dispossess them?" to v. 24's "no man be able to stand," question and answer meet head to tail. [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: first, Moses' whole method of handling fear can be recorded from this section: he does not deny it (v. 17 first speaks the fear aloud), he does not cheer them on (there is not one sentence of "you can do it"), he only has them remember (vv. 18–19: naming, item by item, the history their eyes had seen), and then re-locates them (v. 21: God is among you). In this chapter the opposite of fear is not courage but memory. Second, the "by little and little" of v. 22 is a theology for the impatient: God does things at a slowness that does not suit man's wishes, and inside the slowness there is care (lest the beasts harm you) — man sees only that the process is slow; the text writes the slowness's reason out expressly: it is not that God lacks power; it is that he has reckoned even the beasts' account into your safety. Third, read vv. 19 and 22 together and there is a tension: the hand is "mighty," yet the process is "by little and little"; the mighty hand guarantees the outcome, the little-and-little prescribes the road; believe both sentences, and you have this chapter's comfort. [New Testament reading layer] This section's echoes in the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only: the remembering form of Hebrews 11's discourse on faith (naming the exodus matters item by item, registered as reported paraphrase) shares the motif of this section's "remember — fear not" structure; and the New Testament's taking up of "God among you" at the Immanuel line (Matt 1:23, registered as reported paraphrase). Both are registered as motifs only, without asserting a relation of quotation. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three matters set side by side. First, the readings of the hornet set side by side: (a) the material reading: there really were hornets (or swarms of large hornets) troubling the enemy into disaster, in the same line as the insect plagues among Egypt's ten plagues, the plague of flies and the plague of lice; (b) the symbolic reading: the hornet denotes terror itself — the enemy's army disordered before battle (cross-referred with the "destruction" of v. 23); (c) the military-emblem reading: it denotes the emblem or vanguard of Egypt's army (in ancient Egypt the bee was a symbol of Lower Egyptian kingship; scholarship has this proposal, registered as a proposal); in addition, the 2010 edition renders this word "pestilence" and adds a note, and that rendering itself becomes a further reading. The four proposals stand side by side, without verdict; the only thing that can be fixed is the textual chain: the announcement (Exod 23:28), this verse's restatement, and the retrospect (Josh 24:12) all use the same word. Second, the surface tension between "by little and little" and the conquest narrative: Joshua 10–11 has summary narration of the "all the land taken, none remaining" kind, while Judges 1 lists many peoples remaining — the readings set side by side: (a) the summary-form reading: Joshua's summaries are the triumphal wording of the campaign layer, Judges the factual wording of the settlement layer; the two forms stand side by side; (b) the process reading: the "by little and little" of this chapter's v. 22 is precisely the key of explanation between the two books; the conquest was in itself a process of generations; (c) the theological reading: the peoples left remaining are used in Judges as instruments of testing and discipline (Judg 2:21–3:4, registered as reported paraphrase), annotating this chapter's "snare" warning in return. The three readings stand side by side, not harmonized, not adjudicated. Third, the readings of the extermination command's history of execution (joining the registration of the first section): (a) the full-execution reading; (b) the partial-execution reading (taking Judges' facts as the standard); (c) the ancient-war-rhetoric reading: the language of extermination is the conventional hyperbolic form of ancient Near Eastern war documents (the Moabite king Mesha's inscription likewise uses ḥrm to record the extermination of an enemy city; registered from scholarship), and the biblical text is written within the same genre tradition; the amplitude of its historical reference is to be discussed separately by archaeology and text together. The three readings stand side by side. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: if you are afraid, speak the fear aloud; having spoken it, count, item by item, the things God has done; having counted them, you will know — his hand is mighty, his steps are by little and little, and he is among you.

### V. The Silver and the Gold, and the Cursed Thing (7:25–26; 49:00–57:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 25, quoted in full: "The graven images of their gods shall ye burn with fire: thou shalt not desire the silver or gold that is on them, nor take it unto thee, lest thou be snared therein: for it is an abomination to the Lord thy God." Items registered one by one: first, the command to burn the idols is restated here (v. 5 is its first occurrence; the burning wording occurs twice in this chapter, Endnote 10). Second, what this verse adds is the silver-and-gold clause: the image can be burned; the silver and gold cannot be burned away — the text foresees the difficulty of the disposal and sets down two express prohibitions: thou shalt not desire (ḥāmad) it, nor take it unto thee. Third, "lest thou be snared therein" interlocks with v. 16's "will be a snare unto thee": the snare's first stretch is serving (v. 16), its second stretch is desiring (v. 25) — the same net: it catches the desiring first, and the worshipping afterwards. Fourth, the reason sentence: abomination (of the tôʿēḇâ family) — the word by which the idol's evil is characterized in this chapter appears here, joining head to tail with the next verse's "an abomination." [Fact layer] Verse 26, quoted in full: "Neither shalt thou bring an abomination into thine house, lest thou be a cursed thing like it: but thou shalt utterly detest it, and thou shalt utterly abhor it; for it is a cursed thing." Items registered one by one: first, the chapter's last command advances the battlefield to the doorway of the house: "bring … into thine house" — this chapter's line of space here completes its whole course: from the land (v. 1) to the altar (v. 5), to the eye (v. 16), to the house (v. 26). Second, "lest thou be a cursed thing" (ḥērem) — the ḥāram command of v. 2, directed outward, returns here as a noun: the person who touches a devoted thing enters the category of ḥērem himself; the chapter's heaviest word has a command at one end and a warning at the other, forming a ring head to tail (Endnote 11). Third, two commands of feeling, both in intensified constructions: "utterly detest (šāqaṣ) it," "utterly abhor (tāʿaḇ) it" — here the object of the detesting is registered clearly: it is directed at the thing, not at persons. Fourth, the semicolon (in the base text) stands after "into thine house" (see headnote, item 12); the chapter closes with "for it is a cursed thing" as the whole chapter's last sentence — a reason sentence closing the chapter, answering from afar to the reason sentence that opened the section at v. 6, "For thou art…" [Theological-inference layer] The whole chapter's inferences are gathered up here: first, this chapter's severity is never expansive outward; it is contractive inward: the seven peoples' altars are broken down on the ground; the abominable thing is guarded against inside the house; the greatest danger lies not in the seven nations' might, but in what one's own doorway admits. Second, v. 25's silver-and-gold clause is the chapter's most "modern" sentence: the idol survives in the form of precious metal — however thoroughly the extermination is carried out, if the silver and gold from the image are taken into the storehouse, the snare goes on circulating in another currency; the last mile of separation is whether the account is reckoned clean. Third, read together with the first section: v. 4 says the covenant-breaker brings destruction on himself; v. 26 says the one who draws near the abominable thing becomes a cursed thing — this chapter's logic of judgement is consistent throughout: ḥērem is not an ethnic label but a relational label: to the degree that one is joined to the abominable thing, to that degree one enters its category. [New Testament reading layer] This section's echoes are registered at the reading layer only: Joshua 7's narrative of Achan, who took silver, gold, and a garment from "the accursed thing" and brought the whole army into trouble at Ai (its wording "of the accursed thing" — the verse position of Josh 7:1 was checked directly in the cross-reference column of the per-verse page for 7:26) is this section's narrative echo within the Old Testament (strictly an Old Testament intertext, registered separately at the end of this layer); on the New Testament side, Colossians 3:5's "covetousness, which is idolatry" (registered as reported paraphrase) shares the motif of v. 25's silver-and-gold–idol equation; and the last sentence of 1 John, "keep yourselves from idols" (registered as reported paraphrase), answers to the whole chapter's separation motif. All are registered only, without reading back. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The ethical discussion of the extermination command is set out in full here (the boundaries set up in the preceding four sections flow together here): the outline of the several readings set side by side — (a) the historical-setting reading: the command belongs to the ancient Near Eastern context of war, and ḥērem is a war-and-sacrifice institution common to the region at the time (the Mesha inscription is external evidence); judgement must first be put back into its context, and it is not fitting to measure an ancient text directly by modern laws of war; (b) the judgement reading: the command's self-explanation within the text is the judgement of Canaan's iniquity (Gen 15:16, "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full"; the acts listed in Leviticus 18; registered as reported paraphrase), and the same standard turns back and falls on Israel itself (v. 4 of this chapter; the motif of the land vomiting out its inhabitants at Lev 18:24–28), which is not ethnic superiority; (c) the unique-event reading: the command is bound to a specific moment of redemption history, a specific land, and specific seven peoples, and its form is the boundary-drawing of the land covenant; it does not constitute a reproducible paradigm of war ethics; (d) the canonical-trajectory reading: within the same canon there are already exceptions and corrections — Rahab is preserved (Josh 6, registered as reported paraphrase), the Gibeonites make a covenant and are left (Josh 9, registered as reported paraphrase), Nineveh in the book of Jonah is pardoned, and indeed the New Testament's command to love one's enemies (Matt 5:44, registered as reported paraphrase) recasts the ethics toward outsiders; this chapter must be read within the whole trajectory and no verse may be extracted and used alone; (e) the literal-continuing-efficacy reading: a minority tradition holds that the command's ethical principle (absoluteness toward sin) remains in force beyond its period, while its objects of execution and its manner are determined by the covenant's stage. The four readings plus one stand side by side here; this sermon gives no verdict, and one boundary for application is registered: any reading that transplants this stretch of text as a basis for violence against any group today has no foothold in any of the four readings (a)–(d) above — (a) seals it by context, (b) seals it by the standard's inward turn, (c) seals it by the event's uniqueness, (d) seals it by the canonical trajectory; this is not this sermon adjudicating the ethics, but registering the boundary of conclusion the several readings hold in common. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the whole chapter's last sentence is not spoken to the seven nations; it is spoken to your doorway: do not bring the abominable thing into the house — the command of separation, walking to its end, walks into your living room and your ledger.

Conclusion (57:00–60:00). [Fact layer] The whole chapter rehearsed by its headings: the command (vv. 1–5) rests on the election (vv. 6–11); the promise (vv. 12–16) is tied to hearkening; the comfort (vv. 17–24) is built on remembering; the warning (vv. 25–26) lands in the house. [Theological-inference layer] If this chapter were allowed to leave only one sentence for next week's self, it would be vv. 7–8: not because of you; only because he loved you. Next time your heart counts the number of the enemies — the next time you count your own "fewest" — turn Moses' ledger over and reckon it his way: your worth has never lain in your number, but in his love and in his oath. A person loved like this can keep himself separate, and can also stand firm. [Summary layer] A special people unto himself — this is an identity, the root of the command, the source of the comfort, and also the boundary line of a household's going in and out of its door.

## Devotional Extract

Scripture (base text) — Deuteronomy 7:6, 9: "For thou art an holy people unto the Lord thy God: the Lord thy God hath chosen thee to be a special people unto himself, above all people that are upon the face of the earth." … "Know therefore that the Lord thy God, he is God, the faithful God, which keepeth covenant and mercy with them that love him and keep his commandments to a thousand generations;" (Here quoted in the KJV, the English version's text; the Chinese devotional's base-text verses are the same two verses.)

Reflection: We measure our own worth by counting, always: number, strength, results, applause. Deuteronomy 7 turns this ledger over completely. When God chose Israel, his first sentence struck out every reason man could produce: not because you were more in number — you were in fact the fewest of all people. You think you are loved because you are good enough, many enough, strong enough; Moses says the order is exactly the reverse: it is not that you first qualified and then he loved you; it is that he loved you first, and you then became his special people. That love carries no attached condition; it carries only one piece of historical evidence: with a mighty hand he brought a people out of the house of bondmen. So today, if you feel that you are too few, too weak, too insufficient, do not be in a hurry to count yourself first — count the things he has done. To know that he is the faithful God, keeping covenant and mercy to a thousand generations — this "knowing" is itself comfort. The ground of being loved is not on your side but on his; this is exactly why you can stand firm.

Prayer: Faithful Lord, thank You: You love us not because we are many, nor because we are good, but only because You are a God who keeps covenant and shews mercy. Teach us to reckon the account rightly: not counting our own lack, but counting Your works alone; and having been loved with such a love, make us also willing to be separate and willing to obey, as Your special people. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

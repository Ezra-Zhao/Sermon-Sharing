## Summary

At the end of chapter 24 Balaam went back to his place, and Balak also went his way (24:25) — the whole project of cursing had come to nothing. Chapter 25 opens with the battlefield moved: Israel abode in Shittim, and the people began to commit whoredom with the daughters of Moab, step by step until Israel joined himself unto Baalpeor (25:1-3). Not one of the enemy's curses had taken effect, but the joining within brought wrath and plague. God commanded Moses to hang the heads of the people against the sun, and Moses commanded the judges each to slay his men that were joined (25:4-5); and just as the whole congregation were weeping before the door of the tabernacle, one of the children of Israel brought a Midianitish woman unto his brethren in the sight of them all (25:6). Phinehas took a javelin and followed, and thrust both of them through, and the plague was stayed — and those that died in the plague were twenty and four thousand (25:7-9). Then God himself characterised that one thrust: Phinehas was zealous for God's sake and turned his wrath away; therefore, "Behold, I give unto him my covenant of peace," to be to him and to his seed after him the covenant of an everlasting priesthood (25:10-13). At the chapter's end the two dead are recorded by name and lineage (25:14-15), and God commands Moses to vex the Midianites — for they beguiled Israel with their wiles in the matter of Peor (25:16-18). The camp that the mountain could not break opened its own gate on the plain; what closed that gate was not a louder blessing, but a heart that grieved with God's own grief, and a covenant of peace delivered by God's own hand.

## Main Text

### 1. Abiding in Shittim: From Whoredom to Joining (Numbers 25:1-3) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) Verse 1 — the place and the opening, quoted in full as it stands in the base text: "And Israel abode in Shittim, and the people began to commit whoredom with the daughters of Moab." (25:1). Registered item by item: first, the verb is "abode" — not passing through, but settling down. In chapter 24 they were still encamped "according to their tribes," praised by Balaam (24:2, 5); in this chapter the same camp has moved into Shittim and settled. The word "abode" is itself no sin, yet the narrative hangs the whole chapter's calamity on this act of settling: once the camp stopped and settled, the boundary began to loosen. Second, the subject of the sin is at first "the people" — not a few individuals, but a climate at the level of the people; and the word "began" is registered: this is an act of beginning, and everything later in the chapter is the unfolding of this "began." Verse 2 — the second and third steps of the sin, quoted in full: "And they called the people unto the sacrifices of their gods: and the people did eat, and bowed down to their gods." (25:2). Three steps stand distinct: first they were "called" to the same table, then they "did eat" of the sacrifices, and at last they "bowed down" to their gods. Whoredom is the door, eating the sacrifices is the road, bowing down is the destination — the narrative's order is exact: the joining of the body first, the joining of the table in the middle, the joining of worship last. Verse 3 — the naming of the sin and God's reaction, quoted in full: "And Israel joined himself unto Baalpeor: and the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel." (25:3). Here the subject rises from "the people" to "Israel" — in verse 1 it was still the people's whoredom; by verse 3 it is the whole nation "joined" unto a god's name: a private sin that has walked its three steps has become a corporate breaking of covenant. The word "joined" occurs twice in this chapter (25:3, 5) and is the chapter's formal name for this sin: not a momentary slip, but a joining unto Baalpeor — as if fitted to the same yoke (stated at the Summary layer below). God's reaction is one sentence: his anger "was kindled." Set this beside chapter 24: three times Balaam sought to bring God to curse in anger, and not once did God comply; in this chapter Israel walks into the joining himself, and the anger needs no one to invite it — it is kindled of itself.

(Application) The lesson of Shittim is spoken first to everyone who has "won the battle." In chapters 22 to 24 Israel fought not one battle, the curses all came to nothing, and blessing was spoken three times — the safest three chapters in this book. Yet the moment those safest three chapters are turned over, the most dangerous chapter begins. The enemy's curses could not hurt them; their settling hurt them. The falls of churches and believers mostly do not happen when they are attacked; they happen after the attack has failed to get in, when they relax: what twenty-one altars built on the mountain by the enemy could not do, one meal, one table, one act of bowing did. The curse Balak's money could not buy, Israel delivered to his own door free of charge. This is not to say that rest is sinful — the camp is meant to settle; it is to say that the place of settling most needs watchfulness. The table after the victory deserves more guarding than the weapons on the battlefield: the weapons you will dodge; the dinner invitation you will not. If you ask where the line of defence should be drawn, this chapter's order gives the answer: do not wait until the bowing-down step to cry stop — the bowing begins with the eating, and the eating begins with being "called." On the chain of sin, every link is cheaper than the next: refusing an invitation card is a hundred times easier than refusing an act of worship.

(Application) Notice, too, the small word "called" in verse 2. The women called the people to come — the invitation itself is gentle, everyday, unthreatening: only come and eat a meal. Breaking covenant never begins with a loud crash; it begins with an invitation. What leads men away from God today is likewise rarely open persecution; it is mostly a gentle invitation: a dinner, a relationship, an atmosphere of "everybody does this." The step of eating the sacrifices deserves special weight: when Israel ate, they may not have intended to worship — they may have thought it mere sociability, politeness, doing as the place does. But the narrative accepts no such defence: they ate, and they came to bowing down. The table is never neutral ground: with whom you sit, and for whose sacrifices you lift your chopsticks, there your heart is joined. If the church would keep itself at Shittim, its lesson is not only to explain Baalpeor clearly from the pulpit, but to explain clearly in ordinary days which tables may not be sat at and which invitations may not be accepted. By the time a man has already bowed down, pastoral care has only gathering up the dead and weeping left to it — which is exactly the scene before the door of the tabernacle in the next section.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Whoredom" is zānâ (H2181), to commit fornication or prostitution, often used figuratively of unfaithfulness in worship; it is registered here under the base text's rendering. "Joined" is the passive participle form of ṣāmad (H6775), to be fastened, to be yoked together, as cattle under one yoke; Paul's metaphor of being unequally yoked with unbelievers belongs to the same imagery (registered at the reading layer). "Baal" is Baʿal (H1168); "Peor" is Pəʿôr (H6465), a place name to which a god's name is also attached. "Anger" is ḥārôn (H2740), fierce anger. "Shittim" is Šiṭṭîm (H7851); "Moab" is Môʾāḇ (H4124), its number verified in the sermon on Numbers 24. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing (for the method, see Endnote 5).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The location of Shittim and its relation to "Abelshittim" (Num 33:49, registered at the cross-reference layer: the camp ran from Bethjesimoth unto Abelshittim) — scholarship generally places it in the plains of Moab east of the Jordan, north-east of the Dead Sea, and that it is the same place as this chapter's "abode in Shittim" is not in dispute; it is registered here only. One further matter is stated without adjudication: the women of verse 1 are "the daughters of Moab," but the woman who enters the camp in verse 6 is a "Midianitish woman," and those commanded to be vexed in verse 17 are likewise the Midianites — how Moab and Midian overlap in this chapter (as confederates, as neighbouring peoples worshipping the same god, or as different narrative materials) is variously explained in the commentary tradition. This sermon registers only the places where each people appears, verse by verse as the base text gives them; it does not merge the two into one at this chapter's fact layer, nor does it seek reasons outside this chapter for the command of verse 17 (the sequel in chapter 31 is registered at the later-text layer).

(Fact) This section closes by registering a comparison within the chapter's own book: the Israel of chapter 24 encamps "according to their tribes" (24:2) — dwelling with order in it; the Israel of chapter 25 "abode in Shittim" (25:1) — dwelling with only a place left in it. The same tents, one chapter apart: seen from the mountain, how goodly; settled on the plain, beginning whoredom. The difference is not in the tents but in the object of the joining: in chapter 24 they were joined, without knowing it, to the covenant God; in chapter 25 they are joined to Baalpeor, without knowing the calamity. The narrative sets these two chapters side by side as the sharpest comparison in the whole book: a blessed standing cannot hold back covenant-breaking conduct, and hardly has the voice of blessing fallen before the voice of weeping rises.

### 2. Hung Up Against the Sun: Two Commands (Numbers 25:4-5) (10:00-20:00)

(Fact) Verse 4 — God's first command, quoted in full: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Take all the heads of the people, and hang them up before the LORD against the sun, that the fierce anger of the LORD may be turned away from Israel." (25:4). Registered item by item: the one commanded is Moses; the objects are "all the heads of the people" — not the people in general, but the foremost layer; the manner is "before the LORD against the sun" — openly, before God, under the sunlight, with not an inch of shadow; the purpose is "that the fierce anger of the LORD may be turned away from Israel" — punishment is expressly named here as the way by which anger is turned away, not venting, but the stopping of wrath. Verse 5 — Moses passes the command on, quoted in full: "And Moses said unto the judges of Israel, Slay ye every one his men that were joined unto Baalpeor." (25:5). Set side by side, the additions and omissions stand clear: God's command has as its objects "all the heads," Moses' command has as its objects every man "that were joined unto Baalpeor"; God's manner is "hang them up," Moses' manner is "slay"; the executor shifts from Moses himself to "the judges of Israel," and each one of them — "every one his men," division by division, each according to those under him. How the two commands relate (repetition, implementation, or adjustment), this sermon registers only by setting the texts side by side; the readings are kept at the discussion layer. Whether the punishment of verse 4 was carried out in full, and to what extent the command of verse 5 was carried out, this chapter gives no list and no numbers — the chapter's only number of persons is the dead of the plague in verse 9 (25:9). This sermon registers that blank only, and fills it with no extrapolation (Endnote 8).

(Application) The four words "against the sun" deserve a pause. The sin of those at the head cannot be settled in the dark. The heads are the face of the camp: as the people went back and forth with the daughters of Moab, those at the head either went along, or connived, or pretended not to see — the nation-wide joining of verse 3 did not begin with the people themselves; it began with nobody stopping it. God's command falls first on the heads, and the order itself is a sermon: judgment begins with the leaders, and openness begins under the sunlight. When the church today deals with sin, it should learn this order and this light: what is most to be feared is not that someone falls, but that the leadership rots first and then covers the rot with "family shame must not be spread abroad." This chapter does exactly the opposite: against the sun, before me. Sin that is covered will not turn away wrath; it will only ferment. Punishment laid out in the sunlight is the road by which wrath is turned away. If a leader loves his own reputation more than he loves God's wrath to be turned away, he is standing in the wrong place — the place of a head is, by its nature, the place between the people and God, the place that is judged first. A man who sits in that place enjoying honour only and undergoing no judgment will sooner or later be hung up under the sun for all to see: not because God is cruel, but because that is how the account of the place itself is reckoned.

(Application) Moses' distribution is also worth learning: he did not take a knife alone through the whole camp; he handed the judgment back to the judges — "every one his men," each governing his own portion, each slaying the joined men under himself. Authority is dispersed, responsibility assigned to persons. The two extremes most common when the church handles a problem are these: one leader takes everything on himself, wears himself out without finishing, and finishes covered in resentment; or responsibility belongs to everyone, which means it belongs to no one, and in the end nobody moves. This chapter gives a third road: the command issues from one place, the execution returns to every place; every judge must face, with his own hand, the sin of his own people in his own portion. That is hard — those to be slain are "his men," acquaintances, subordinates, perhaps friends. But precisely because it is hard, it shows the difference between a judge and a peacemaker: the peacemaker preserves everyone's face; the judge preserves the turning away of God's wrath. Everyone who sits today in any "judge's" seat — pastor, elder, parent, supervisor — has a Shittim lesson: in your own portion, where men are joined unto Baalpeor, do you pretend not to see, or does every one slay his men? The "slaying" here, at today's application layer, is resolute dealing and separation, not literal violence (the literal punishment belongs to this chapter's fact layer; only its historical setting is registered, and it is not extrapolated into a norm for today — stated further at the discussion layer).

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Heads" belongs to the rōʾš (H7218) family, head, chief; registered under the base text's rendering. "Judges" is the participle form of šāpaṭ (H8199), one who judges. "Slay" is hārag (H2026). The original word-form and number behind "hang them up" are marked in this sermon as awaiting verification; no number is filled in from memory — see Endnote 5. "Sun" is registered under the base text's rendering, as the sunlight, the open place, without philological expansion. All numbers filled in above were verified one by one before writing (for the method, see Endnote 5).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The relation of the two commands in verses 4 and 5 is set out in the commentary tradition in at least three lines, without adjudication: one takes verse 5 as the executive repetition of verse 4 — Moses, by the judges' enforcement division by division, carries out the sentence on the heads, those punished being in fact the foremost among the joined; one takes the two commands as parallel — the hanging of the heads and the slaying of the joined as two coordinate measures; one takes the command of verse 4 as one for which this chapter's narrative gives no record of execution, verse 5 being the command actually advanced. All three lines share the common premise that this chapter gives no execution list; they differ in how they read the silence. The sermon text argues only from the textual fact of the two commands set side by side (the differences of object and manner registered item by item) and adopts none of the three as fact. Again, the nature of the punishment in verse 4 (exposure of the corpse after execution, or death by hanging) is read differently in the original and in the translation traditions; it is stated without adjudication.

(Fact) This section's account of persons and speech closes here: two speakers in this section — the LORD speaks once (25:4), Moses speaks once (25:5); the judges have no lines, the heads have no lines, the joined people have no lines. The chapter's structure of speech has taken shape by this point: God speaks, Moses passes it on, and the rest have actions only and no voice. And the next verse, verse 6, happens precisely at the moment when these two commands have already been issued and the punishment ought to be advancing — someone, under the very eyes of the commands, brings the sin into the very centre of the camp. Command and defiance are, in this chapter, events of the same day: the hanging order of the morning is not yet cold before someone enters the camp openly in the afternoon. The narrative's whole tension rests on that one "behold" of verse 6.

### 3. Tears Before the Door of the Tabernacle, and One Javelin (Numbers 25:6-9) (20:00-34:00)

(Fact) Verse 6 — the chapter's most glaring scene, quoted in full: "And, behold, one of the children of Israel came and brought unto his brethren a Midianitish woman in the sight of Moses, and in the sight of all the congregation of the children of Israel, who were weeping before the door of the tabernacle of the congregation." (25:6). Registered item by item: the time is while they "were weeping" — the whole congregation weeping before the door of the tabernacle, weeping over the wrath, over the plague, over those who were dying; the place is "before the door of the tabernacle of the congregation" — before the holiest door in the camp; at just this time and in just this place, a man brought a Midianitish woman unto his brethren "in the sight of Moses, and in the sight of all the congregation." The words "in the sight of" are registered: this is not a sin in the dark, but an open, declared act, aimed at the weeping congregation and the door of the tabernacle. The weeping crowd and the man entering the camp stand in one frame — the narrative leaves this man not a shred of "he did not know": he walked past in full view of the whole camp's tears. Verse 7 — Phinehas' reaction, quoted in full: "And when Phinehas, the son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest, saw it, he rose up from among the congregation, and took a javelin in his hand;" (25:7). Three actions: he saw, he rose up, he took a javelin. The genealogy is issued in full here — the son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest: the name of the man entering the camp is recorded in this chapter only after his death (25:14), but Phinehas' name is solemnly brought forward by his genealogy before he acts. The narrative first lets the reader see clearly: whose descendant this man with a javelin is, and on what office he stands. Verse 8, quoted in full: "And he went after the man of Israel into the tent, and thrust both of them through, the man of Israel, and the woman through her belly. So the plague was stayed from the children of Israel." (25:8). Registered, in restrained terms as the base text gives them: one thrust, two persons; the result sentence follows at once — "So the plague was stayed." Verse 9 — the total account of the cost, quoted in full: "And those that died in the plague were twenty and four thousand." (25:9). The words "those that died" close the whole section: before the thrust, the plague had already taken twenty and four thousand. The staying is real, and the lateness is real — by the time the javelin was lifted, twenty and four thousand graves were already dug.

(Application) The tears before the door of the tabernacle are this section's first lesson. The whole congregation is weeping — the weeping is real, and the repentance is real, yet tears do not stay a plague. Between weeping and action stands a Phinehas. The church does not lack people who can weep: when something happens, everyone grieves together, prays together, sighs together — all right, and all not enough. This chapter's tears must be matched with a javelin: someone must rise up from among the congregation. Notice the place from which Phinehas rises — "from among the congregation": he had first been sitting among the weeping crowd; he is not a judge come from outside the camp; he was weeping like everyone else. The difference is not in weeping or not weeping, but in whether, after the weeping, a man rises. His zeal is not hardness without tears; it is decisiveness carrying tears. When the church today faces sin within, two diseases are equally fatal: one has the javelin only and no tears — dealing with people crisply and cleanly, with not one tear in the heart for the sinner or for the church: that is cruelty; the other has tears only and no javelin — everyone can weep, and no one is willing to rise and offend: that is connivance. Phinehas has both: he weeps among the congregation, and he rises from among the congregation. May God give today's church such people: when weeping, sitting among the congregation; when standing, not afraid of standing alone.

(Application) The man of "in the sight of" must also be spoken of. He was not ignorant — Moses was in sight, the whole congregation in sight, the tabernacle in sight, the tears in sight. He saw everything, and still brought her in. This is the deepest kind of hardening: not ignorance, not weakness, but contempt — contempt for tears, contempt for the holy place, contempt for the wrath then being kindled. When sin has developed to this step, words are of no more use: exhort him, and he will not hear; weep, and he looks on it as nothing. For such a man this chapter gives not another call, but a javelin. We who preach the gospel must acknowledge this boundary: gentle recovery has its time limit, and open contempt needs open dealing. The severity of church discipline was never for the sake of looking strong; it is because someone has already walked to the step of "in the sight of" — if a public sin is not publicly stopped, it is not he alone who falls: the whole camp's twenty and four thousand have already paid the tuition for this lesson. Every church slow to exercise discipline should count the number in verse 9: connivance is never free; its bill is reckoned by heads.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Weeping" is bākâ (H1058). "Javelin" is rōmaḥ (H7420), a spear, a lance. "Thrust through" is dāqar (H1856), to pierce through. "Plague" is maggēpâ (H4046), a blow, a pestilence, occurring twice in this section (25:8, 9); the same word stands at verse 18 in "in the day of the plague." The word behind "tent" at verse 8 is qubbâ (H6898), a tent-like inner chamber; registered under the base text's rendering. "Tabernacle" is ʾōhel (H168), its number verified in the sermon on Numbers 24. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing (for the method, see Endnote 5).

(New Testament Reading) Paul sets the matter of this chapter before the church in the tenth chapter of First Corinthians (cross-reference, at the reading layer): he records it as a warning, and commands, in the KJV's wording, "Neither let us commit fornication, as some of them committed, and fell in one day three and twenty thousand" (1 Cor 10:8; the gist of 10:6-8). Two items are registered at the reading layer: first, Paul's reading of this chapter as a warning has its centre in prevention — the same temptation comes to the New Testament church, and the outcome can be different; second, the number Paul records as fallen in one day differs at one point from this chapter's total; the dispute over the numbers is kept at the discussion layer, and this chapter's base-text figure of twenty and four thousand is neither reconciled nor altered here. The reading layer registers only how the New Testament uses this chapter; it does not fill it back into this chapter's facts.

(Fact) This section's account of actions and numbers closes here: those who appear in this section are the weeping congregation, the one man who brought the woman into the camp, the Midianitish woman, and Phinehas; speakers, none — this section has not one line of dialogue, only a chain of four verbs: weeping, walking, taking a javelin, thrusting through. The dead of the plague are twenty and four thousand (25:9), the chapter's only total of deaths; the number of those who died under the punishments of verses 4 and 5 is not recorded in this chapter (the blank is registered in Endnote 8). One further comparison is registered: before Phinehas entered the tent, this chapter contains no oracle commanding him to do so — he did not act under orders; he "saw it," and acted. This joins exactly with the characterisation in the next section: God afterwards set his own seal, in his own words, on an act that had no command under it. There is only one man in this chapter who saw and acted; and his reward is one that only God could give.

### 4. Behold, I Give unto Him My Covenant of Peace (Numbers 25:10-13) (34:00-48:00)

(Fact) Verse 10 — God speaks to Moses a second time, quoted in full: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying," (25:10). The formula "the LORD spake unto Moses" occurs twice in this chapter (25:10, 16), both times concerning the characterisation of this matter and its sequel. Verse 11 — God's verdict, quoted in full: "Phinehas, the son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest, hath turned my wrath away from the children of Israel, while he was zealous for my sake among them, that I consumed not the children of Israel in my jealousy." (25:11). Registered item by item: first, the genealogy is issued in full again — this is the second complete writing in this chapter of "Phinehas, the son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest" (25:7, 11), and this time it is God himself who speaks his full name and descent, which is to set his name before the whole camp. Second, the verb of the merit is "hath turned my wrath away" — the purpose of hanging the heads in verse 4, "that the fierce anger of the LORD may be turned away," is here declared accomplished, and the way of its accomplishment was not the hanging but Phinehas' one thrust. Third, the reason God gives is the theological heart of the whole chapter: he was "zealous for my sake" — in the Chinese base text's wording, he took God's own jealousy as his heart; what filled his heart was not his own indignation, but God's jealousy; and God's jealousy, had it run its full course, would have "consumed" them — Phinehas, by being zealous with God's jealousy, instead held back the consuming. Fourth, "among them" is registered: he was in the midst of the congregation, not outside the camp, when he was zealous for God's sake. Verse 12 — the reward is pronounced, quoted as it stands: "Wherefore say, Behold, I give unto him my covenant of peace:" (25:12). Verse 13 — the covenant's content and reason, quoted in full: "And he shall have it, and his seed after him, even the covenant of an everlasting priesthood; because he was zealous for his God, and made an atonement for the children of Israel." (25:13). Three items registered: the covenant's recipients are "he... and his seed after him" — passing beyond the individual to his line; the covenant's title is "the covenant of an everlasting priesthood" — the reward is not a one-time payment, but a hereditary office; the covenant's reason is a double sentence — "he was zealous for his God" and he "made an atonement for the children of Israel": the former toward God, the latter toward men. "Covenant" occurs three times in this chapter (25:12, "my covenant of peace"; 25:13, "it," and "the covenant of an everlasting priesthood"), all of them in this section.

(Application) "Zealous for my sake" — in the Chinese wording, with God's jealousy as his own heart — must be spoken to everyone who calls himself zealous for God. Phinehas' javelin was approved not because of the javelin, but because of the heart — the jealousy in that heart was God's, not his own. Where is the difference? A man zealous out of his own indignation strikes at the people he cannot stand; a man zealous with God's jealousy grieves over the sin God grieves over. The man of his own indignation aims his point at persons, and strikes with growing satisfaction; the man zealous with God's jealousy first sits among the congregation and weeps with them all (25:6-7), and what his action wins afterwards is the whole camp's peace — his one thrust was struck to hold back a consuming, not to vent himself. What today's church and today's internet least lack is self-appointed Phinehases: men who take up the banner of "zealous for God," pass judgment, expose faults, and decide men's lives and deaths, lacking only the two things Phinehas had — the tears before the door of the tabernacle, and God's own seal in his own words. The standard for telling true from false has already been given in this chapter: the fruit of a man truly zealous with God's jealousy is "wrath turned away" and a "covenant of peace"; the fruit of the counterfeit is only more division and more tears. If your zeal cannot bring men into peace, it is not God's jealousy; it is only your own temper wearing spiritual clothing.

(Application) Look again at the manner in which God pays the reward: he did not give Phinehas silver or gold, land, or military power; he gave him a covenant — and a covenant concerning the priesthood. The depth of this reward is this: that one thrust of Phinehas performed precisely the priest's office — standing between the living and the dead, making atonement for the people (Aaron's censer in Numbers 16, at the cross-reference layer). The priest's proper work is not killing, but atonement; the thing Phinehas accomplished with a javelin, God entered into his account as atonement (25:13, "made an atonement for the children of Israel"). To a man who made atonement with a javelin, God gives the covenant of an everlasting priesthood — the covenant amounts to a declaration: your seed shall do, generation after generation, what you have done today, redeeming my people back out of wrath. This is a sobering medicine for those who serve today: what God remembers is never how violent our actions are, but how closely our heart keeps time with his heart; keep time once, and he records a covenant. How many men have laboured in the church a whole lifetime seeking to be seen by men; Phinehas did one thing only, at a time when there was no command and no audience (the audience was all weeping), he did the one thing that was of one heart with God — and God wrote his name, together with his seed, into a covenant. The reward men give is finished when it is used up; the covenant God gives is written into a lineage.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Jealousy" is qinʾâ (H7068), an ardent possessiveness, a jealous anger; its verb form is qānāʾ (H7065). The jealousy word occurs three times in this chapter (twice at 25:11 and once at 25:13). "Covenant" is bərît (H1285); "peace" is šālôm (H7965); "seed" is zeraʿ (H2233); "everlasting" is ʿôlām (H5769); "priest" is kōhēn (H3548); "made an atonement" is kipper (H3722), to cover, to make reconciliation. "Phinehas" is Pînəḥās (H6372); "Eleazar" is ʾElʿāzār (H499); "Aaron" is ʾAhărōn (H175). All numbers above were verified one by one before writing (for the method, see Endnote 5).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters are stated without adjudication. First, the ethical evaluation of Phinehas' act: the later tradition stands in two lines side by side — Psalm 106 records of this act that it "was counted unto him for righteousness unto all generations for evermore" (Ps 106:30-31, at the cross-reference layer), Jewish tradition has much praise for it, and rabbinic literature also questions his motive and sets limits to it; modern readers, for their part, mostly raise ethical objections to an immediate execution without trial. The several accounts differ in their evaluation of the same text. This sermon registers only the divergence and the level of its sources; the sermon text argues only from the characterisation God himself gives in this chapter (25:11-13), fills no line's evaluation back in as this chapter's fact, and does not extrapolate this chapter's historical measure into a norm of conduct for today. Second, the relation between the "covenant of peace" and Malachi's covenant with Levi — "My covenant was with him of life and peace" (Mal 2:4-5, at the cross-reference layer): at both places priest, covenant, and peace are spoken together; whether the two refer to one and the same covenant, the commentary tradition has a common-source account and a parallel account side by side; it is stated without adjudication.

(Fact) This section closes by registering the turn in the chapter's structure of speech: in chapters 22-24 God's words were all spoken outward, through Balaam's mouth; in this chapter God's words are pronounced inward, through Moses' ear — first the verdict (25:11), then the reward (25:12-13). Note also the recipient: these words are spoken unto Moses, and Moses is to go and "say" them (25:12, "Wherefore say") — God does not speak to Phinehas directly; the reward is delivered through Moses' mouth. Phinehas himself has not one word in this chapter: he did not speak when he acted, and he did not speak when he was rewarded. In the whole chapter he has three verbs only: he saw, he rose up, he took a javelin. Such a man needs no self-defence; God speaks for him personally. Set this entry beside the man of verse 6: that man acted publicly, was thrust through publicly, and his name was recorded only after death; Phinehas acted without words, was rewarded without words, and his name, with his genealogy, is written out in full twice (25:7, 11), the second time pronounced by God's own mouth (in the reward of verses 12-13 he is pointed to as "him"). How a man's name is recorded is this chapter's quietest and loudest verdict.

### 5. The Names Recorded: Vex the Midianites (Numbers 25:14-18, and the Whole Chapter) (48:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verses 14 and 15 — the dead recorded by name, quoted in full: "Now the name of the Israelite that was slain, even that was slain with the Midianitish woman, was Zimri, the son of Salu, a prince of a chief house among the Simeonites." (25:14) "And the name of the Midianitish woman that was slain was Cozbi, the daughter of Zur; he was head over a people, and of a chief house in Midian." (25:15). Registered item by item: both are named only after death — at the time of the act they had no names (25:6, "one of the children of Israel," "a Midianitish woman"); at the verdict they have names. Their lineages are strikingly symmetrical: both are children of the head of a chief house — he, the son of a prince of a chief house among the Simeonites, himself standing in the layer of princes; she, the daughter of the head of a chief house in Midian. This is not the private affair of two obscure nobodies; it is the joining of two princely houses, standing in exact correspondence with the command of verse 4 to hang "all the heads of the people": the source of the sin was always in the upper layer, and the pair pierced by the thrust were in the upper layer too. Verses 16 and 17 — the chapter-closing command, quoted in full: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Vex the Midianites, and smite them:" (25:16-17). Verse 18 — the reason, quoted in full: "For they vex you with their wiles, wherewith they have beguiled you in the matter of Peor, and in the matter of Cozbi, the daughter of a prince of Midian, their sister, which was slain in the day of the plague for Peor's sake." (25:18). Three items registered: first, "wiles" occurs twice (25:18) — the calamity of this chapter, in God's own characterisation, is not an accidental case of immorality, but a vexing carried out with wiles; second, Cozbi is here called "their sister" — the Midianites gave their own daughter as the bait, and in God's eyes the whole people owns that device; third, this verse nails Cozbi's death together once more with "the day of the plague" — her matter is part of the matter of Peor, not a sideshow. The execution of this chapter (the war against Midian) does not lie within this chapter; this sermon registers the command and the reason only, and the fighting belongs to the sequel (chapter 31, at this book's later-text layer), not developed here.

(Application) The recording of the names must be spoken to everyone who sits in a "prince's" seat. Scripture records names usually as grace: enrolling, registering genealogies, remembering. But the recording in this chapter is a verdict: Zimri's and Cozbi's names are written into Scripture not because they did something to be remembered by, but because their sin was great enough that it had to be put on record. You and I all want to be remembered — the question is how we are remembered. Zimri is remembered in the words "the Israelite that was slain, even that was slain with the Midianitish woman"; Phinehas is remembered in the words "hath turned my wrath away from the children of Israel." One chapter, two names, two ways of recording. No one can choose not to be recorded — everything you do adds a stroke to God's register; what you can choose is only whether that stroke is written at verse 14 or at verse 11. Those at the head should tremble still more: your lineage, your position, your house, on the day of judgment are not a talisman but a magnifying glass — when the children of princes sin, Scripture writes the father's name out along with theirs. While the name is not yet fixed, be the man of verse 11; do not be the man of verse 14.

(Application) The word "wiles" at the chapter's end gives the whole chapter its final characterisation, and gives us the last lesson in recognising today's Peor. Seen from verse 1, the matter begins with relations between men and women; seen from verse 2, it advances to dinners and sociability; at verse 18 God himself lifts the bottom: this is wiles — there is design, there is bait, there is a target (to vex you). The enemy's most skilful method has never been open swords, but wrapping a trap up as love, as a dinner, as "just once." Israel lost twenty and four thousand men not because they did not know Baalpeor was a false god — they knew; it was because they did not recognise that the dinner set before them was a weapon. If today's church can only refute paganism from the pulpit, but cannot recognise wiles in daily life, Shittim will be repeated again and again. The method of recognising them is given in this chapter: look at the fruit — whatever invitation pulls you toward a "joining," whether it is packaged as affection, as profit, or as fame, treat it as wiles first and distinguish slowly afterwards; distinguish wrongly, and at worst you miss a dinner; distinguish too late, and it is twenty and four thousand men.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Vex" is ṣārar (H6887), to be hostile to, to straiten, occurring twice in the command and the reason of this section (once in the command at 25:17 and once in the reason at 25:18; for the details, see Endnote 4). "Zimri" is Zimrî (H2174); "Salu" is Sālûʾ (H5543); "Simeonites" (Simeon) is Šimʿôn (H8095); "Cozbi" is Kozbî (H3579); "Zur" is Ṣûr (H6698); "Midian" is Midyān (H4080). The original word-form and number behind "wiles" are marked in this sermon as awaiting verification — see Endnote 5. All numbers filled in above were verified one by one before writing (for the method, see Endnote 5).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters are set side by side without harmonisation. First, the numbers: the dead of the plague in verse 9 are "twenty and four thousand," while Paul in 1 Corinthians 10:8 records that they "fell in one day three and twenty thousand" (in the base text's own wording) — for the difference between the two numbers the commentary tradition has a segmented reckoning (what Paul records being one day's number, what this chapter records being the total) and accounts of transmission and selection side by side. This sermon argues with this chapter's base-text figure of twenty and four thousand; the New Testament number is registered at the reading layer only, and neither number is rewritten into the other. Second, the relation of Balaam to this chapter: this chapter's text does not mention Balaam in a single word; the sequel, at 31:16, identifies the matter of Peor as coming by Balaam's counsel (at this book's later-text layer), and Revelation 2:14 likewise says that Balaam "taught Balac to cast a stumblingblock before the children of Israel" (at the New Testament reading layer) — both places are registered only as to their existence and their level; whether this chapter is on that basis to be read as the execution of Balaam's device is a cross-chapter synthetic judgment, and this sermon does not read it back into this chapter's fact layer; it is stated without adjudication.

(Fact) The whole chapter's data close here, every item already counted (for the details, see Endnote 4): the chapter has 18 verses; the sections cover, in order, 3 (25:1-3), 2 (25:4-5), 4 (25:6-9), 4 (25:10-13), and 5 (25:14-18) verses, totalling 18. "Joined" occurs twice (25:3, 5); "Baalpeor" occurs twice (25:3, 5), and "Peor" standing alone twice (both at 25:18); the jealousy word occurs three times (twice at 25:11, once at 25:13); "covenant" occurs three times (once at 25:12, twice at 25:13); "plague" occurs three times (25:8, 9, 18); "the LORD spake unto Moses" occurs twice (25:10, 16); Phinehas' genealogy in full occurs twice (25:7, 11); the LORD's direct speech stands in three passages (25:4, 11-13, 17-18), Moses' speech in one passage (25:5), and every other person has no lines in the whole chapter — the weeping congregation is silent, the man who entered the camp is silent, Phinehas is silent, the dead are silent. The total account of deaths: twenty and four thousand by the plague (25:9), and two more thrust through (25:8, named after death at 25:14-15); the number under the punishments of verses 4 and 5 is not recorded in this chapter. This chapter's close set against the former chapter's: chapter 24 ends with two men each going his own way (24:25); this chapter ends with a command that has not yet fallen closed (25:17-18) — the story of the curses is finished; the account of the wiles is not yet settled.

(Application) The whole chapter closes leaving one sentence for each of three kinds of men. First, for those abiding in Shittim: you are only one river away from the promised land (at the cross-reference layer: Shittim is in the region of the last camp before crossing the Jordan, Num 33:49), yet the nearest distance is precisely the farthest distance — twenty and four thousand men fell one step from their own door. The nearer the end, the less a man may settle and indulge himself; if the devil cannot bar your road, he will set a table for you before the finishing line. To keep the last stretch is harder, and weightier, than to have walked the forty years before it. Second, for those weeping among the congregation: the tears are real, but be prepared to rise from among the congregation. In your church, your family, your workplace, there will always be one scene of "in the sight of" about to happen — at that moment what is needed is not more tears, but a man willing to take up a javelin. Before taking it up, first learn to weep with them all; after rising, do not look back at anyone's face. Third, for everyone who wants to be recorded by God: this chapter's reward was not given to the busiest man, nor to the loudest voice, but to the man who, while they all wept, was zealous with God's own jealousy. The covenant of peace still stands today (at the reading layer): the men God seeks are men whose hearts are of one heart with his — if you are willing to let his jealousy become your heart, his peace will become your covenant. Nowhere else, but in that portion of the camp where you stand today.

## Devotional Excerpt

The place where Israel fell at Shittim was only one step from Canaan. Yet they were not struck down by the enemy — Balaam's three cursings had not touched a hair of their heads — they were brought down by a meal, a table, an act of bowing, and twenty and four thousand died in the plague. Today God reminds us: the most dangerous moment is often not the moment of attack, but the moment after the battle is won and we have settled down. What the enemy could not do on the mountain, one relaxed invitation did. If you are standing in the place of "at last I can breathe out," keep one extra watch: look carefully at the table before the finishing line before you sit down at it. And when the whole camp was weeping and no one dared to move, Phinehas rose up from among the congregation — he first wept with them all, and then took up the javelin alone. What God approved in him was not the javelin, but his heart: he was zealous for God's sake among them (25:11). And so God said, "Behold, I give unto him my covenant of peace:" (25:12). Today God is likewise seeking men who take his heart as their heart: uncompromising toward sin, tearful toward men, willing to stand up at the critical moment. If you are willing, his peace will become your covenant — not a momentary comfort, but a covenant spoken by his own mouth and reckoned upon your seed after you.

English (KJV-based):

"Wherefore say, Behold, I give unto him my covenant of peace: And he shall have it, and his seed after him, even the covenant of an everlasting priesthood; because he was zealous for his God, and made an atonement for the children of Israel." (Num 25:12-13, KJV) Phinehas did not act out of temper; he was zealous with God's own jealousy, and the reward was not gold but a covenant. Ask God today for a heart that grieves what He grieves and stands where He stands — and His peace will not be a passing feeling, but a covenant He Himself has spoken over you and your house.

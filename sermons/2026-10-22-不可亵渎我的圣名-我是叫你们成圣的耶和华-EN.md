## Abstract

Leviticus 22 follows directly on chapter 21: the earlier chapter treats the person of the priest, this chapter the holy things he handles. Its first half requires the priest to be clean before he eats of the holy things, and draws the boundary of who may eat by membership in the priest's household; its second half requires Israel's offerings to be perfect and without blemish if they are to be accepted. The whole chapter is framed by "you shall not profane my holy name" and "I am the LORD who sanctifies you," showing that holiness rests not on human rule-keeping but on God himself, who brought his people out of Egypt and sanctifies them.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 Opening: From the Person to the Things, the Order of Holiness Advances One Step

(Application) Brothers and sisters, in Leviticus 21 we looked at the person: the priest's body, the priest's marriage, the priest's mourning — all under restriction, because he is the one who draws near to offer the food of his God. In chapter 22 the camera moves from the person to the things: the holy things the priest handles daily — how are they to be treated? Who may eat them? What must the cattle and sheep the people bring look like? The topic has not changed; the same question is being viewed from its other side. The one who offers must be set apart, and what he offers and eats must be set apart as well. Person and thing are never two separate matters in the sanctuary: if a man treats the things lightly, his own consecration is empty talk; if the things do not meet the standard, the man cannot stand firm before the altar either.

(Fact) First, the map of the chapter. It falls into two large parts. The first, verses 1-16, concerns priests and holy things: a priest who is unclean must not approach or eat the holy things (vv. 1-9), and who is qualified to eat them, who is not, and how an unintentional eater makes restitution (vv. 10-16). The second part, verses 17-33, concerns the people and their offerings: animals offered must be without blemish to be accepted (vv. 17-25); detailed rules about newborn cattle and sheep, a mother and her young on the same day, and the thanksgiving offering eaten on the same day (vv. 26-30); and a conclusion calling Israel to keep the commandments and not profane the holy name (vv. 31-33). The addressees differ: the first part is spoken mainly to Aaron and his sons, the second to Aaron and his sons together with all the children of Israel (see the scope of address in 22:18; summary). The shift itself speaks: in the matter of holy things, priests bear a priestly responsibility and the people a people's responsibility; neither side escapes.

(Fact) Note also the framing sentence of the chapter. Verse 2 lays down the general rule at the outset: "Speak unto Aaron and to his sons, that they separate themselves from the holy things of the children of Israel, and that they profane not my holy name in those things which they hallow unto me: I am the LORD." (Lev 22:2, KJV) "Separate themselves" does not mean the priests should avoid the holy things — the holy things are precisely their charge; the Hebrew means that when a priest bears uncleanness he must hold himself apart from the holy things and not approach them as usual (summary at the lexical level). The rule lands on "that they profane not my holy name." To profane is ḥillēl (חִלֵּל, Strong's H2490, Piel stem), whose basic sense is to make something common, to strip a holy thing of its set-apart status (see the BDB entry ḥālal). The word occurs four times in this chapter: in verse 2 of the priests' handling of holy things, lest they profane my holy name; in verse 9 of the priests keeping what God commanded, lest they profane what he commanded; in verse 15 of the priests not profaning the holy things the people offer; and in verse 32 of all Israel not profaning God's holy name. The object profaned in verse 9 is what God commanded (the holy things / his commandments), distinct at the level of object from the profaning of the holy name / holy things in verses 2, 15, and 32, and the two should be read in layers. Verse 32 is the echo of the opening rule: "Neither shall ye profane my holy name; but I will be hallowed among the children of Israel: I am the LORD which hallow you," (Lev 22:32, KJV) Same word, same theme, at head and tail; the Chinese title of this sermon is taken from these very words in the base text. Between the opening and the close, every regulation in the chapter is framed by one sentence: all the rules exist, in the end, so that one Name is not profaned.

(Fact) One more thread runs through the chapter: God's first-person self-declaration. "I am the LORD" recurs again and again — verses 3, 8, 9, 16, 30, 31, 33 — verse after verse driving in a stake. Verses 9 and 16 go further, "I the LORD do sanctify them" (summary of their sense), and verse 32 reads "I am the LORD which hallow you." The verb "to sanctify" is qiddēš (קִדֵּשׁ, H6942, Piel participle məqaddēš), and its subject is always the LORD, never man. Men keep regulations in response; God sanctifies at the root. To read this chapter and see only a checklist of prohibitions is to read it backwards: under every line of the list lies the same declaration — the one who sanctifies you is I.

(Application) Chapters 21 and 22 together form one complete picture: in chapter 21 the offerer must be without blemish and must keep himself; in chapter 22 the offering must be without blemish and the holy things must be eaten within the boundary. Next we look at the first half: why must a priest be clean before he eats the holy things, and why does the text ask so precisely who may eat?

### II. 08:00-24:00 The Priest Must Be Clean to Eat the Holy Things: To Approach While Unclean Is Profanation

(Fact) The first part begins with a severe principle. Verse 3: "Say unto them, Whosoever he be of all your seed among your generations, that goeth unto the holy things, which the children of Israel hallow unto the LORD, having his uncleanness upon him, that soul shall be cut off from my presence: I am the LORD." (Lev 22:3, KJV) Notice carefully: the regulation does not punish "uncleanness" itself. Uncleanness has many unavoidable sources — touching a corpse, having a discharge, contact with an unclean person or thing; these happen at any time in daily life, and the regulations never treat contracting uncleanness as a sin. What is forbidden is approaching the holy things and eating them while still bearing uncleanness. In other words, the problem lies not in the condition but in the attitude: to know oneself unclean and still treat the holy things as common things — that is profanation. Verses 4-6 list the situations that suspend a priest from eating the holy things; for accuracy they are given as summary only: (Summary) Among Aaron's descendants, one with a leprous disease or a discharge shall not eat of the holy things until he is clean; one who touches anything made unclean by a corpse, or a man who has had an emission, or who touches a creeping thing or an unclean person, is unclean until evening and must wash his body with water before he may eat. Verse 7 gives the sequence of restoration (summary): after sunset he is clean, and then he may eat of the holy things, because they are his food. The suspension is temporary, the path to cleanness is open, and the seat is not cancelled — the same measured restraint as chapter 21's treatment of the blemished priest, "he shall eat": what is restricted is present approach, not permanent removal from the roll.

(Fact) Verse 8 adds a dietary prohibition: "That which dieth of itself, or is torn with beasts, he shall not eat to defile himself therewith: I am the LORD." (Lev 22:8, KJV) An animal that died of itself was never slaughtered and drained of blood; one torn by beasts likewise carries blood and tearing wounds; neither fits the priest's dietary boundary. Verse 9 closes this subsection (summary): the priests shall keep God's charge, lest they treat it lightly, bear sin, and die, for it is the LORD who sanctifies them. One contrast here deserves close attention: the "cutting off" of verse 3 and the "bear sin and die" of verse 9 are among the heaviest formulations in the whole book, and what triggers them is not a technical ritual error but contempt — treating what is holy as though it were common. Leviticus has a lenient provision for unintentional offences: in this very chapter, verse 14 requires only restitution from an outsider who eats a holy thing by mistake, with no further penalty. But toward knowing contempt the wording turns severe at once. The known and the unknown, the mistaken and the contemptuous, are kept clearly apart on this chapter's ethical scale.

(Fact) This subsection also introduces the chapter's key term: the holy thing, Hebrew qodeš (קֹדֶשׁ, H6944), a noun whose basic sense is a thing set apart, a holy thing. In this chapter it refers concretely to the portions of the people's offerings that are set apart for the LORD and assigned by rule to the priests for food, such as the heave offering, the wave offering, and specified portions of the peace offerings (see the scheme in Lev 7; summary). For a priest to eat the holy things is not a perk but part of his office: the holy things are his food (see the summary of 22:7), and his provision comes from the altar. Eating the holy things therefore has two faces in the regulations at once: it is a right, to be eaten by rule; and it is a responsibility, of which not one mouthful may be eaten while the body is unclean. Right and responsibility are governed by the same boundary, and that boundary is called holiness.

(Fact) One more designation deserves attention: at the end of the chapter the holy things are called "the bread of your God." In verse 25, concerning animals received from a foreigner's hand, the text says that blemished animals must not be offered as the bread of your God (summary). Chapter 21, verse 6 likewise calls the fire offerings "the food of God." Food is leḥem (לֶחֶם, H3899), whose basic meaning is bread, grain, food. The offerings are called God's food, and the priest's eating of the holy things is called eating his food (see the summary of 22:7); one image of food joins altar and table together: what is offered belongs to God, and what belongs to God is in turn apportioned by rule to those who serve, as their food. This does not mean that God needs food; it marks ownership in the most ordinary language available: once a thing has gone up on the altar it is no longer a common thing, and what is apportioned to the priest's table still bears the mark of holiness. That is why this chapter regulates eating in such detail: in the order of Leviticus, eating is never merely eating; it is a public acknowledgment of ownership. Who eats, in what condition he eats, and at whose table he eats, all declare to whom the food belongs and to whom the man belongs.

(Application) For today's reader, the application takes one point only, and its limit must be stated plainly. The old-covenant rules for priests eating holy things belong to the sanctuary system and are not transplanted directly as dietary or ministry rules for the church today. What may be taken is their logic of reverence: those who handle holy things every day are the most likely to treat the holy as the familiar, and the familiar as the casual. This chapter's warning is aimed precisely at contempt born of familiarity: it does not demand that a person first prove himself worthy; it asks only that, in a condition that is not fitting, he stop first — wash, wait, and then approach. To pause, in Leviticus, is never weakness; it is the shape reverence takes.

### III. 24:00-42:00 Who May Eat the Holy Things: A Holy-Common Order Bounded by the Household

(Fact) From verse 10 the question shifts from "when may the priest eat" to "which persons may eat." The general rule is stated first: "There shall no stranger eat of the holy thing: a sojourner of the priest, or an hired servant, shall not eat of the holy thing." (Lev 22:10, KJV) Three words must be checked one by one. Stranger is zār (זָר, H2114), one who does not belong to the priestly house, an outsider (see the BDB entry for the zûr word family); in the context of holy things it does not refer specifically to foreigners, but to everyone who lacks standing within the priestly household, even if he is a fellow Israelite. The sojourner is tôšāḇ (תוֹשָׁב, H8453), a guest temporarily residing in the priest's house; the hired servant is śākîr (שָׂכִיר, H7916), one employed by the day or by a term. These two kinds of person live under the priest's roof and eat the priest's food, yet they may not eat the holy thing. The reason lies not in closeness or distance but in belonging: sojourning is temporary lodging and hiring is contractual labour; neither brings a person into the priest's "house."

(Fact) Verse 11 then gives the positive boundary (summary): a person the priest has bought with his money may eat of the holy things, and one born in his house may also eat of his food. Purchased persons and those born in the house were, in the ancient household structure, formally reckoned under the name of that house, and so shared the house's food, including the holy things. Note that the criterion throughout is consistent: not how near a person lives, nor how much he works, but whether he belongs to this house by standing. The sojourner lives in the house yet does not belong to it; the bought person was an outsider yet, by being bought, has entered the house. The line is drawn at belonging, not at the roof. Verses 12-13 test the same criterion once more through the marital circumstances of a priest's daughter (summary): if a priest's daughter marries a stranger, she may no longer eat of the heave offering of the holy things, because her belonging has been transferred to her husband's house; but if she is a widow or divorced, has no child, and returns to her father's house as in her youth, she may eat of her father's food, because her belonging has returned to her father's house; yet no stranger may eat of it. Read today, this set of rules carries the colouring of the ancient patriarchal household; the exposition offers it only as an analysis of the order the text presents, and draws from it no judgement on any person in any marital circumstance today. Its textual function is clear: the qualification to eat moves with belonging; where belonging is, there the seat is.

(Fact) Verse 14 treats an exceptional case: "And if a man eat of the holy thing unwittingly, then he shall put the fifth part thereof unto it, and shall give it unto the priest with the holy thing." (Lev 22:14, KJV) To eat unwittingly is to eat without intent, not to steal deliberately. The remedy is restitution: repay the full value, add a fifth, and give it to the priest together with the holy thing. The pattern of adding a fifth appears more than once in Leviticus: wherever a holy thing is unintentionally misappropriated or the sanctuary suffers loss, restitution with an added fifth is the norm, so that the injured party receives more than the original amount and the careless party pays a felt price for his negligence (see the parallel scheme in Lev 5:16 and 27:13; summary). Verses 15-16 then turn the responsibility back to the priests' side (summary): the priests must not profane the holy things the children of Israel offer, nor cause the people to bear sin by eating them, for it is the LORD who sanctifies them. The boundary of qualification is thus not one-directional: the people must not cross it by mistake, and the priests must not let it lapse through neglect. If the guardians of the boundary fail to guard it, the sin falls first on the guardians.

(Application) Read as a text about the holy-common order, this section shows one feature of how Leviticus handles a "boundary": its boundaries are identifiable, enforceable, and repairable. Identifiable, because they do not rest on feeling but list belonging item by item; enforceable, because sojourner, hired servant, bought person, house-born person, married-out daughter, and returned daughter are each given an explicit rule; repairable, because one who crosses the boundary by mistake has a road of restitution and is not cut off the moment he crosses. Holiness in Leviticus is not an ineffable aura of majesty but an order with gradations and with an exit. Nor is the order's purpose to push people ever further apart: in the very next section the same God will speak to the offering people and require them to bring their best — only when the boundary is kept do the people know what it is they are offering.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 What Is Offered Must Be Without Blemish to Be Accepted: The Correspondence of the Two Lists, for Man and for Beast

(Fact) At verse 17 the second part begins, and the addressees widen to all the people (summary): whether a man of the house of Israel or a stranger sojourning in Israel, whoever presents an offering as a burnt offering — whether to pay a vow or as a freewill offering — must follow the standard that now comes. Verse 21 states the standard most fully: "And whosoever offereth a sacrifice of peace offerings unto the LORD to accomplish his vow, or a freewill offering in beeves or sheep, it shall be perfect to be accepted; there shall be no blemish therein." (Lev 22:21, KJV) Two words form the skeleton of the verse. Perfect is tāmîm (תָּמִים, H8549), whole and without defect (see the BDB and HALOT entries under tmm). Accepted comes from the Hebrew root rāṣāh (רָצָה, H7521, a verb meaning to accept, to be pleased with); its noun form rāṣôn (רָצוֹן, H7522) means acceptance or favour, and verse 19's "to be accepted" uses the noun form in the Hebrew, lirṣōnkem, "for your acceptance." The verse's syntax deserves a close reading: it does not begin with God's feelings; it begins with the specification of the offering — perfect and without blemish, then accepted. "Acceptance" here is a verifiable ritual judgement stated in the text: what meets the specification is accepted; what does not meet it is not accepted (see the same form of statement in 22:20 and 22:25; summary). The exposition makes only this textual observation: the chapter binds the statement of "acceptance" to the actual condition of the offering, and does not here propose any method for measuring the offerer's inner motives. What the text certifies is that the specification is public and checkable by all, and that acceptance does not depend on the offerer's rank.

(Fact) Verse 22 lists the disqualifying defects; for accuracy they are given as summary only: (Summary) The blind, the broken, the maimed, and animals with a wen, with scurf, or with scabs shall not be offered to the LORD, nor placed on the altar as a fire offering. The correspondence between this list and the blemish list for priests in chapter 21 is the most important structural observation in this chapter, and it must be seen in layers. First layer, the correspondence of the word: blemish is mûm (מוּם, H3971), used in chapter 21 of the priest's body (Lev 21:17-23) and in this chapter of the animal's body (Lev 22:20, 21, 25) — the same word in both places. Second layer, the correspondence of the items: the list in chapter 21 includes blindness and an over-long limb; this chapter's list likewise includes blindness, together with brokenness and maiming that answer to bodily damage; the two lists do not match item for item, yet they belong to one system of categories. Third layer, the correspondence of function: in chapter 21 a blemished priest may not draw near to offer; in this chapter a blemished animal may not be offered. On one side the man who offers, on the other the offering itself; the symbolic order of the sanctuary is measured on both sides with the same rule. This is an observation at the level of the text's editing; its evidence lies in the wording and arrangement of Scripture itself and depends on no imported framework. Both Milgrom and Wenham note the parallelism between these two sets of regulations (see the endnotes); the exposition adopts the observation but keeps its inferences restrained, stating the textual facts and no more.

(Fact) After the list come two detailed rules that push the standard to its finest point. Verse 23 treats a borderline case (summary): a bullock or a lamb with a limb too long or too short may be offered as a freewill offering, but for a vow it shall not be accepted. The KJV wording itself is: "Either a bullock or a lamb that hath any thing superfluous or lacking in his parts, that mayest thou offer for a freewill offering; but for a vow it shall not be accepted." (Lev 22:23, KJV) "Too long" is śārûaʿ (שָׂרוּעַ, H8311), a limb stretched out too long, out of proportion; its counterpart is qālûṭ (קָלוּט, H7038), a limb shrunk too short (see the BDB entries śāraʿ and qālaṭ). This word also appeared in chapter 21, verse 18, of a priest with an over-long limb — man and beast correspond once more through the same word. A further term appears here: freewill offering is nədəḇāh (נְדָבָה, H5071), an offering presented voluntarily (see the BDB entry). The regulation grades the freewill offering and the vow offering differently: for a freewill offering, which arises from voluntariness and carries no fixed obligation, the borderline animal may still be received; for a vow offering, which arises from a promise and carries an obligation of repayment, the standard must be met in full. The more definite the obligation, the less the standard is discounted — this is the text's own scale, and the exposition points out the scale without adding to it any theory of motives. Verses 24-25 add two further rules (summary): an animal whose testicles are bruised, crushed, torn, or cut shall not be offered, and this shall not be done in the land; nor shall such animals received from a foreigner's hand be offered as the food of God, for they shall not be accepted. The standard applies regardless of source: one's own or a foreigner's, one rule measures them all.

(Fact) From verse 26 comes the lower half of the second part, treating three seemingly minor matters, each tied to "acceptance." Verse 27: "When a bullock, or a sheep, or a goat, is brought forth, then it shall be seven days under the dam; and from the eighth day and thenceforth it shall be accepted for an offering made by fire unto the LORD." (Lev 22:27, KJV) A newborn animal must stay with its mother seven days; from the eighth day onward it may serve as an offering. Verse 28: "And whether it be cow, or ewe, ye shall not kill it and her young both in one day." (Lev 22:28, KJV) A mother animal and her young shall not be slaughtered on the same day. Verses 29-30 treat the thanksgiving offering (summary): a sacrifice of thanksgiving must be offered so as to be accepted, and its flesh must be eaten on the same day, with nothing left until the morning; the KJV of verse 30 reads: "On the same day it shall be eaten up; ye shall leave none of it until the morrow: I am the LORD." Thanksgiving offering is tôdāh (תּוֹדָה, H8426), in full zeḇaḥ tôdāh, a peace offering presented for thanksgiving (see the BDB entry under the ydāh word family); among the peace offerings it already carried the tightest time limit, for Leviticus 7 had ruled that its flesh be eaten on the same day (see Lev 7:15; summary), and this chapter restates the rule here. The common point of these three details is that they pull sacrifice out of the realm of "getting the task done": the animal's age must be waited out in full, the bond of mother and young must be regarded, and the flesh of thanksgiving must not be dragged out. The regulations govern not only the moment on the altar but also how people treat animals, and how they treat time, below the altar.

(Fact) Consider now an asymmetry that is easily read past, which locks section III and this section together. In the scope of address in verse 18, the stranger sojourning in Israel is also counted among those who offer (summary): a stranger may lead his own cattle and sheep to the altar with a burnt offering, governed by the same blemish-free standard as the house of Israel. Yet verse 10 states plainly that no stranger shall eat of the holy thing. The same stranger: to offer, yes; to eat, no. The asymmetry is no contradiction, for the two actions differ in nature. To offer is to assign one's own gift to God, and God accepts everyone who offers according to the specification, native or stranger alike. To eat the holy things is to enter the seat of the priestly house; that is the belonging of an office, not the threshold of worship. The door stands open toward the one who offers; the seat belongs only to the house that bears the office. The boundary of Leviticus is therefore not a wall shutting people out from God but a set of clearly divided lines: the road of worship does not distinguish insider and outsider, while the seat of office permits no confusion. To read this chapter and see only exclusion is to miss the open door of verse 18; to see only acceptance is to miss the clear line of verse 10. Read together, the two are the text's own balance.

(Application) For today's reader the application lands on one point only: to keep the second-best for oneself and send the defective thing toward the altar is the most natural human reckoning, and this chapter's list is aimed straight at that reckoning. God did not require people to offer what they could not afford: to take one animal that met the specification from one's herd or flock was feasible in the farming and herding life of the day. What he required was that the offerer not tamper with the specification. A public standard with clear gradations is itself a protection for the poor: acceptance does not depend on who offers the most expensive thing, but on whether what is offered meets the one rule that all can check.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: I Am the LORD Who Brought You Out of Egypt

(Fact) The last three verses of the chapter gather every regulation back onto one foundation. Verse 31 commands the people to keep and do the commandments (summary). Verse 32 restates the general rule: "Neither shall ye profane my holy name; but I will be hallowed among the children of Israel: I am the LORD which hallow you," (Lev 22:32, KJV) Verse 33 then gives this God's own record: "That brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God: I am the LORD." (Lev 22:33, KJV) Note the order of the argument in the conclusion: it does not first proclaim God's majesty and then press down the rules; it first tells what he has done — brought you out of Egypt, to be your God — and the authority of the rules rests on that accomplished deed. The LORD who sanctifies and the LORD of the Exodus are one and the same: first salvation, then requirement; first he makes himself their God, then he commands them to be set apart. This order is in tune with the preface to the Ten Commandments, and it is the foundation under the whole Holiness Code of Leviticus.

(Fact) Looking back at the parallel between the two chapters, chapter 22 completes the picture chapter 21 began. Chapter 21: the person — the priest's body must be without blemish for him to draw near, yet seat and food are preserved for the one under restriction. Chapter 22: the things — the offering must be without blemish to be accepted, and for mistaken eating and mistaken offering there are explicit provisions of repair and remedy. The closing word of both chapters is the same sentence: the one who sanctifies is the LORD. Holiness in Leviticus is never an altitude human beings climb; it is an action God stoops down to perform. Man's part is to keep the boundary, offer what meets the standard, eat what he ought to eat, and stop when he ought to stop. That is all — and everything lies in that.

(Application) So what this chapter finally leaves us is not two lists but the name of one God: the name that must not be profaned is his; the one who sanctifies us is he; the one who brought us out of Egypt is he as well. Today we draw near to God in Christ, resting not on a blemish-free record of our own but on this God's own initiative in salvation and sanctification. May we learn, on the one hand, not to be careless in small things, coming before him with what is up to standard, real, and whole; and may we set our hope, on the other hand, in the right place — not in how well we have kept the rules, but in who he is. Glory be to the LORD who sanctifies us. Amen.

## Devotional Extract

The lists in Leviticus 22 are long, but one short sentence lies underneath the whole chapter: "I am the LORD which hallow you" (Lev 22:32, KJV). A priest who is unclean must stop and wash; one who eats a holy thing by mistake must make restitution; an offering with a blemish is not accepted. The rules grow finer one by one, yet the subject of sanctification is never man. The one who sanctifies you is the LORD. Today you may be blaming yourself for your own incompleteness, feeling that whatever you bring out carries a defect — prayer, service, daily life, everything bearing traces of brokenness. Hear the order of this chapter clearly: God first brought his people out of Egypt and made himself their God, and only then handed down, one by one, the regulations of being set apart. His requirement is real, and his priority is real. You need not repair every defect before you dare to draw near to him. What you are to do is refuse carelessness: where there is a defect, do not disguise it or send it carelessly to the altar; come honestly before him and ask him to sanctify you according to his own promise. The end of the regulations is not human perfection but God's name — the holy name that must not be profaned, and the One who himself takes the initiative to sanctify you. Today, lay down both the pride and the self-reproach of your defective offering heart, and quietly receive this one sentence: the one who sanctifies you is the LORD, and the one who brought you out of Egypt is he.

## Endnotes

1. The two-part division, Leviticus 22:1-16 and 22:17-33, follows the shift of addressees (22:17 begins again with "And the LORD spake unto Moses," and 22:18 widens the address to all the children of Israel) and the shift of theme (priests eating holy things; the people presenting offerings). It is a structural observation, not the base text's own paragraphing.
2. ḥillēl (H2490): the Piel sense in the BDB entry ḥālal is profane, defile — to make a holy thing common. It occurs four times in this chapter (22:2, 22:9, 22:15, 22:32); the object profaned in 22:9 is what God commanded, distinct from the profaning of the holy name / holy things in 22:2, 22:15, and 22:32, while 22:2 and 22:32 still form the chapter's frame; the second half of the Chinese title, like its first half, is taken from the base text's own wording at 22:32.
3. məqaddēš and qiddēš (H6942): the phrase "who sanctifies" at 22:9, 22:16, and 22:32 all use this verb, and in every case the subject is the LORD; the Chinese base text at 22:32 reads "I am the LORD who sanctifies you," from which the sermon's title is taken.
4. zār (H2114; 22:10, 12, 13): the BDB entry gives strange, foreigner; in the holy-things context it denotes anyone who lacks standing in the priestly household, not foreigners only. The body of the sermon builds its textual analysis on this.
5. tôšāḇ (H8453) and śākîr (H7916; 22:10): the former is the temporarily residing sojourner, the latter the hired servant. Both stand opposite the "bought person" and the "born in his house" of 22:11, showing that the criterion of qualification is belonging, not residence or labour.
6. Restitution with an added fifth (22:14): the usual pattern in Leviticus for unintentional misuse of holy things (see Lev 5:16; 27:13, 15, 19, 27, 31). Here it governs mistaken eating of a holy thing and is remedied by repayment with the addition, with no penalty of cutting off — in deliberate contrast to the knowing approach of 22:3.
7. tāmîm (H8549; 22:21): BDB and HALOT, under tmm, both give complete, whole, sound; the Chinese base text renders "pure and whole." The word is also used beyond priests and sacrificial animals (e.g., the "perfect man" of Gen 17:1 uses the same root), noted here as a lexical side-reference only.
8. rāṣôn (H7522) and the verb rāṣāh (H7521): at 22:19 "acceptance" is the noun form in the Hebrew (lirṣōnkem); at 22:23 and 22:27 the verb form is used. The task brief's citation of rāṣôn as H7522, a noun, is correct; the sermon marks noun and verb forms separately to avoid confusion.
9. mûm (H3971): the BDB entry gives blemish, defect. Leviticus 21:17-23 applies it to priests and Leviticus 22:20-25 to sacrificial animals; this same-word parallelism is the core internal textual evidence for the structural observation in the body. Milgrom (Leviticus 17–22, AB 3A) and Wenham (NICOT) both discuss the parallelism of the two blemish codes, with differing inferences; the sermon takes the fact of the parallelism itself and no more.
10. śārûaʿ (H8311; found at both 21:18 and 22:23) and qālûṭ (H7038; 22:23): checked word by word against BibleHub, the Strong numbers at 22:23 are H8311 and H7038 respectively; BDB, under śāraʿ, gives have a limb too long, and under qālaṭ, be contracted, stunted. That man and beast share the single word śārûaʿ is a further internal witness to the parallelism of note 9.
11. nədəḇāh (H5071; 22:21, 23): the BDB entry gives voluntariness, freewill offering. Verse 23 grades the freewill offering against the vow (neder, H5088): the borderline animal may serve as the former but not as the latter. Milgrom treats the ritual logic of this distinction at length; the sermon adopts the fact of the distinction without extending his inferences.
12. tôdāh (H8426; 22:29): in full zeḇaḥ tôdāh, one class of peace offering. Leviticus 7:15 already fixed the rule that its flesh be eaten on the same day; 22:29-30 restates it within the general treatment of offerings and ties it directly to "acceptance" (22:29). Hartley's structural analysis of the chapter's second half (WBC 4) is a reference for the sermon's division.

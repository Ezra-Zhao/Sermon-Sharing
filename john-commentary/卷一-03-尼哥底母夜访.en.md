# Volume One · Chapter 3 — Why Being a Good Person Is Still Not Enough

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «卷一 · 第 3 篇　做好人，为什么还不够».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

Guangyi Zhao

(John 3:1-21)

---

In the previous chapter, Jesus turned water into wine at a wedding feast, and overturned the tables of the traders in the temple. The news spread quickly. At the opening of this chapter, the one who came to see Him was not an ordinary commoner, but a person of considerable weight. He had learning, he had status, and ordinarily when he spoke others had to listen. When he came, he deliberately chose the night.

John records this conversation as a conversation that did not succeed. The guest asked very politely, yet Jesus' answers did not follow the direction of his words, and the two seem to speak on two different channels. Yet it was precisely this night conversation that could not be brought together that left behind the most widely known sentence in the whole Bible. Reading this chapter, you may go a little more slowly, and see why Jesus said a sentence that sounds so weighty to such a good person: you must be born again.

## I. The Scripture Text

First, let us meet this guest:

> "There was a man of the Pharisees named Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews. This man came to Jesus by night and said to Him, 'Rabbi, we know that You are a teacher come from God; for no one can do these signs that You do unless God is with him.' Jesus answered and said to him, 'Most assuredly, I say to you, unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God.'" (John 3:1-3, NKJV)

Nicodemus was utterly confused; he took it in the literal sense:

> "Nicodemus said to Him, 'How can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb and be born?' Jesus answered, 'Most assuredly, I say to you, unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God. That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Do not marvel that I said to you, "You must be born again." The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear the sound of it, but cannot tell where it comes from and where it goes. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit.'" (John 3:4-8, NKJV)

In the second half of the conversation, Jesus pointed the words toward His own future outcome:

> "'And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have eternal life.'" (John 3:14-15, NKJV)

Immediately following is that sentence many people can recite, yet may not know the source of:

> "'For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life. For God did not send His Son into the world to condemn the world, but that the world through Him might be saved.'" (John 3:16-17, NKJV)

There are also a few words to clarify here first.

One is "born again." The Greek word here has two layers of meaning: one layer is "once more," and another layer is "from above." Nicodemus heard the former layer, which is why he asked whether one could enter the mother's womb again. What Jesus was really speaking of was the latter layer: not living the old days over again, but receiving a new life from above, from God. When the Lü Zhenzhong Translation handles such double meanings, it often brings out this layer of meaning of "from above," and may be taken as a reference. The Chinese rendering "rebirth" translates only the meaning of "once more"; that layer of meaning of being born from above must be recovered from the context.

Another is "wind." The wind in verse 8 and the Spirit are, in the original text, the same word. Jesus was using the wind as an analogy: the wind cannot be seen, yet you hear its sound and see the leaves move, and know that it really has blown. The work of the Holy Spirit within a person is also like this: not a set of procedures that can be laid out on a table for inspection, but a real change whose source cannot be seen, yet whose result can be seen.

There is also "only begotten Son." This word speaks not only of a unique closeness, but also of a relationship between this Son and the Father of which there is no second copy. When Jesus said afterward that God sent His Son not to condemn the world, half the weight of that sentence rests on this word: what God brought out was the One dearest to Him.

## II. Exposition

First, look at who Nicodemus was. The Pharisees were the group of people at the time who most earnestly kept the law; "a ruler of the Jews" means that he had entered the Sanhedrin, which was the Jews' own highest council and court. "The teacher of Israel" (3:10) is Jesus' form of address for him, meaning that he himself was a teacher who taught the people the law. Such a person had all three: learning, conduct, and status. He came at night, and John does not state the reason plainly. Some guess he was afraid of being seen by colleagues; some guess that the crowds were many by day, and only at night could one talk deeply. In the Gospel of John, night and light are often a pair, and this background may be noted, but guesses should not be written as established conclusions. At least one point is clear: he came in earnest. He opened by acknowledging that Jesus had come from God, and acknowledged the signs as evidence — in the circle of leaders at the time, this was already a very difficult sentence of fairness to speak.

But Jesus did not continue chatting along with his politeness. Nicodemus was talking about who You are and what signs You have done; Jesus directly changed the topic onto the man himself: unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God. This sentence is not an answer; it is a diagnosis. It amounts to saying: what you really need to ask tonight is not about My origin, but about whether you yourself can enter. Nicodemus' life's capital was precisely that he felt he was already inside: born a Jew, keeping the law, being a teacher. But Jesus said entering the kingdom of God does not depend on these accumulations, but on another birth.

Why use birth as an analogy? Because birth is a matter with several characteristics that no one can alter. A person cannot give birth to himself; birth is not an achievement, it is something received; after birth, what is obtained is a new life, not a few patches sewn onto the old life. Jesus' following sentence, "That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit," means exactly this. However good the life given by parents, it is only at this layer of the flesh. The layer of life that the kingdom of God requires, the flesh cannot give; it must be born of the Holy Spirit. These words are weighty for Nicodemus, and weighty for everyone who relies on self-cultivation to save themselves: cultivation can change habits; it cannot change the source.

The sentence "born of water and the Spirit" had a shadow long ago in the Old Testament. The prophet Ezekiel prophesied that God would sprinkle clean water on people to make them clean, and would put a new spirit within them (see Ezekiel 36:25-27). Water speaks of washing clean, the Holy Spirit speaks of new life, and the two put together are exactly the being born again that Jesus speaks of here. Nicodemus was a teacher of the Old Testament, and Jesus' sentence "Are you the teacher of Israel, and do not know these things?" was no light rebuke — precisely because these words were written in the very book he taught every day, yet he had never applied them to himself.

The story of the bronze serpent was even more familiar to him. The Israelites complained against God in the wilderness, and many were bitten to death by poisonous serpents, so God told Moses to make a bronze serpent and set it on a pole, and everyone who was bitten, when he looked at the bronze serpent, lived (see Numbers 21:4-9). Jesus says the Son of Man must likewise be lifted up. "Lifted up" is a double meaning in the Gospel of John: both being hung up high on the cross, and being glorified. The method of salvation was as strange as it was back then: those who were saved back then were not saved by taking medicine, nor by driving the serpents away, but by lifting their heads and looking; so today, obtaining eternal life is not by first perfecting oneself, but by believing in the One who was lifted up. Verse 16, which follows immediately, is then not a lone slogan standing by itself. It is explaining why God was willing to let His own Son be lifted up. The answer is only one word: love. And as for the purpose for which God sent His Son, Jesus, afraid that people would misunderstand, specifically added a sentence: not to condemn the world, but that the world through Him might be saved. Words about judgment come later as well (3:18-21), but the order must not be reversed. He came first to save people.

The end of this passage is the contrast between light and darkness. The light has come into the world, and some people are unwilling to come to the light — not because they have not understood the principle, but because they are afraid their deeds will be exposed. Those who do the truth, on the contrary, are willing to come to the light. Reading these sentences back against Nicodemus' night visit, the flavor comes out: he came at night. No wonder that after this chapter John records Nicodemus two more times. Once was when his colleagues wanted to seize Jesus, and he stood up and spoke a fair sentence, asking that a person be heard first before being condemned (see John 7:50-51); the other time was after Jesus' death, when he brought a hundred pounds of myrrh and aloes and, together with Joseph of Arimathea, buried Jesus' body (see John 19:39). A hundred pounds of spices is the scale of a royal burial. How far a man who came at night to ask questions finally traveled, to bury the body of a condemned criminal in full view of everyone — John does not write it plainly, leaving it for the reader to measure for himself.

## III. Application

Why is being a good person still not enough? This chapter's point of entry begins with Nicodemus.

Chinese people have a very complete set of ideas about a good person: do not steal, do not rob, be filial to one's parents, act according to conscience, and at festivals burn some incense and worship a little, seeking peace and safety. A person who has lived a whole lifetime like this, hearing that he must be born again, will mostly feel unconvinced in his heart: I have not done anything bad — why say I cannot enter? Nicodemus has asked this feeling of being unconvinced for us. His qualifications were harder than most of ours: religion was his own profession, his conduct had a reputation, his status was right there. Jesus, toward him, did not first praise him with a "you have done quite well," and then tactfully supplement a few shortcomings. What Jesus said was: you must be born again. This sentence does not say that good behavior has no value. Filial piety, honesty, doing good — of course all these are good, and society is held up by them too. It speaks of another matter: good behavior cannot solve the problem of the source of life. However clean the leaves of a tree are wiped, if the root is bad, the leaves will not stay clean for long. Our deepest fault is not in some particular wrong thing we did, but in the fact that we are the kind of people who again and again do wrong. Changing a particular wrong is easy; changing the person — by oneself, one cannot change it.

Next, why is religion not enough either? Nicodemus was not without religion; he was an expert within religion. The rules he knew best, the feasts he kept most completely, the Scriptures he could teach to others. Yet sitting before Jesus at night, he still did not understand the most essential matter. This gives a reminder to many people who seek peace within religion: when the rituals have all been performed and the merits recorded in full, that uneasiness in the heart may not necessarily leave with them. Many people burn incense and go home, and at night still cannot sleep, still fearing the same things. Being born again does not speak of adding another set of rules, but of God placing new life directly within a person. This is also why Jesus said the same sentence to Nicodemus and to ordinary commoners. The higher one's status, the easier it is to mistakenly think oneself nearer to the answer. According to this chapter, distance is not reckoned by status.

John 3:16 is often printed on its own, posted on stadium stands, printed on cards. Viewed alone, it is easy to see it as a warm word of blessing. Read back in this chapter, it is actually very hard. It first acknowledges that people will perish, and only then says God made those who believe not perish; it first says God gave His only begotten Son, and only then says this is called love. Love here is not a feeling, but an action that paid a price. For those who have not yet believed, there is here a question worth thinking about seriously: if God were only the One high above who condemns, He could very well not have sent His Son. He sent His Son, and stated plainly that it was not to condemn, but to save people. Then people's rejection of Him can hardly be explained any longer by the sentence that He is too severe. Verse 19 states it very plainly: people do not come to the light, often not because the light is too glaring, but because there are things they do not want exposed. You may not accept this judgment, but it is worth testing against your own days.

There is also the analogy of the wind, spoken to those who want to understand faith completely before beginning. Nicodemus asked twice "how can these things be?" — he truly wanted to understand, and Jesus did not laugh at his ignorance. Jesus only reminded him: the principle of the wind — you cannot explain it fully, yet you experience it every day. A life born of the Holy Spirit is also like this: it comes first, and understanding follows. You need not wait until you have answered all your questions before being willing to admit the wind has already blown upon you.

In the end, the most moving thing about Nicodemus on this night is that he was willing to come. For a person with status to be willing at night to lay down his airs, sit before a young rabbi with no academic credentials, and ask questions — this step was already not easy. Many people are stuck for a lifetime, not because they are not clever, but because they cannot lay down that face, afraid to admit they have in fact not yet entered. If, reading to here, there is also a sentence in your heart — "it seems I have not yet entered" — there is no need to feel ashamed. The first person to ask this sentence out loud was a teacher.

## IV. Response

After reading this chapter, take a quiet moment and think about a few questions.

- If you gave your conduct, your efforts, and your religious life a total score, how many points do you think would count as being able to enter? Who set this passing line, and are you at peace with it in your own heart?
- Nicodemus had everything, yet Jesus said he must be born again. Where does this sentence make you feel unconvinced? Is it that you feel it is unfair to good people, or that you are afraid it also holds true for you?
- "Not to condemn the world, but that the world through Him might be saved." Does the image of God in your heart look more like a judge waiting to condemn, or like a person who came to look for people and save them? Where did this image come from?
- The wind cannot be seen, yet its results can be seen. In these years, have you experienced a kind of power whose source you cannot explain, yet which has truly changed you?

If you are willing, you may follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, I have always felt that I am fairly a good person, and thought that this was enough. Tonight I heard You say to Nicodemus that a person must be born again. If I truly need a new life that comes from above, please let me first understand this point, and then lead me, step by step, into it. Amen."

There is also an entrance for continuing onward. In the previous chapter we read chapter 2: Jesus kept the good wine until now; in this chapter, He received a teacher at night. In the next chapter we will read John chapter 4, and the person is completely different: a Samaritan woman who came alone at noon to draw water, with no status, and with a past she could not speak of. What Jesus spoke to her about was living water. You may also find a Christian friend you trust and ask him to tell you about his own experience of being born again, and hear what sound that wind left upon him.

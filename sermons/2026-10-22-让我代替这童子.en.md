## Summary (about 100 words)

Joseph ordered his silver cup to be hidden in Benjamin's sack, setting up one final test. The cup was found, yet none of the brothers abandoned the youngest and fled; together they tore their clothes and together returned to the city. Judah stepped forward with a long plea, recounting his aged father's pain of losing a son and the life bound up with the lad's life, restating his own surety, and finally saying: let me abide instead of the lad as a bondman. The man who sold his brother was willing to be a slave in his brother's place — true repentance is willing to pay the cost and stand in.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

This chapter begins with an order and ends with a request. At the beginning, the governor of Egypt tells his steward: put my silver cup in the sack of the youngest brother. At the end, a middle-aged man kneels before this governor and says: please let me abide instead of the lad, staying here as a bondman, and let the lad return with his brothers. One chapter of the Bible, beginning with a cup and ending with one person willing to stand in for another. Between the two lies the whole process by which eleven brothers were remade.

Before preaching this chapter, let us first get the names and the old account straight. The governor who gave the order is Joseph, sold into Egypt by his brothers more than twenty years ago. The one into whose sack the cup was put is his youngest brother by the same father and mother, Benjamin. The one kneeling to plead is Judah — the very man who years ago proposed selling Joseph to the Ishmaelites and received twenty pieces of silver for him. More than twenty years ago, he stood by the pit and with his own hands pushed his brother onto the road of slavery; more than twenty years later, he stands in the hall and with his own mouth asks to be left in the place of a slave, so that another brother may go home. Brothers and sisters, this is the chapter in which the Bible writes human change most solidly.

Let us first read several key passages and set up the skeleton of the whole chapter:

"And he commanded the steward of his house, saying, Fill the men's sacks with food, as much as they can carry, and put every man's money in his sack's mouth. And put my cup, the silver cup, in the sack's mouth of the youngest, and his corn money." (Genesis 44:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"Then they speedily took down every man his sack to the ground... and he searched, and began at the eldest, and left at the youngest: and the cup was found in Benjamin's sack. Then they rent their clothes." (Genesis 44:11-13, excerpt, KJV)

"For thy servant became surety for the lad unto my father, saying, If I bring him not unto thee, then I shall bear the blame to my father for ever. Now therefore, I pray thee, let thy servant abide instead of the lad a bondman to my lord; and let the lad go up with his brethren." (Genesis 44:32-33, excerpt, KJV)

"Now therefore when I come to thy servant my father, and the lad be not with us; seeing that his life is bound up in the lad's life; it shall come to pass, when he seeth that the lad is not with us, that he will die." (Genesis 44:30-31, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today we are going to watch a group of men who once sold their brother walk, step by step, to the point of being willing to be slaves in their brother's place. Examine our repentance as well: does it have only tears, or is it also willing to pay the cost? Let that sentence, "let me abide instead of the lad," knock on our hearts today as well. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Silver Cup in the Sack — A Test, Not a Frame-Up (44:1-13)

First, connect the scene. The brothers had come down to Egypt a second time, bringing the youngest brother Benjamin with them, and had eaten a feast at Joseph's table, at which Benjamin received five times as much food as the others. Having eaten and drunk their fill, the cord that had been stretched tight over two journeys seemed at last to loosen a little. The youngest brother their father had entrusted to them was safe and sound, the grain they had bought was loaded on the donkeys, and tomorrow they could set out for home and bring the good news to their aged father Jacob. On just such a most relaxed early morning, Joseph gave that order.

The order had three layers. The first layer: fill the sacks with grain, as much as the donkeys could carry — given in full. The second layer: put each man's money back in his sack — as on the previous journey, no payment taken. The third layer, and the fuse of the whole chapter: "And put my cup, the silver cup, in the sack's mouth of the youngest." The steward did as he was told. At daylight, the men and donkeys left the city and had not gone far when Joseph gave another order: Up, follow after the men; and when thou dost overtake them, say unto them, Wherefore have ye rewarded evil for good? Is not this it in which my lord drinketh, and whereby indeed he divineth? ye have done evil in so doing.

Here we must first say a distinguishing word on Joseph's behalf: this order was a test, not a frame-up for condemnation. The two things look alike on the surface, but their weight is entirely different. A frame-up fastens guilt on an innocent person, and its purpose is condemnation; a test places a person in a situation where he can freely choose, and its purpose is to see whether he has really changed. If Joseph had truly wanted revenge, he had plenty of means at hand: on the previous journey he had already detained Simeon, and he held a governor's authority; he could have fastened any charge he liked on them and thrown all eleven into prison together. He did not. The prop he used was a cup; the situation he set up left a way out at every point — the cup was placed in Benjamin's sack alone, and the brothers were entirely free to abandon this one man, mount their donkeys, and flee back to Canaan each on his own. The door of escape stood open. Precisely because the door of escape stood open, the choice they made next counted.

What exactly was Joseph testing? He was testing only one thing: whether they would abandon a brother again. More than twenty years ago, beside the pit at Dothan, what had the ten brothers done to the youngest brother? They had stripped him of his coat of many colors, thrown him into the pit, sat down beside it to eat bread, and afterwards sold him, then taken a kid of the goats, dipped the coat in its blood, and deceived their aged father, saying Joseph had been torn by a wild beast. That time, the one abandoned was the son their father loved most. This time, it was the turn of another son their father loved most — Rachel's other son, Benjamin — to have "stolen goods" searched out of his sack and, by the logic of the situation, to have to remain as a slave. The question was exactly the same: a beloved youngest brother is about to lose his freedom — will you keep yourselves safe and leave first, or will you stay and accompany him? The question Joseph set was not a test of cleverness; it was the most painful old question of their lives. With this one cup he meant to measure the brothers' hearts: were they still the hearts of more than twenty years ago, or had they been exchanged for new ones?

We should also take this occasion to speak the matter of divination through clearly, so that hearers do not get stuck here. In the words the steward relayed when he overtook them there is divination, and later Joseph himself also says, "wot ye not that such a man as I can certainly divine?" This word, in the original, is the verb nachash (נָחַשׁ, H5172), to practice divination, to seek omens [1]. Scholarship has always debated whether Joseph really practiced divination: on one view, the Egyptian court did have a custom of divination by cup, and Joseph was merely speaking in his governor's identity; on another view, this line was itself part of the script of the test, matching the Egyptian dignitary he was playing, so that the brothers would fully believe the situation before their eyes. Judging by the narrative of Genesis as a whole, Joseph interpreted dreams by God's revelation, and he said with his own mouth that interpretations belong to God and that God would give Pharaoh an answer of peace; the text never narrates him actually performing divination. The steady reading, therefore, is this: the line is more likely an accommodating statement matching his identity as Egypt's governor within the test, identity language performed for the brothers, and one may not assert on its basis that Joseph practiced divination. With the facts and the debate laid out clearly, we do not pronounce judgments that go beyond what the Bible says.

The scene of the pursuit is written tightly. The steward overtook them and first delivered a stern speech. The brothers' response is the foreshadowing of the first turn of the whole chapter. They said: Wherefore saith my lord these words? God forbid that thy servants should do according to this thing: behold, the money which we found in our sacks' mouths we brought again unto thee ... how then should we steal out of thy lord's house silver or gold? With whomsoever of thy servants it be found, both let him die, and we also will be my lord's bondmen. These words were spoken hurriedly and to the full, carrying a sense of grievance and also the tone of the old self — words spoken to the absolute first. The steward drew the terms back one notch: let it be according to your words, but he with whom it is found shall be my servant, and ye shall be blameless. Then the searching began at the eldest and ended at the youngest — the narrative pen deliberately slows down here, one sack after another, like a knife cutting inch by inch — "and the cup was found in Benjamin's sack."

At what comes next, please fix your eyes on one action: "Then they rent their clothes." Rent clothes are the mark of extreme grief. But more important than the rending is what they did after the rending. Every man loaded his donkey — note, not each fleeing for his own life — "and they returned to the city." Eleven men, not one missing, all turned back. What awaited them in the city? An angry governor, a charge that could not be explained away, and possible imprisonment. The road back to the city was a road into danger. More than twenty years ago, they had sat down by the pit to eat and watched their brother sent away with their own eyes; more than twenty years later, they stood outside the city, and not one man said, "The cup was his, the trouble is of his making, we will go first." Brothers and sisters, whether a person has changed is not seen in fair weather; it is seen in the first reaction in a desperate situation. If anyone had wanted to flee at that moment, the narrative would surely have written it; the Bible does not mention fleeing in a single word, because truly no one fled. They had already changed — this passage is the first piece of evidence. Joseph was waiting in the hall, and the answer he wanted, they had already half answered with their footsteps.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Confrontation in the Hall — No One Keeps Himself Safe, and Judah Steps Forward (44:14-17)

Back in the city, Judah and his brothers entered Joseph's house. The Bible specially records a stroke: Joseph was still in the house. The governor was waiting for them. Before him "they fell before him on the ground." Please note the name at the head: on this journey of the narrative, the one leading has quietly become Judah. Formerly it was mostly the eldest son Reuben who led, but the counsel Reuben offered in that year was to throw his brother into the pit, and when he came home he cleared himself by saying, "Spake I not unto you, saying, Do not sin against the child?" — he was a man soft-hearted but unable to stand firm. On these two journeys down to Egypt, the one who truly stood up to speak, stood surety before his father, and opened his mouth before the governor was, gradually, always Judah. A person's capacity to bear is recognized, bit by bit by everyone and also by the narrative, in the times when he stands up at the moments when it is his to stand up.

Joseph opened his mouth, still with that identity line: What deed is this that ye have done? wot ye not that such a man as I can certainly divine? The meaning of this sentence was not to boast of sorcery, but to press down with the full weight of a governor: did you think you could hide it from me? The one who answered was Judah. This first speech of his is short, yet every word lands in place: "What shall we say unto my lord? what shall we speak? or how shall we clear ourselves? God hath found out the iniquity of thy servants: behold, we are my lord's servants, both we, and he also with whom the cup is found." This sentence has three layers. The first layer: he does not argue about the cup — it was searched out, the evidence of person and thing is all present, and to argue further would be dishonest evasion; he does not evade dishonestly. The second layer: what he confesses is not the single matter of stealing a cup; he speaks of "iniquity": God hath found out the iniquity of thy servants. This mind in the plural, the Egyptian governor could not understand, but we can — what he confesses in his heart is the crime of more than twenty years ago, the selling of his brother. Heaven's net is vast; whether the cup was true or false can be set aside for the moment — the account they owed God, owed their brother, and owed their father, God has today found out all at once. The third layer is the most important: he binds himself and Benjamin together in his speech — we, and he also with whom the cup was found, are servants. Not he alone as a bondman, but we together. If you punish, punish us all; if you keep, keep us all. The youngest brother does not bear it alone. Brothers and sisters, this is already the complete opposite of the pit at Dothan: that time, ten men joined hands to push one man out; this time, one man at the head first binds himself together with that one man.

Joseph's answer twists the test to its last turn. He said: God forbid that I should do so: but the man in whose hand the cup is found, he shall be my servant; and as for you, get you up in peace unto your father. These words sound fair, but they are in fact the harshest question in the whole chapter. With his own hand he pushed the door of escape even wider and set a golden rim on it: go, go rightfully, even I am letting you go; leave only one, just one. Go back to Canaan, and when your aged father asks, say that the youngest brother stole the governor's cup and was kept as a slave — these words would even be, sentence by sentence, "the facts." The script of more than twenty years ago, in a changed version, lay before them again today: sacrifice one youngest brother, preserve all the rest, and still be able to give their father an account. What Joseph wanted to see was precisely this one second: would any one of the eleven men step out through that door first?

No one did. The Bible writes: "Then Judah came near unto him." "Came near" — these words are the hinge of this chapter. At Dothan, Judah had also come forward once — he had come forward and said, let us sell him to the Ishmaelites, and let not our hand be upon him. The two comings forward ran in exactly opposite directions: one coming forward pushed a brother out in exchange for silver; this coming forward sends himself up in exchange for his brother. To what degree a person has repented is seen in whether, at the very point of his old sin, he is willing to do a precisely opposite action. Judah did not first consult with his brothers, did not wait for Reuben to declare his position; he himself came near and said to the governor: O my lord, let thy servant, I pray thee, speak a word in my lord's ears, and let not thine anger burn against thy servant: for thou art even as Pharaoh. The longest soliloquy begins from this one step.

Before entering this plea, let us honestly settle the account of Judah's first half of life, so that we know the weight of this speech. He was not a bearer by birth. He had sold his brother. In Genesis chapter 38, he left his brothers, went down to an Adullamite and stayed with his friend, married and had sons, and in the matter of his daughter-in-law Tamar, he first refused to give her his youngest son according to the custom, and afterwards, when she produced the evidence she had obtained by her plan, he confessed before everyone in one sentence: she hath been more righteous than I. That sentence was the beginning of the turn in Judah's life — for the first time he confessed his own wrong before everyone, without covering and without shifting blame. Later still, before the second journey down to Egypt, their father would not let Benjamin go with them; Reuben's surety was, "Slay my two sons, if I bring him not to thee," and their father rejected it in one breath; Judah's surety was another sentence: "I will be surety for him; of my hand shalt thou require him: if I bring him not unto thee, and set him before thee, then let me bear the blame for ever." Their father believed this sentence, and only then released the youngest brother. This word "surety," in the original 'arav (עָרַב, H6148), to stand surety, to give a pledge, to put oneself up as collateral [2]. What Judah pledged before his father was not property, not sons; it was himself — the one who would bear the blame forever was he. Now, in this moment in the hall, the day of honoring the surety has arrived. Whether the words a person spoke in his youth count is not seen in how earnestly he spoke them at the time; it is seen in whether, when the day of honoring them arrives, he still acknowledges the account. Judah acknowledged the account. That one step of his coming near trod precisely on the sentence he had spoken with his own mouth months before: "I will be surety for him."

So the second part closes here: by this point in the test, the brothers' change already had two pieces of evidence — outside the city no one fled, and in the hall no one kept himself safe. But Joseph had not yet revealed himself, because there was still one last question unanswered: to what price is bearing willing to pay? Merely saying "we will all be bondmen" is not yet the deepest; if one is willing to leave another behind and go back oneself, bearing is just a pleasant sentence. The deepest sentence had to be spoken by Judah himself. The next part is the longest individual speech in Genesis, and the page of heaviest feeling in the whole book of Genesis.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Judah's Plea — The Life Bound Up and "Let Me Abide Instead" (44:18-34)

Judah's speech, from verse 18 to verse 34, seventeen verses in one breath, is the longest individual speech in Genesis. Among so many figures in the whole book, God's pen gave the longest soliloquy to Judah, and to this one plea. Let us read it slowly; its structure is very clear, going down in four layers: first restating how they came here, then restating the aged father's pain, then restating his own surety, and finally offering himself as the substitute.

The first layer: restating how they came here. Judah began from the beginning: my lord asked his servants, saying, Have ye a father, or a brother? And we said unto my lord, We have a father, an old man, and a child of his old age, a little one; and his brother is dead, and he alone is left of his mother, and his father loveth him. Twice you sent us to bring him down, saying, bring him down unto me, that I may set mine eyes upon him. Why did Judah repeat once more what everyone already knew? Because a plea is not a quarrel; a plea brings the other party's heart, bit by bit, into the situation of the person concerned. The governor sat in a high place and saw a case of a lost cup; what Judah wanted him to see was a whole household behind the case: an old man, two young sons who had lost their mother, and a pain of losing a son that had lasted more than twenty years. He was not presenting new evidence; he was appearing in court on behalf of a person who was not present — his aged father Jacob. Brothers and sisters, this is what a plea looks like: the one who pleads stands in the middle, with one hand holding the majesty of the judge and the other hand holding the reality of the sufferer, bringing the hearts of both sides into one place. In our praying for others, our speaking up for family, and our speaking on behalf of the weak, we should all learn this patience: do not shout slogans; tell the facts through fully.

The second layer: restating the aged father's pain. This is the heart of the whole speech. Judah said, we went back and told our father, and our father said: Ye know that my wife bare me two sons: and the one went out from me, and I said, Surely he is torn in pieces; and I saw him not since: and if ye take this also from me, and mischief befall him, ye shall bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to the grave. Behind every word is something real: Jacob's pain at losing Joseph had not closed for more than twenty years; the scene of his holding the bloodied coat and identifying the body, the sons had seen with their own eyes — and more piercing still, that pain was made by their own hands. For Judah today to recount this pain in its entirety to "the governor" was equal to reciting, in the presence of the victim, what the crime he himself had committed in that year had caused. He omitted nothing and softened nothing. He went on: my father's life is bound up in the lad's life. In the original this is nefesh qeshurah (נֶפֶשׁ קְשׁוּרָה, nefesh, H5315, "life, soul," plus qashar, H7194, "to bind, to tie"), translated literally, "his life is bound up in his life" [3]. The thread by which the old man lives is tied to this child. If the child cannot return, the thread breaks, and when our father sees that the lad is not with us, he will die — the words "ye shall bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to the grave" would be fulfilled on this journey. When Judah said this, he knew most clearly in his heart: if their father truly died this time, the one who killed their father would not be the governor of Egypt, but this group of sons — a second time. He refused to let history repeat itself a second time. This refusal is the bone that repentance has grown.

The third layer: restating the surety. Judah repeated in Egypt's hall, word by word, what he had said in Canaan months before: "For thy servant became surety for the lad unto my father, saying, If I bring him not unto thee, then I shall bear the blame to my father for ever." Note that he did not hide this relationship from the governor; on the contrary, he brought it into the open: I am not an outsider; I am the surety. If this child cannot return, the blame falls on my head first; with my father I have already spoken words like a signed bond. The identity of surety was originally effective only between father and son, yet Judah raised it before the judge, meaning: when you dispose of this case, please count me in; I am not a bystander come to beg for mercy, I am a person who has a share in this matter. When people of the world stand surety, most of what they guarantee is money, and when the money is not enough, goods are pledged against it; this surety of Judah's guaranteed a person, and what was pledged against it was also one person — himself.

The fourth layer: offering himself as the substitute. The highest point of the whole speech is only two sentences: "Now therefore, I pray thee, let thy servant abide instead of the lad a bondman to my lord; and let the lad go up with his brethren. For how shall I go up to my father, and the lad be not with me? lest peradventure I see the evil that shall come on my father." First savor that word: instead — let me stand in his place. He would be a bondman; let me be. He would lose his freedom; let me lose mine. If a man is to be kept, keep me. Spoken from Judah's mouth, this sentence outweighs every other word in the chapter. Why? Because twenty-two years ago, the one who proposed selling Joseph as a slave was he. At that time what he said was: come, and let us sell him to the Ishmaelites, and let not our hand be upon him, for he is our brother and our flesh — at that time he stood by the pit reckoning an account: sold, there are twenty pieces of silver; killed, there is nothing. Today, standing in the hall, he reckoned a different account: if I return, my father dies; if I stay, my father lives and the lad is free — then keep me. The same mouth, the same question of slavery, gave an exactly opposite answer. Brothers and sisters, the evidence of true repentance is never tears. Tears can flow in emotion, and when they have flowed, life goes on as before. True repentance is walking back to the very point of the old sin and doing an exactly opposite action, one that costs oneself. We have not read a single word of Judah's tears; his cost we see with complete clarity: the freedom of the second half of his life.

Place this bearing back into the whole family and look again. Judah was the fourth son, not the eldest. By human order, it was not his turn to step in front first; by the sin of that year, the idea of selling his brother was his, and in bearing blame he indeed should stand near the front — and this is exactly the logic in his words: I have a share in the sin, the surety was mine, so keep me alone. He did not push it to Reuben with a sentence, "you are the eldest"; he did not push it to Benjamin with a sentence, "the cup was in his sack"; he did not push it to his father with a sentence, "it was your favoritism." The words of one who bears have a common mark: the subject is always "I." I became surety, I bear the blame, let me abide instead. Between a surety and one who truly bears, what lies between is not age; it is whether one is willing to take all the responsibility in with the word "I."

Then, the pulpit must here lift its head gently and with measure: Judah's substitution is a shadow, not the substance. The shadow points to the substance and says: in days to come there will be One who will accomplish completely what Judah wished to do but could not do in full. What Judah could substitute for was only one brother; he could only be a bondman in his place, he could not buy back the Joseph who had already been sold, still less wash clean the sins of the whole family. But the One who later came from the tribe of Judah did precisely the complete version of the four words "let me abide instead": Christ pleads for us before the Father, not exchanging His own freedom for one person's freedom, but "He hath borne our griefs and carried our sorrows"; the New Testament states this matter plainly: Christ, having offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down — and God made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might be made the righteousness of God in Him. The words we cite today from Galatians and First Peter are so that everyone may see this typological line — Galatians 1:4 says Christ gave Himself for our sins, according to the will of God our Father; First Peter 3:18 says Christ hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that He might bring us to God. The measure must be kept here: today's main figure is still Judah; we are not forcibly fitting Judah onto Christ, but saying that God first let us understand, in Judah, how the word "substitution" is written, so that afterwards, when we read the cross, we know the true weight of that word. However beautiful a shadow is, it must not seize the place of the substance; yet without the shadow first laid on the ground, many people would not even know where to look for the substance.

For application, take three things home. First, examine your own repentance: for what matter have you shed tears? After shedding them, at the very point of your old sin, have you done an exactly opposite action? Has the apology you owe been spoken, has what must be repaid been repaid, has the position that must be given up been given up? Repentance without cost is mostly only emotion. Second, learn to be a surety-like family member and co-worker: at home and in the church, when something happens to someone, are you willing to stand out first and say, "count me in"? Surety is not clearing oneself when trouble comes; surety is knowing, at the moment of signing, that on the day of honoring it, you yourself must stand in front. Third, learn Judah's plea: for that person in your family whose life is bound up with yours — an aged father or mother, a child far away, a spouse in sickness — bring the facts before God, and also before those who can help, telling them through patiently and fully, without shouting slogans. A plea is not the work of one sentence; it is persistence as long as a speech of seventeen verses.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Let us draw to a close. In Genesis chapter 44, one silver cup measured the human heart: it measured out that the brothers would no longer abandon the youngest brother, and it measured out that Judah had grown, from the man who sold his brother, into a man willing to be a slave in his brother's place. What Joseph set up was never a dead end; it was a mirror — and only after the mirror had done its work could the tears and the recognition of the next chapter follow.

Take two sentences home with you. The first: true repentance is not tears; it is being willing to pay the cost and stand in. The second: a surety grows into one who bears, not by speaking words to the full, but by standing in front on the day of honoring them. And at the end of these two sentences stands the true Substitute — the Lord Jesus. Judah said, "let me abide instead of the lad"; He said, "let me abide instead of the sinner." What Judah offered was the freedom of the second half of his life; what He offered was His own life. Every heart of ours that learns from Judah today should first kneel at His feet.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You that in Judah You let us see what repentance truly looks like: not crying once, but paying the cost; not pushing responsibility away, but saying, "let me abide instead." Forgive us our repentances that stopped at tears, and give us courage to return to the very point of our old sins and do the opposite thing. Thank You also for sending Your Son to be our Substitute forever — the just for the unjust, to bring us to You. May we, all our lives, be willing for family, for brothers, and for those whose lives are bound up with ours, to stand out and stand in front. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] nachash: Hebrew נָחַשׁ (H5172), verb, to practice divination, to seek omens, to infer by omens; Genesis 44:5, the steward's words "whereby indeed he divineth," and 44:15, Joseph's self-description "such a man as I can certainly divine," both use this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Scholarship debates this: against the background of the Egyptian court's custom of divination by cup, one view holds that Joseph's line is test dialogue matching his identity as Egypt's governor; in the narrative of Genesis, Joseph's interpretation of dreams is always attributed to God's revelation (see Genesis 40:8 and 41:16), and he is never narrated as actually performing divination; this manuscript takes the "accommodating statement" as the steady reading and states plainly that one may not assert that Joseph practiced divination; this is an exegetical judgment, marked separately from the facts of word meaning.

[2] 'arav: Hebrew עָרַב (H6148), verb, to stand surety, to give a pledge, to put oneself up as collateral; Genesis 44:32, Judah says, "thy servant became surety for the lad," echoing Genesis 43:9, where Judah said to his father, "I will be surety for him; of my hand shalt thou require him"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "what he pledged was himself" is a homiletical exposition based on the two passages, Genesis 43:9 and 44:32, and is noted as exposition.

[3] nefesh qeshurah: Hebrew נֶפֶשׁ (nefesh, H5315, life, soul, person) with the participial form qeshurah of קָשַׁר (qashar, H7194, to bind, to tie, to be bound together); Genesis 44:30, Judah says, "his life is bound up in the lad's life," translated literally, "his life is bound up in his life"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Genesis 44:31, "when he seeth that the lad is not with us, that he will die," as the scriptural basis for this manuscript's expression that the thread of the father's life is tied to the child.

[4] gevi'a: Hebrew גָּבִיעַ (H1375), noun, a cup, a goblet; Genesis 44:2, Joseph ordered "my cup, the silver cup" to be put into Benjamin's sack, and Genesis 44:5, 44:12, and 44:16-17 repeatedly mention this cup in the narrative; it is the prop of the test in the whole chapter; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's distinction that "the cup is a test, not a frame-up" is a narrative judgment based on Genesis 44:16-17, where Joseph states plainly that he would keep only one man and the rest could go up in peace; it is a homiletical exposition, marked separately from the facts of word meaning.

[5] 'eved: Hebrew עֶבֶד (H5650), noun, a servant, a bondman; Genesis 44:33, Judah pleads, "let thy servant abide instead of the lad a bondman to my lord," and Genesis 44:16 and 44:18 also use it repeatedly; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This word is the silver-cup test theme word chosen and supplemented by this manuscript on its own: the cost Judah offered falls precisely on the words "as a bondman," forming a narrative contrast with his selling his brother as a slave in that year (Genesis 37:27-28); the contrast is exposition and the word meaning is fact, the two marked separately.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "His life is bound up in the lad's life... Now therefore, I pray thee, let thy servant abide instead of the lad a bondman to my lord; and let the lad go up with his brethren." (Genesis 44:30, 33, KJV, excerpts)

In his youth Judah had an idea: sell his brother. That idea won him twenty pieces of silver and cost him more than twenty years of peace. On this day, with another brother about to be kept as a slave, he stood before the judge and said the opposite: let me remain instead of him. God measures a person's repentance not by tears, but by whether he is willing to return to the very point of his old sin and do the opposite thing, at cost to himself. Is there an old matter you cannot put down? Is there a person to whom you owe an apology, or an act of bearing? Do not only grieve in your heart today. Learn from Judah and step forward: confess what must be confessed, repay what must be repaid, stand in for whom you must stand in for. Words of surety most fear the day of honoring them, yet bearing is precisely what grows on the day of honoring them: on ordinary days anyone will say, "if something happens, count me in," but when the day truly comes to stay behind, to suffer loss, and to bear responsibility, only the one still willing to step forward truly counts. May we be such people today — at home, in the church, and among friends, not onlooking commentators, but bearers willing to pay the cost. And when you cannot stand firm, remember the One who has already stood in your place — the righteous for the unrighteous. He has paid in full; you need only live out that bearing, with gratitude.

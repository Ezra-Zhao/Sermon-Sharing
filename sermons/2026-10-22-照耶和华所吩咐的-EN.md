# As the LORD Commanded

**Preacher:** Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries

**Status:** Draft · Pending cross-review (Sermon Mass-Production Batch 2, 2026-10-22, unpublished) — Initial translation, pending review; translated from the Chinese original

**Review:** Passed two rounds of self-review on 2026-10-23, with minor fixes applied at parent level (see the Group Y review record); Gemini independent review pending (prepaid credits exhausted) — publication held pending that re-run.

**Scripture:** Exodus 39:1-43 (KJV)

**Continuity:** Continuing from Exodus chapter 38 — chapter 38 closed the making of the altar, the laver, and the court, and ended with a full accounting of the gold, silver, and bronze; chapter 39 completes the final group of work, the priestly garments, and brings all the finished work to Moses for inspection.

**Abstract:** This chapter is not a repetition of the commands in chapter 28, but their echo and fulfilment: the garments are made piece by piece, the tabernacle is handed over item by item, and each section closes with the words, “as the LORD commanded Moses.” Moses looks on all the work, sees that it was done as commanded, and blesses the people. Here obedience is no slogan; it is a completion that can be checked, presented, and blessed.

## I. An Echo Is Not a Repetition: The Meaning of the Execution Narrative (Exod 39:1a, and the Refrain of the Whole Chapter) | 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, when we reach the last chapters of Exodus, it is easy to feel that we have just read all this before. The materials, colours, and making of the holy garments were already set out in detail in chapter 28; the components of the tabernacle were likewise commanded one by one in chapters 25 to 31. Why, then, do chapters 35 to 39 say almost everything again, item by item? If the purpose were only to inform us of the design, the earlier commands would have been enough; to write it all again looks like repetition. The opening of this chapter gives us the answer: this second telling is not command but execution; it is not God saying once more what He wants, but the people presenting what they have made, proving that they made it as commanded. The command reveals God's intent; the execution reveals the people's response. Between the two lie the failure of the golden calf, and also the willing offerings of the people and the skilled work of Bezalel, Oholiab, and the craftsmen filled with the Spirit of God. By chapter 39 the narrative focus is no longer the design itself, but whether the three words “done as commanded” can withstand an item-by-item check.

This chapter has a signature sentence, and we must not slide past it. The Hebrew כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוָּה יְהוָה אֶת־מֹשֶׁה, “as the LORD commanded Moses,” appears in exactly the same fixed form seven times in verses 1 to 31, the section on the making of the garments — in verses 1, 5, 7, 21, 26, 29, and 31 — like seven seals stamped, section by section, on the ephod, the onyx stones on the shoulder pieces, the breastpiece, the robe, the sash, and the plate of pure gold. [1] Then in verses 32, 42, and 43 the author closes three more times with summary forms: the Israelites did “according to all that the LORD commanded Moses,” and Moses saw that they had done it “as the LORD had commanded.” Seven occurrences of the fixed refrain plus three summary statements make this the chapter in all of Exodus where the sentence is most densely concentrated. This is not poverty of vocabulary; it is deliberate rhythm: each time a group is finished, the narrative stops and checks it; and the standard for the check lies neither in the craftsman's taste nor in the people's eye, but in what the LORD commanded through Moses.

We must see this clearly at the factual level before we speak of application. Scripture here praises no craftsman for creativity, says nothing about their doing better than commanded, and records no alteration of their own in any single place. The excellence of the tabernacle work lies precisely in the fact that it does not take innovation as its honour, but faithfulness. The pattern was shown to Moses on the mountain; human skill was not given to set up another pattern, but to make the pattern already received into a real object without distortion. The gold had to be beaten into thin plates and cut into wires and worked in with the blue, purple, and scarlet yarn — work of the highest skill; the onyx stones had to be engraved with names, like the engravings of a signet — work of the finest care. The greater the skill, the more it shows in the detail; yet the direction of the skill is governed from first to last by one sentence: as the LORD commanded. This gives us a solid starting point: those who worship God first learn to listen, then to make; first receive what He has ordained, then offer up our skill. Reverse the order, and however fine the skill, it is not the obedience recorded in this chapter.

Note also that what these seven seals stamp is no scattered set of small objects, but the holy garments worn on the priest's body, representing all Israel. Obedience, in the end, must be worn on a person, brought into the holy place, and brought before God. It is not done when it is written in a ledger, nor when the materials are ready, but when it is made, put on, and used in service. So let us now follow the order of the text and see, group by group, how the garments were made, and how they correspond item by item to chapter 28.

## II. Garments for Service: The Ephod and the Breastpiece (Exod 39:1-21) | 08:00-24:00

Verse 1 first gives the whole group of garments a general name: of the blue, purple, and scarlet they made “cloths of service,” in Hebrew בִּגְדֵי שְׂרָד, the garments worn when ministering in the holy place, and they made the holy garments for Aaron. [2] The words “for service” remind us that these are not festival finery, nor vestments for human viewing, but working clothes — worn when handling the things of God in the holy place. Their splendour is not to show off the wearer; their separation is not to elevate the wearer. Aaron wears them in order to stand between God and the people, handling what no one may handle at will. So the first lesson of the holy garments is that office precedes glory: first the responsibility of service, then the clothing that befits the office.

The making of the ephod is recorded in verses 2 to 7, corresponding item by item to the command in 28:6-12. The materials are gold, blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, and fine twined linen. Verse 3 adds one detail of the making of the gold thread that the command did not spell out: they beat the gold into thin plates, cut it into wires, and worked it in with the coloured yarns. A command is often like that: the instruction is a single sentence, but the fulfilment passes through many unseen processes. No one would stand at the door of the tabernacle counting how the gold wires were beaten; yet the “gold” God commanded had to be real gold, really woven into the ephod, not fudged with a near substitute. Faithfulness often hides in just such unapplauded processes.

The ephod had two shoulder pieces joined at its two edges, so that it was coupled together; and the skilfully woven band on it was of the same work and the same materials — no second-rate material used to make up the difference. Verses 6 and 7 record the setting of the onyx stones: two onyx stones, enclosed in settings of gold, engraved with the names of the children of Israel like the engravings of a signet, and set on the shoulder pieces of the ephod as stones of memorial for the children of Israel. The names are engraved on the shoulders, and the position matters. The shoulder is where weight is borne. The priest does not enter the holy place empty-handed to display his dignity; he enters bearing the names of the twelve tribes on his shoulders. Six names on one stone, six on the other, not one missing. Memorial is not a fine-sounding phrase; it is engraving those one represents, name by name, bearing them up, and bringing them before God. From the very beginning Aaron's office is not for himself: however splendidly he is dressed, what his shoulders bear is still the names of others.

Verses 8 to 21 record the breastpiece, corresponding to 28:15-28. It was made by a skilled workman, of the same materials and work as the ephod, doubled into a square, a span long and a span wide, and set with four rows of stones. The text lists the four rows one by one: the first row a sardius, a topaz, and a carbuncle; the second row an emerald, a sapphire, and a diamond; the third row a ligure, an agate, and an amethyst; the fourth row a beryl, an onyx, and a jasper, all enclosed in settings of gold. The stones are all different, and the names are engraved on them too — verse 14 says plainly that the stones, twelve in number, were according to the names of the children of Israel, engraved like signets, each with its name. The breastpiece was set over the priest's heart, fastened with chains of gold, rings, and a blue cord, and joined to the ephod; verse 21 notes in particular that they bound the breastpiece by its rings to the rings of the ephod with a blue cord, so that it stayed above the skilfully woven band of the ephod and did not come loose from the ephod — and then comes the refrain again: as the LORD commanded Moses. [3]

The memorial on the shoulders and the memorial over the heart are two sides of one office. Borne on the shoulders, it is bearing in strength; fastened over the heart, it is remembrance in affection. Chapter 28 said that when Aaron went into the holy place he was to bear the breastpiece of judgment, with the names of the children of Israel, over his heart before the LORD continually as a memorial; and in the breastpiece were placed the Urim and the Thummim, over Aaron's heart, so that he would bear the judgment of the children of Israel before the LORD. In the execution in this chapter the function of the Urim and Thummim is not narrated again, but the position, the fastening, and the requirement that it not come loose are all made good, item by item. In other words, chapter 28 explained what this office means; chapter 39 proves that everything this office requires has been prepared and is in place, waiting only for the wearer to stand in his position. When we read an execution narrative, we should not complain that it speaks only of making and not of meaning; the meaning has been commanded, and what matters now is this: when the day of actual service comes, nothing is missing. Many spiritual failures are not failures to understand meaning, but the discovery, at the moment of need, that what should have been prepared was not prepared, and what should have been fastened tight had come loose. That the breastpiece must not come loose from the ephod is a detail worth pondering slowly: the heart that represents the people must be held fast to the office of service — not slack, not swinging, not liable to fall in the holy place.

See further that the materials of the garments came from the people's offerings, the handiwork from craftsmen filled with the Spirit, the pattern from God's command, and the names from the twelve tribes of Israel. One garment weaves together God's command, the people's offering, the craftsman's skill, and the remembrance of the whole congregation. That is the tabernacle work in miniature: not the work of any one person, but the whole congregation, each doing his part under the word of God, woven into a single garment. The next group, beginning with the all-blue robe, records the same principle — only this time even sound is written into the obedience.

The ephod and the breastpiece also share a point easily missed: both must be joined fast. The ephod's two shoulder pieces must be coupled at the edges; the breastpiece's chains must be fastened to the gold settings and rings above the shoulder pieces, and the blue cord must bind the lower rings of the breastpiece to the rings of the ephod. Scripture records these joins again and again, and they are not surplus tailoring detail. The joins are precisely where things come loose most easily, and where the whole garment is under the greatest strain in the movements of service. The priest must lift his hands to bless, stoop to handle the offerings, and go in and out of the holy place; if a join slackens, the breastpiece slips from over the heart, and the names of memorial tilt awry. The command specified every join, and the execution made every join good, showing that the remembrance God wants is not a remembrance hung on for show, but one that withstands a whole day's service and does not slip midway. Our remembrance of others and our intercession often fail at the joins: fervent at the start, slack afterwards; remembrance in words, broken in practice when the moment comes. This chapter teaches no theory of intercession; it simply shows us the fastening — bind it tight, and let it not come loose. If remembrance is to last, it must have just such concrete, unwearying binding.

## III. The All-Blue Robe, the Linen Coats, and the Plate of Pure Gold (Exod 39:22-31) | 24:00-42:00

Verses 22 to 26 record the robe, corresponding to 28:31-35. The robe of the ephod was woven all of blue; in Hebrew it is called מְעִיל. [4] The opening of the robe was in the middle, like the opening of a coat of mail, with a woven binding around the opening, “that it should not tear.” These words are very practical: however splendid the holy garment, it must withstand being worn and the movements of service; if the robe tears at the neck opening, the whole robe is ruined. The woven reinforcement looks unremarkable, yet it is the key to the robe's lasting service. The pattern God commanded provided even for this protection, “that it should not tear.” In the order of worship no detail is mere decoration: every binding exists so that service can continue.

The making of the hem is more striking still: on the hem they made pomegranates of blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, and bells of pure gold, and they put the bells between the pomegranates all around the hem — a golden bell and a pomegranate, a golden bell and a pomegranate. “Bell” in Hebrew is פַּעֲמֹן and “pomegranate” is רִמּוֹן. [5][6] Why such labour on a hem? Chapter 28, verse 35, already gave the answer: when Aaron wore this robe to minister, its sound was to be heard, so that when he went into the holy place before the LORD and when he came out, he would not die. Verse 26 of this chapter does not repeat that life-and-death sentence; it simply makes the alternating bells and pomegranates, item by item, and stamps the refrain over it. But the reader should join the two passages: the “that its sound may be heard” commanded in chapter 28 has in chapter 39 been done. Sound here is not performance but a sign: the high priest's coming is not secret or arbitrary; his footsteps carry a sound, showing that he comes according to his office, bearing those he represents, into the presence of God and back out again. Bells and pomegranates alternate, pairing sound with fruit: there is a ringing, and there is fruit. Ringing without fruit is empty sound; this chapter weaves the two onto the same hem, so that wherever the ministering man's steps go, sound and fruit appear together. We may leave the picture there, without forcing an allegory onto every item; the text itself joins only the making and the reason given in chapter 28, and we should receive it as the text joins it.

Verses 27 to 29 record the linen garments, corresponding to 28:39-42. For Aaron and his sons they made coats of fine linen, the work of a weaver — in Hebrew כְּתֹנֶת, the long garment worn next to the skin. [7] They also made the turban of fine linen, the ornate caps of fine linen, the linen trousers of fine twined linen, and the sash of fine twined linen with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, the work of an embroiderer. The high priest's garments are splendid; the ordinary priests' garments are chiefly the whiteness of fine linen. The ministering person must first be wrapped in clean fine linen, and only then can one speak of the glory outside. The order cannot be reversed: one does not first put on gold thread and stones and then find a way to be clean; the layer next to the skin is fine linen first, and only then the ephod, the breastpiece, and the robe. The garments wear this order on the body, reminding the wearer at every service: the one who draws near to God seeks cleanness first, and glory second.

Verses 30 and 31 are the summit of all the garments, and the last piece of the whole group: they made the plate of the holy crown of pure gold, and engraved on it, like the engraving of a signet, “HOLINESS TO THE LORD,” and they tied it with a blue cord and fastened it on the front of the turban. The plate is called צִיץ in Hebrew, properly a shining, flower-like object, made of pure gold and set in the most visible place. [8] All the garments — from shoulder pieces to hem, from breastpiece to sash — close at last on this small gold plate on the forehead. It is small, yet it stands highest and most visible; and the words on it are neither Aaron's name nor the craftsmen's names, but “HOLINESS TO THE LORD.” These words are the inscription of the whole garment project: the garments are holy, the wearer is set apart as holy, and the work handled is the LORD's. Chapter 28 said that this plate was to be always on Aaron's forehead, so that he would bear the iniquity committed in the holy things and the Israelites would be accepted before the LORD. Chapter 39 makes the plate and ties it on, which means: even the sign needed for bearing iniquity and being accepted by God has been prepared. Standing before the people, the first thing the high priest's forehead declares is not his merit, but his belonging.

Having read verses 1 to 31, we can make a simple check: the garments commanded in chapter 28 — ephod, breastpiece, robe, coat, turban, sash, trousers, and the plate of pure gold — appear in chapter 39 group by group, with no group missing; and each time a group is finished, the refrain appears once. Command and execution are like two lists that can be ticked off against each other. With this almost ledger-like manner Scripture tells us: when God's word is taken seriously, it can be checked item by item. Not roughly done, not nearly done, but this piece present and that piece present too; this fastening right and that position right too. In verse 32 the camera pulls back from the garments, the whole tabernacle work comes to its general close, and we enter the final and most important action of the chapter: presentation and inspection.

## IV. All the Work Was Finished: Presentation, Inspection, and Blessing (Exod 39:32-43) | 42:00-56:00

Verse 32 is the hinge of the whole chapter: “Thus was all the work of the tabernacle of the tent of the congregation finished,” and the children of Israel did according to all that the LORD commanded Moses. The phrasing of this sentence will not sound unfamiliar to a reader of Genesis. Genesis 2:1 says, “Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them”; in Genesis 2:2-3 God finished His work on the seventh day, rested, and blessed the seventh day. Here Exodus 39:32 says that all the work of the tabernacle was finished. Neither passage speaks of a small thing completed, but of a complete work, unfolded according to God's word, reaching the moment when “all was complete.” [9] This is an observation at the structural level: the narrative of the building of the tabernacle deliberately echoes the narrative of creation. In creation God Himself works; in the tabernacle, human beings work as He commanded. The endpoint of creation is rest and blessing; the endpoint of the tabernacle, as we are about to see, is inspection and blessing. By narrating the tabernacle within the frame of creation, the author wants us to understand: the tabernacle is no ordinary tent, but the place where God will unfold anew, among His people, the order of His dwelling with them — a rehearsal of new creation in the camp of Israel. This observation is a structural reading recognised by commentators; we mark it as the structural-observation level, and do not present it as the literal self-statement of this verse, lest we pass off our inference as the words of the text. [10]

Verses 33 to 41 record the presentation. The people brought the tabernacle to Moses: the tent and all its furnishings — its clasps, boards, bars, pillars, and sockets; the coverings of skins and the veil; the ark and its poles, and the mercy seat; the table and all its utensils, and the Bread of the Presence; the lampstand of pure gold and its lamps; the altar of gold, the anointing oil, and the incense; the screen for the entrance of the tent; the bronze altar, its grating, and its poles; the laver and its base; the hangings of the court, its pillars and sockets, the screen for the gate of the court, its cords and pegs; and all the utensils for the service of the tabernacle — and last, the cloths of service, the holy garments for Aaron, and the garments of his sons. This long list reads like an account being rendered, and like a handover. We should not begrudge its length. Chapter 38 had just accounted for the totals of the metals: that was the account of materials. The list in chapter 39 sets out the finished products item by item: that is the account of the work. Materials have an account, finished products have an inspection, and there is no vagueness in between. The people did not pile a heap of finished things before Moses and say, “It is probably all here”; they brought each item and presented each item. The act of presentation itself shows that the workers did not regard the finished products as their private property. The things were made as God commanded and from the people's offerings; when they were finished they had to be handed over, brought to Moses for inspection, to await the next command — when to erect it and how to place it would still await the word of the LORD in chapter 40. The obedient do not only make as commanded; they gladly hand over what they have made for inspection, do not declare completion themselves, and do not bypass the gate of inspection.

Verses 42 and 43 are the climax of the chapter and the full stop of the whole building narrative. Verse 42 makes the summary once more: according to all that the LORD commanded Moses, so the children of Israel did. Verse 43 says: “And Moses did look upon all the work, and, behold, they had done it as the LORD had commanded, even so had they done it: and Moses blessed them” (KJV). Note the order of the verbs here: first “looked upon,” then “blessed.” Moses did not bless on hearing a report that the work was done; he inspected all the work with his own eyes, and only when he saw that everything had been done as commanded did he open his mouth to bless. How similar this order is to Genesis 1:31: God saw everything that He had made, and behold, it was very good. In the creation narrative God looks day by day and sees that it is good; on the sixth day He sees that everything He has made is very good; and then in 2:3 He blesses the seventh day. In the tabernacle narrative Moses inspects item by item, sees that everything has been done as commanded, and blesses the people. The one who inspects and blesses in creation is God Himself; in the tabernacle work it is God's servant Moses — and Moses's position stands parallel to God's position in the creation narrative. This does not equate Moses with God; it says that God, through His servant, speaks over the people's obedience that word of acceptance first spoken at creation. The people are blessed not because of how much gold and silver they offered, but because they did as commanded, and could withstand inspection. [11]

After the blessing, the text does not erect the tabernacle at once. Only in chapter 40, on the first day of the first month, when the LORD commands Moses to set up the tabernacle, will the glory fill it. This chapter stops at the blessing, a precisely placed structural pointer: the work has passed inspection, the glory has not yet descended; the obedience on the human side is complete, and the presence on God's side will be revealed in His own time. We stop here and do not seize chapter 40's content. Chapter 39 alone leaves us a complete picture: a people who once worshipped a golden calf now bring every finished article, made as commanded, item by item before Moses, and quietly await inspection; Moses looks, and what he opens his mouth to speak is not rebuke, but blessing. A people who had failed, by walking as commanded, stand again in the place of blessing. This picture is worth carrying back into our own lives for slow comparison.

One more layer deserves notice: the presentation list ends not with the largest component of the tabernacle, but with the smallest and most personal item, the garments. Verse 41 closes with the cloths of service, the holy garments for Aaron, and the garments of his sons, bringing the whole handover to its end at “people dressed for service.” However complete the tabernacle, without people prepared to minister the work has not reached its end; with every utensil present, if there are no set-apart people to use them according to their office, they are only exhibits. The list closes with the garments, answering to this chapter's opening with the garments: the chapter begins with making garments and ends with handing over garments, with the transfer of the whole tabernacle in between. This reminds us that what God inspects is never only the objects of a project, but also whether the people who will use those objects are ready to stand in their places. Moses's blessing falls on “them,” not on the objects of the tabernacle — verse 43 says Moses blessed them, and those blessed are the people who did the work. That the objects were made as commanded is a joy; but what God ultimately wants to gain is people who walk as commanded. This makes the blessing of this chapter especially warm: it is not an acceptance stamp on a consignment of goods, but acceptance spoken over a people who turned back and faithfully finished.

## V. Those Who Do as Commanded Stand Under Blessing (Exod 39:43) | 58:00-60:00

Brothers and sisters, this chapter speaks to us very quietly. There is no miracle, no thunder — only the sound of gold being beaten, the sound of weaving, the alternating sound of bells, and at last one sentence of blessing from Moses. Yet the lesson it leaves us is weighty. First, God's word can be carried out item by item. Faith is not only being moved in the heart, nor only confessing with the mouth; it must land on materials, processes, positions, and fastenings — on every concrete thing we do each day. The One we believe in is not vague even about the woven binding of a robe's opening or the cord of a breastpiece; in serving Him we too should be careful in small things. Second, when the work is finished, we must be willing to present it. Many are willing to work but unwilling to be inspected; willing to offer, but hoping to escape checking. The people of this chapter brought the finished products item by item and let Moses look. Obedience that can withstand inspection is mature obedience. Third, blessing follows obedience. Moses's blessing was not a reward paid in advance, but acceptance after inspection. Let us not bypass “doing as commanded” and reach directly for blessing; walk as He has commanded, and His blessing will come — the acceptance He Himself speaks through His servant.

Finally, let us remember the gold plate on the forehead: HOLINESS TO THE LORD. The garments would be finished, the tabernacle erected, and the priests' service begun, but the inscription of it all is one only — unto the LORD. In every project of ours, great or small, at home, in the church, or at work, may we ask ourselves in these words: Was this done as He commanded? Is this holy unto Him? May the Lord make us such people: hearing, we do as commanded; having done, we willingly hand it over for inspection; and then we stand quietly under His blessing. Amen.

## Notes

[1] The fixed refrain כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוָּה יְהוָה אֶת־מֹשֶׁה (kaʾasher tsiwwah YHWH ʾet-Mosheh, “as the LORD commanded Moses”) appears in exactly the same form seven times in Exod 39:1-31: verses 1, 5, 7, 21, 26, 29, 31. Verses 32 and 42 read כְּכֹל אֲשֶׁר־צִוָּה יְהוָה אֶת־מֹשֶׁה (“according to all that the LORD commanded Moses”), and verse 43 reads כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוָּה יְהוָה (“as the LORD had commanded”); these are summary echoes and are not counted within the seven occurrences of the fixed refrain. The count follows the Hebrew text (BHS); some popular works say “nine times,” counting the summary forms together, a different basis of counting. See Childs, *Exodus*, the form-critical remarks on ch. 39; Durham, *Exodus*, the verse-by-verse notes on the refrain.

[2] שְׂרָד (serad) H8278: noun, woven work, woven fabric; בִּגְדֵי שְׂרָד (bigdei serad) denotes specifically the garments worn in sanctuary service, occurring only in Exod 31:10; 35:19; 39:1, 41. BDB glosses woven garment / plaited cloth; HALOT glosses woven fabric, specifically cultic garments. The semantic core lies not in splendour but in the use “for service.”

[3] Correspondence of breastpiece and ephod: command, Exod 28:15-28; execution, Exod 39:8-21. The Urim and Thummim (אֻרִים וְתֻמִּים, ʾUrim we-Tummim) appear in 28:30, placed within the breastpiece; the execution narrative in ch. 39 does not restate their placement, recording only the making and fastening of the breastpiece itself — a common omission in execution narratives, not an indication of a missing item. See Durham, *Exodus*, ad loc.

[4] מְעִיל (meʿil / me'il) H4598: noun, robe, mantle, a garment worn as an outer layer; in Exodus it denotes specifically the high priest's robe of the ephod (28:31-35; 39:22-26), all of blue. BDB glosses robe; HALOT glosses mantle / robe, noting it as a priestly garment.

[5] פַּעֲמֹן (paʿamon / pa'amon) H6472: noun, bell, from the root idea of פעם (p-ʿ-m, “to strike, step”); occurring only in Exod 28:33-34; 39:25-26, denoting the gold bells on the hem of the robe. BDB glosses bell; HALOT glosses bell (on high priest's robe).

[6] רִמּוֹן (rimmon) H7416: noun, pomegranate (the fruit and its imitation in decoration); in the garment context it denotes the pomegranate-shaped ornaments woven in yarn (28:33-34; 39:24-26). BDB glosses pomegranate; HALOT likewise, distinguishing fruit, imitation ornament, and place name; here the imitation-ornament sense applies.

[7] כְּתֹנֶת (kethonet) H3801: noun, a long garment worn next to the skin, a tunic, worn by men and women alike (Gen 3:21; 37:3, etc.); in the priestly context it denotes the fine linen coat (28:39-40; 39:27). BDB glosses tunic; HALOT glosses a garment worn next to the skin, tunic.

[8] צִיץ (tzitz) H6731: noun, properly a shining thing, a flower; specifically the plate of pure gold on the high priest's turban (28:36-38; 39:30-31), engraved קֹדֶשׁ לַיהוָה (qodesh la-YHWH, “HOLINESS TO THE LORD”). BDB glosses plate, flower; HALOT glosses plate (of gold) on high priest's diadem. Fastening: tied with a blue cord to the front of the turban (39:31).

[9] Exod 39:32, וַתֵּכֶל כָּל־עֲבֹדַת מִשְׁכַּן (wattekhel kol-ʿavodat mishkan, “all the work of the tabernacle was finished”), uses the same completion formula from the root k-l-h as Gen 2:1, וַיְכֻלּוּ הַשָּׁמַיִם וְהָאָרֶץ (“the heavens and the earth were finished”); Gen 2:2-3 records God finishing, resting, and blessing, and Exod 39:43 records Moses inspecting and blessing. The correspondence is one of formula and structure.

[10] The structural observation that the building of the tabernacle is presented as a “new creation” is a reading recognised by commentators such as Childs and Durham: Childs, *Exodus*, on the theological significance of the tabernacle accounts and the notes on ch. 39; Durham, *Exodus*, notes on 39:32-43. This sermon marks it as the structural-observation level: the text itself does not say in so many words “this is a new creation”; the echo arises from the parallel of formula and sequence (completion — inspection — blessing) with Gen 1-2.

[11] Exod 39:43, וַיַּרְא מֹשֶׁה (wayyarʾ Mosheh, “and Moses looked upon”), uses the same verb as Gen 1:31, וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים (wayyarʾ ʾElohim, “and God saw”); the blessing, וַיְבָרֶךְ (wayevarekh), echoes the wording of God's blessing in Gen 1:28; 2:3. The parallel is limited to narrative role and sequence, and does not equate Moses's identity with God's.

[12] The “that its sound may be heard” of 28:35 is וְנִשְׁמַע קוֹלוֹ (we-nishmaʿ qolo) in Hebrew; the execution narrative in 39:26 records only the alternating arrangement and the refrain, without restating the reason. Read together, ch. 28 gives the reason and ch. 39 the fulfilment — the regular handling of command-and-execution reading.

[13] The English names of the stones in this sermon follow the KJV; the modern mineral identification of the Hebrew stone names is in many cases disputed, and this sermon makes no claim of identification, listing them only in the order of the text. See the cautionary remarks in the relevant entries of HALOT and BDB.

[14] The order of the presentation list (39:33-41) runs broadly from the tabernacle structure, to its utensils, to the court, and closes with the garments; it does not exactly match the order of making in chs. 35-38. Its function is handover inventory, not a retelling of the process of making. The metal totals of ch. 38 (38:24-31) are the account of materials; the list in this chapter is the account of finished products; the two accounts agree.

## Devotional Excerpt

When the work came to its last piece, the people did not celebrate themselves. They brought every finished article before Moses and waited for his inspection. This speaks deeply to me: obedience is not afraid to be examined. What fears inspection is usually what was done roughly, approximately. What was done as commanded can stand quietly, because every step has its warrant. I have my own small works today — a word at home, a detail at work, a small promise made to someone. I want to learn from the people in this chapter: not to aim at being noticed, but at being true to the pattern; not to rush to declare completion, but first to hand it over willingly and let the word of God check it item by item. On the gold plate were the words, “HOLINESS TO THE LORD.” If what I do is truly His, it needs neither hiding nor boasting. Finish it, hand it over, and wait quietly for His blessing. Such a day is a blessed day.

I also want to learn from the bells and pomegranates woven in alternation: a sound to the step, and fruit to the deed. Not only sounding loud before others, but bearing real fruit in secret; not only finishing the task, but keeping on my heart, like the names over the breast, the people I represent and remember — unloosened, not come apart. If a small thing comes to me today, may the Lord let me ask first: What is it that You have commanded? I want to do it as commanded, even if the process is laborious and unseen — beating the gold thin, hammer by hammer, and weaving the binding firm, stitch by stitch. And in the evening I can hand this day over openly and say: Lord, please look. Your acceptance is the greatest blessing of my day.

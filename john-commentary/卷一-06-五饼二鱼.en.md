# Volume One · Article 6: Full, Yet Why Still Empty?

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «卷一 · 第 6 篇　吃饱了，为什么还是空».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

- Author: Guangyi Zhao
- Core Scripture: John 6:1-40

---

In the previous article, the man who had lain there for thirty-eight years took up his bed and walked out of the pool of Bethesda. In this article, the people suddenly multiply — not lying down, but walking and chasing. Several thousand people followed Jesus up the mountain, and when mealtime came, there was no meal. The first half of this chapter tells of a meal that fed five thousand people; the second half, however, tells of a sentence that makes one somewhat uncomfortable: the people who were fed were hungry again the next day, and they chased across the sea looking for Jesus — and what they were looking for was still food.

John placed these two segments together not to make up length. First he lets you see how Jesus fed a crowd of people, then he lets you hear how He said to this same crowd: you seek Me because you ate of the loaves and were filled. One meal leads into a discourse of extremely great weight in the whole Gospel of John. If your days are also like this — you eat not badly, you have what you should have, yet in your heart there is always a place that cannot be filled — this chapter is spoken to you.

## I. The Scripture

The events took place by the Sea of Galilee. John specifically recorded one sentence: the Passover was near at that time. This feast will be used later in the text; note it down first.

> Then Jesus lifted up His eyes, and seeing a great multitude coming toward Him, He said to Philip, "Where shall we buy bread, that these may eat?" But this He said to test him, for He Himself knew what He would do. Philip answered Him, "Two hundred denarii worth of bread is not sufficient for them, that every one of them may have a little." (John 6:5-7)

> One of His disciples, Andrew, Simon Peter's brother, said to Him, "There is a lad here who has five barley loaves and two small fish, but what are they among so many?" Then Jesus said, "Make the people sit down." Now there was much grass in the place. So the men sat down, in number about five thousand. (John 6:8-10)

> And Jesus took the loaves, and when He had given thanks He distributed them to those sitting down; and likewise of the fish, as much as they wanted. So when they were filled, He said to His disciples, "Gather up the fragments that remain, so that nothing is lost." Therefore they gathered them up, and filled twelve baskets with the fragments of the five barley loaves which were left over by those who had eaten. (John 6:11-13)

The crowd reached its conclusion on the spot:

> Then those men, when they had seen the sign that Jesus did, said, "This is truly the Prophet who is to come into the world." (John 6:14)

They even wanted to take Him by force to make Him king, but Jesus departed alone to the mountain (see John 6:15). In the middle of the night, the disciples first went across the sea by boat, and Jesus walked on the sea to them, saying only one sentence: "It is I; do not be afraid" (see John 6:20); this article will not say much about that matter. The next day, the crowd took boats and chased to Capernaum, and found Jesus. Jesus opened His mouth with no small talk, and first laid their purpose bare:

> Jesus answered them and said, "Most assuredly, I say to you, you seek Me, not because you saw the signs, but because you ate of the loaves and were filled. Do not labor for the food which perishes, but for the food which endures to everlasting life, which the Son of Man will give you, because God the Father has set His seal on Him." (John 6:26-27)

The crowd asked, What shall we do, then, that we may work the works of God? Jesus's answer was surprisingly short:

> Jesus answered and said to them, "This is the work of God, that you believe in Him whom He sent." (John 6:29)

The crowd then asked for a sign, and even brought up the old matter of their ancestors eating manna in the wilderness. Jesus corrected them:

> Then Jesus said to them, "Most assuredly, I say to you, Moses did not give you the bread from heaven, but My Father gives you the true bread from heaven. For the bread of God is He who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world." (John 6:32-33)

> Then they said to Him, "Lord, give us this bread always." And Jesus said to them, "I am the bread of life. He who comes to Me shall never hunger, and he who believes in Me shall never thirst." (John 6:34-35)

This discourse, in this article, is read through to verse 40 as its ending; that is a promise given to those who believe:

> "And this is the will of Him who sent Me, that everyone who sees the Son and believes in Him may have everlasting life; and I will raise him up at the last day." (John 6:40)

First, let us clarify three expressions.

One is the two hundred denarii. In the Chinese Union Version this is rendered as twenty taels of silver; the original is two hundred denarii, about two hundred days' wages for an ordinary laborer. Philip calculated quickly, and accurately too: throwing in more than half a year's wages, each person could still only get a little. His arithmetic was not wrong; what was wrong was that he stopped here once he had calculated, thinking that once the account was settled clearly, there was no way to do the thing.

Another is barley loaves. Barley was the food ration of the poor at that time — cheap, coarse, and not served at the tables of rich households. The five barley loaves and two fish that lad carried were one child's lunch; set before five thousand people, even Andrew himself said, What are they among so many? Yet the axis on which the whole chapter turns is precisely this shabby lunch.

Another is manna. After the Israelites came out of Egypt, they wandered in the wilderness for forty years, and every day God sent down a food they had never seen before; they named it manna, and ate it for a full forty years (see Exodus chapter 16). In Deuteronomy, Moses looked back and summed up this matter, saying that God let them hunger and gave them manna to eat, "that He might make you know that man shall not live by bread alone; but man lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of the LORD" (Deuteronomy 8:3). This Old Testament sentence is almost the key to chapter 6; what Jesus spoke in the second half is precisely its continuation. The Lü Zhenzhong Translation renders "Do not labor for the food which perishes" as do not work for the food that perishes; the meaning is the same: some food is gone once it is eaten down, some food endures — for which kind a person labors in this lifetime, the difference lies right here.

## II. Exposition

First, look at the two answers of Philip and Andrew. One reckoned the account, the other took stock of inventory — both are our instincts when we encounter difficulty. Philip's account was reckoned correctly, but a correct account cannot save people; Andrew did a little better — he did, after all, bring a child and a lunch, although as he brought them he himself first lost heart. Jesus did not use Philip's money, nor did He disdain Andrew's littleness. He took over the five loaves and two fish, and the first thing He did was give thanks — that is, thank God for this pitifully little amount. Only then did He distribute. Pay attention to this order: it is not that He thanked once the things were enough; He thanked first, and the things multiplied in the process of distributing. The text does not state at which moment the loaves multiplied; it only states that He distributed to those sitting down, as much as they wanted. Five thousand people were fed, and the fragments left over filled twelve baskets. At the start it was not enough; at the end there was surplus; in between it passed only through Jesus's hands and one giving of thanks.

The detail of the twelve baskets — John remembered it very clearly. On one hand Jesus let the crowd eat until they were filled; on the other hand He commanded that the fragments be gathered up, so that nothing would be lost. A sign is not a reason for waste. In His hands, abundance and restraint hold at the same time: what should be given is given in full, and what is left over is not wasted. This point is a quiet reminder for people today who at every turn treat surplus as a matter of face.

Next, look at the matter of the crowd wanting to acclaim Him as king. This was not the crowd being momentarily muddled; their reasoning had roots and evidence: Moses gave manna in the wilderness, the prophet Elisha once also fed a hundred people with twenty loaves and had some left over (see 2 Kings 4:42-44), and now this One fed five thousand people with five loaves — is He not then that Prophet who was to come, the King who could lead everyone to live good days? Was their expectation wrong? What they truly wanted was a king who would take care of the food. Jesus saw through it very clearly, so He withdrew. He had not come to run for office; He did not accept acclaim bought with one full meal. Let this matter rest here for now; the discourse of the next day was His own personal explanation of why He refused.

That conversation of the next day must be read slowly. The crowd chased across the sea, and what they opened their mouths with was, Rabbi, when did You come here? — which sounded like small talk. Jesus, however, lifted the lid directly: You seek Me not because you understood the sign, but because yesterday you were fed. These words left no face, yet they are the turning point of the whole chapter. Signs in the Gospel of John have a specific purpose: they are markers pointing to the One who performed the sign; but the crowd saw only the meal the marker pointed to, and did not see the marker itself pointing to a Person. It is as if a road sign stood there, and people picnicked under the road sign and said, Let us come again tomorrow. What Jesus corrected was not their hunger — hunger was not wrong; yesterday He fed them with His own hands. What He corrected was that they stopped there: they treated the One who gives the bread as a tool for delivering bread.

Then comes that sentence about the work of God. What the crowd asked was, What shall we do — they asked for a project list. What Jesus answered was, Believe. Note: He was not lowering the requirement; He was changing the root: the work God wants — the first item is not how much you do for Him, but whether you believe in this One whom He sent. This sentence is the hardest to digest for people who rely on doing things to earn a sense of security; in the application below we will return to this.

The crowd immediately asked for a sign, and even named manna: Our fathers ate the bread given from heaven — what will You do for us to see? Reading here truly makes one shake one's head: yesterday's five thousand had only just been fed, and today they seemed to have lost their memory, asking again for a new credential. Appetite is fed big this way; yesterday's satisfaction no longer counts today. Jesus did not perform another one; He changed the foundation of the topic: first, manna was not given by Moses, but by the Father; second, manna is past tense — "My Father gives you the true bread" is a present, ongoing matter; third, that true bread is not a kind of food, it is a Person — the One who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world. The crowd heard this with enthusiasm and said, Lord, give us this bread always. This sentence is almost identical to the Samaritan woman's sentence in chapter 4, Give me this water always — both heard the words as a long-term meal ticket. Jesus's answer pushed the whole chapter to its highest point: I am the bread of life. The bread, it turns out, is Himself.

Why did John mention at the beginning that the Passover was near? What the Passover commemorates is precisely the Exodus: the people eating the lamb, crossing the Red Sea, entering the wilderness, eating manna — a whole set of stories. Now, on the eve of the Passover, Jesus fed the crowd in a wilderness-like place, and personally said that He Himself is that true bread from heaven. Jewish hearers who understood the background should have lit up in their hearts: the story of the Exodus was told for over a thousand years, and what it was telling of, it turns out, was this One. John did not break open this correspondence; he only lightly set down one sentence — the feast was near — and left the rest for readers to connect themselves. This is exactly John's way of writing: the clues are buried shallow; it all depends on how finely you read.

Reading the discourse through to verse 40, the most important sentence has already landed: the will of the Father is that everyone who sees the Son and believes in Him may have everlasting life. This matter of obtaining everlasting life, throughout this chapter from beginning to end, is not hung on how many works one does or how many rules one keeps; it is hung on only one thing — believing — and the object of belief is specific down to one Person, namely the Son. The words after this in chapter 6 are deeper and harder; this article stops at verse 40 for now, stands this sentence firm, and leaves what follows for readers to read onward themselves, or to find someone to read it with them.

## III. Application

The entry point of this article is that crowd who were hungry again the next day — that is, ourselves.

First, that most familiar cycle. Hungry, fed, and after a while, empty again. Set at the dining table, this is called digestion; set in our days, this is called the norm. Wages rose a stretch, happiness lasted two months, expenses rose along with them, and the purse is still tight. Promoted in position, once the freshness passed, one discovers there is a higher position above, and at night one still cannot sleep soundly. Moved into a bigger house, for the first half year one walks around the rooms with the wind, and a year later one starts finding the garage small. This is not a problem of anyone's character; this is the common fate of food which perishes: they are all good, and they are all real, but they cannot endure. Jesus's words, "Do not labor for the food which perishes," are not telling people not to work or not to earn money — He personally fed five thousand hungry stomachs; He never despised a meal. What He spoke of was order: do not stake your whole life on things that cannot endure, and even more, do not expect them to fill that place which they cannot fill.

Next, the matter of treating faith as a meal ticket — Jesus's heavy words to the crowd still hold today. Many people draw near to faith with, at the start, very practical thoughts: seeking a bit of peace, protecting health, hoping the children test well and business goes a bit smoothly. Honestly, such a start is not shameful; the crowd in that year also came this way, and Jesus did not refuse to give them food because their motives were impure. The problem is stopping there. When the food does not come on time, or what comes is not the dish one wanted, one feels that believing was believing in vain, and turns and walks away. Jesus refused to be a king who takes care of the food; to this day He also refuses to be a wish-granting machine. What He wants to give is far bigger than one meal, so He refuses to be used pressed within the dimensions of one meal. If you feel that after believing for a long time you have obtained none of what you sought, you might change the question: perhaps it is not that He did not give; it is that what you wanted was too small — enough only for one meal — while what He prepared is your whole life.

That child's five loaves — I also want to say a sentence about them alone. To take out five barley loaves before five thousand people takes a bit of courage; it is too shabby; one fears people will laugh just taking them out. Andrew put it very discouragingly: But what are they among so many? The reckoning was not wrong, but what the story finally remembers is precisely this "what are they." Many people's difficulty is that they have only a little in their hands: an ordinary wage, a résumé with no background to speak of, a bit of goodwill they are not good at expressing, a fragment of time. They feel this bit of stuff cannot be taken out, and cannot change anything either, so they simply clutch it. The reckoning in this chapter is different: whether the things are many or few is first not for you to reckon; whether you are willing to hand them into Jesus's hands is another account. Five loaves clutched in a child's hands are just one lunch; handed over, passing first through the giving of thanks, they became the fullness of five thousand people, with twelve baskets left over besides. That bit of yours, too small to not be embarrassed about — what it may lack is not becoming more, but being handed over.

There is another layer, for people who do things. The crowd asked what they should do to work the works of God — this is the question especially diligent people most love to ask: do a little more of what, and my heart will be at ease? Jesus's answer, however, is to believe in Him whom He sent. This is not telling people to lie down and do nothing. Believing here is an action: it is loosening the hand that relies on oneself to clutch tight, and changing it into receiving. In this life of yours, you have labored for the food which perishes, and labored hard enough — overtime, social engagements, calculations, favors — which one does not consume a person? Jesus did not add another item to your list; He said, first stop that way of living that relies on desperately proving yourself, and come and believe. What wears a person down is often not the work itself, but that sentence: I must do a little more, have a little more, only then am I safe, only then am I worth it. To this sentence, chapter 6 does not agree.

Finally, let us return to that emptiness. It always comes after being fed, after being promoted, after the liveliness disperses, when the phone has been scrolled until there is nothing left to scroll. Many people fear this emptiness, and plug it with more eating, more buying, more gatherings; plug it for a while, and it leaks again. This chapter gives the emptiness a different explanation: emptiness is not necessarily a malfunction; it may be a marker, like a sign, pointing in a direction. It is reminding you: you, this person, do not live by bread alone; inside you there is a place that food cannot feed. Jesus's words, "He who comes to Me shall never hunger," speak of precisely this place. He did not promise that those who believe will from now on not need to eat or not need to work; what He promised is that that place which cannot be filled has, in Him, true bread, and this bread endures to everlasting life and will not perish the next day. You may not believe this sentence, but please be serious about it once: do not swipe past it again as a religious slogan; take your own emptiness and test it.

## IV. Response

Having read this far, you might pause and think about a few questions.

- What was the meal that fed you yesterday? A promotion, an income, a relationship, a bout of liveliness? How long did it fill you, and how much remains today?
- Philip reckoned accounts, Andrew took stock — when you encounter difficulty, which one are you more like? After reckoning that it is not enough, where do you generally stop?
- Whether you are drawing near to faith, or opening this book, what is it your heart truly seeks? If Jesus today does not give you that meal, but only gives you Himself, are you still willing to keep walking onward?
- What are those five barley loaves in your hands? That thing so small you think, What is it — the thing you have been clutching and never handed over — are you willing to hand it over once first?

If you are willing, you can follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, I have labored for so many years for things that cannot endure; I have been fed many times, yet my heart is still empty. You said You are the bread of life. I am willing to no longer only ask You for a meal; I come to You — please first fill that place that food cannot reach. Amen."

There are also entrances for continuing onward. In the previous article, beside the pool of Bethesda, Jesus asked a man who had lain there for thirty-eight years, Do you want to be made well? In this article, by the sea He asked people who had been fed, What exactly are you seeking? In the next article we will read John chapter 7: at the Feast of Tabernacles, Jesus stood among the crowd and cried out, If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink. The bread has been spoken of; next comes the water. You can also find a Christian friend you trust, and frankly tell him of that place in you that cannot be filled — without embellishment. Speaking it out is itself the first step of walking before the bread.

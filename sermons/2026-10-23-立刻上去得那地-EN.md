# Let Us Go Up at Once and Possess It — A Sermon on Numbers 13

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Draft — awaiting cross-review (Sermon Production Batch 3, 23 October 2026, unpublished); first translation awaiting proofreading
Text: Numbers 13 (the whole chapter, 33 verses)
Base texts: Scripture quotations in this English manuscript are embedded from the King James Version (KJV) verbatim; the companion Chinese manuscript follows the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation edition (和合本新标点本), checked verse by verse on 23 October 2026. The two manuscripts each follow their own base text and are not translations of each other's quotations.
Title verification: The title is taken from the continuous wording of Caleb's speech in 13:30 KJV — "Let us go up at once, and possess it". The Chinese title 立刻上去得那地 is taken independently from the continuous wording of the same verse in the Chinese base text. Candidates examined verse by verse and set aside: "it floweth with milk and honey" (13:27, the spies' shared description of the land only), "one cluster of grapes" (13:23, a single scene), and Caleb's act of stilling the people (13:30, an action rather than the call itself). See Endnote 2.
Base-text notes (where versions or secondary summaries differ, the base text governs): 13:30 KJV "stilled"; the Chinese base text reads 安抚百姓 ("calmed/soothed the people"), not 镇静 ("sedated/stilled" as in some other Chinese versions). 13:20 KJV "be ye of good courage"; Chinese 放开胆量 ("take courage, let your courage loose"), not 壮胆. The season in 13:20 is "the time of the first ripe grapes" (Chinese 葡萄初熟的时候, "when the grapes were first ripening"), not "when the grapes were ripe". 13:22 Hebron (Chinese 希伯仑, not the older transliteration 何伯仑) and Zoan (琐安). 13:16 KJV spells the names Oshea and Jehoshua, and this manuscript keeps the KJV spellings.
Form: The sermon is in five sections with verse ranges and time codes (00:00-08:00 / 08:00-24:00 / 24:00-42:00 / 42:00-56:00 / 58:00-60:00). Paragraphs are tagged (Fact), (Application), (Summary), (New Testament Reading), or (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) to mark their level; Hebrew words appear only in (Summary) paragraphs as philological statements. Chapter 14 is never read back into this chapter; it appears only as cross-reference summary, marked as such.

## Summary

This chapter records the twelve tribal chiefs sent to search out Canaan. The LORD commands and Moses sends (13:1-3); the roster of twelve is given in full, and Oshea is renamed Jehoshua (13:4-16). Moses defines the mission in ten charges — see the land, the people, the cities, the soil — ending with courage and with evidence: bring back the fruit (13:17-20). The spies go up to Hebron and the brook of Eshcol, where one cluster of grapes takes two men to carry (13:21-24). Forty days later the report begins well — the land does flow with milk and honey — and then turns at one word, "Nevertheless", to walled cities and giants (13:25-29). Caleb stills the people and gives a single conclusion: "Let us go up at once, and possess it; for we are well able to overcome it" (13:30). The ten answer with an evil report, in which they see themselves as grasshoppers (13:31-33). One land, one cluster, one company of giants — two reports. The lesson of the chapter lies not in the data but in the conclusion.

## Main Text

### 1. The Command and the Twelve Names (Numbers 13:1-16) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) The chapter opens with no narrative preamble. The first sentence is a command: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Send thou men, that they may search the land of Canaan, which I give unto the children of Israel: of every tribe of their fathers shall ye send a man, every one a ruler among them" (13:1-2). Three fixed points stand in this command. First, the initiative is the LORD's: the mission is neither the congregation's motion nor Moses' improvisation; the text gives the first word to the One who commands. Second, the land's status is declared before Israel ever enters it: it is already "the land of Canaan, which I give unto the children of Israel" — in the grammar of the text, the land is gift before it is battlefield. Third, the men have a specification: one from every tribe, and "every one a ruler among them". These are not twelve conscripts chosen by lot but twelve men who can speak for their tribes; what they bring back will be heard with a ruler's authority.

(Fact) Moses' execution follows immediately: "And Moses by the commandment of the LORD sent them from the wilderness of Paran: all those men were heads of the children of Israel" (13:3). The point of departure is the wilderness of Paran. The previous chapter ends, "And afterward the people removed from Hazeroth, and pitched in the wilderness of Paran" (12:16). The two chapters join here: the camp has only just moved to Paran when the sending goes out from Paran. And once again their rank is confirmed — "heads of the children of Israel", answering to "ruler" in verse 2. Twice within three verses the text insists: this report will not be camp gossip; it will be an official return.

(Fact) The roster occupies twelve verses (13:4-15), one man per verse, recorded here in full from the KJV: of the tribe of Reuben, Shammua the son of Zaccur; of Simeon, Shaphat the son of Hori; of Judah, Caleb the son of Jephunneh; of Issachar, Igal the son of Joseph; of Ephraim, Oshea the son of Nun; of Benjamin, Palti the son of Raphu; of Zebulun, Gaddiel the son of Sodi; of Joseph, namely of Manasseh, Gaddi the son of Susi; of Dan, Ammiel the son of Gemalli; of Asher, Sethur the son of Michael; of Naphtali, Nahbi the son of Vophsi; of Gad, Geuel the son of Machi. Twelve men, twelve verses, a roll call as regular as a register. Everything that turns later in this chapter grows out of this roster: it contains Caleb, and it contains the ten; it contains Jehoshua, and it contains the men of the evil report. The text nails the names down first, so that credit and blame afterwards have somewhere to belong.

(Fact) Two features of the roster's order are stated as facts, without harmonizing. First, the sons of Joseph are listed as two tribes, and not adjacently: Ephraim stands fifth (13:8) and Manasseh eighth (13:11), with Benjamin and Zebulun between them. Second, Reuben stands first and Gad last. Checked name by name against the roster of tribal leaders in Numbers 1:5-15, not one of those twelve men reappears here — that is a different company altogether, and the tribal sequence also differs in places. The two lists serve different tasks: the list in chapter 1 belongs to the census and the ordering of the camp; the list in chapter 13 belongs to a single reconnaissance. The text itself offers no explanation for the change of personnel, and this sermon offers none either.

(Fact) After the roster comes a renaming: "These are the names of the men which Moses sent to spy out the land. And Moses called Oshea the son of Nun Jehoshua" (13:16). The text records the act and nothing more: no occasion, no reaction from the man renamed, no stated reason. The KJV spellings should be noticed and kept — Oshea becomes Jehoshua; later English usage (Hoshea, Joshua) is not the KJV's. And within this chapter the renamed man is silent: he receives a new name and speaks not one word in Numbers 13. The narrator spends his ink on the roster and the report, and leaves this man standing in the list like a sentence not yet finished. What can be said from this chapter alone is only what the chapter says: Moses changed his name. Why, and what that name will later carry, the chapter does not tell us.

(Application) The rank of these men is the source of the chapter's weight. Had twelve ordinary men come back, a false report would have been no more than talk. But these were rulers — the voice of each tribe in the camp. If their seeing is skewed, the whole camp skews with them; if their fear is large, the whole camp shrinks with it. A community rarely goes wrong beginning with its last member; it goes wrong when the people whose words carry weight first divide in their own hearts. This chapter is therefore a mirror for anyone who leads: your report will be received as fact, and your fear will be followed as judgment. The position from which you speak decides the size of the consequences you must be ready to answer for.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) One narrative seam must be registered side by side, not harmonized away. Numbers ascribes the initiative to the LORD's command (13:1-2), executed by Moses (13:3). The retrospect in Deuteronomy 1:22-23 ascribes the proposal to the people — they came near to Moses asking that men be sent first to search the land, and the saying pleased Moses well. The two accounts assign the initiative differently; that is a fact of the text itself. Commentators handle it in more than one way: either as differing narrative perspectives on one event — in the retrospect Moses stresses the people's share of responsibility to the new generation — or as distinct narrative traditions placed side by side. Which account to take is a discussion-level question; this sermon registers the difference and does not declare one of the two texts invalid. Likewise, Exodus 17:9 already calls the son of Nun "Joshua" before this chapter's renaming is narrated — a tension of sequence that scholarship discusses at the level of narrative and redaction. The facts are simply placed on record: both texts stand, and the renaming is narrated here.

### 2. Ten Charges and One Cluster of Grapes (Numbers 13:17-24) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) Moses' charge occupies 13:17-20, one complete briefing: "And Moses sent them to spy out the land of Canaan, and said unto them, Get you up this way southward, and go up into the mountain: And see the land, what it is, and the people that dwelleth therein, whether they be strong or weak, few or many; And what the land is that they dwell in, whether it be good or bad; and what cities they be that they dwell in, whether in tents, or in strong holds; And what the land is, whether it be fat or lean, whether there be wood therein, or not. And be ye of good courage, and bring of the fruit of the land. Now the time was the time of the first ripe grapes" (13:17-20). The structure is exact: first the route, then the items to inspect, and at the end courage and evidence. Moses is not asking for impressions; he is asking for a report that can be checked — item by item, against the return.

(Fact) The ten charges, counted one by one, are these: (1) go up southward, into the mountain (the route); (2) see the land, what it is; (3) the people, whether strong or weak; (4) whether few or many; (5) the land they dwell in, whether good or bad; (6) the cities, whether in tents or in strong holds; (7) the land, whether fat or lean; (8) whether there be wood therein or not; (9) be ye of good courage; (10) bring of the fruit of the land. One charge fixes the route, seven are acts of seeing — covering land, people, cities, soil — the ninth is not inspection but courage, and the tenth is not inspection but evidence. The count of ten holds on this parsing, which is registered in Endnote 4 for the reader to verify verse by verse. Two wordings must be kept exactly as the base texts have them: the season is "the time of the first ripe grapes" — the first ripening, not full ripeness, and the earliness of the season matters for the fruit that follows; and the courage charge stands in the Chinese base text as "take courage / let courage loose" (放开胆量), its sense matching the KJV's "be ye of good courage".

(Fact) The season note in 13:20 — "Now the time was the time of the first ripe grapes" — is the narrator's aside, not part of Moses' speech. In the Chinese base text it stands in parentheses, marking the level: the charge is Moses'; the season is the narrator's supplement. The same kind of aside occurs twice more in this section (13:22 and 13:23-24). Readers should keep the levels separate: text to the text, aside to the aside.

(Application) The order of the ten charges hides Moses' pastoral wisdom. The seven inspections come first, courage after. Moses does not begin with slogans. He first makes them look — fully and in detail: what the land is, whether the people are strong, whether the cities hold, whether the soil is fat. Nothing is to be skipped. Only then: "be ye of good courage". Courage is not a substitute for facts; courage is the decision, made after the facts are fully seen, still to go forward. And last, the evidence: "bring of the fruit of the land". Moses knows that when men return, words drift and impressions fade; what is carried back cannot lie. He wants something that can be set down in front of the whole camp. The pattern holds today: before you decide, see everything there is to see; after you have seen it, do not let fear edit what you saw; and before you open your mouth, ask — is there fruit in my hands?

(Fact) The journey itself takes two verses. "So they went up, and searched the land from the wilderness of Zin unto Rehob, as men come to Hamath" (13:21) — the whole traverse in one line, south to north, from the wilderness of Zin to Rehob at the entering in of Hamath. Then the camera narrows to the south: "And they ascended by the south, and came unto Hebron; where Ahiman, Sheshai, and Talmai, the children of Anak, were. (Now Hebron was built seven years before Zoan in Egypt.)" (13:22). Here the children of Anak appear for the first time, and they are named — three men: Ahiman, Sheshai, and Talmai. The parenthetical aside measures Hebron's age against Zoan in Egypt: seven years earlier. That is the text's own chronological note; this sermon records it as written and does not expand it into an archaeological conclusion.

(Fact) The most concrete scene of the chapter is at Eshcol: "And they came unto the brook of Eshcol, and cut down from thence a branch with one cluster of grapes, and they bare it between two upon a staff; and they brought of the pomegranates, and of the figs" (13:23). "The place was called the brook Eshcol, because of the cluster of grapes which the children of Israel cut down from thence" (13:24). The objects, counted one by one, are five: a branch, one cluster of grapes, the staff, pomegranates, figs. One cluster requires two men and a staff between them — the text gives no weight, only the manpower: a single cluster too heavy for one man to carry. Two base-text details are registered, not harmonized: the KJV calls Eshcol a "brook", where the Chinese base text has 谷, "valley" (hence the common rendering "valley of Eshcol"); and the naming note in the Chinese text is a parenthesis spanning verses 23-24, opening at the end of verse 23 and closing at the end of verse 24. The valley — or brook — is named from the cluster, as verse 24 itself explains.

(Summary) Three philological notes are registered as language facts only. First, the chapter's standard verb for the mission, tûr (H8446, "to search out, spy"), recurs across the chapter; in word-for-word concordance it is verified at 13:25 and twice at 13:32 (see Endnote 5). Second, the "staff" of 13:23 is môṭ (H4132), a carrying pole — the same word used for the poles that bore the tabernacle furniture in Numbers 4:10 and 4:12; the pole that carried holy furniture here carries a cluster of grapes. Third, the name Eshcol, ʾeškôl (H812), is the ordinary noun for a cluster of grapes used as a place name, which is precisely the link verse 24 uses to explain the name. These are facts of the language; no allegory is built on them.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two discussions are registered without adjudication. First, the seven-years note comparing Hebron and Zoan (Zoan is commonly linked with Tanis): scholarship has handled the correlation of this note with the dating of Zoan's founding in more than one way — whether as an old chronological tradition or as the narrator's comparison meant to underline Hebron's antiquity. This sermon quotes the note and marks its level; it announces no archaeological verdict. Second, "Eshcol" also appears in Genesis 14:13 as a man's name — Eshcol the Amorite, brother of Mamre, in league with Abram. The same form serves there as a personal name and here as a place name bound to the word "cluster". Both usages stand in the text; the explanation of the valley's name is given by this chapter itself (13:24) and needs no supplement.

### 3. Forty Days Later: The Fruit First, Then "Nevertheless" (Numbers 13:25-29) (24:00-42:00)

(Fact) The return takes one sentence: "And they returned from searching of the land after forty days" (13:25). Forty days is the chapter's only number of days: out from Paran, the length of the land, and back to Paran. Within this chapter it is simply the record of the journey's length. The weight this number later carries in the book (Numbers 14, summary only, Endnote 7) lies outside this chapter and is not read back into it.

(Fact) The setting of the report is 13:26: "And they went and came to Moses, and to Aaron, and to all the congregation of the children of Israel, unto the wilderness of Paran, to Kadesh; and brought back word unto them, and unto all the congregation, and showed them the fruit of the land." The audience has three levels — Moses, Aaron, and all the congregation; no one is left out. This is a formal, public return, not a private briefing. And notice the sequence within the verse: word is brought back, "and showed them the fruit of the land". Before the spoken report of verse 27 begins, the fruit is already on display. The congregation sees first and hears second. The grapes that took two men to carry, with the pomegranates and the figs, stand in the middle of the camp, and every sentence of the report will be heard — and tested — beside them.

(Fact) The report's first sentence is preserved in 13:27: "And they told him, and said, We came unto the land whither thou sentest us, and surely it floweth with milk and honey; and this is the fruit of it." Two short clauses: an assertion and a pointing finger. The assertion — "surely it floweth with milk and honey" — concedes that the land matches what had been said of it: in 13:2 it was "the land of Canaan, which I give unto the children of Israel", and the spies now verify with their own mouths that the land is good. The pointing — "and this is the fruit of it" — needs no adjectives; all its force comes from the produce standing beside the speakers. The Chinese base text reads 流奶与蜜之地, where the verb "floweth" (流) is never absent: the land's abundance is pictured as flowing, overflowing, not as a static store.

(Fact) The turn is a single word: "Nevertheless the people be strong that dwell in the land, and the cities are walled, and very great: and moreover we saw the children of Anak there" (13:28). Then the distribution: "The Amalekites dwell in the land of the south: and the Hittites, and the Jebusites, and the Amorites, dwell in the mountains: and the Canaanites dwell by the sea, and by the coast of Jordan" (13:29). Five peoples in three zones, each as the base text has it: the Amalekites in the south; the Hittites, Jebusites, and Amorites in the mountains; the Canaanites by the sea and by the coast of Jordan. Not one clause of this is false: the people are strong, the cities are walled and very great, and the children of Anak had in fact been seen at Hebron (13:22). Verses 28-29 answer charges three and six of Moses' ten point by point — the spies did look, and did answer. Formally, this is the most compliant stretch of reporting in the chapter: everything inspected has an answer.

(Application) Then where is the failure? Not in the data — in the position of the conclusion. The fruit is real, and the walled cities are real; but the report places "Nevertheless" after the fruit and the giants at the end of the sentence, inviting the whole camp to interpret everything it saw first by the last thing it hears. Order is stance. A report's first item, last item, and concluding item teach its hearers how to read all the rest. The ten spies need not have fabricated a single fact; their decisive act was reordering — moving the gift forward as a preamble and the enemy backward as the conclusion. We do the same today: you do not need to invent falsehoods; you only need to end every sentence, steadily, with fear, and the people around you will slowly conclude that fear is the truth. The discipline of faith is often not the gathering of new data but the guarding of the order — refusing to let the enemy take the last sentence.

(Application) One level more: the fruit is seen, the report is heard. The order of 13:26 is display first, speech second — the narrator deliberately records "showed them the fruit of the land" before the formal address begins. For everyone who testifies, reports, or leads, this is a concrete lesson: present what can be verified before you pronounce conclusions; let the evidence stand in front of your words; let people see what you have received before they hear what you have decided. Reverse the order — verdict first, evidence afterward — and you have built the greenhouse of an evil report, because once a verdict is fixed in advance, evidence can only be made to serve it. The chapter writes this order out plainly; nothing needs to be added.

(New Testament Reading) The New Testament's fullest reading of this chapter stands in Hebrews 3-4, registered here as summary. The writer sets the wilderness generation as a warning: they heard the word and had themselves come through the Exodus and the wilderness provision, yet fell in the wilderness through unbelief; his diagnosis is that the word did not profit them because it was not mixed with faith in those who heard it (Heb 3:16-19; 4:2). First Corinthians 10:1-12 likewise takes the events of that generation "for our admonition" and warns the one who thinks he stands. Read back into this chapter — at the level of reading, not of fact — the seed of the tragedy is not what the spies saw but what the congregation mixed with what it heard: the fruit, or the giants. The New Testament reading does not alter this chapter's facts; it sets this chapter's choice before every hearer of the word.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The ethnic geography of 13:29 must be read according to its textual nature: it is the spies' first-person statement inside a report, part of this chapter's narrative — not an ethnographic survey delivered in the narrator's omniscient voice. The five peoples in their three zones agree broadly with the distributions given elsewhere in Numbers and Joshua, but the historical reconstruction of each people and territory is discussion-level work, and scholarship differs on the composition and date of such lists. In the same way, "the children of Anak" appear three times in this chapter (13:22, 28, 33) and form, with the later cross-references (Joshua 14-15, Endnote 7), a chain of internal textual evidence. This manuscript's rule stands: quotations follow the base text, statements carry their level, and extrapolations belong to the discussion level — the words of a report are not to be treated as an excavation report, nor are discussion-level questions allowed to wash out what the text plainly says.

### 4. Caleb Stills the People: Go Up at Once (Numbers 13:30) (42:00-56:00)

(Fact) The hinge of the chapter is a single verse: "And Caleb stilled the people before Moses, and said, Let us go up at once, and possess it; for we are well able to overcome it" (13:30). First the action: Caleb "stilled" the people. The verb (H2013, see Endnote 5) means to make quiet, to silence; the Chinese base text renders it 安抚, "calmed, soothed". One word tells us that before he spoke, the people were no longer quiet: after verses 28-29 landed, a noise had risen in the camp, and Caleb had first to bring the noise down before there could be a hearing. This chapter does not describe that noise in detail — the weeping belongs to the next chapter (14:1, summary) — it records only this: before Moses, in the middle of the uproar, one man first made the congregation quiet, and then spoke.

(Fact) Then the words — two sentences only. "Let us go up at once, and possess it": action (go up), object (it, the land), timing (at once), complete in one breath. "For we are well able to overcome it": a judgment, bare, with no attached reasoning. Set these two sentences beside the three preceding verses and one important fact emerges: Caleb corrects not a single item of 13:28-29. He does not say the cities are unwalled; he does not say the people are weak; he certainly does not say the children of Anak do not exist. His speech is not a correction — it is a conclusion. Nor does he bring any new data: his grounds are what 13:2 had already said — the land is given — and what the whole team had conceded in 13:27, that the land flows with milk and honey. At the level of fact, Caleb stands on exactly the same data as the ten. The fork is only in the last sentence: is the land a devourer, or is it to be gone up and possessed?

(Summary) The force of the Hebrew at this verse deserves registration as philology, without allegory. Both of Caleb's verbs are doubled with the infinitive absolute: ʿālô naʿăleh — literally "going up, we shall go up" (H5927) — and yākôl nûḵal — "being able, we are able" (H3201). The KJV renders the first "Let us go up at once", and the Chinese base text likewise 立刻上去, "go up immediately": both translations catch the urgency of a form that stacks two layers of the verb to nail down "now, without delay". And the stilling verb, wayyahas (H2013), means to silence, to hush: first the crowd is hushed, then the doubled verbs are spoken. The shortest speech in the chapter is its heaviest verse.

(Application) Caleb's faith contests not the level of fact but the level of ownership. The question was never "how high are the walls?" — the walls are high; that is fact. The question is "whose is the land?" — and 13:2 had answered: it is the land "which I give unto the children of Israel". The ten halted at the level of inventory: how high the cities, how strong the people, how small we are. Caleb re-reads the same inventory inside the frame of the gift: if the land is given, then strong enemies are stages on the road of the gift, not evidence that the gift was revoked. Faith does not shrink the giants — in 13:33 they have not grown shorter. Faith enlarges the gift until it is big enough to decide which side the conclusion is written on. We stand at the same fork constantly: inventory only your own resources, and the conclusion is forced to be "we be not able"; set the One you believe back into the conclusion, and "we are well able" has a root.

(Application) "At once" is the keyword of the verse and the chapter's lesson for anyone who would act. Caleb does not say "let us go up after further consultation", nor "let a later generation go up" — he says at once. Some windows of decision open only once: the heart of the camp is still suspended between the fruit it heard first and the strong enemy it heard second; one night later it will set hard in weeping (14:1-2, summary). Delay is harmless in many things; in a decision of faith it is frequently fear's ally, granting dread a whole night to organize itself, letting "so we were in their sight" grow into the consensus of the camp. Caleb is racing that clock — while the fruit is still standing in view, while the report has only just been delivered, to speak the conclusion out first. This verse stands at 13:30 like a gate between the strong-enemy statement and the evil report: how much it holds back depends on whether anyone is willing to speak first in the middle of the noise.

(Summary) Within the bounds of this chapter, Jehoshua remains silent. The man renamed in verse 16 does not speak in verse 30; the chapter's positive voice is Caleb's alone. The two men stand together only in the next chapter — tearing their clothes and exhorting the congregation (Num 14:6-9, summary level; the detail belongs to a sermon on chapter 14), where the verdict that Caleb wholly followed and would enter the land also stands (14:24, summary). This chapter keeps the facts straight: in Numbers 13 the one who stills the people alone is Caleb, and Jehoshua's portion is in the next chapter. Readers must not pour the next chapter's lines back into this chapter's mouths: the text gives each speech to its own speaker.

### 5. The Evil Report and the Grasshoppers: Two Ways of Seeing Ourselves (Numbers 13:31-33) (58:00-60:00)

(Fact) Caleb's words had barely landed when the answer came: "But the men that went up with him said, We be not able to go up against the people; for they are stronger than we" (13:31). "The men that went up with him" — the ten who went and returned with him are here named, for the first time, as opponents. Set the verse against 13:30: Caleb said "we are well able"; they say "we be not able". The Hebrew uses one and the same verb (H3201) in the positive and the negative — the same data, the same word, read by one side as "able" and by the other as "not able". One reason only is given: "for they are stronger than we" — which is verse 28 restated, not new evidence. Within three verses the report has completed its turn: from "surely it floweth with milk and honey" (13:27), through "Nevertheless" (13:28), to "we be not able" (13:31).

(Fact) In verse 32 the narrator gives the report its name: "And they brought up an evil report of the land which they had searched unto the children of Israel, saying, The land, through which we have gone to search it, is a land that eateth up the inhabitants thereof; and all the people that we saw in it are men of a great stature." The word rendered "evil report", dibbâ (H1681), denotes defaming speech, a whispered bad report (Endnote 5); the Chinese base text sums the verse up as 报恶信, "they brought an evil report". "A land that eateth up the inhabitants thereof" is quoted exactly as the base text has it — and it is the spies' rhetoric, not the narrator's assertion. The narrator does not say the land devours its inhabitants; he says the spies said so. The levels must be kept apart: the text is responsible for recording their words, not for certifying them. The land that had just yielded a cluster two men could barely carry becomes, in the same mouths, a land that eats people up. The land has not changed; what has changed is the definition the speakers have laid upon it.

(Fact) The last verse measures the distance between the two reports to the bottom: "And there we saw the giants, the sons of Anak, which come of the giants: and we were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight" (13:33). The relation, as the base texts punctuate it, is registered as it stands: the giants come first, and the sons of Anak derive from them. The Hebrew word is nəp̄îlîm (H5303): the KJV renders it "giants" in both places; the Chinese base text renders it 伟人, "great men"; some modern versions transliterate "Nephilim". The three renderings are registered side by side and not harmonized; in this manuscript's quotations the KJV's wording governs. The same word occurs earlier at Genesis 6:4 (cross-reference, Endnote 7). Then the grasshoppers (ḥăḡāḇîm, H2284): "we were in our own sight as grasshoppers" — a self-assessment; "and so we were in their sight" — an inference about the eyes of others. The text records the spies' words: the first half tells how they saw themselves; the second half tells how they guessed they were seen. The narrator nowhere says the sons of Anak actually saw them so. The evil report, at its final step, admits even an imagined enemy gaze as evidence.

(Application) The grasshopper saying exposes the sequence of surrender before battle: first grow small in your own eyes; then assume you are small in the eyes of others; finally use that assumed gaze to prove that you really are small. Men are not first defeated by giants; they are first defeated by their own estimate. In this verse the ten complete the whole circuit of self-persuasion. They had seen the fruit with their own eyes — the sentence of 13:27 came from their own mouths; they had carried the grapes with their own shoulders; they had walked the forty days and answered the ten charges; and at the last step they stamped themselves with "as grasshoppers". The stamping hand, notice, is their own: "in our own sight". God's people fall into the grasshopper mind by the same sequence today: measure yourself against the circumstances, report the measurement on the small side, guess that everyone else measures you the same, and treat the guess as the conclusion. Caleb's order is exactly reversed: measure the land first by the promise — "we are well able to overcome it" — and only then measure the strong enemy, who remains on the road but is not allowed to become the conclusion.

(Application) To close the chapter. Numbers 13 has, from first to last, a single chain of fact: command (13:1-2), roster (13:4-16), charge (13:17-20), journey (13:21-24), return (13:25-29), two conclusions (13:30-31), and one verdict-word (13:32-33). One company walked one road, carried back one cluster, saw one company of giants — and divided at the camp gate into two reports. This chapter passes no sentence on the ten; sentence and judgment stand in the next chapter. What this chapter does is quieter and sharper: it sets the two reports side by side before us and lets us see for ourselves that a people's direction is decided not by its facts but by its conclusion. Every report you hear — including the one inside your own heart — must be closed with one sentence or the other: either "Nevertheless… we be not able", or "at once… we are well able". May our generation, at its own Kadesh, stand beside the fruit and speak Caleb's words.

(New Testament Reading) The chapter closes with Paul's use of it, as summary: these things were written "for our admonition", and "let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall" (1 Cor 10:11-12, summarized). The ten were not men without a history with God: they had come out of Egypt, through the sea, eaten manna, and handled with their own hands the fruit of the promised land. What they lacked was never experience; it was, at the decisive verse, the mixing of faith with what they heard (Heb 4:2). That is the question this chapter leaves with every hearer today: the fruit in your hands is real, and the giants before you are real — which sentence will you carry home tonight as your conclusion?

## Devotional Excerpt

“And Caleb stilled the people before Moses, and said, Let us go up at once, and possess it; for we are well able to overcome it” (Num 13:30, KJV). Before that word was spoken, the evidence was already standing in the camp: one cluster of grapes carried between two men upon a staff, and the first sentence of the report — “surely it floweth with milk and honey; and this is the fruit of it” (13:27). The ten saw the same fruit, yet finished by seeing themselves “as grasshoppers” (13:33). Faith does not deny the giants; it refuses to let the giants write the conclusion. Set the fruit on the table first, remember whose gift the land is, and then speak in Caleb's order — the promise first, and go up at once.

## Endnotes

1. Base texts and quotations. The English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version; the companion Chinese manuscript's quotations follow the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation edition (simplified), checked verse by verse across all 33 verses on 23 October 2026. Two matters of punctuation and level: (a) in the Chinese text the narrator's naming note opens a parenthesis at the end of 13:23 that closes only at the end of 13:24, and quotations there are given as one block; (b) in the Chinese text the spies' speech opens quotation marks at 13:27 and closes them at the end of 13:29, while this English manuscript quotes verse by verse. At 13:26 the verb "showed" follows the KJV text as consulted (ebible.org); some KJV printings read "shewed".
2. Title verification. Four candidates were examined verse by verse: (a) "it floweth with milk and honey" (13:27) — in continuous reading the sentence is "surely it floweth with milk and honey", the whole team's shared description of the land; it states the land's goodness but contains neither the strong enemy nor the decision, and cannot carry the chapter; (b) "one cluster of grapes" (13:23) — the object of a single scene; (c) "Caleb stilled the people" (13:30) — Caleb's action, not his words; (d) "we are well able to overcome it" (13:30b) — from the same verse, but a judgment without action or object. "Let Us Go Up at Once and Possess It" is the continuous wording of 13:30 ("Let us go up at once, and possess it"), containing action, object, and timing, and standing at the chapter's hinge. The Chinese title 立刻上去得那地 was fixed independently from the Chinese base text's continuous wording of the same verse (「我们立刻上去得那地吧」); the two titles follow their own base texts and are not translations of each other.
3. Divergences from the commissioning summary, resolved in favour of the base texts and recorded here: (a) the summary spoke of Caleb "镇静" (stilling/sedating) the people — the Chinese base text at 13:30 reads 安抚百姓, "calmed the people"; 镇静 is the wording of a different Chinese version (the Chinese New Version). The KJV reads "stilled". (b) The summary spoke of a charge to "壮胆" (brace one's courage) — the Chinese base text at 13:20 reads 你们要放开胆量, "take courage, let courage loose"; 壮胆 is likewise the other version's wording. The KJV reads "be ye of good courage". (c) The summary named the season "葡萄成熟的时候" ("when the grapes were ripe") — the base text reads 葡萄初熟的时候, "when the grapes were first ripening"; KJV, "the time of the first ripe grapes". (d) The summary wrote "何伯仑" for Hebron — an older transliteration; the base text reads 希伯仑. (e) The summary quoted "奶与蜜之地" ("land of milk and honey") — the continuous reading at 13:27 is 流奶与蜜之地, "a land flowing with milk and honey"; the verb "floweth/flowing" is not omitted. In all five points both manuscripts follow the base texts.
4. Counts, each performed and registered: the chapter has 33 verses. The spies are 12 men, listed in 13:4-15, twelve verses, one man per verse. Moses' charges number ten, on this parsing of 13:17b-20: one route charge; seven inspections (the land, what it is; the people, strong or weak; few or many; the land they dwell in, good or bad; the cities, tents or strong holds; the soil, fat or lean; wood, whether there be any or not); one charge of courage; one charge of evidence (bring the fruit). The objects at Eshcol are five: a branch, one cluster of grapes, a staff, pomegranates, figs (13:23). The report names five peoples in three zones: Amalekites — the south; Hittites, Jebusites, Amorites — the mountains; Canaanites — by the sea and the coast of Jordan (13:29). The children of Anak named at Hebron are three: Ahiman, Sheshai, Talmai (13:22). The journey is forty days (13:25). Hebron was built seven years before Zoan (13:22, narrator's note). The cluster was carried by two men (13:23).
5. Strong's numbers — list and method of verification. Each number below was verified individually before writing, by cross-checking BibleHub and Blue Letter Bible lexicon entries against word-for-word interlinear tagging (23 October 2026); only verified items carry numbers. tûr, H8446, "to search out, spy" (the chapter's standard verb; interlinear-verified at 13:25 and twice at 13:32); nāśîʾ, H5387, "ruler, chief" (the word behind "ruler" in 13:2); ʾeškôl, H812, "a cluster (of grapes)" — the common noun and the place name are the same word, the link 13:24 itself uses for the naming; môṭ, H4132, "carrying pole, staff" (13:23; the same word for the tabernacle poles at Num 4:10, 12); ḥāḡāḇ, H2284, "grasshopper, locust" (13:33, plural ḥăḡāḇîm); nəp̄îlîm, H5303, the word rendered "giants" in the KJV and 伟人 in the Chinese base text (13:33); dibbâ, H1681, "defamation, evil report" (13:32); hāsâ, H2013, "to be silent, to silence" (13:30 wayyahas, "and he stilled/calmed"; KJV "stilled"); ʿĂnāq, H6061, "Anak" (13:22, 28, 33); neḡeḇ, H5045, "the south (land)" (13:29); ʿālâ, H5927, "to go up" (13:30, doubled form ʿālô naʿăleh); yākôl, H3201, "to be able" (13:30 doubled, "we are well able"; negated at 13:31, "we be not able"); yāraš, H3423, "to possess, take possession" (13:30, KJV "possess"). 6. Roster comparison — method. The twelve leaders of Numbers 1:5-15 were checked against two current Chinese versions and then compared name by name with Numbers 13:4-15: not one name recurs. Near neighbours such as Gamaliel (Num 1) and Gaddiel / Gaddi (Num 13) are differently written and are not the same men. The tribal sequences also differ in places (Ephraim stands fifth in Num 13, sixth in Num 1; Gad stands last in Num 13). The differences are registered as facts, not harmonized and not explained.
7. Cross-references — each counted and levelled: (a) Num 12:16, the Paran link — fact level; quoted in section 1. (b) Num 14:1-2; 14:6-9; 14:24; 14:37 — the next chapter's weeping, Jehoshua and Caleb's formal speech, the verdict that Caleb would enter the land, and the death of the men of the evil report — summary only, nothing quoted, nothing read back. (c) Num 32:9 — Moses recalls the Eshcol episode to Reuben and Gad — summary. (d) Deut 1:22-23 — the retrospect assigning the initiative to the people, with Moses' approval — retrospect level, registered alongside Num 13:1-2 (see section 1, discussion paragraph). (e) Josh 14:6-15; 15:13-14 — Caleb in old age receives Hebron, and the three sons of Anak are named again there — summary. (f) Gen 6:4 — the same word nəp̄îlîm occurs earlier — summary. (g) Gen 14:13 — "Eshcol" as a man's name, the brother of Mamre — fact-level registration of a same-form different use. (h) Heb 3:16-19; Heb 4:2; 1 Cor 10:1-12 — New Testament reading level, summary only; they do not alter this chapter's fact level.
8. Renaming and silence (13:16). The text records only that Moses called Oshea Jehoshua; no reason is given, and Jehoshua speaks no word in this chapter — the positive speech of 13:30 belongs to Caleb alone. Attributing the words of Num 14:7-9 to Jehoshua within this chapter would be reading backwards, and this manuscript does not do it. That "Joshua" already appears at Exodus 17:9, before the renaming narrated here, is registered at the discussion level in section 1.
9. "A land that eateth up the inhabitants thereof" (13:32) is the spies' rhetoric, quoted exactly. The narrator records it and labels the whole a brought-up "evil report"; he does not assert that the land devours its inhabitants. Both the sermon body and the summary keep this level distinction.
10. Paran and Kadesh (13:3, 26). Departure and return are both in the wilderness of Paran, the return point named as Kadesh, joining Num 12:16 ("pitched in the wilderness of Paran") as fact. The relation of this Kadesh to the Kadesh of Numbers 20:1 is a discussion-level cross-reference and is not developed in this chapter.
11. The boundary of the two men's positions within this chapter. The "men that went up with him" of 13:31 are the ten. Within this chapter Jehoshua's position is not recorded, and Caleb's extends only as far as his speech in 13:30. The phrase of 13:32, "they brought up an evil report" (Chinese: 探子中有人, "some of the spies"), is quoted as the base text has it; this manuscript does not assign it to named individuals beyond what the text itself names.

(c) Thirty-seven Chinese quotation fragments were machine-checked against the verified base text; mismatches: none.

# The Feasts of the LORD and the Holy Convocations — A Sermon on Leviticus 23

- Text: Leviticus 23:1-44
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft — awaiting cross-review (Sermon production batch 2, 2026-10-22; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Leviticus 23; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations.
- Note on the title: The words "feasts" and "holy convocations" are the KJV wording of Leviticus 23:2 itself, and "节期" / "圣会" are likewise the actual wording of the Chinese base text at 23:2; the title is not built on any paraphrase.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Leviticus 23 is the most complete festival calendar in the Old Testament. With the Sabbath as its preface, it sets the Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread in the first month, the sheaf of firstfruits, and the fiftieth day, alongside the memorial of trumpet blasts on the first day of the seventh month, the Day of Atonement on the tenth, and the Feast of Tabernacles from the fifteenth. This sermon does not expound every offering in detail; it follows the chapter's structure and theological thread. The feasts belong to the LORD — time is His first. The calendar is anchored twice over, in the exodus from Egypt and in the agricultural rhythm of the promised land. Rest is not one item in the calendar but its skeleton. The counting from one sheaf to the fiftieth day turns harvest into worship. And the autumn group moves in one breath from awakening trumpets, through self-affliction and atonement, to booth-dwelling joy that remembers the exodus.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 The feasts belong to the LORD: the frame of the whole chapter

(Fact) The chapter opens with this declaration: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, Concerning the feasts of the LORD, which ye shall proclaim to be holy convocations, even these are my feasts." (Lev 23:2, KJV) Notice the order of the sentence: the feasts are first called "the feasts of the LORD," and only then is Israel told what to do — proclaim. Proclaiming is not inventing. Israel may not create its own feasts; it may only announce the days the LORD has already appointed, and call the assembly together on them. The same formula returns in verse 4 as the formal heading for the Passover and all that follows, and at the end of the chapter verse 37 gathers it up a third time, before verse 44 seals the whole: "And Moses declared unto the children of Israel the feasts of the LORD." (KJV) Three occurrences of "the feasts of the LORD" stand like three nails fastening the chapter into one whole: declaration at the opening, restatement in the middle, delivery at the close.

(Fact) The two title words must be handled according to the original text. The first is "feasts," Hebrew môʿēd (Strong's H4150), whose basic sense is "an appointed time, an appointed meeting"; both BDB and HALOT give the fixed, appointed time and the festival season as its core meanings. What it stresses is not festive atmosphere but appointment: the time is set by the LORD, and the people come at the set time to keep the appointment. The second phrase is "holy convocations," miqrāʾ qōdeš (miqrāʾ, Strong's H4744; qōdeš, H6944), literally "a holy calling-together, a holy proclamation": a feast is not a day of private commemoration at home, but a day on which the whole congregation is publicly summoned and set apart to assemble in worship. (Application) Put the two words together and you have the chapter's first theological thread: sovereignty over time rests with God. The calendar looks like the most private thing a person owns — what I do today, whether I rest tomorrow, seems entirely mine to decide. But Leviticus 23 opens by reclaiming the calendar for the LORD: some days are not yours; they are His. You are not the master of the calendar; you are the one who keeps the appointment.

(Fact) Structurally, the chapter falls into five blocks: the Sabbath (v. 3) as preface; the spring group (vv. 4-22), comprising Passover, Unleavened Bread, Firstfruits, and the fiftieth day; the autumn group (vv. 23-43), comprising the trumpet memorial, the Day of Atonement, and Tabernacles; and the general conclusion (vv. 37-38, 44, overlapping the preceding block). One further feature deserves emphasis: the dates are given with extreme concreteness — the fourteenth day of the first month, the fifteenth, the first day of the seventh month, the tenth, the fifteenth — all stated in so many words, not one day off. (Application) Concrete dates are themselves a proclamation: faith is not vague emotion but obedience located in a particular year, month, and day. Which day to stop work, which day to assemble, which day to afflict oneself, which day to rejoice in booths — none of this is left to feeling; all of it follows the appointed day. A sermon that gets these dates wrong, or alters the numbers of the offerings, fails at the most basic level; so in this sermon every date and number is stated only as the text gives it, neither added to nor taken away.

(Fact) One more structural observation must be made at the outset: this festival calendar is doubly anchored. One anchor is historical — Passover remembers the exodus, and Tabernacles is expressly said to remember the booth-dwelling of the exodus journey. The other anchor is agricultural — the firstfruits are the first sheaf of the land's harvest, the fiftieth day falls at the wheat harvest, and Tabernacles comes after the produce of the land has been gathered in (v. 39, Summary). (Application) Redemptive history and the rhythm of the land are woven into a single calendar, which means Israel's faith governs two things at once: remembering how God once saved them, and acknowledging where today's bread comes from. Forget the history, and the feasts shrink into farming customs; forget the land, and they float free as empty ritual. Lose either anchor, and the calendar will not stand.


### II. 08:00-24:00 The Sabbath first, and the spring group: from Passover to firstfruits

(Fact) The first item in the festival calendar is, surprisingly, not an annual feast at all but the weekly Sabbath: "Six days shall work be done: but the seventh day is the sabbath of rest, an holy convocation; ye shall do no work therein: it is the sabbath of the LORD in all your dwellings." (Lev 23:3, KJV) In form, the Sabbath is weekly, not annual, and could well have been left out of an annual calendar; yet the editor places it at the very head of the chapter, before Passover. (Application) That placement is itself a sermon: every feast begins from rest. The Sabbath is not one item in the festival calendar; it is the calendar's foundation. Learn first to stop — to confess that the world does not exist because you keep it turning — and only then can annual commemoration and rejoicing even be discussed. A person who cannot keep the weekly rest, who packs his days airtight, may observe feast after feast, but it will be religious activity, not rest.

(Fact) One word in verse 3 calls for attention: behind the KJV's "sabbath of rest" stands šabbātôn (Strong's H7677), "complete rest, solemn cessation." The word recurs in this chapter for the first day of the seventh month (v. 24), the Day of Atonement (v. 32), and the first and eighth days of Tabernacles (v. 39, Summary), running like a hidden thread that extends the theme of rest from the seventh day of every week to the core days of every great feast. (Application) In other words, every peak of the annual calendar is, in essence, an enlarged Sabbath. The skeleton of the feasts is rest; the offerings and the banquets are the flesh on that skeleton.

(Fact) The spring group opens with Passover and Unleavened Bread (vv. 5-8, Summary): at twilight on the fourteenth day of the first month is the LORD's Passover; from the fifteenth day of the same month is the Feast of Unleavened Bread — seven days of eating unleavened bread, a holy convocation on the first day and on the seventh, no servile work, and offerings made by fire to the LORD for seven days. The dates and the number of days are all in plain sight: the fourteenth, the fifteenth, seven days. This sermon summarises these verses rather than quoting them in full, in order to give its space to the structure of the whole chapter; but every number in the summary corresponds to the text exactly, unaltered. (Application) That Passover and Unleavened Bread stand at the front of the year is no accident of ordering: the year begins by retelling the night of the exodus. The whole nation's time is reckoned from the night they were brought out. First redemption, then life; first Passover, then harvest. That is the first meaning of the spring group.

(Fact) Next comes the sheaf of firstfruits (vv. 9-14, Summary): when Israel has come into the land the LORD gives them and reaps its harvest, they are to bring a sheaf — Hebrew ʾōmer (Strong's H6016, here "sheaf"; in Exodus 16 the same word serves as the dry measure "omer") — of the firstfruits to the priest, and the priest shall wave the sheaf before the LORD as a wave offering (tenûpāh, Strong's H8573), so that the people may be accepted. On the same day a male lamb of the first year, without blemish, is offered as a burnt offering; the grain offering is two tenth deals of fine flour mingled with oil, and the drink offering a fourth part of a hin of wine. Until the offering has been brought, neither bread, nor parched grain, nor fresh ears may be eaten (v. 14, Summary). The quantities — two tenth deals, the fourth part of a hin — are stated as the text gives them, without conversion or elaboration. (Application) The gesture of the firstfruits is tiny — a single sheaf — yet it stands before the entire harvest: offer the first sheaf first, and only then begin to eat the new grain. This is the most direct correction imaginable to the assumption that the harvest is simply ours. If the first sheaf is not offered, abundance will be misread as a purely human achievement; once it is offered, every subsequent grain is redefined as a gift.

(Fact) The firstfruits command also carries a premise worth noticing (v. 10, Summary): it opens with the condition "when you come into the land that I give you and reap its harvest." In other words, when this feast was commanded there was as yet no land and no harvest — the people were still in the wilderness, yet the calendar already legislated for a land not yet possessed. (Application) This is worship paid in advance on a promise: learn the liturgy of thanksgiving first, then go in and receive the inheritance. Reverse the order — receive the harvest first and decide afterwards whether to be thankful — and thanksgiving will mostly be diluted by abundance. By writing thanksgiving into the calendar, the text refuses to let it depend on mood. There is one more layer: verse 3 says the Sabbath is to be kept "in all your dwellings" (quotation above), and verses 14 and 21 tie the perpetual statute to all dwellings as well (Summary). The calendar's jurisdiction is not a sacred zone around the sanctuary; wherever the people live, the calendar goes with them. The territory of faith expands with the dwelling places.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) The sheaf raises a famous question of reckoning: verse 11 says the priest shall wave it "on the morrow after the sabbath" (Summary), and verse 15 counts the seven sabbaths from that same morrow. The question is whether "the sabbath" here means the weekly seventh day, or the festal rest day at the head of Unleavened Bread. In historical tradition, the Pharisaic reading generally took "the sabbath" as the first day of Unleavened Bread, fixing the waving on the sixteenth day of the first month; the Sadducees insisted on the weekly Sabbath, so that the waving always fell on a Sunday. The two readings made the date of the fiftieth day differ throughout history. This sermon sets both readings side by side, with their provenance, and decides neither; to pronounce dogmatically for one side would go beyond what the text of Leviticus 23 itself can bear. (Fact) On one point, however, both sides agree: the counting begins on the day the sheaf is waved — and that is the subject of the next section.

### III. 24:00-42:00 Numbering fifty days: from one sheaf to a new grain offering

(Fact) After the sheaf, the text gives not the date of another feast but an exercise in counting: "And ye shall count unto you from the morrow after the sabbath, from the day that ye brought the sheaf of the wave offering; seven sabbaths shall be complete: Even unto the morrow after the seventh sabbath shall ye number fifty days; and ye shall offer a new meat offering unto the LORD." (Lev 23:15-16, KJV) Count seven complete sabbaths, and on the morrow after the seventh sabbath — the fiftieth day — offer a new grain offering to the LORD. That is the feast later called Pentecost, "the fiftieth." The text gives this feast no calendrical date of its own; its date is counted out — seven sevens, forty-nine, and one more: the fiftieth day. (Application) This is one of the most moving structures in the chapter: the fiftieth day does not wait for you on the calendar; you must count your way to it, day by day. For fifty days Israel reaped barley and then wheat, and counted as they reaped; labour and counting proceeded together, and the days were sanctified in sweat. Faith here is not a red circle printed on a calendar but a lesson spoken aloud every morning: day one, day two — all the way to the fiftieth. Counting gives waiting a shape.

(Fact) What is offered on the fiftieth day stands in sharp contrast to the single sheaf (vv. 17-21, Summary): two wave loaves are brought from the dwellings, made of two tenth deals of fine flour and baked with leaven, as firstfruits to the LORD; with the bread are offered seven lambs of the first year without blemish, one young bullock, and two rams as a burnt offering, one kid of the goats for a sin offering, and two lambs of the first year as peace offerings, the priest waving the bread with the lambs as a wave offering; on that same day a holy convocation is proclaimed, and no servile work is done. The numbers are listed as the text gives them: two loaves, two tenth deals, seven, one, two. (Application) Mark the leaven. Unleavened Bread purged leaven with utmost strictness; fifty days later the new grain offering is expressly baked with leaven and offered. Head and tail answer each other across the count: the haste of the exodus and the settled ordinariness of life in the land are strung on the single thread of the counting. Leavened bread is daily bread, the real bread of people's homes; and it is precisely this offering, fragrant with everyday life, that God accepts.

(Fact) The section on the fiftieth day closes, unexpectedly, not with sacrifice but with a social statute (v. 22, Summary): when you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor gather the gleanings; you shall leave them for the poor and for the stranger — and the verse ends once more with "I am the LORD your God" (the verse's closing words in the text, here cited in summary). That sentence, "I am the LORD your God," is one of the chapter's theme sentences, and it returns again at the end of the Tabernacles section (v. 43). (Application) The climax of the fiftieth day is not the sacrificial list; it is the corner of the field. On the very day of the fullest harvest, the text commands: do not reap it all. If feast-day worship cannot leave a corner standing in the field for the poor, then the wave offering, however correctly waved, lacks its final stroke. Worship and mercy meet in a single verse, and that is the sharpest lesson the fiftieth day leaves the church today: the truth of your thanksgiving is verified by the corner you leave for somebody else.

(Fact) The corner law is no isolated case in this book: Leviticus 19 gives an almost identical command about reaping (Lev 19:9-10, Summary), likewise assigning the leavings to the poor and the sojourner. That chapter 23 restates the law inside the fiftieth-day passage is itself an act of interpretation — the author deliberately lodges social mercy inside the harvest feast rather than only in the social law collections. (Application) In Leviticus's theology, then, a feast is never merely a vertical line between man and God; every feast carries a horizontal line as well, out to the poor and the stranger in the field. The person who counts fifty days must also count how much he has left. Count only your own harvest and not the portion left for others, and the counting is only half done. Offer one sheaf at firstfruits, two loaves at the fiftieth day, and leave a corner in the field — those three together make the complete worship of the harvest: firstfruits to God, new grain before the congregation, and room left for the poor.

(New Testament reading) The New Testament takes up this very thread of counting: in Acts 2, on the day of Pentecost, the Spirit descends and the church is born — "And when the day of Pentecost was fully come, they were all with one accord in one place." (Acts 2:1, KJV) The New Testament reading takes the fiftieth day from a grain-harvest feast and reads it as a day of spiritual harvest. This sermon labels that layer explicitly as a New Testament reading: it is the church's faithful interpretation, with its own New Testament textual basis, but it must not be read back into Leviticus so that the text of Leviticus is left with nothing but the shadow of a type — the fiftieth day of Leviticus is first of all itself: harvest, counting, and the corner of the field.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The autumn group: trumpets, atonement, booths

(Fact) The second half of the year falls in the seventh month, where three observances are packed within fifteen days, in a clear order. First, the first day of the month: "In the seventh month, in the first day of the month, shall ye have a sabbath, a memorial of blowing of trumpets, an holy convocation." (Lev 23:24, KJV) The mark of this day is sound — the "blowing of trumpets," Hebrew terûʿāh (Strong's H8643), denotes the blast of the horn and the shout; BDB gives horn-blast, alarm, and shout of joy among its senses. The text does not say what this day memorialises, nor why the trumpets are blown; it commands only rest, assembly, and the blast. (Application) The silence itself deserves preaching: the autumn group begins with a trumpet blast for which no reason is given. The worship of the second half of the year starts with being awakened — a person must first be roused by the sound before there can be any talk of afflicting the soul ten days later. The trumpet's function is reminder and summons: before the Day of Atonement is entered, there is first a blast to shake a person out of the numbness of the everyday.

(Fact) Then, the tenth day: "Also on the tenth day of this seventh month there shall be a day of atonement: it shall be an holy convocation unto you; and ye shall afflict your souls, and offer an offering made by fire unto the LORD." (Lev 23:27, KJV) "Atonement" is kippurîm (Strong's H3725), the plural form of the word for covering, for atonement. This day's rules are the sternest in the chapter (vv. 28-32, Summary): no work at all may be done; whoever does not afflict himself on that day is cut off from his people, and whoever does any work is likewise cut off; the day is called a sabbath of rest, kept from the evening of the ninth day to the evening of the tenth, from even unto even, with self-affliction throughout. (Fact) Structurally, this chapter's Day of Atonement legislation interlocks with Leviticus 16: chapter 16 details the high priest's entry into the Holy of Holies and the rite of atonement for sanctuary and people, while this chapter legislates only from the congregation's side — the date, the holy convocation, the cessation of work, the afflicting of souls. The two are two angles on one day, and this sermon treats them as structural intertext only, without importing chapter 16's ritual detail into chapter 23. (Application) Here the inner order of the autumn group shows its depth: trumpets on the first to awaken, atonement on the tenth to humble — awakening first, self-affliction after; hearing first, bowing after. A Day of Atonement without trumpets easily decays into formality; trumpets without a Day of Atonement are merely noise. The nine intervening days are the time left for a person to let the sound of the horn sink into the heart.

(Fact) Then, on the fifteenth day, the Feast of Tabernacles: "The fifteenth day of this seventh month shall be the feast of tabernacles for seven days unto the LORD." (Lev 23:34, KJV) The "booths" are sukkôt (the plural of sukkāh, Strong's H5521), temporary shelters. The feast's rules are written in two movements (vv. 35-38 and vv. 39-43, Summary): offerings made by fire to the LORD for seven days, a holy convocation and no servile work on the first day, and on the eighth day another holy convocation, a solemn assembly, with no servile work; on the fifteenth day of the seventh month, when the produce of the land has been gathered in, the feast is kept for seven days, the first day and the eighth day each being a sabbath; on the first day the fruit of splendid trees, branches of palm trees, boughs of leafy trees, and willows of the brook are taken up, and the people rejoice before the LORD for seven days; and the feast is kept to the LORD seven days every year (v. 41, Summary). All native Israelites are to dwell in booths for seven days. (Fact) The reason for booth-dwelling is given by the text itself, and no other feast in the chapter states its reason so directly: "That your generations may know that I made the children of Israel to dwell in booths, when I brought them out of the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God." (Lev 23:43, KJV) The reason is summed up in one word: remembering. What is remembered is not the harvest itself but the days on the exodus road when there were no walls and no roof-tiles — the days in which God sustained them. (Application) To set the Feast of Booths in the season of the fullest harvest is a deliberate contrast: the barns are full, and precisely then you move out into a booth; the house is secure, and precisely then you return to temporary shelter. Remember with the body: security was not always so; security is the grace given to a people who once lived in booths. If remembering moves the mouth but not the body, the next generation is left with knowledge and no memory; booth-dwelling lets memory grow on the body — the sound of wind in the booth's branches is the book of Exodus being read aloud to the children.

(Application) Look at the autumn group as a whole: trumpets awakening (the first), atonement bowing (the tenth), booths rejoicing (from the fifteenth, seven days, with the solemn assembly on the eighth). The emotional curve runs from awakening, to humbling, to joy — and the joy is placed after atonement, and it is the longest and gladdest feast in the chapter: seven days of rejoicing, and an eighth day besides. This is no casual arrangement of a calendar: in Israel's faith, joy is joy that has passed through atonement, not joy that has bypassed sin. Reverse the order, and joy turns cheap.

(Fact) The name of the eighth day of Tabernacles also deserves attention: the text calls that day ʿăṣeret (Strong's H6116), "a solemn assembly, the closing gathering of a feast." After seven days in booths, the feast does not end in dispersal but is gathered up in a yet more solemn assembly. (Application) That puts the full stop not only to the autumn group but to the feasts of the entire year: the last stroke of the calendar is not the afterglow of a banquet but the solemnity of assembly. Look back over the year: spring sets out from the redemption-memory of Passover and walks through fifty counted days to the fiftieth day; autumn sets out from the trumpet, passes through the humbling of atonement to the joy of booths, and is gathered up by the solemn assembly. Redemption, harvest, repentance, joy, solemnity — this calendar ranges over nearly the whole terrain of a believer's life, and every stretch of it has a pinned-down date, a fixed measure, and a stated reason. It permits no one to live only in his favourite season: the man who will not afflict his soul has no share in the joy of the booths, and the man who will not stop work for the sabbath cannot so much as enter the door of the feasts.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: I am the LORD your God

(Fact) The chapter's conclusion stands in verses 37-38 (Summary): the fire offerings, burnt offerings, grain offerings, sacrifices, and drink offerings of the feasts each belong to their own day; these are besides the sabbaths of the LORD, and besides the people's gifts, vow offerings, and freewill offerings. Verse 44 seals the whole: "And Moses declared unto the children of Israel the feasts of the LORD." (KJV) Beginning and end answer each other: in verse 2 the LORD speaks to Moses and tells him to speak to Israel; in verse 44 Moses has done it — the word came out from God, passed through Moses's mouth, and landed on the people's calendar. (Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, time belongs to God: the feasts are the LORD's feasts, and man is the keeper of the appointment, not the master of the calendar. Second, rest is the skeleton: from the seventh day of every week to the great sabbaths that enlarge it within the feasts, stopping is the first lesson of worship. Third, remembering shapes identity: Passover remembers the exodus, and so does Tabernacles, head and tail of the year, with the fifty counted days and the merciful field-corner between them — a people knows who it is by retelling, year after year, where it was brought out from.

(New Testament reading) The New Testament takes up this calendar at several clear points; as throughout, the layer is labelled, and what follows is stated as a layer of reading, not mixed into the original sense of Leviticus: First Corinthians 5 reads Christ as the Passover lamb — "For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us:" (1 Cor 5:7, KJV); Acts 2 and the day of Pentecost were treated in section III; the story of John 7 unfolds during the Feast of Tabernacles — "Now the Jews' feast of tabernacles was at hand." (John 7:2, KJV) — and Jesus's going up to Jerusalem for the feast is that chapter's narrative background; and Hebrews 4 reads the rest as a hope still remaining for the people of God — "There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God." (Heb 4:9, KJV) (Application) What these readings mean for the church is this: Leviticus 23 is not an expired calendar. The questions it raises are still in force — Whose hand governs your time? Will you stop? Do you remember where you were brought out from? Every generation must stand before this calendar and answer those three questions again.

(Application) One last word on preaching and calendars. The least ambiguous thing about Leviticus 23 is its precision: which month, which day, how many days to count, what measure to offer — the text writes all of it out clearly. Whoever preaches this chapter must first be trained by that precision: quotations according to the base text, numbers according to the text, disputes set side by side as they stand, and ignorance honestly confessed. Half the beauty of a calendar is its theology; the other half is its exactness, to the last fraction. A preacher who is casual with dates and numbers cannot preach the reverence of this calendar. Be faithful in the small things first; then you are fit to speak of the great remembering. May we learn from this chapter first to be keepers of the appointment, then keepers of the covenant, and at last those who hand the covenant on to the next generation — as Moses did: he heard, and he declared it.

## Devotional Excerpt

What Leviticus 23 gives me is not a festival table but an audit of my time. My calendar, I realise, is never neutral: what fills it is either the pressure of work or the appointments of God. The text says the feasts are the LORD's feasts — some days belong to Him first, and only then do I learn to hand over my schedule. The Sabbath stands at the head of the chapter to remind me that stopping is not waste; it is the confession that the world does not run on me. From the first sheaf to the fiftieth day I learn to count grace as I live, instead of letting the days slip by in a blur. When the seventh month comes, the trumpet wakes me first, the Day of Atonement bows me down, and the Feast of Booths then teaches me to rejoice inside a flimsy shelter, remembering that I am one of those brought out of Egypt. To dwell in a booth when the barns are full, and to leave the field's corner when there is plenty — every stroke of this calendar is rescuing me from taking things for granted. May I spend this year not merely passing the days, but keeping the appointments. I will start this morning with the smallest step: circle this week's Sabbath in my schedule and stop using it to plug the gaps of work; give thanks at the table for this season's grain instead of eating in a hurry; and if there is anything to spare at hand, learn to leave a little of it for someone in need. The calendar is large; the road is small — keep the next day well first, then count the next day.

## Endnotes

1. The KJV quotations in this manuscript were checked verse by verse against the KJV text of Leviticus 23 (Bible Gateway / Bible.com chapter texts). The Chinese original's quotation of Lev 23:2 follows the New Punctuation Union Version, which carries no closing clause corresponding to the KJV's "even these are my feasts"; each manuscript quotes its own base text and neither is supplemented from the other.
2. môʿēd (H4150): BDB, s.v. מוֹעֵד, gives appointed time, appointed season, set feast; HALOT likewise centres on the fixed point in time and the festival. In this chapter it occurs at 23:2, 4, 37, 44 and elsewhere, the keyword of the inclusio that frames the chapter.
3. miqrāʾ qōdeš (H4744 + H6944): miqrāʾ is "that which is proclaimed, the convocation," from the root qārāʾ, "to proclaim, to call"; hence "holy convocation" carries both public proclamation and congregational assembly, and is not a static "meeting."
4. šabbātôn (H7677): an intensive formation from šabbāt, denoting complete, solemn rest. In this chapter it stands at 23:3 (KJV "sabbath of rest"), 24, 32, and 39 — the hidden thread of rest running through the chapter.
5. ʾōmer (H6016): BDB distinguishes two senses — the sheaf (Lev 23:10-15; Deut 24:19) and the dry measure, the omer (Exod 16, a tenth of an ephah). This chapter takes the sheaf sense; the sermon notes both so that hearers do not confuse it with the manna measure of Exodus.
6. tenûpāh (H8573): the wave offering, from the verb nûp, "to wave, to move to and fro." In this chapter it marks the sheaf of firstfruits (23:15) and the two loaves of the fiftieth day (23:17, 20), bracketing the harvest season at its opening and its close.
7. The "morrow after the sabbath" dispute: Pharisaic tradition read "the sabbath" as the first day of Unleavened Bread, waving the sheaf on the sixteenth of the first month; the Sadducees read the weekly Sabbath, so the waving always fell on a Sunday. The dispute carries over into the date of the fiftieth day; see the discussions in Milgrom and Hartley. This sermon sets the two side by side and decides neither.
8. The historical divergence over the date of the fiftieth day follows from note 7: a different starting day yields a different fiftieth day; later mainstream Judaism counted from the sixteenth of the first month. The number "fifty days" itself is not in dispute on either side.
9. terûʿāh (H8643): BDB's senses include horn-blast, alarm signal, and shout of joy. The phrase at Lev 23:24, zikrôn terûʿāh, is rendered "a memorial of blowing of trumpets" in the KJV; the chapter does not state what is memorialised, and the sermon's observation of a deliberate blank rests on that textual fact.
10. kippurîm (H3725): the plural form, found in the Day of Atonement texts (Lev 23:27-28; 25:9, etc.). The relation between Leviticus 16 and Leviticus 23 on this day is treated in this sermon as structural intertext only: the former details the priestly rite, the latter legislates for the congregation; neither replaces the other.
11. sukkôt (plural of sukkāh, H5521): the booths of Lev 23:42 carry the definite article in the plural. The historical reason for booth-dwelling is stated by the text itself at 23:43, quoted verbatim in this manuscript from the KJV; the Chinese original quotes the same verse from its own base text, which prints a reverential space in "你们的 神" — retained in that quotation only, as the base text prints it, and nowhere imitated in the sermon's own prose.
12. Calendar versions compared: Exodus 23:14-17 and Deuteronomy 16:1-17 give the "three times a year" frame (Unleavened Bread, the Feast of Weeks, Tabernacles), with dates and details differing at several points from this chapter. This sermon records the comparison as an intertextual observation only, without harmonising and without ranking the versions.

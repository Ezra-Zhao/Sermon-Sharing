# Holy unto Me, Lest the Land Spue You Out

## The Structure and Legal Logic of the Penalty Clauses in Leviticus 20

**Abstract**: Leviticus 20 is the penalty counterpart to the prohibitions of Leviticus 18. This sermon does three things only: it compares the literary division of labour between the two chapters and the correspondence of their items; it maps the distribution of the three penalty registers - death, cutting off, and bearing one's own sin - and analyses their graded legal logic; and it reads the exhortation frame of verses 22-27, in which the warning of the land and the call to be holy unto the LORD bring the chapter to its theological close, sealed by the final verse on mediums and wizards.

---

## I. Prohibition and Penalty: The Division of Labour between Two Chapters (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) Leviticus 18 and Leviticus 20 treat the same set of matters, yet their literary forms differ sharply. Chapter 18 is built mainly of prohibition clauses, item by item declaring what must not be done, framed at head and tail by the warning not to imitate the customs of Egypt and Canaan; its tone is admonitory. Chapter 20 rewrites the same matters as clauses with stated penal consequences: each item first states the act, then immediately declares the corresponding penalty; its tone is casuistic and judicial. Read together, the two chapters show the complete shape of this body of law: one chapter draws the boundary, the other assigns the liability; one tells the people what must not be done, the other tells them what consequence follows if it is done. To preach this chapter apart from chapter 18 is to turn its clauses into an isolated catalogue of severity; to preach chapter 18 apart from this chapter is to turn its prohibitions into exhortations without consequence. The correspondence between the two chapters is the first key to this sermon.

(Fact) The items correspond in broad sequence. The prohibition in chapter 18 against giving one's children to pass through the fire to Molech receives its fullest penal treatment in verses 2-5 of this chapter, which fix not only the penalty of the one who offers, but also the responsibility of bystanders and of the family. The whole set of prohibitions in chapter 18 against approaching near kin is rearranged in verses 9-21 of this chapter according to the gravity of the penalty. The warning in chapter 18 against turning to mediums and wizards appears in verse 6 as the penalty for the one who consults them, and again in verse 27 as the penalty for the practitioners themselves, sealing the chapter. The rearrangement itself carries information: chapter 18 is ordered by category of act, this chapter by category of penalty - death clauses first, then cutting-off clauses, then bearing-one's-sin clauses, and finally exhortation and the sealing verse. In other words, the organising logic of this chapter is not an ethical catalogue but a sentencing schedule. Read in its own order, the chapter presents a penalty table descending in clearly marked grades.

(Application) This division of labour suggests a restrained way of reading: the weight of the chapter lies not in cataloguing which acts are abhorrent, but in showing by what responsibility the boundary of the community is guarded. The severity of the penalty clauses is first a measure of the gravity of the matters, and only second a regulation of execution. In this sermon the preacher treats only the structure of the clauses and their graded legal logic; no direct inference is drawn from these ancient penalties to the law of any modern society. Historical setting is stated only as the existing position of scholarship, and judgements of application are left to hearers to weigh carefully in their own settings. A solemn and academic treatment is not an evasion of the text; it respects the text's own genre as codified law written for a covenant community, not as a curiosity written for later onlookers.

(Fact) The addressees of the chapter also deserve notice. The introductory formula of verse 1 is the same as throughout the book: the words are spoken by the LORD to Moses, and by Moses to Israel. Yet the clauses of verse 2 at once extend their scope to the sojourner (summary: the sense of the verse is that Israelite and sojourner alike, if they give their children to Molech, stand under the same clause). The penal system of this chapter therefore binds not blood membership alone, but everyone who lives in the land and is sheltered by the land's order. The order of the land and the responsibility of those who dwell in it are mutually conditioned - a theme gathered up at the chapter's end in the warning of the land in verse 22, to which the final section of this sermon is devoted.

## II. Molech and the Consultation of Spirits: The First Penalty Group (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) The first penalty group of the chapter is verses 2-8, in two parts. Verses 2-5 treat the giving of children to Molech; verse 6 treats turning to mediums and wizards; verses 7-8 insert an exhortation to self-consecration, joining the penalty clauses directly to the motive of holiness. Leviticus 20:2 in the KJV reads: "Again, thou shalt say to the children of Israel, Whosoever he be of the children of Israel, or of the strangers that sojourn in Israel, that giveth any of his seed unto Molech; he shall surely be put to death: the people of the land shall stone him with stones." The verse supplies three legal elements at once: the subject of the clause is not limited to the native-born; the penalty is death; and the mode of execution is specified as stoning by the people of the land - that is, public execution by the community, not private vengeance. That the clause is written with such specificity shows that, in the legislator's judgement, this is no private religious choice within a household, but a public offence that shakes the foundation of the community.

(Fact) Verses 3-5 immediately add the upward transfer of responsibility when the community defaults (summary: the sense of verses 3-5 is that God Himself sets His face against the one who offers, and cuts him off from among his people; and if the people of the land deliberately hide their eyes and do not put him to death, God sets His face against that man and his clan, and cuts off him and all who follow him in this practice). This supplement carries great legal weight: it presupposes the possibility that human execution may fail - may hide its eyes out of local loyalty, fear, or indifference. The clause answers not by increasing the human penalty, but by declaring that God's own judgement intervenes directly. A human court may default; the covenant Judge does not. This structure gives the chapter's penal system a double layer: earthly execution and divine cutting off run in parallel, the first being the community's responsibility, the second the final safeguard when that responsibility collapses. Once this is seen, the repeated appearance of "cutting off" later in the chapter no longer looks vague: from the start it is uttered in a context where God Himself executes.

(Fact) On the nature of the Molech offering, this sermon maintains the position of the Leviticus 18 sermon and marks the matter as belonging to the level of scholarly discussion. Long-debated questions include whether "Molech" names a deity or a category of offering, how the relevant Hebrew phrases should precisely be construed, and how the archaeological material from Canaan and the Phoenician world relating to child offering should cautiously be correlated with the text. Commentators differ in their judgements, and this sermon reaches no conclusion of its own. One point commands broad agreement: whatever the precise referent of Molech, the object of the clause's condemnation is clear and consistent - the act itself of handing over one's children to an alien power, and the community's acquiescence in it. Archaeological and ancient Near Eastern material is cited in this sermon only in a limited way, at the level of scholarly discussion, with sources named, and never as sensational filler.

(Fact) Verse 6 turns to the one who consults. Leviticus 20:6 in the KJV reads: "And the soul that turneth after such as have familiar spirits, and after wizards, to go a whoring after them, I will even set my face against that soul, and will cut him off from among his people." Note the displacement of subject and object: the one punished here is not the practitioner but the person who turns after them and follows them; the practitioner's own penalty is deferred to verse 27, forming an inclusio across the whole chapter. Lexically, the medium is the 'ob (אוֹב, Strong's H178), denoting one who evokes the dead, or the medium identity by which this is done; the second term paired with it is yidde'oni (יִדְּעֹנִי, Strong's H3049), literally related to knowing, glossed by commentators as a knowing one, a spirit-knower. The pair already appears in Leviticus 19:31, and appears again in verses 6 and 27 of this chapter, showing that this is no casual example but a practice repeatedly named by the Holiness Code: seeking the dead and hidden knowledge in place of seeking the covenant God. The penalty for the consulter in this verse is cutting off, executed by God; the penalty for the practitioner in the final verse is death, executed by the community. Two roles on one chain of practice, two penalties - the first instance of the chapter's fine grading.

(Fact) Verses 7-8 interrupt the case law with exhortation. Leviticus 20:7 in the KJV reads: "Sanctify yourselves therefore, and be ye holy: for I am the LORD your God." Verse 8 reads: "And ye shall keep my statutes, and do them: I am the LORD which sanctify you." The sequence repays attention: first the human responsibility to consecrate oneself, then the declaration that it is the LORD who sanctifies. Human obedience comes first in the sentence, yet the divine action named after it is the ground on which that obedience is possible at all. That a holiness motive is inserted in the middle of penalty clauses shows that the legislator does not intend the chapter to be read as a mere table of punishments: the purpose of the clauses is not to manufacture fear, but to mark out the life-boundary of a community that belongs to God in holiness. The same motive returns, in fuller form, in the exhortation frame of verses 24-26, foreshadowed here in advance.


## III. "He Shall Surely Be Put to Death": The Legal Logic of the Capital Group (24:00-42:00)

(Fact) Verses 9-16 form the chapter's capital group. Verse 9 treats cursing father or mother; verse 10, adultery with another man's wife; verses 11-16 treat, in order, lying with a father's wife, with a daughter-in-law, with a male, taking a wife and her mother, and lying with a beast (summary: the sense of these verses is that for each listed act both parties, or the actor, shall surely be put to death, with their blood upon themselves; the case of taking a wife and her mother additionally specifies burning with fire, so that no such wickedness remain among the people). The whole group shares one penal formula, with a uniformity rare in the Pentateuch's laws, indicating that the editor presents them deliberately as a single sentencing unit rather than a scatter of cases. In reading this group, the point is not to identify each act in turn, but to identify the formula: the same act-clause, the same penalty declaration, and the same attribution-of-responsibility clause recur, producing the distinctive cadence of a code.

(Fact) The core of the formula is the emphatic construction "he shall surely be put to death." The Hebrew is mot yumat (מוֹת יוּמָת), formed by placing the infinitive absolute of the verb mut (מוּת, Strong's H4191, "to die") before its cognate passive form; the related noun mawet carries Strong's number H4194. The infinitive absolute set before a cognate verb is a common device of emphasis in Hebrew legal and declarative style, intended to exclude exception and mitigation; it may be rendered literally "dying, he shall be put to death - without fail." As commentators such as Milgrom observe, this formula clusters in the Holiness Code precisely in matters touching the foundations of the community's survival; what it emphasises is not the mechanics of execution but the legislator's classification of the matter: here, in the covenant order, there is no negotiable grey zone. This sermon confines itself to that literary and legal observation and draws from it no claim about modern jurisprudence.

(Fact) The other half of the formula is the attribution clause. Leviticus 20:9 in the KJV reads: "For every one that curseth his father or his mother shall be surely put to death: he hath cursed his father or his mother; his blood shall be upon him." The underlying Hebrew idiom is of the type damav bo (דָּמָיו בּוֹ); the word for blood, dam, is Strong's H1818, and the idiom translates literally as "his blood is upon himself - his blood be on his own head." It recurs in this group in verses 9, 11, 12, 13 and 16, and returns at the chapter's close in verse 27 (its absence from a clause such as verse 10 is abbreviation of the formula, not an omission). Its legal function, on the analysis of commentators such as Hartley, is to declare where the justification lies: the bloodguilt of the executed person rests not on the executors or the community, but on the actor himself; the community, in executing, carries out a sentence already declared, and does not itself shed blood privately. The idiom stands in one line of descent with the ancient principle of Genesis 9, that the blood of man shall be shed by man, and places every execution back inside the frame of the covenant order, so that execution itself does not become a new act of bloodshed.

(Fact) Differentiation remains visible inside the group. Verse 9, on cursing parents, sentences a single person, and the attribution clause is singular; from verse 11 onward most clauses sentence both parties, and the clause is plural - showing that the code sentences according to the shape of participation, not by blanket rule. Verse 14, on taking a wife and her mother, departs from the usual mode and specifies burning (summary: the sense of the verse is that this is wickedness, and they shall be burnt with fire, so that there be no wickedness among you), adding a purpose clause that shows the penalty also aims to remove the continuing presence of the act from the community. Verses 15-16, concerning a human being and a beast, put the beast to death as well (summary: the sense is that the actor shall surely be put to death, and the beast shall be slain); commentators traditionally explain this as removing the site and the memorial of the act, so that the matter does not persist in the community's memory and living space. This sermon states these modes of execution only at the level of the clauses and their scholarly explanation, without elaboration and without any word of imitation.

(Application) A limited comparison with ancient Near Eastern codes helps bring the distinctiveness of this group into focus, provided the comparison keeps to the level of scholarly discussion. On the comparative studies cited in the commentaries of Milgrom and Wenham, ancient Near Eastern collections (such as the Code of Hammurabi and the Middle Assyrian Laws) also prescribe severe penalties, including death, for a number of family and sexual matters, with the legislative aim of guarding family order and inheritance. The difference of the Israelite code lies not in the weight of the penalties themselves, but in the way its grounds are stated: the ground-clauses of this chapter appeal almost never to property or family honour, and repeatedly to holiness and the cleanness of the land. Beneath penalties of comparable external weight stand two different legal narratives. To note this is to understand this chapter's self-presentation accurately, not to offer a general treatise in comparative law; the comparative material serves in this sermon as background only, never as a load-bearing argument.

## IV. Cutting Off and Bearing One's Sin: The Grading of the Penalties (42:00-56:00)

(Fact) Verses 17-21 form the chapter's second sentencing unit, in which the penalties visibly step down and their constituent elements change accordingly. Verse 17, on taking one's sister, reads in the KJV: "And if a man shall take his sister, his father's daughter, or his mother's daughter, and see her nakedness, and she see his nakedness; it is a wicked thing; and they shall be cut off in the sight of their people: he hath uncovered his sister's nakedness; he shall bear his iniquity." Verse 18 treats lying with a woman in her menstrual period (summary: the sense is that both shall be cut off from among their people); verse 19, uncovering the nakedness of a mother's or father's sister (summary: the sense is that they shall bear their iniquity); verse 20, lying with an uncle's wife (summary: the sense is that they shall bear their sin, and shall die childless). Verse 21 closes the unit, in the KJV: "And if a man shall take his brother's wife, it is an unclean thing: he hath uncovered his brother's nakedness; they shall be childless." Across this unit the penalty vocabulary concentrates in three expressions: being cut off, bearing one's iniquity or one's sin, and childlessness. The three overlap with one another, but the formula "he shall surely be put to death" no longer appears, and no mode of execution is specified. The sentencing scale has here moved clearly downward.

(Fact) The inner logic of the grading, as the commentary tradition broadly presents it, may be summarised as follows; this sermon transmits and organises it without adding a verdict of its own. First, compared with the preceding unit, the matters here are mostly transgressions within the kinship structure itself; in the form of the act and the presentation of social harm they differ from the earlier group, whose items either directly violate another's marriage rights or are classified by the community as disorders of the fundamental order, whereas several items here (such as the sister and the brother's wife) belong to questions of prohibited degrees, where in ancient kinship structures ambiguity and disputed space were in fact greater than in the earlier group. Second, the penalties of this unit are centred on relational consequence: cutting off is relational exclusion, childlessness is the severing of family continuity, and bearing one's iniquity is the declaration that the liability is not transferred. None of the three enlists the community to execute a death sentence; the outcome is entrusted to God's governance and the passage of time. Third, the two units together form not simply a table of graver and lighter offences, but a table of divided jurisdiction: wherever a mode of execution and an attribution clause are specified, execution belongs to the community; wherever only cutting off, bearing, and childlessness are declared, the matter belongs to God's own judgement and governance. This division coheres with the double structure presupposed in verses 2-5: the human court has its jurisdiction, and beyond that jurisdiction God Himself disposes.

(Fact) The nature of "cutting off" is the chapter's largest scholarly question, and this sermon follows the rule of presenting the dispute without settling it. The verb is karat (כָּרַת, Strong's H3772, basically "to cut off, cut down"), and its nominal form karet (כָּרֵת) is the conventional term of the discussion; in this chapter it appears repeatedly both in the passive and with God as subject, as in verse 6, "will cut him off from among his people," verse 17, "they shall be cut off," and verse 18, "shall be cut off from among their people." Two main readings coexist in the scholarship. One, represented by Milgrom, holds that cutting off is primarily a divine, theocratic penalty: God Himself terminates the offender's line - by premature death, by lack of offspring, or by removal of the name from the covenant community - and it is not a sentence any human court can execute; supporting evidence is that in this chapter the declarations of cutting off mostly have God as their executing subject and stand next to declarations of childlessness. The other reading holds that, in at least some contexts, cutting off denotes an exclusionary action by the human community - the community's removal of the offender from among the people - in continuity with the cutting-off declarations in Exodus and Numbers where human agency is in view. Each reading has its textual grounds, and the commentary tradition has reached no consensus to this day. This sermon sets out both and decides neither. What is certain is that, on either reading, cutting off stands in this chapter's grading below death and above no penalty: it is the central marker of the middle register.

(Fact) The expression "he shall bear his iniquity" is a fixed idiom of bearing guilt, appearing in this unit in verses 17 and 19, and as "they shall bear their sin" in verse 20. Its legal meaning, on the exposition of commentators such as Wenham, is the declaration that liability is not transferable: for these matters no vicarious procedure is provided and the community does not execute in the offender's place; the actor must bear the consequence himself before God. Set beside "their blood shall be upon them" in the capital group, the two idioms are near in form and distinct in function: the first is an attribution clause at the execution of a sentence, clearing the executors; the second is a retention clause where no death sentence is executed, leaving the liability standing against the actor. Between the one clearing and the one retention, the chapter's graded legal logic shows in fine detail. And the "die childless" of verse 20 and the "they shall be childless" of verse 21 were, in an ancient society that prized family continuity above almost everything, anything but a light consequence: in the commentary tradition it is frequently read as the concrete form of cutting off at the level of the family - the offender's name and offspring do not remain in the remembrance of the covenant community.

(Application) The chapter's penal system may therefore be set out in three registers. The first is the death register: the formula is "he shall surely be put to death," mostly with a mode of execution or an attribution clause attached; the executing agent is the community; the matters covered are those classified as shaking the foundations of the covenant order - the giving of children to Molech, the cursing of parents, the items of verses 10-16, and, in the final verse, the practitioner of mediumship and wizardry. The second is the cutting-off register: the formula is "cut off from among his people"; the executing agent is principally God; the matters covered are those of verses 17 and 18 and the one who consults mediums, and its precise nature remains disputed between the two scholarly readings. The third is the bearing-one's-own-sin register: the formulas are "he shall bear his iniquity," "they shall bear their sin," and childlessness; the executing agent is God's governance; the matters covered are those of verses 19-21. The boundaries are not everywhere absolute: individual clauses combine two registers, as verse 17 speaks both of cutting off and of bearing iniquity, showing that the editor permits the registers to overlap in order to present the composite character of a matter. This arrangement is offered as an observation at the level of structure, for the reader's orientation in the text; it asserts nothing about the modern applicability of any clause.


## V. The Warning of the Land and Holiness unto the LORD: The Exhortation Frame as Close (58:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verses 22-26 form the chapter's exhortation frame and the key to all its penalty clauses. Leviticus 20:22 in the KJV reads: "Ye shall therefore keep all my statutes, and all my judgments, and do them: that the land, whither I bring you to dwell therein, spue you not out." The image of spuing out is no new coinage in this chapter: the closing section of chapter 18 already warned, in the same image, that if the people did the deeds of the Canaanites the land would spue out its new inhabitants as it had spued out the old (summary: the sense of the close of chapter 18 is that the land, having spued out the former nation, will likewise spue out the new people who defile it). The image of the land is here no ornament of rhetoric but a statement of law: the land is not a neutral stage; it belongs to the covenant order, and there is a limit to the way of life it can bear. A community that crosses the boundary forfeits its qualification to dwell in the land, as a body expels a foreign substance. It is precisely this boundary - the one that allows the community to dwell securely - that the penalty clauses guard item by item.

(Fact) Verses 23-24 turn the warning into the positive narrative of election (summary: the sense of verse 23 is that the people must not walk in the customs of the nation being cast out, for they did all these things and God abhorred them; and the sense of verse 24 is that God has promised to give Israel the land flowing with milk and honey for a possession, and declares Himself to be the God who has separated them from the peoples). Verse 25 then descends to the most ordinary level of life, in the KJV: "Ye shall therefore put difference between clean beasts and unclean, and between unclean fowls and clean: and ye shall not make your souls abominable by beast, or by fowl, or by any manner of living thing that creepeth on the ground, which I have separated from you as unclean." Verse 26 is the chapter's theological close, in the KJV: "And ye shall be holy unto me: for I the LORD am holy, and have severed you from other people, that ye should be mine."

(Fact) The word "separated" in verses 24-26 is the chapter's thematic pivot, and its original term must be fixed before any argument is built on it. The verb is badal (בָּדַל, Strong's H914), basically "to divide, to distinguish"; in verses 24-26 of this chapter it appears repeatedly in its causative stem (Hiphil), of God separating Israel from the peoples, and of Israel being commanded to make the same distinction between clean and unclean. The human act of distinction in verse 25 is the mirror image of God's act of separation in verses 24 and 26: as God separated His people from the peoples, so the people learn separation at the level of daily food. The word for holy is the adjective qadosh (קָדוֹשׁ, Strong's H6918); in verse 26 its plural form denotes the state required of the people, and its ground clause is "for I the LORD am holy." Both BDB and HALOT, in their entries for qadosh, stress its association with being set apart and exclusively belonging, rather than merely moral faultlessness. Accordingly, the title of this sermon - "Holy unto Me" and "spue you out" - is taken from the actual wording of verses 26 and 22 in the versions used, not from any other version or from back-translation.

(Fact) Verse 27 seals the chapter, in the KJV: "A man also or woman that hath a familiar spirit, or that is a wizard, shall surely be put to death: they shall stone them with stones: their blood shall be upon them." This verse and verse 6 form an inclusio: verse 6 fixes the cutting off of the consulter, the final verse the death of the practitioner. The chapter's penalties begin with stoning for the Molech offering and end with stoning for the medium and the wizard; at both ends stands death publicly executed by the community, and between them the several registers unfold in order. Such ring closure is no accident in the Holiness Code; it presents the chapter as a closed and complete legal unit: where the boundary is breached, there the declaration of penalty begins and ends.

(Application) In conclusion, this sermon draws only three legal and theological conclusions. First, this chapter is the penalty counterpart of chapter 18, and the sound way to read it is to read the two chapters against each other; either chapter read alone falls out of balance. Second, the chapter's penalties form a graded system: death, cutting off, and bearing one's own sin each have their formula, their objects, and their executing agent, and the logic of the grading is the division of labour between the community's responsibility and God's judgement, not an arbitrary scale of severity. Third, the chapter's destination is not penalty but holiness: the warning of the land and the exhortation frame of holiness unto the LORD show that the purpose of the clauses is to preserve a community set apart, able to dwell securely in the promised land. As to how these ancient clauses should be understood and applied today, positions in scholarship and in the church's traditions are many; this sermon states the text and the scholarly positions and reaches no verdict of modern law or ethics. Hearers are urged, in reverence and caution, to continue examining them within the context of the whole of Scripture.

## Endnotes

1. The correspondence of the two chapters is an observation at the level of structure: Leviticus 18 is built mainly of prohibition clauses, Leviticus 20 of clauses carrying penal consequences; the items broadly overlap while the principles of arrangement differ. This is the shared observation of the Wenham and Hartley commentaries.
2. On Molech, at the level of scholarly discussion: the deity-name reading and the offering-category reading coexist, and the precise construal of the Hebrew phrases is disputed. Following the position of the Leviticus 18 sermon, this sermon cites the discussion only in a limited way and reaches no conclusion.
3. The pair 'ob (H178) and yidde'oni (H3049) appears in Lev 19:31; 20:6, 27; Deut 18:11, among other places. The glosses follow the corresponding entries in BDB and HALOT.
4. The mot yumat formula: the infinitive absolute set before a cognate verb as a structure of emphasis; the verb mut is H4191 and the noun mawet is H4194. The distribution of the formula was checked verse by verse against Leviticus 20 in Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia (BHS).
5. The damav bo idiom: the word for blood, dam, is H1818. In the Chinese original, the Union Version renders the word in Lev 20:9 as "sin" with a parenthetical note that the original reads "blood"; the Chinese sermon quotes the parenthesis with the text, as printed.
6. On burning (Lev 20:14): the priest's daughter in Lev 21:9 is likewise sentenced to burning; the commentary tradition generally reads this as an aggravated penalty carrying the sense of removing all remnant. This sermon states the clause only.
7. On karat (H3772) and the noun karet: the forms follow the BDB and HALOT entries. The coexistence in this chapter of passive constructions and constructions with God as subject is itself one of the textual reasons the two scholarly readings stand side by side.
8. The two readings of cutting off: the theocratic-termination reading is represented by Milgrom (in the relevant entries of his Leviticus 17-22 commentary); the communal-expulsion reading appears in a number of traditional commentaries and in Hartley's discussion. This sermon presents both and settles neither.
9. "Bearing iniquity" (expressions of the nasa' 'awon type) appears elsewhere in Leviticus in the contexts of the sin offering and the scapegoat (Lev 5; 10; 16); the contexts differ, and the sense must be determined by the executing agent in each place, not generalised.
10. Childlessness ('ariri) appears in Lev 20:20-21, and also in Gen 15:2 and Jer 22:30, among other places. Its weight in the ancient family-centred society follows the discussion in Wenham's commentary.
11. Badal (H914) is also a key verb in the separations of Genesis 1 and in the veil separating the Holy Place in Exod 26:33. The causative-stem uses in Lev 20:24-26 were checked against the BHS text.
12. Qadosh (H6918): its plural form denotes the state of the people as a body. The ground clause of Lev 20:26 and the "Ye shall be holy, for I am holy" of Lev 19:2 correspond to each other, but the wording of the two verses in the versions used differs; each is quoted in this sermon as its own verse reads, and neither wording is imported into the other.

## 灵修摘取 Devotional Excerpt

### 中文

本章的刑罚清单读来沉重，章末的话却把心带回安稳之处。神把祂的百姓从万民中分别出来，不是为要他们活在惧怕里，乃是为使他们作祂的民，安居在祂所赐的地。分别从来不是孤芳自赏，而是在每日最平常的选择里，学习与祂的心意对齐：祂怎样分别洁净与不洁净，我们就怎样在饮食起居、言语行事上留心界限。今天也当如此安静省察：我的生活里，有没有一处早已越过界限、自己却掩目不看的地方？求主光照，使我不靠自己的定意站立，只靠祂的恩典，归祂为圣，行在祂所喜悦的路上，一步一步，稳稳当当。这句话的次序很要紧：不是我们先圣洁，祂才接纳我们；乃是祂先拣选，祂先分别，我们才在祂的恩典里学习圣洁。每一天的分别都很具体：一句不该说的话忍住不说，一件不该占的便宜甘心放下，一次不该妥协的场合安静退开。这些小小的顺服，祂都看见，也都记念，必亲自保守到底，永不丢弃。

### English

The penalties in this chapter are solemn reading, yet its closing words lead the heart back to rest. The LORD separated His people from the nations not so that they might live in fear, but that they should be His own, dwelling securely in the land He gave them: "And ye shall be holy unto me: for I the LORD am holy, and have severed you from other people, that ye should be mine" (Lev 20:26, KJV). To be set apart is not self-congratulation; it is learning, in the smallest daily choices, to align our lives with His will. Today, quietly examine one place where a boundary has been crossed and left unexamined, and ask Him, by grace alone, to make you holy unto Him, step by step.

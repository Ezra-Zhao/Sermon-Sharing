## Summary of Contents

Before Moses died, he left behind not an autobiography but a song. The song takes heaven and earth as its audience and "the Rock" as the name of God. Its first half recounts how God bore Israel as an eagle bears her young and guarded him as the apple of his eye; its middle, unsparing, foretells that the day the people grow full and fat will be the day they kick and forsake him; its latter half declares that vengeance belongs to God, that he alone is God, and it closes in mercy. This sermon follows the song's three movements: first hear the witness, then receive the warning, and at last receive the comfort — this song is not a vain word; it is the hearers' life.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, we come before your word, and our hearts first grow quiet. When Moses sang this song, he stood east of the Jordan; he could see Canaan, but he could not enter Canaan; he laid down all the rights and wrongs and merits of his life, and left only your name to the people. Speak to us today in the same way, we pray: let our ears be opened like the heavens, and our hearts receive like tender grass receiving the rain. You are the Rock, and your work is perfect; if any among us stands today at the bank of a river in his own life, unable to go forward and unwilling to go back, hold him up with this ancient song. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: A Dying Man's Song — Why Does It Call Heaven and Earth to Listen First?

Brothers and sisters, the text before us today is not a sermon; it is a song, and one of the last public utterances of Moses in this world. Deuteronomy 31 records that the LORD commanded Moses to write this song and teach it to the children of Israel, "that this song may be a witness for me against the children of Israel" (Deut 31:19). Notice the order: the song comes first, the events after. The people have not yet entered Canaan, have not yet eaten their fill, have not yet forsaken him, and God sets up the witness in advance. When they go astray in days to come, this song will stand up like a witness, point at them, and say: all of this was told to you long ago.

Yet when the song opens, its audience is not man: "Give ear, O ye heavens, and I will speak; and hear, O earth, the words of my mouth." (Deut 32:1) Why call heaven and earth first? Because men die and generations change, but heaven and earth endure; and because men forget — heaven and earth stand as witnesses, so that forgetfulness has no excuse. Isaiah opens his book with the same form of words: "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth:" (Isa 1:2). Two prophets, centuries apart, use the same opening — this is no coincidence of rhetoric; it is the summons of a courtroom: the Creator calls the heaven and earth he made as witnesses to hear the case of his own people.

But Moses says at once that his teaching shall "drop as the rain," his speech "distil as the dew, as the small rain upon the tender herb, and as the showers upon the grass:" (Deut 32:2). The summons of the courtroom falls as spring rain. Words of judgment spoken in a way that nourishes — this is the keynote of the whole song, and the keynote of this whole message today: when God's word comes to us, it may pierce the heart, but it never comes to destroy us; the rain falls so that the tender grass may grow.

"Doctrine" in the original is לֶקַח (leqah, H3948), from the verb lāqah, "to take, to receive" — teaching that has been received and stored up in the heart (after the BDB entry leqah).[1] Moses is not publishing his own lofty opinions; he is passing the baton: what I have received, I now place into your hands. Brothers and sisters, faith is never an invention; it is a transmission. It is not that one generation thinks up a doctrine; it is that each generation takes what it has received and hands it, with its own hands, to the next. Today this song has been handed to us.

## Part One: First Hear the Witness — the Rock Is Perfect, and Grace Comes First (Deut 32:3–14)

### 1. The song begins with God's name, not with man's story

"Because I will publish the name of the LORD: ascribe ye greatness unto our God. He is the Rock, his work is perfect: for all his ways are judgment: a God of truth and without iniquity, just and right is he." (Deut 32:3–4)

The first content word of the whole song is God's name; its first foundation stone is "the Rock." "Rock" in the original is צוּר (sûr, H6697), a firm, massive rock, a towering crag of stone (after BDB and Strong's entry).[2] In Deuteronomy 32 this title occurs five times (vv. 4, 15, 18, 30, 31); it is the theological anchor of the whole song. In the Psalms, David takes up the same title: "The LORD is my rock, and my fortress, and my deliverer; my God, my strength, in whom I will trust;" (Ps 18:2; the Lü Zhenzhong translation here uses the rendering registered in the Chinese source as "盘石" — carried over as a registration, not verified against the Lü text, see the production record). In the New Testament, Paul joins it to Christ: Israel in the wilderness "did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ." (1 Cor 10:4) From Moses' song to David's psalm to Paul's epistle, one Rock runs through the three — God's character does not migrate with the generations. That is what "his work is perfect" means.

"A God of truth and without iniquity" in the original is אֵל אֱמוּנָה וְאֵין עָוֶל (ʾēl ʾĕmûnâ wəʾên ʿāwel): אֱמוּנָה (ʾĕmûnâ, H530) is "faithfulness, steadfastness," from the same root ʾāman as "Amen"; עָוֶל (ʿāwel, H5766) is "injustice, perverseness" (after BDB).[3] God's word can be "Amen"-ed because God's own being is faithfulness; there is no crooked place in his way. A man needs in this life one object on which he can set his whole weight. Moses says: there is one — this Rock.

Henry's commentary, on this verse, points out that the song sets God's perfection before the people first, and only then speaks of the people's corruption, so that men may know: the problem is never on God's side.[4] This order is of the greatest importance. Much of men's complaint against God reasons backward from their own circumstances to God's character; Moses' song does exactly the reverse: first fix the Rock, then look at the waves.

### 2. Remembrance is the medicine for a forgetful generation

"Remember the days of old, consider the years of many generations: ask thy father, and he will shew thee; thy elders, and they will tell thee." (Deut 32:7)

Psalm 78 develops this verse into a whole historical psalm: Asaph says that God's works are to be made known "to their children," "That the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born; who should arise and declare them to their children:" (Ps 78:5–6). Proverbs says the same: "My son, forget not my law; but let thine heart keep my commandments: For length of days, and long life, and peace, shall they add to thee." (Prov 3:1–2) Remembrance is not nostalgia. Nostalgia is a mood; remembrance is a spiritual discipline: deliberately, in order, leafing back through what God has done in the past, page by page. The method Moses gives is very concrete — ask your father, ask your elders. The memory of faith is not in the cloud; it is at the dinner table, in the evenings when the elders open their mouths and tell the stories. If a family never tells how God led them in the past, that family's memory is left with nothing but savings and property, and the third generation will certainly be lost.

"When the most High divided to the nations their inheritance, when he separated the sons of Adam, he set the bounds of the people according to the number of the children of Israel. For the LORD'S portion is his people; Jacob is the lot of his inheritance." (Deut 32:8–9) This verse carries a well-known textual difference: the Masoretic Text reads "according to the number of the children of Israel," while the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Septuagint read here "according to the number of the sons of God"; the Chinese Union Version follows the Masoretic Text. How the two readings are to be settled, scholarship holds side by side to this day; from the pulpit we register only, and do not adjudicate.[5] But whichever reading is followed, the landing point of the verse does not change: the nations each have their bounds in God's hand, and Israel is the "portion" God chose for himself — not because Israel was stronger than others, but because God bound himself together with this people. Election was never a certificate of merit; it is a letter of responsibility.

### 3. The apple of the eye and the eagle: two close-ups of grace

"He found him in a desert land, and in the waste howling wilderness; he led him about, he instructed him, he kept him as the apple of his eye." (Deut 32:10) "The apple of his eye" in the original is אִישׁוֹן (ʾîšôn, H380), literally "the little man (in the eye)" — lean close and look into a person's eye, and in his pupil you will see a tiny image of yourself; the Hebrews used this word for the pupil (after BDB).[6] A man protects his eyes without thinking: a grain of sand flies in, and the eyelid shuts at once. God says he kept Israel as a man keeps the apple of his own eye — instinctively, instantly, without negotiation. Zechariah carries the word further: "for he that toucheth you toucheth the apple of his eye." (Zech 2:8)

"As an eagle stirreth up her nest, fluttereth over her young, spreadeth abroad her wings, taketh them, beareth them on her wings: So the LORD alone did lead him, and there was no strange god with him." (Deut 32:11–12) "Eagle" in the original is נֶשֶׁר (nešer, H5404), a large bird of prey (after BDB).[7] The eagle stirs up the nest: she tears the comfort apart and drives the eaglet out of the nest to learn to fly; and when the eaglet falls, the mother eagle dives beneath it and catches it on her back. In Exodus 19 God himself used the same figure: "Ye have seen what I did unto the Egyptians, and how I bare you on eagles' wings, and brought you unto myself." (Exod 19:4) Brothers and sisters, when God tears your nest apart, it is not to dash you to death; it is so that you will fly. When he lets you fall a little way, it is because his back is already waiting underneath. Look back at the most panic-stricken moments of your life — which of them was not like this? The nest was torn, the heart panicked, the back caught you.

Verses 13 and 14 describe the fullness: riding on the high places of the earth, sucking honey out of the rock and oil out of the flinty rock, butter of kine, fat of lambs, the finest wheat, the pure blood of the grape. Grace written to this point is abundant almost to luxury. Keep this picture in mind, because the next movement of the song falls from exactly this picture of fullness.

Notice also a detail that is easily read past: the honey was sucked out of the rock, and the oil out of the flinty rock. That is to say, Israel's abundance was not luck that grew naturally out of the ground; it was grace pressed out by God's own hand from the place least likely to produce anything. Psalm 81 uses the same picture to win the people back: "He should have fed them also with the finest of the wheat: and with honey out of the rock should I have satisfied thee." (Ps 81:16) If a man is willing to trace his fullness back to the Rock, he will not easily kick the Rock away in his fullness. The discipline of thanksgiving comes down to one thing: do not let your mouth remember only the taste and forget the source. Every mouthful of honey must be labelled in the heart with its place of origin — the Rock.

## Part Two: Then Receive the Warning — Jeshurun Waxed Fat, and Kicked (Deut 32:15–29)

### 1. The danger is not in want, but in fullness

"But Jeshurun waxed fat, and kicked: thou art waxen fat, thou art grown thick, thou art covered with fatness; then he forsook God which made him, and lightly esteemed the Rock of his salvation." (Deut 32:15)

"Jeshurun," יְשֻׁרוּן (Yəšurûn, H3484), is God's pet name for Israel, from yāšār, "upright" — "the upright one"; it occurs twice more in the next chapter (Deut 33:5, 26).[8] A people nicknamed "the upright one" by God is written in the song as growing fat and kicking — this is the most heart-piercing irony in the whole song. "Waxed fat" in the original is שָׁמַן (šāman, H8080), "to grow fat, greasy"; the "kicked" is בָּעַט (bāʿaṭ, H1163), the action of livestock kicking out with the leg after it has eaten its fill (after BDB).[9] Moses' words show no politeness: after the people had eaten and drunk their fill in God's hand, their answer to God was like a beast, fed full, kicking away the master who fed it.

Notice the word "gradually" in the Chinese fixed text. Forsaking does not happen overnight: the fattening is gradual, the thickening is gradual, and by the time the kick lands, everyone around is astonished — how did he become like this? In fact the inside had been travelling a long way for a long time. Proverbs warns along the same path: "Lest I be full, and deny thee, and say, Who is the LORD?" (Prov 30:9) Agur's prayer and Moses' song, generations apart, diagnose the same sickness in one taste: hardship rarely makes men leave God; prosperity often makes men forget God. The church is often pure under persecution and soft in comfort — this is not one generation's problem; it is Jeshurun's sickness, the sickness of human nature.

"They provoked him to jealousy with strange gods, with abominations provoked they him to anger. They sacrificed unto devils, not to God; to gods whom they knew not, to new gods that came newly up, whom your fathers feared not. Of the Rock that begat thee thou art unmindful, and hast forgotten God that formed thee." (Deut 32:16–18) The mark of the new idols is that they "came newly up": every age has its fashionable gods of the season — what this age's are, I need not name; the one your phone keeps you on the longest is, most likely, it. An idol was never defined as the statue in the temple; it is whatever takes the Rock's place in your heart.

### 2. The discipline is real, but God's anger has a rein

In verses 19 to 27 God's words grow heavier sentence by sentence: "I will hide my face from them, I will see what their end shall be:" (Deut 32:20) — fire kindled, arrows spent, mischiefs heaped up. I will not avoid these words, and I will not soften them on God's behalf. The God of the Bible is not an elder who only smiles; his holiness answers sin with real anger, and the hiding of his face is real judgment. A pulpit that preaches grace without discipline has preached the Rock into cotton wool.

But look carefully at verses 26 and 27: God says, "I said, I would scatter them into corners," and then immediately holds back — "Were it not that I feared the wrath of the enemy, lest their adversaries should behave themselves strangely, and lest they should say, Our hand is high, and the LORD hath not done all this." God's discipline has boundaries set by God himself: he disciplines his own people, but he will not let the enemy use it to shame his name. This sentence (Deut 32:35) was taken up twice in the New Testament, by Paul and by the author of Hebrews, as an exhortation — "Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord." (Rom 12:19; Heb 10:30, "Vengeance belongeth unto me, I will recompense, saith the Lord.") To the one who suffers, this word is a great release: vengeance is not in your hand; you do not have to spend your whole life extracting a justice. Justice is in the Rock's hand; he will not forget, and he will not mistake.

"They have moved me to jealousy with that which is not God; they have provoked me to anger with their vanities: and I will move them to jealousy with those which are not a people; I will provoke them to anger with a foolish nation." (Deut 32:21) This verse was later taken by Paul twice into the argument of the gospel: in Romans 10 he quotes this word to show that God will use the mercy shown to the Gentiles to move Israel to jealousy (Rom 10:19; cf. Rom 11:11, 14). You see, God's mercy is buried inside God's discipline: his people worshipped that "which is not God," so he raises up those "which are not a people" to move them — not because he has cast them off, but to wake them with the heaviest hand. If the church today sleeps in its comfort, God will also use unexpected people and unexpected revivals outside to move us. When you are moved, do not fly into shame and anger; hear in it a Father's hand.

The judgment imagery of verses 22 to 25 is so dense it takes the breath away: a fire kindled in anger, burning to the lowest hell; arrows spent; mischiefs heaped; hunger, burning heat, the teeth of beasts, the sword, terror — named one by one. I pause here and say in earnest to my brothers and sisters: these words are not pleasant to hear, but they are God's words. Whoever cuts judgment out of the Bible cuts out not a few pages but the holiness of God himself; a god who shows no reaction to sin is not a Rock either, but a clay idol. If our gospel preaching has nothing left but comfort, the cross becomes superfluous — it is precisely because the end of sin is this real that the Lord who "wounds, and heals" is this precious.

**Ignition point (1)**: Brothers and sisters, I must say a heavy word to you who are standing in fullness today: Jeshurun's tragedy was not that God gave too little; it was that his memory was too poor. Your day is today, not tomorrow. While you still remember whose child you are, while the kick has not yet been kicked, turn back today and count: every mouthful of rice in your bowl, every coin in your hand, every member of your family asleep beside your bed — which of these was not honey sucked out of the Rock? Forgetting begins with fullness; thanksgiving begins today. Do not wait until you have lost them to remember; remembering at loss is called regret; remembering today is called worship.

### 3. O that they were wise, and would consider their latter end

"For they are a nation void of counsel, neither is there any understanding in them. O that they were wise, that they understood this, that they would consider their latter end!" (Deut 32:28–29)

This is God's own voice inside the whole warning: "O that they were." God here is not pronouncing sentence; he is sighing. To consider the latter end is to look at the ending once, at the beginning of a thing: if this kick is kicked, what is the end? If this idol is worshipped, what is the end? In Deuteronomy 30 Moses had already said the same thing through: "I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life," (Deut 30:19). The man who considers the latter end slows down at the moment of choosing. May God give us that slowness: at every crossroads of life, first ask about the end, then take the step.

## Part Three: At Last, Comfort — He Alone Is God; He Wounds, and He Heals (Deut 32:30–47)

### 1. One chasing a thousand: victory and defeat were never in the numbers

"How should one chase a thousand, and two put ten thousand to flight, except their Rock had sold them, and the LORD had shut them up?" (Deut 32:30) Moses here teaches the people to read history: Israel's defeats never came from the balance of forces; and when they won, that did not come from the balance of forces either. Leviticus had long promised that the state of obedience would be "five of you shall chase an hundred, and an hundred of you shall put ten thousand to flight" (Lev 26:8). The coefficient of victory and defeat is not on man's side; it is on the Rock's side. It is the same with the church: whether a church is strong or weak is not read off its numbers, its budget, or its building, but off whether the Rock is still in its midst. If any among us is holding on, one against a thousand — holding up a family, an illness, a shop about to fall — you must hear this word: the day you can no longer hold on has not yet come, because the one who holds you up is not yourself.

Verse 31 carries a surprising testimony: "For their rock is not as our Rock, even our enemies themselves being judges." (Deut 32:31) Even the enemies can see it: Israel's victory and defeat do not rest on Israel, but on whether their Rock is greater than the other rocks. Brothers and sisters, your testimony is the same: the world may not take in your reasoning, but it can see your Rock — whether you fall in the valley or stand, whether you forget grace in fullness, whether you take your own vengeance when you are wronged. A life held up by the Rock is itself a sermon, and of the kind even the enemies must acknowledge. May God make us such people: no arguing needed; standing there, we are the evidence.

### 2. "I, even I, am he": the declaration of the One, and the hand that heals

"See now that I, even I, am he, and there is no god with me: I kill, and I make alive; I wound, and I heal: neither is there any that can deliver out of my hand." (Deut 32:39)

This is one of the sharpest declarations of the one God in the Old Testament, in the same tone as Isaiah's "I am the LORD, and there is none else, there is no God beside me:" (Isa 45:5). Hannah's prayer answers it almost word for word: "The LORD killeth, and maketh alive:" (1 Sam 2:6). Notice that the landing point of this declaration is not philosophy but comfort: "I wound, and I heal" — the hand that wounds and the hand that heals are the same hand. If God were only a bystander, his oneness would have nothing to do with us; his oneness is gospel precisely because this one God lays his own hand to the work: the wound he permits, he undertakes to heal; when he hides his face, his ears are not closed. Verse 36 is the turning point of temperature in the whole song: "For the LORD shall judge his people, and repent himself for his servants, when he seeth that their power is gone, and there is none shut up, or left." When the people have no recourse left at all — none shut up, and none left — that is exactly when God acts. Man's end is the beginning of God's mercy; this old saying has its hardest textual root in Deuteronomy 32.

### 3. A joyful close: this is not a vain word; it is your life

Before the declaration of the One, two more verses deserve a slow reading: "Is not this laid up in store with me, and sealed up among my treasures? To me belongeth vengeance, and recompence; their foot shall slide in due time: for the day of their calamity is at hand, and the things that shall come upon them make haste." (Deut 32:34–35) God's judgment is no outburst of mood; it is a sealed verdict kept in his treasuries, with a date on it; God's timetable is not inside man's urging, nor inside man's forgetting. In verses 40 to 42 God lifts up his hand to heaven and swears by his everlasting life, whets his glittering sword, and takes hold on judgment — these war images belong to God's sovereignty; the Bible has never put this sword into the hand of any man or any group. The New Testament turns this same "vengeance is mine" into an instruction to the persecuted: do not avenge yourselves, but rather give place (Rom 12:19). The right posture for reading this passage is to bow in reverence, not to roll up one's sleeves. God will repay; therefore I need not repay — that is the whole use of these two verses for an ordinary believer; bend it one inch, and it becomes fanaticism.

The last line of the song is a command for the nations to rejoice together: "Rejoice, O ye nations, with his people: for he will avenge the blood of his servants, and will render vengeance to his adversaries, and will be merciful unto his land, and to his people." (Deut 32:43) The word rendered "will be merciful" in the KJV is in the original כִּפֶּר (kippēr, H3722), the very word "to make atonement" used of the sin offering (after BDB).[10] Paul, in Romans 15, quotes this line as the ground for the nations sharing in the gospel (Rom 15:10); and in Revelation the song of the Lamb is sung side by side with "the song of Moses" (Rev 15:3). A song of judgment lands at last on the rejoicing of all nations — because the end point of God's vengeance is not destruction but cleansing; the end of his repayment is that his people are redeemed, and the nations too come in and sing together.

When the song was finished, Moses spoke his last charge to the people: "Set your hearts unto all the words which I testify among you this day, which ye shall command your children to observe to do, all the words of this law. For it is not a vain thing for you; because it is your life: and through this thing ye shall prolong your days in the land, whither ye go over Jordan to possess it." (Deut 32:46–47) Not a vain thing — the Hebrew says this word is "not empty"; it has weight; it is loaded with life. Then the camera of the text pulls back: that same day God commanded Moses to go up Mount Nebo and view the land from afar, "but thou shalt not go thither" (Deut 32:52). The man who had sung of the Rock all his life himself stood on this side of the river. This is not God treating him ill; it is the lesson every preacher and every parent must learn at last: the song you sing travels farther than you do; what you sow, you may not reap with your own hands, but the song will enter that land in your place.

Wiersbe, in his exposition of Deuteronomy, reminds the reader that the Song of Moses is a song for "the next generation": Moses knew he could not enter, so he made the faith into a form that could be sung and left it behind.[11] Henry likewise points out that God commanded Moses to teach the song to the people to sing precisely so that the truth would ride the melody into the memory — a man may forget a sermon he has heard, but he can hardly forget a song he has sung.[4] This is exactly where today's message lands: what do you intend to leave to your children? Houses grow old, savings get spent; only the song you have sung to them will, on the day they are farthest from home, sound of itself.

**Ignition point (2) · The closing call**: Brothers and sisters, this song has been sung today in front of you. The heavens are listening with bowed ear; the earth is listening; your children are listening to how you answer. I ask you who stand in fullness: will you go on kicking, or will you turn back today? I ask you who stand at the end: you have no strength left, have you? None shut up and none left — that is you, is it not? I tell you, verse 36 was written for you — at exactly this moment he will judge his people, and he repents himself for his servants. Do not wait until tomorrow; do not wait until you feel a little better. Today, set your whole life on the Rock: he wounds, he heals; he kills, he makes alive; and out of his hand no one can snatch you. Now, please stand, or, where you sit, lay your hand on your heart, and make the response prayer below together with me.

## Application to Six Groups of Hearers

**1. Seekers — those still looking on.** You may feel this song has nothing to do with you, but Moses says this word is "not a vain thing for you." All your life you have looked for a support that does not change: people change, markets change, the body changes. The God the Bible introduces to you calls himself the Rock. You may begin by testing him with one prayer: "God, if you truly are the Rock, let me meet you." His keeping of men as the apple of his eye includes you, who today hear his name for the first time.

**2. New believers — those who have just come in.** The homework Moses gives you is "remember," and "set your hearts unto" the words: from today, take a small notebook and write down every small thing God does for you. Memory is the granary of faith; the man who does not keep record will, three years from now, be unable to say why he believes. Singing is the other homework: learn the songs of worship well, and the melody will keep the truth in custody for you; on the days when you are in the valley and cannot read the Bible, the song will sound of itself and hold you up.

**3. Believers in the workplace — those striving in the market.** Your danger is exactly Jeshurun's danger: not the season when there is no food, but the season when the orders run smoothly and the income rises. Set yourself three boundaries: when income increases, first increase the proportion of offering and giving, lest the money fatten your heart first; when you are too busy for worship, treat that day as a red alert, not as a mark of success; every quarter, stop for half a day and remember, after Deuteronomy 32:7 — step by step, whose back has been underneath, bearing me?

**4. Parents — those who must pass the baton.** Verse 7 says, "ask thy father, and he will shew thee" — the premise is that the father is willing to tell, and able to tell. Do not leave your children only property, and not testimony. This week, find an opening at the dinner table and tell one concrete thing God has done in you, with its year, month, and day. If you cannot tell one, begin by asking your own parents. The break in the generations of faith most often breaks at a silent dinner table.

**5. The elderly — those who have crossed the wilderness.** You are the "elders" of verse 7, the living archive of this generation. Do not think your stories are out of date: the authority of the Song of Moses comes precisely from its being sung by a man who had finished walking his whole life. Please open your mouths and tell; writing it down is even better. What the young lack is not information; it is living witnesses who have seen God's faithfulness. Your remembrance is the next generation's map.

**6. Those who are suffering — those with no strength left.** Verse 36 is the most important sentence in today's whole sermon for you: God sees, God will judge his people, God repents himself for his servants. Please stop extracting justice with your own hand — vengeance is his, and that hand is steadier than yours. And please do not misread discipline as abandonment: the hiding of his face has a term; the Rock has none. There is only one thing for you to do now: before he returns to heal, do not leave the shadow of the Rock; even if you can only lie at the Rock's foot, do not walk away.

## Closing Response Prayer

O Rock, our God, we set this life of ours upon you. The days you remember, we have forgotten; the wings that bore us, we thought we were flying ourselves. Today we turn back: for those in fullness, save us, we pray, from the pride that kicks; for those in suffering, judge for us and heal us, according to your promise. May this song not slip away from our ears; may it enter the hearts of our children, and be sung until the day we see your face. Your word is not a vain word; it is our life. So we pray, in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Extract (for daily devotions, 300–500 words)

Before Moses died he left behind a song. It opens by calling heaven and earth to witness, and in its middle one "Rock" is named five times. God is called the Rock because his work is perfect and his word is faithful and without iniquity; the people, though, are called Jeshurun — "the upright one" — who, after eating and growing fat, kicked away the master who fed him. A man's most dangerous moment is often not want, but fullness; not hardship, but gradually forgetting. Today, do one small thing: take paper and pen, and after Deuteronomy 32:7 remember three things God has done for you in the past; write down the year and the details, and then tell them to one person in your family. Memory is the granary of thanksgiving; the man who does not keep record will soon be unable to say why he believes, while the man who tells them aloud has turned the granary into the next generation's map. At the end, remember verse 36: when man has no strength left, when there is none shut up and none left, God will judge his people and repent himself for his servants. He wounds, and he also heals; the hiding of his face has a term, but the Rock has no term. The hand that measured your wound will also bind it up for you with its own touch. Today, set your whole self upon this Rock; the man who sets himself on it, however great the waves, will not sink.

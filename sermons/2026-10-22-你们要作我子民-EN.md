## Summary (about 100 words)

Three full months after the Exodus, the Israelites came to Sinai. God first recalled the grace of bearing them on eagles' wings, then invited them to be His treasured possession, a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation. All the people answered together that they would obey; then they consecrated themselves and kept the boundary, preparing to meet the God who descended amid thunder, lightning, and the sound of the trumpet. The covenant comes before the law: first identity, then commandments.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

From the Red Sea to Sinai, the people had already walked for three months. In these three months they had drunk the water of Marah made sweet, eaten the manna that came down from heaven, drunk water from the rock at Rephidim, and fought that battle against the Amalekites. Grace followed grace station by station, step by step. In the previous chapter Jethro helped them put the governance of the camp in order, so that Moses no longer judged the people alone from morning until evening. Now that the organization had been put in order and the company had arrived at the place, God would do something deeper than providing food and drink and deeper than winning a battle: He would formally and publicly make a covenant with this people, and tell them who, after all, they are, and what kind of people they are to become.

This chapter has no battle, no miraculous provision, and not yet even a single article of the law promulgated. Exodus chapter 20 is the Ten Commandments; chapter 19, a whole chapter, does only one thing: the preparation for and invitation of the covenant. God first sent Moses up the mountain, and Moses brought back words of invitation; the people first heard how God had borne them, then heard what God wanted them to become; then all the people answered together, and with three days they consecrated themselves, washed their clothes, and kept the boundary, preparing to meet God's appearing. The pace of the whole chapter is surprisingly slow, and yet surprisingly weighty. People often hurry to ask, What does God want me to do, what rules am I to keep? At Sinai God first asked the reverse: Are you willing to be My people? The order must not be reversed — identity first, conduct afterward — and this is the line our whole message today must hold.

Let us read several key passages of today's text together:

"In the third month, when the children of Israel were gone forth out of the land of Egypt, the same day came they into the wilderness of Sinai. For they were departed from Rephidim, and were come to the desert of Sinai, and had pitched in the wilderness; and there Israel camped before the mount." (Exodus 19:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"Ye have seen what I did unto the Egyptians, and how I bare you on eagles' wings, and brought you unto myself. Now therefore, if ye will obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be a peculiar treasure unto me above all people: for all the earth is mine: And ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation." (Exodus 19:4-6, excerpt, KJV)

"And all the people answered together, and said, All that the LORD hath spoken we will do." (Exodus 19:8, excerpt, KJV)

"And it came to pass on the third day in the morning, that there were thunders and lightnings, and a thick cloud upon the mount, and the voice of the trumpet exceeding loud; so that all the people that was in the camp trembled." (Exodus 19:16, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, thank You that You bore us first and called us afterward; that You declared us to be Yours first, and afterward entrusted Your words to us. Through the words of Mount Sinai today, let us hear our name again: we are Your treasured possession, a kingdom of priests, a holy nation. Cleanse us, that we may both dare to draw near to You and stand before You with a reverent heart. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Arrived at Sinai — The Invitation on Eagles' Wings and the Threefold Identity (19:1-6)

First look at the journey and the time. At the very opening the text nails down the day: in the third month, when the children of Israel were gone forth out of the land of Egypt, the same day came they into the wilderness of Sinai. The wording of the text itself is worth noting — "the same day": it is not roughly after three months, but the very day the three months were full; the day of the Exodus and the day of arrival at Sinai answer each other in month and date, as if God intended the people to remember: redemption has a birthday, and the covenant has a birthday too. They departed from Rephidim, came to the wilderness of Sinai, and camped before the mount. From Rephidim to Sinai the road was not far, but the meaning was wholly different. Rephidim was a place of drinking water and fighting battles, a place grace reached again and again: when there was no water, God made the rock yield water; when there was an enemy, God gave them victory; when there was no food, God had already given manna and quail in the wilderness of Sin. Along the way, the manna taught them to trust His provision, the rock taught them to trust His presence, and the victory taught them to trust His deliverance. Now that all these memories of grace were complete, God brought them to the mountain of the covenant. In other words, Sinai was not merely the next station of the journey; Sinai was the destination to which all the preceding grace was meant to arrive: God saved them out, not merely as far as the wilderness, but all the way to before Himself. The destination of the Exodus was never a wilderness without Pharaoh; it was the mountain where they would meet God, hear God speak, and become God's people.

The people camped below the mountain, but Moses first went up alone. God called to him from the mountain, and the first sentence He opened with was not a command, but a recollection. He said: Thus shalt thou say to the house of Jacob, and tell the children of Israel: Ye have seen what I did unto the Egyptians. The first sentence of the covenant words is, Ye have seen. God did not tell the people first to recall their own performance; He told them to recall God's acts. How the ten plagues came upon Egypt, how the Red Sea was divided, how Pharaoh's army sank into the waters — all this they had seen with their own eyes. The starting point of the covenant is not in human resolve, but in what God had already done. When people make a covenant with God, they do not start from a blank page; they start from a history of grace that has already been written for three chapters. We today must hear this clearly too: when you come before God, you do not need first to prove that you are worthy; God first lays out what He has already done for you. The Exodus was their testimony; the cross is ours; they looked back to the Red Sea, we look back to Calvary. In both cases, deliverance first, covenant afterward.

Then comes the tenderest sentence in the whole chapter: and how I bare you on eagles' wings, and brought you unto myself. "Eagle," in the original nesher ( נֶשֶׁר , H5404), a large bird of prey, often used in the Bible for the swift-winged, mighty vulture-like eagle [6]. The original wording deserves a precise note: the verse reads "on eagles' wings" ('al-kanphey nesharim, "upon the wings of eagles"), with the preposition "upon" and no word for "like"; the simile form kanesher ("like an eagle") belongs to Deuteronomy 32:11, as noted in endnote [6]. Borne on eagles' wings — this image is told in more detail in Moses' song in Deuteronomy 32: the LORD found His people, He led them about, He instructed them, He kept them as the apple of His eye; as an eagle stirreth up her nest, fluttereth over her young, spreadeth abroad her wings, taketh them, beareth them on her wings (Deuteronomy 32:10-11, cited in sense). The eagle teaches the eaglet to fly by first stirring up the comfortable nest and forcing it to leave, then spreading its wings beneath it, so that when the eaglet's strength fails and it falls, it lands on the eagle's back. There are two sides to this picture: one side is stirring — God did not let the people stay forever in a comfortable nest, and every station of the wilderness stirred them; the other side is bearing — even if the eaglet falls, it falls onto wings, not onto the ground. In these three months the Israelites had grumbled, striven, and doubted, yet not one of them fell away back to Egypt. Why? Not because their wings were strong, but because God's wings were underneath, upholding them. At this point the preaching must make a distinction: that the eagle bore the people is a fact of the text; applying the details of learning to fly back onto the three months' journey is homiletical exposition made on the basis of the Deuteronomy intertext, and this manuscript identifies it here, rather than passing it off as the original words of chapter 19.

What matters even more is where this sentence lands: and brought you unto myself. God bore them not only to bring them out of Egypt; He brought them unto Himself. The Exodus has two directions: leaving Egypt is the negative side, and being brought to God is the positive side. Many people, after years of faith, stop only at the side of leaving: I no longer do this, I no longer go there. The words of Sinai remind us that the end of redemption is not leaving, but belonging; it is not that masterless people gain freedom, but that homeless people are brought home. God said, brought you unto myself — these words are the whole motive of the covenant: He wants this people to be His.

Only after the recollection comes the invitation, and the sentence pattern of the invitation is grace first, covenant afterward. The text says: Now therefore, if ye will obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be a peculiar treasure unto me above all people. This word "if" is the easiest to read wrongly. Some reverse the order, supposing that the people had first to keep the covenant before God would be willing to show grace, so that the Sinai covenant becomes a price paid in obedience in exchange for grace. But the order of the text is right there: first, Ye have seen what I did unto the Egyptians; first, I bare you on eagles' wings, and brought you unto myself; and only then, Now therefore, if ye will obey my voice indeed. Deliverance comes before the conditional sentence, and grace before the response. A covenant, in the Bible, was never a commercial contract between strangers; it is the responsive covenant of those who have received grace toward the One who gave the grace: He has already acted, He has already borne, He has already brought — now it is the people's turn to answer whether they are willing to obey and willing to keep. "Obey indeed" carries in the original the sense of listening and listening again, of heeding attentively — not in one ear and out the other, but taking God's word in and doing it. The word "covenant," in the original berit ( בְּרִית , H1285), a covenant, a pact, is one of the central words of biblical theology [4]. From the covenant with Noah and the covenant with Abraham to the covenant at Sinai, again and again God took the initiative to make a covenant, fixing His relationship with people by a covenant, so that grace is not only a momentary feeling, but a lasting relationship with a name, with promises, and with responsibilities.

The content of the invitation is a threefold identity, each layer higher than the last. The first layer: be a peculiar treasure unto Me above all people. "Peculiar treasure," in the original segullah ( סְגֻלָּה , H5459), a special possession, a treasured people — that portion which a king, out of all his goods, keeps by itself and especially cherishes [1]. The people were not picked up casually by God; they were treasured by God. And the text immediately adds, for all the earth is mine. This sentence suddenly opens up a universal horizon: it is not that God could be God only if Israel alone were His people; all peoples and all the earth originally belong to Him; Israel was chosen not because God is narrow and can see only this one people — precisely because all the earth is His, He chose one people out of all peoples to bear a mission toward all peoples. So the second identity follows at once: ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests. "A kingdom of priests," in the original mamlekhet kohanim ( מַמְלֶכֶת כֹּהֲנִים , H4467 plus H3548), a royal kingdom composed of priests [2]. What does a priest do? A priest is one who stands between God and people: on one side bringing people before God, on the other side bringing God's blessing and God's words to people. For Israel to be a kingdom of priests means that the election of the whole people was not for monopolizing grace, but for serving as mediator, bridge, and witness among all peoples: that all peoples might come to know the LORD through them. Election was never a certificate for monopoly; it is a commission to serve all peoples. The third layer: a holy nation. "A holy nation," in the original goy qadosh ( גּוֹי קָדוֹשׁ , H1471 plus H6918), a nation set apart as holy, belonging to God as holy [3]. The first meaning of holiness is not moral flawlessness; it is being set apart, reserved for God's use alone: a vessel set apart as holy is one no longer used for common purposes, but for God alone; a nation set apart as holy is one that no longer lives after the manner of all peoples, but according to God's word alone. Read the threefold identity together: the treasured possession speaks of belonging, the kingdom of priests speaks of mission, and the holy nation speaks of form. Before a single commandment was promulgated, God made these three things plain: you belong to Me, you are for all peoples, you are to be like Me. Identity first — this is Sinai's first lesson.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: All the People Answered — The Preparation of Consecration and Boundary (19:7-15)

Moses came down the mountain and laid God's words, one by one, before the people. The text says Moses came and called for the elders of the people, and laid before their faces all these words which the LORD commanded him. Notice the manner of the covenant-making: God did not bypass the people and impose the covenant directly upon their heads; He laid out the invitation, made the identity plain, and then waited for the people to answer. A covenant must have two sides: God had spoken His half first, and now it was the people's turn to speak theirs. The people's answer was crisp and direct: And all the people answered together, and said, All that the LORD hath spoken we will do. "Together" is a voice united as one; "we will do" is a promise without reservation. Several million people speaking one sentence together — this is an extremely solemn moment in Israel's history: people scattered among tents and tribes became, in this one sentence, a covenant community. Moses went up again and returned the words of the people unto the LORD, like a mediator going back and forth between God and people, carrying the words of both sides to the other.

Here we must look forward for a moment, and honestly identify it as a forward look. In Exodus chapter 24, after the Ten Commandments and the Book of the Covenant were promulgated, Moses built an altar and offered sacrifices, sprinkled the blood of the covenant on the altar and also on the people, and there the people again answered together, All that the LORD hath said will we do. That was the formal completion of the covenant ceremony. The answer in verse 8 of this chapter was the first promise after hearing the invitation; the answer in chapter 24 was the renewed confirmation after hearing the law, with the blood of the covenant sprinkled in between. The two answers, one before and one after, tell us one thing: making a covenant is not finished when a moved slogan has been spoken once; the promise must be spoken again after God's words have been heard clearly, and sealed in the sign of blood. God is not afraid of the people's deliberateness; what God wants is precisely a deliberate promise: first hear the invitation, then hear the commandments, and let every step count, and let every "we will do" not be spoken casually. Of course, as later history also shows, the people soon made a golden calf below the mountain, and they did not keep this promise. Yet God still records their answer today, because the solemnity of the covenant is not discounted by people's later failure; people may break faith, but God treats this promise according to His own word, and His faithfulness is the root by which the covenant can finally endure. This point is homiletical exposition gained from looking back over the whole scroll, and this manuscript identifies it as such, rather than mixing it with the facts of that moment.

After the answer, God spoke to Moses again, and this time He spoke of the arrangement for the appearing. God said, Lo, I come unto thee in a thick cloud, that the people may hear when I speak with thee, and believe thee for ever. God's appearing had an explicit purpose: that the people would hear with their own ears God speaking with Moses, and from then on believe for ever that Moses was the mediator God had established. A covenant community needs an established mediator, and God would not let Moses' authority rest only on seniority and experience; He would personally attest him before all the people. Immediately after, God told Moses: Go unto the people, and sanctify them to day and to morrow, and let them wash their clothes; and be ready against the third day: for the third day the LORD will come down in the sight of all the people upon mount Sinai. To sanctify themselves is to set themselves apart as holy and cleanse themselves, answering from afar the "holy nation" spoken of before: the identity is declared by God, and consecration is the preparation the people live out. The small matter of washing clothes is deliberately recorded in the text, because outward cleanness here is a sign of inward preparation: if a person is to meet God, even his garments must be washed — how much more his heart. When we today prepare for worship and prepare to receive God's word, we should have this same orderliness, and not come before God unkempt and careless. Consecration is not a performance for God to watch; it is a person's own acknowledgment first: the One I am to meet is holy, and I cannot meet Him carrying filth and contempt.

The other side of preparation is the boundary. God told Moses: Thou shalt set bounds unto the people round about. "To set bounds," the verb in the original is higbil ( הִגְבִּל , H1379), to set a boundary, to fix a limit [5]. God said, Take heed to yourselves, that ye go not up into the mount, or touch the border of it: whosoever toucheth the mount shall be surely put to death: there shall not an hand touch it, but he shall surely be stoned, or shot through; whether it be beast or man, it shall not live. This boundary is startlingly severe: the God who had just declared them His treasured possession and a kingdom of priests now, in a moment, drew at the foot of the mountain a line that no one could cross. This is not God being changeable; this is exactly the other side of holiness. Holiness is not an abstract concept and not a pleasant-sounding adjective; holiness is preparation, holiness is boundary — it is a sacred order in which God can be approached and yet has a place that must not be transgressed. A kingdom of priests is called to draw near to God and serve, but the drawing near must be in the manner God has appointed, not according to human enthusiasm and curiosity. Human enthusiasm rushes in; human curiosity reaches out to touch; God's boundary is precisely what protects people: to come near the holy fire not according to the rule is to be burned by the fire. The boundary is not God pushing people away; the boundary is God teaching people how to draw near to Him and live.

In the instructions there is one more article, which we must read honestly and preach honestly. Moses went down and sanctified the people, and also said to them, be ready against the third day: come not at your wives. This was a special arrangement for the day of the appearing, a temporary abstinence set for concentrating wholly on preparing to meet God, appearing together with the consecration rites of that time; it is not a daily ethical norm, and still less does it mean that marriage and intimacy are themselves unclean. Scripture elsewhere plainly holds marriage as honourable and established by God, and the relation of husband and wife is not defilement. This article is recorded here to show how solemn those three days of preparation were: even ordinarily proper things were temporarily laid aside, in single-hearted waiting for God's descent. This manuscript makes this note according to the context of the text and the consistent teaching of the whole Bible, lest hearers mistake a temporary rite for a permanent statute. To read Scripture honestly, a preacher must dare to draw such distinctions clearly: what was for that day, and what is for every day — only when the boundary is clear will the application not go wrong.

Three days — two whole days of preparation, plus waiting on the third day. In those three days the Israelites below the mountain washed clothes, consecrated themselves, kept the boundary, and abstained; the camp must have been unusually quiet. To be quiet before making a covenant, and to be cleansed before hearing a command — this too is a lesson left for us today: we are too accustomed to rushing straight before God carrying all our busyness and dust and demanding that He speak at once; Sinai teaches us that some meetings take three days to prepare for.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Appearing and the Repeated Warning — Nearness and Boundary Standing Together (19:16-25)

The third day came. With a string of words that open every sense, the text writes God's descent: And it came to pass on the third day in the morning, that there were thunders and lightnings, and a thick cloud upon the mount, and the voice of the trumpet exceeding loud; so that all the people that was in the camp trembled. Thunder is heard, lightning is seen, the thick cloud both conceals and is a presence, and the trumpet was so loud that the whole camp trembled — even the body could feel it. God's appearing was not a concept lighting up briefly in a person's heart; the whole mountain, the whole wilderness, and the whole person's senses were filled together with His glory. Moses brought forth the people out of the camp to meet with God; and they stood at the nether part of the mount. And mount Sinai was altogether on a smoke, because the LORD descended upon it in fire: and the smoke thereof ascended as the smoke of a furnace, and the whole mount quaked greatly. The mountain smoking like a furnace, the whole mountain quaking — this is not exaggerated rhetoric for a natural phenomenon; this is the sign that the God who made heaven and earth personally set foot on the mountain He had made: how could created things not quake before their Creator?

The voice of the trumpet sounded long and waxed louder and louder; Moses spake, and God answered him by a voice. This is a deeply moving scene in the chapter: between the thunder and the trumpet, Moses opened his mouth, and God answered him by a voice. Earlier God had said He would let the people hear Him speak with Moses, so that they would believe him for ever; now it was fulfilled. The people could not hear the whole content of the conversation, but they could hear that a conversation was taking place: their leader was not self-appointed; he was a man who spoke with God amid the thunder. And the LORD came down upon mount Sinai, on the top of the mount: and the LORD called Moses up to the top of the mount; and Moses went up. Notice this movement: God came down, and man went up; God came down first, and only then could man go up. The way of drawing near to God is always that God first takes the initiative to come near, and man then responds by going up. Without God's descent, Moses going up the mountain would only be climbing a mountain; because God came down first, going up became a meeting.

Yet at the very peak of the meeting, the first thing God said was once again a warning. The LORD said unto Moses, Go down, charge the people, lest they break through unto the LORD to gaze, and many of them perish. And let the priests also, which come near to the LORD, sanctify themselves, lest the LORD break forth upon them. Moses, who had just gone up the mountain, was immediately sent down again to deliver a warning. God seemed in no hurry for the ceremony of the covenant; He was first in a hurry to protect the people's lives. Here lies the deepest tension of the whole chapter: on one side is the nearness of "brought you unto myself," and on the other side is the boundary of "lest they break through" — both standing at the same time, and neither cancelling the other. God wants people to draw near to Him, near enough to be His people and His priests; and God does not permit people to draw near in their own way — breaking through, touching, gazing in curiosity are all roads of death. Reverence and nearness, at Sinai, are not two moods that appear in turns; they are two postures that must be held at the same moment. Worship today is the same: to speak only of nearness and not of reverence, worship will become presumption; to speak only of boundary and not of nearness, faith will become fear. Sinai gives us both together: God is in the thick cloud, near enough that His voice can be heard; and God is behind the boundary, holy enough that even the priests must consecrate themselves.

At this point Moses made a plea. He said unto the LORD, The people cannot come up to mount Sinai: for thou chargedst us, saying, Set bounds about the mount, and sanctify it. Moses meant that the boundary had already been set and the word already delivered, and there was no need for repeated urging. Moses had no ill intent; he was even quoting God's own instruction. But God insisted: Away, get thee down, and thou shalt come up, thou, and Aaron with thee: but let not the priests and the people break through to come up unto the LORD. God's insistence tells us that He knows people better than Moses did. That people have heard a boundary does not mean people will keep a boundary; curiosity will push people forward, zeal will push people forward, and even those who serve — the priests — can be pushed across the line by an impulse in the name of nearness, and so the warning must be spoken again and again, and the boundary guarded repeatedly. "Again and again" here is not verbosity; it is care: God would rather speak once more than have one more person die. Parents and pastors should understand this heart: an important boundary is not finished by being spoken once; when children and the flock come to the edge of the line, it must be spoken again. So Moses went down unto the people, and spake unto them. This plain final sentence of the chapter shows Moses' faithfulness: he did not find God verbose, nor did he vouch for the people; he delivered the warning once more, exactly as it was. The faithfulness of a mediator lies not in saving God words, but in carrying God's words to the people without missing one.

Finally, look at one detail: Aaron was permitted to come up the mountain. God said, thou shalt come up, thou, and Aaron with thee. The people could not come up; the priests could draw near only after consecrating themselves; Moses and Aaron alone were called up together by name. Aaron was the foreshadowing of the later high-priestly office; his being named and brought up the mountain in this chapter shows that drawing near to God has order and office: not everyone draws near in the same manner, yet everyone has a share within this order. A kingdom of priests does not mean abolishing priests; the mission of the whole people as priests is to be realized step by step through offices set apart as holy.

To gather the theological positioning of the whole chapter, there are three main points. First, the covenant comes before the law. Chapter 19 is the frame of the covenant, and chapter 20 is the content of the Ten Commandments; the order itself is a message: God first used a whole chapter to declare identity, recall grace, and establish relationship, and only then used a chapter to give the commandments. If the order were reversed, what the Israelites heard would be only a code of law; because the order is as it is, what they heard was: first become My people, then live according to My words. Christians, reading the law and all commandments, should hold this order: commandments are not a threshold for exchanging for an identity; commandments are the manner of life of those who have already received grace and an identity. Second, election carries a universal mission. The sentence "for all the earth is mine" makes the Sinai covenant open toward all peoples from the very beginning: if a kingdom of priests cared only for its own blessing, it would betray its own name. The church today receives the same identity and also receives the same direction: grace reaching us is not a terminal station, but a transfer station, to flow through us to all peoples. Third, the New Testament echoes must be honestly identified as a way of reading. The apostle Peter said to the church: But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people (1 Peter 2:9, excerpt) — this is a direct quotation of the words of Exodus 19:6, except that Peter restated "kingdom of priests" in New Testament usage as "royal priesthood," and slightly rearranged the order. This is the New Testament's application to the church — the apostle re-reading Sinai in Christ: the identity once spoken to Israel now finds its fulfilling echo in the church. And Hebrews chapter 12 sets the fearful Mount Sinai against Mount Zion in contrast: the former mountain had burning fire, blackness, darkness, tempest, and the sound of a trumpet, and people feared so greatly that they begged that the word should not be spoken to them anymore; what we have now come to is the heavenly Jerusalem, and to Jesus the mediator of the new covenant (Hebrews 12:18-24, cited in sense). This is New Testament reflection, not the teaching on the spot in chapter 19, and this manuscript separates them here: the people of Sinai stood trembling at the foot of the mountain, but the children of the church, through a better Mediator, may come boldly to Mount Zion. Reading the two mountains in contrast, Sinai's reverence has not been cancelled, but has been led toward thanksgiving: Wherefore we receiving a kingdom which cannot be moved, let us have grace, whereby we may serve God acceptably with reverence and godly fear (Hebrews 12:28, cited in sense).

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today, standing below Mount Sinai, we have heard three things. The first: God first recalled — I bare you on eagles' wings, and brought you unto myself. Grace first, covenant afterward; our faith always begins with God's acts, not with our performance. The second: God declared an identity — ye shall be a peculiar treasure unto me, a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation. First know who you are, then learn how to live; this is the order Sinai left to believers of every generation. The third: God set a boundary — consecration, keeping the bound, warning again and again, with even the priests to be set apart as holy. Nearness and reverence stand together; we are called before Him, and we should also stand before Him with a holy heart. Tomorrow the Ten Commandments will be promulgated; but without this chapter today, tomorrow's commandments would have no root. Today, first take the identity home: you are His treasured possession, you are called to be a priest for all peoples, and you have been set apart to be like Him.

Let us pray together: Lord, God who bore us, we thank You. In Egypt You did great things to save us; on the way You bore us on eagles' wings; and before the mountain You did not despise our weakness, but gave us so honoured an identity: to be Your treasured possession, a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation. Forgive our hearts that read the covenant as a transaction and leave grace behind us; teach us first to receive the identity, and then to obey Your words. Cleanse us, give us reverence, that we may draw near to You without contempt and obey You without fear, standing before You all our lives as Your witness for all peoples. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[1] segullah: Hebrew סְגֻלָּה (H5459), a special possession, a treasured people, a king's privately kept treasure; Exodus 19:5, "if ye will obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be a peculiar treasure unto me above all people" — "peculiar treasure" renders this word, emphasizing being specially treasured and belonging to God among all peoples; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Deuteronomy 7:6, 14:2, 26:18, Psalm 135:4, and Malachi 3:17, where God repeatedly calls Israel His segullah, as the basis for this manuscript's rendering of "treasured."

[2] mamlekhet kohanim: Hebrew מַמְלֶכֶת כֹּהֲנִים (H4467 mamlekhet, kingdom, kingship, plus H3548 kohanim, the plural of priests), Exodus 19:6, "ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests," literally a kingdom composed of priests; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See 1 Peter 2:9, the New Testament application, and Revelation 1:6 and 5:10, "made us kings / a kingdom and priests," as intertextual basis for this manuscript; the New Testament usage has been identified in the text as the apostles' way of reading.

[3] goy qadosh: Hebrew גּוֹי קָדוֹשׁ (H1471 goy, nation, people, plus H6918 qadosh, holy, set apart as holy), Exodus 19:6, "and an holy nation"; the core sense of qadosh is to be separated out and to belong to God; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Leviticus 11:44-45, "be holy, for I am holy," Leviticus 19:2, and 1 Peter 1:16 quoting them, as the basis for this manuscript's "holiness is being set apart."

[4] berit: Hebrew בְּרִית (H1285), covenant, pact; Exodus 19:5, "keep my covenant"; this word runs through the Old Testament, appearing in the covenant with Noah (Genesis 6:18), the covenant with Abraham (Genesis chapters 15 and 17), and the covenant at Sinai; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "a covenant is not a commercial contract, but the responsive covenant of those who have received grace" is homiletical exposition made on the basis of this chapter's order of grace first and covenant afterward, and is identified separately from the lexical facts.

[5] higbil: Hebrew הִגְבִּל (H1379), to set bounds, to fix a boundary, the causative form of the verb gabal; Exodus 19:12, "thou shalt set bounds unto the people round about," and Exodus 19:23, where Moses restates the same instruction; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Deuteronomy 19:14, the use for a landmark, as the original-text basis for this manuscript's "holiness is realized as a boundary."

[6] nesher: Hebrew נֶשֶׁר (H5404, eagle, a large vulture-like bird of prey). Exodus 19:4 reads עַל־כַּנְפֵי נְשָׁרִים ('al-kanphey nesharim, "on eagles' wings"): the preposition עַל ("upon") plus "wings" (construct) plus nesharim (plural of nesher), with no כְּ (ke-, "like"). The form כְּנֶשֶׁר (kanesher, "like an eagle") occurs in Deuteronomy 32:11, Job 9:26, Psalm 103:5 and elsewhere, not in Exodus 19:4. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Deuteronomy 32:11, the unfolding of the same image, and Isaiah 40:31, "they shall mount up with wings as eagles," as the basis for this manuscript's eagle intertexts; the development of the details of the eaglet learning to fly is homiletical exposition, as identified in the text.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Ye have seen what I did unto the Egyptians, and how I bare you on eagles' wings, and brought you unto myself. Now therefore, if ye will obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be a peculiar treasure unto me above all people ... And ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation." (Exodus 19:4-6, excerpt, KJV)

Before you rush on to the Ten Commandments, stop for a while in chapter 19. Before God gave His commands, He gave an identity: He bore you first, and called you afterward. He said all the earth is His, yet He treasured you out of all peoples, and then sent you to be a priest for the nations. If today you feel unworthy or not good enough, first count how He has carried you: every time you fell, you did not land on the ground; you landed on His wings. Receive the identity first, and obedience will have a root. Then learn from the people below the mountain: prepare in quietness, be cleansed, and keep the boundary God has set, rather than rushing in by mere enthusiasm. You are His treasured possession — not only a comfort, but a commission: live set apart, so that through you, those around you may come to know the God who carries His people. Today, take ten minutes in quietness before Him: name, one by one, three ways He has carried you, give Him thanks for them, and then, carrying the steadiness of being treasured, go among people and hold up one person who is falling.

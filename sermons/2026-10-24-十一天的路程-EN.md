# Eleven Days' Journey — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 1

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: First translation — pending proofreading (初译待校), unpublished (translated in full, paragraph by paragraph, from the finalized Chinese manuscript "Eleven Days' Journey" on Deuteronomy 1, which has passed strong re-verification in Chinese; this English version has not yet had native-speaker proofreading)
Text: Deuteronomy chapter 1 (46 verses in full)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), taken from the anchor text of this chapter held for this batch (KJV-申1.txt, 46 lines, fetched in full from the BibleGateway KJV whole-chapter page) and checked against it verse by verse; no quotation is back-translated from the Chinese. The companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (CUNPSS) as its base text, checked against the whole-chapter text, with 1:2, 1:17, and 1:38 further verified directly against the verse-by-verse comparison pages at wd.bible; its English devotional extract uses the KJV. Points where the letter of the two base texts differs are registered separately and neither is made to conform to the other: (i) at 1:1 the base text of the Chinese manuscript reads the place name "Suph" (the five place names being Paran, Tophel, Laban, Hazeroth, and Dizahab), whereas the KJV at 1:1 reads the word Sûp as "the Red sea" — the two readings are registered separately (see the discussion in Endnote 12); (ii) at 1:4 the base texts record the two kings' dwellings and the order of the victories as the chapter gives them — "Sihon the king of the Amorites, which dwelt in Heshbon" and "Og the king of Bashan, which dwelt at Astaroth in Edrei"; (iii) at 1:15 the order of the grades in the Chinese base text puts the heads first — heads, captains over thousands, captains over hundreds, captains over fifties, captains over tens — which differs from the order registered in Exodus 18, and the two are registered separately without being conformed to each other (on the KJV letter at 1:15, see the count register in Endnote 5); (iv) at 1:17 the Chinese base text reads "I will judge it," not "I will hear it and decide," while the Revised Chinese Union Version of 2010 reads "let me hear the case" — the KJV reads "I will hear it," and each is registered as it stands; (v) at 1:21 the Chinese base text reads "be discouraged" (驚惶), not "be dismayed"; the KJV reads "fear not, neither be discouraged"; (vi) at 1:31 the Chinese base text renders the verb as "bore/reared" (撫養, "to carry as a father rears a son"); the underlying word nāśāʾ (H5375) means at root to lift up, to bear; the KJV reads "bare" — the three forms are registered separately and none replaces another; (vii) at 1:38 the Chinese base text reads "Joshua the son of Nun, which standeth before thee" — "Nun" (nûn) is the name of Joshua's father, in the same form as Numbers 13:16 and 27:18; the KJV reads the same, "Joshua the son of Nun, which standeth before thee"; (viii) at 1:44 the Chinese base text reads "as bees swarm" and "even unto Hormah," and at 1:45 "wept aloud" (哭號), not "wept"; the KJV at 1:44 reads "as bees do" and at 1:45 "wept" — each registered as it stands.
Title verification: Four candidates were considered — (i) "Eleven Days' Journey," taken from the parenthetical note that fixes the text at 1:2: "There are eleven days' journey from Horeb by the way of mount Seir unto Kadeshbarnea"; (ii) "The Land Set Before You," taken from 1:8, which covers the command but not its outcome; (iii) "As a Man Doth Bear His Son," taken from 1:31, which covers the warmest sentence in the chapter but not the sentence of judgment or the defeat; (iv) "Not One of These Men of This Evil Generation," taken from 1:35, which covers the judgment but not the exceptions or the mercy. Candidate (i) is adopted: all forty-six verses of the chapter — the command at Horeb (vv. 6–18), the refusal to enter at Kadesh (vv. 19–33), the sentence and its exceptions (vv. 34–40), and the defeat of those who went up presumptuously (vv. 41–46) — answer one and the same question: why did a journey that could be walked in eleven days take forty years to walk? The parenthesis of verse 2 is set down before all the narrative, laying the answer out first; the forty-four verses that follow unfold, layer by layer, the distance between those eleven days and those forty years. The English title is fixed as: Eleven Days' Journey.
Form: The sermon is divided into five parts, each headed with a time code (a preaching structure of 60 minutes in all). Within each part, paragraphs are marked [Fact] / [Argument] / [Summary] / [New Testament Reading] / [Scholarly and Historical Discussion] to keep the layers distinct: the Fact layer records only what can be checked in this chapter's base text, together with its arithmetic; the Argument layer makes theological inferences and marks them as inferences; the Summary layer gives one governing sentence for the part; the New Testament Reading layer registers only the New Testament text's echoes of unbelief and judgment, without reading them back as the Old Testament's original meaning; the Discussion layer registers the divergent readings of scholarship and tradition and the textual differences from the parallel accounts in Numbers and Exodus — divergences are set out side by side, and adjudication is left aside. The Hebrew word-forms that have been checked are: dābār (word, H1697, 1:1), ḥōrēb (Horeb, H2722, 1:2), šāp̄aṭ (to judge, H8199, 1:16), mišpāṭ (judgment, H4941, 1:17, checked directly against the BHS line on the verse-by-verse page at wd.bible), nāśāʾ (to lift up, to bear, H5375, 1:9, 12, 31), heʾĕmîn (to believe, H539, 1:32), ʿănāqîm (the Anakims, H6062, 1:28, registered for this verse from the BDB entry), kālēb (Caleb, H3612), yəp̄unneh (Jephunneh, H3312), yəhôšûaʿ (Joshua, H3091), nûn (Nun, H5126), midbār (wilderness, H4057), dereḵ (way, H1870) — thirteen words, their numbers verified against the verse pages at BibleHub and the Strong's dictionary pages. Five words have not each been checked directly against an original-language page and are marked pending verification, without a number being forced in: ṭōraḥ (cumbrance, proposed H2960, 1:12), maśśāʾ (burden, proposed H4853, 1:12), rîḇ (strife, proposed H7379, 1:12), gēr (stranger/sojourner, proposed H1616, 1:16), and barnēaʿ (Barnea, number not fixed). The numerical accounts (verse counts, occurrence counts, numbers of place names, numbers of grades, numbers of exceptions) have all been recounted by hand and are set out in the endnotes. This manuscript quotes no preacher's reported words that cannot be traced back to a source. Wherever the parallel accounts of this chapter — Numbers 13–14, Exodus 18, and Numbers 20 — differ, the difference is only registered and the readings set out side by side; they are not harmonized and nothing is read back.

## Main Text

Introduction (00:00–05:00). [Fact] The first chapter of Deuteronomy is the first address Moses delivered to the second generation of Israel east of the Jordan, in the land of Moab (1:1, 5; registered from the surrounding context: the chapters that follow in this book are the continuation of this address). The chapter has forty-six verses and not one new event in it; all of it is old history: how God commanded the departure at Horeb (vv. 6–18), how the people would not go in at Kadesh (vv. 19–33), how the sentence fell (vv. 34–40), and how the people then went up the mountain on their own initiative and were sent away defeated (vv. 41–46). [Fact] The timestamp is nailed down at 1:3: "And it came to pass in the fortieth year, in the eleventh month, on the first day of the month, that Moses spake unto the children of Israel, according unto all that the LORD had given him in commandment unto them;" — the fortieth year after the exodus: the exodus was the first year, and now it is the fortieth. A little over two months after this address was finished, the new generation would cross the Jordan (Joshua 4:19 registers the crossing on the tenth day of the first month; registered as a cross-reference report). The people standing at the doorway must first hear out the history of everything before the doorway. [Argument] Moses' farewell address does not open with a new command but with old history, and this is an order of teaching: if the next generation does not know where the previous generation fell, it will walk its fathers' wilderness over again as though it were a new road of its own. The method of the whole book of Deuteronomy is to tell the history through first, and only then to expound the law clearly — this chapter is the first lesson in that method. [Summary] The governing sentence of the chapter can be put this way: from Horeb to Kadesh, eleven days; from Kadesh to the east of the Jordan, forty years. The road was never far; what was far was whether men were willing to believe. What this chapter examines, part by part, is exactly what lay between those eleven days and those forty years. [Fact] The speaking structure of the chapter is registered first, so that it is clear: a narrative preface of five verses (vv. 1–5), after which all forty-one verses (vv. 6–46) are Moses' address in the first person, within which are nested, layer upon layer, the words of God forty years earlier, the words of the people, and Moses' own words — when reading, the layers must be kept distinct, for the assignment of responsibility in the whole chapter follows the layers: God's words at Horeb (vv. 6–8), Moses' words at the time (vv. 9–18, 20–21, 29–33), the people's words at the time (vv. 14, 22, 27–28, 41), God's later sentence (vv. 34–40) and warning (v. 42). If the layers are confused, the chapter's assignment of blame will be confused with them. [Fact] The book's name is also registered first: the Hebrew name is taken from the opening words of the book, "These be the words which…" — that is, "the words" of 1:1 (dəbārîm, H1697). The Chinese name "申命記" (Shēnmìngjì, "the record of the repeated command") and the English name Deuteronomy ("the second law") share a common origin in the Septuagint's summary of this book's character as a repetition of the law (connected with the ancient rendering of the phrase "a copy of this law" at 17:18; registered as a cross-reference report). The name itself is the book's programme: this book is not a new law; it is the law already given, spoken again to a new generation — and this chapter's retelling of the history is precisely the first step in retelling the law.

### Part One: Moses Opens His Mouth — Six Place Names, One Date, Two Defeated Kings (1:1–5; 05:00–14:00)

[Fact] Verse 1 first reports the place of the address. In the base text: "These be the words which Moses spake unto all Israel on this side Jordan in the wilderness, in the plain over against the Red sea, between Paran, and Tophel, and Laban, and Hazeroth, and Dizahab." (On the KJV's reading of the first name as "the Red sea," see the register below and Endnote 12.) The place names of the Chinese base text, counted by hand, are these: taking "Suph" as the reference point, there are five further places beneath it — Paran, Tophel, Laban, Hazeroth, and Dizahab — six place names in all including the reference point. Of these six, Paran and Hazeroth are registered in the itinerary records of Numbers (Numbers 12:16: the people removed from Hazeroth and pitched in the wilderness of Paran; registered as a cross-reference report); the sites of the remaining places are not fixed, and the proposals are left to the Discussion layer. [Fact] Next comes the key to the whole chapter, given at verse 2 in the form of a parenthesis in the base text: "(There are eleven days' journey from Horeb by the way of mount Seir unto Kadeshbarnea.)" Note three things: first, this is a parenthesis — the narrator sets the main subject aside and first inserts a sentence about the journey; second, the two ends of the journey are Horeb and Kadeshbarnea, one end the place of the covenant, the other the southern gate of the promised land; third, the number is only eleven days. This is the chapter's only parenthesis, yet it compresses the tragedy of the whole book this cleanly: the road that had to be walked was only eleven days, and they took forty years to walk it. [Fact] Verse 3 gives the date and the character of the address: "And it came to pass in the fortieth year, in the eleventh month, on the first day of the month, that Moses spake unto the children of Israel, according unto all that the LORD had given him in commandment unto them;" The address is not Moses' personal farewell reflections; it is "all that the LORD had given him in commandment" — the source of the content is registered first: Moses is the man who delivers the message. [Fact] Verse 4 registers the historical moment of the address: "After he had slain Sihon the king of the Amorites, which dwelt in Heshbon, and Og the king of Bashan, which dwelt at Astaroth in Edrei:" — two kings, two victories, both already past (for the course of them see Numbers 21, registered from the surrounding context; chapters 2–3 of this book will have Moses recount them himself). [Fact] Verse 5 closes the preface: "On this side Jordan, in the land of Moab, began Moses to declare this law, saying," — "to declare" is to expound, to make plain (the sense is registered; no Strong's number is assigned, pending verification): the book's own positioning of itself is not the promulgation of a new law, but the clear exposition of the law already given, to a new generation. [Argument] The order of the preface is worth learning: the first two things Moses speaks of when he opens his mouth are the place and the victories, not the failure. First let the second generation know where they are standing — east of the Jordan, at the doorway of the promised land; then let them know what God has just won for them — the two kings east of the river have already fallen. Only then does he turn back to tell of the failure of forty years before. A teacher of history who opens his mouth with nothing but failure teaches his hearers only to despise themselves; Moses sets God's victory before the failure, and only then can the failure be told, and only then do the hearers know that the failure was not God's inability — it was man's unbelief. [New Testament Reading] The echoes of this chapter's historical retelling in the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only: Hebrews 3–4 takes the unbelief of this generation in the wilderness as a warning, exhorting its readers not to harden their hearts "while it is called To day" (registered as a report); 1 Corinthians 10 calls these things "examples" — warnings (registered as a report). How the New Testament uses this history is registered at this layer only and is not read back as the original meaning of this chapter of Deuteronomy. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion] On the location of the place names in verse 1, the Discussion layer registers two points side by side, without adjudication: first, for the sites of Suph, Tophel, Dizahab, and several other places in the verse, scholarship to date has no settled conclusion — some infer their approximate positions from the itinerary records of the wilderness of Paran and Hazeroth, and some hold that several of the names can no longer be identified; this manuscript registers only "not fixed." Second, the KJV rendering at 1:1 reads "Suph" as "the Red sea," an ancient translation tradition that connects the word with the name of the Red Sea (yam-sûp); the New Punctuation Chinese Union Version and most modern versions alike make it a place name, "Suph." The two forms are registered separately; the reader may simply read with the base text in hand. [Summary] The governing sentence of this part: the five verses of the preface nail down four things — the place is east of the Jordan, the journey is only eleven days, the date is in the fortieth year, and the two kings have already been defeated. In the four parts that follow, Moses will explain with his own mouth: the gate was plainly this near — why did men stand at it for forty years?

### Part Two: Horeb — You Have Dwelt Long Enough; Now Set Out (1:6–18; 14:00–27:00)

[Fact] The address begins at Horeb. Verse 6 quotes in the base text the words God spoke at that time: "The LORD our God spake unto us in Horeb, saying, Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount:" Note what this command reproves: not the people's sin, but the people's staying — "Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount." The mountain is a good mountain, the place of the covenant and the place where the law was given, but the mountain is not the destination; dwell too long in the memorial place of grace, and the memorial place becomes a place of stagnation. [Fact] Verse 7 gives the direction of the departure and the map, in the base text: "Turn you, and take your journey, and go to the mount of the Amorites, and unto all the places nigh thereunto, in the plain, in the hills, and in the vale, and in the south, and by the sea side, to the land of the Canaanites, and unto Lebanon, unto the great river, the river Euphrates." The regions of the Chinese base text, counted by hand, are seven items in all: the Arabah, the hill country, the lowland, the south, the land of the Canaanites along the sea coast, Lebanon, and the great river Euphrates — from the Jordan valley to the river Euphrates, this is the largest outline of the territory God promised, far beyond anything the people had then seen. (On the KJV letter, the verse strings together eight phrases; see the register in Endnote 5.) [Fact] Verse 8 is where the command lands, quoted in full in the base text: "Behold, I have set the land before you: go in and possess the land which the LORD sware unto your fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, to give unto them and to their seed after them." Not one of the three patriarchs' names is missing: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The promise is not a newly invented plan; it is the old covenant sworn to three generations of patriarchs. "I have set the land before you" occurs twice in the chapter; this is its first occurrence (the second is at verse 21). [Fact] Moses then speaks of the division of the burden he carried. Verse 9: "And I spake unto you at that time, saying, I am not able to bear you myself alone:" Verse 12 says it again: "How can I myself alone bear your cumbrance, and your burden, and your strife?" — In the Chinese base text the word "burden" (重任) occurs twice (vv. 9, 12), and "cumbrance" and "strife" once each (v. 12). Verse 10 registers the reason why he could not bear it alone: not that the people were too bad, but that the people were too many — "The LORD your God hath multiplied you, and, behold, ye are this day as the stars of heaven for multitude." That the people were many was itself the fulfilment of the promise (the covenant of the stars in Genesis 15:5, registered from the surrounding context); yet the fulfilment brought with it the weight of governing. [Fact] Verse 11 is the blessing Moses spoke before his complaint, quoted in full in the base text: "(The LORD God of your fathers make you a thousand times so many more as ye are, and bless you, as he hath promised you!)" The order is registered clearly: Moses first says "make you a thousand times so many more," and only then says "I am not able to bear you myself alone" — he was not weary of the people; he blessed first, and divided the labour afterwards. [Fact] Verse 13 gives the standard for the choosing, in the base text: "Take you wise men, and understanding, and known among your tribes, and I will make them rulers over you." The standard has three items: wise, understanding, and known among the tribes — the first two are ability, the last is the people's recognition. The choosing was done tribe by tribe; the appointment was completed by Moses; the procedure has two stages, clearly distinct. The people's answer in verse 14 is a single sentence: "And ye answered me, and said, The thing which thou hast spoken is good for us to do." [Fact] Verse 15 is the account of the appointment, quoted in full in the base text: "So I took the chief of your tribes, wise men, and known, and made them heads over you, captains over thousands, and captains over hundreds, and captains over fifties, and captains over tens, and officers among your tribes." The grades in the Chinese base text, counted by hand, are five classes in all: heads, captains over thousands, captains over hundreds, captains over fifties, and captains over tens; the base text puts "heads" first (its order differing from that registered in Exodus 18; see the separate register in the header). [Fact] Verses 16–17 are Moses' charge to the judges; verse 17 is quoted in full in the base text: "Ye shall not respect persons in judgment; but ye shall hear the small as well as the great; ye shall not be afraid of the face of man; for the judgment is God's: and the cause that is too hard for you, bring it unto me, and I will hear it." The skeleton of the charge: verse 16 first states the scope of the hearing — the causes are to be judged righteously whether they are between brethren or between a man and the stranger that is with him; the sojourning stranger stands in the same court as the brother. Verse 17 sets up three prohibitions — you shall not respect persons, you shall hear the small as well as the great without distinction, you shall not be afraid of the face of man — the word "not" (不可) occurring three times, counted by hand. The reason is a single sentence: "for the judgment is God's." The way out is also single: a cause too hard is to be brought to Moses. Verse 18 closes the part: "And I commanded you at that time all the things which ye should do." [Fact] One small account is also registered here: the people's answer to the proposal for appointing officers is recorded in the whole chapter in this one sentence alone, "The thing which thou hast spoken is good for us to do" (v. 14) — of the people's four speeches in the chapter, this is the shortest and also the most obedient (the other three are at vv. 22, 27–28, and 41). At the setting up of the institution, the congregation was willing to listen; in the Horeb scene there is no murmuring and no refusal — and this reminds us that the first generation's overthrow was not at Horeb: the mountain was good, the command was good, and their receiving of it was good. The overthrow was in a single moment of decision at the gate of Kadesh. Read the people of the two scenes against each other, and the chapter's assignment of blame is accurate: it was not that a generation was stubborn from birth, but that a generation made one concrete refusal at one concrete gate. [Argument] Hidden in this part is a thread in the original text, which can only be spoken of at the Argument layer: the "bear" of Moses' "I am not able to bear you myself alone" and the "bare" of verse 31, "the LORD thy God bare thee," are in the original one and the same word, nāśāʾ (H5375, to lift up, to bear). The word occurs four times in this chapter: at verse 9 Moses is not able to bear, at verse 12 Moses asks how he can bear, and twice at verse 31 God bears Israel as a man bears his son. The weight that man could not bear, God had been bearing all along; the burden Moses laid down at Horeb never fell to the ground — it fell into the hands of the God who had borne them all the way. This is an observation at the level of the word-root; this manuscript marks it as Argument and does not assert it as fact. [Argument] The sentence "the judgment is God's" is the theological centre of gravity of the whole part: the seat the judge sits in is not his own, but God's; and precisely because the judgment is God's, the judge must not respect persons — to respect persons is to sit in God's seat as though it were a man's seat. The first rule Moses lays down for the institution is not efficiency but reverence: first know whose case this is, and only then know how the case is to be judged. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion] In the matter of appointing officers, the parallel account in Exodus 18 shows one marked difference from this chapter; the two are set out side by side and not harmonized: on one reading of Exodus 18, the man who proposed it was Moses' father-in-law Jethro, who, after watching Moses judge alone for a whole day, gave the counsel, and Moses listened and appointed officers; in Moses' retelling in this chapter there is no Jethro, and the whole passage begins "And I spake unto you at that time," the proposal and the decision alike being ascribed to Moses' own account. Two readings of this are set out side by side: one holds that Deuteronomy is an address in Moses' own first person and by its genre sums the matter up from Moses' point of view, so that the omission of Jethro is a choice of genre and constitutes no contradiction; another holds that the two accounts have different emphases — Exodus 18 records the origin of the institution, while this chapter records the institution's charge (vv. 16–17, which Exodus 18 does not have) — each records one part, and they need not be supplemented into one. At the Fact layer this manuscript registers only this: Exodus 18 has Jethro, this chapter does not; the charge of verses 16–17 is peculiar to this chapter. [Summary] The governing sentence of this part: the story of Horeb has only two items — God says, "You have dwelt long enough; set out," and Moses says, "I cannot bear it alone; divide the labour." One is a command toward the promised land, the other an institution toward righteousness: the land is to be entered and possessed, and the days are to be lived righteously. Forty years later Moses retells these two things first, because the life the second generation is about to enter is exactly this kind of life.

### Part Three: Kadesh — the Gate Was Reached, and the People Would Not Enter (1:19–33; 27:00–41:00)

[Fact] Verse 19: the company really arrived. In the base text: "And when we departed from Horeb, we went through all that great and terrible wilderness, which ye saw by the way of the mountain of the Amorites, as the LORD our God commanded us; and we came to Kadeshbarnea." — "Horeb" occurs three times in the chapter (vv. 2, 6, 19), and the full name "Kadeshbarnea" twice (vv. 2, 19; verse 46 has the short form "Kadesh"). The eleven days' journey — they finished walking it. This chapter does not say how many days this leg took; it only registers the whole journey as eleven days back at verse 2. The problem was never on the road; the problem was at the gate. [Fact] Verses 20–21 are the words Moses spoke at the gate; verse 21 is quoted in full in the base text: "Behold, the LORD thy God hath set the land before thee: go up and possess it, as the LORD God of thy fathers hath said unto thee; fear not, neither be discouraged." Register one change of address: at verse 8 God says "I have set the land before you," in the plural; at verse 21 Moses says "hath set the land before thee," in the singular — the whole congregation addressed as one man (so the base text; registered separately). At the end of the words are two prohibitions: "fear not, neither be discouraged" — the prohibitions of fear in the chapter, counted by hand, are five in all: one at verse 17 ("ye shall not be afraid of the face of man"), two at verse 21 ("fear not, neither be discouraged"), and two at verse 29 ("Dread not, neither be afraid of them"). What Moses says again and again in this chapter is: do not be afraid. [Fact] Verse 22: the proposal of the spies comes on stage; the people's words are quoted in full in the base text: "And ye came near unto me every one of you, and said, We will send men before us, and they shall search us out the land, and bring us word again by what way we must go up, and into what cities we shall come." Look carefully at the content of the proposal: to search out by what way they must go up and into what cities they shall come — the question asked is "how shall we enter?" not "shall we enter?"; the proposal itself takes entering as its premise. Moses' reaction in verse 23 is also registered clearly: "And the saying pleased me well: and I took twelve men of you, one of a tribe:" — twelve men, one of a tribe; the number and the composition agree with the sending registered in Numbers 13 (registered from the surrounding context). [Fact] Verses 24–25 are the searching out and the report: "And they took of the fruit of the land in their hands, and brought it down unto us, and brought us word again, and said, It is a good land which the LORD our God doth give us." The account of the spies' report in this chapter must be registered as it stands: in forty-six verses, the spies' report is this one sentence only — "It is a good land." The divided reports of the ten and the two spies recorded in Numbers 13–14, and the exhortations of Caleb and Joshua, are none of them carried in this chapter (registered from the surrounding context, not read back). Deuteronomy's retelling compresses the spies' words down to a single good sentence, as though to make men notice: the bad news did not even come out of the spies' mouths. [Fact] Verse 26 is the turning point of the whole chapter, in the base text: "Notwithstanding ye would not go up, but rebelled against the commandment of the LORD your God:" — with that "Notwithstanding," everything good that went before is turned over: "ye would not" is a refusal of the will; "rebelled" occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 26, 43), and this is its first occurrence. Verse 27 is the talk inside the tents, quoted in full in the base text: "And ye murmured in your tents, and said, Because the LORD hated us, he hath brought us forth out of the land of Egypt, to deliver us into the hand of the Amorites, to destroy us." Note the logic of the accusation: the exodus itself had not changed; what had changed was its interpretation — deliverance was read as a trap, and leading out was read as "hatred." Verse 28 is the melted heart, quoted in full in the base text: "Whither shall we go up? our brethren have discouraged our heart, saying, The people is greater and taller than we; the cities are great and walled up to heaven; and moreover we have seen the sons of the Anakims there." — "Our brethren" means the spies: what melted the congregation's heart was not the enemy; it was their own brethren's words. "The sons of the Anakims" (in the original, ʿănāqîm, H6062) occurs once in the chapter, yet it became the sum name of the chapter's whole fear. [Fact] Verses 29–33 are Moses' last exhortation. Verse 29: "Then I said unto you, Dread not, neither be afraid of them." Verse 30 gives the ground for not fearing, quoted in full in the base text: "The LORD your God which goeth before you, he shall fight for you, according to all that he did for you in Egypt before your eyes;" Verse 31 is the warmest sentence in the whole chapter, quoted in full in the base text: "And in the wilderness, where thou hast seen how that the LORD thy God bare thee, as a man doth bear his son, in all the way that ye went, until ye came into this place." — The bearing occurs twice, both in this verse: God bore you, as a man bears his son. Verse 33 adds one more stroke of God's going with them, in the base text: "Who went in the way before you, to search you out a place to pitch your tents in, in fire by night, to shew you by what way ye should go, and in a cloud by day." — God not only fought ahead of them; He also went ahead of them to find the camping place: where there was fighting, He arrived first; where they would camp, He looked first. [Fact] Verse 32 is Moses' verdict on this whole passage, quoted in full in the base text, in one short sentence: "Yet in this thing ye did not believe the LORD your God," — "did not believe" occurs only this once in the chapter (the negative form of heʾĕmîn, H539). The chapter has no second charge: not that they cursed God, not that they worshipped an idol, not that they committed adultery — only that they did not believe. Moses uses a whole chapter's length to lay out the evidence, and a single word to sum up the charge. [Argument] In this part, see clearly how unbelief does its work: unbelief is not the absence of evidence; it is giving the evidence a different set of interpretations. The same exodus: the man who believes reads it as "He loved us," and the man who does not believe reads it under the words "Because the LORD hated us" (v. 27). The same land: the spies read it as "a good land" (v. 25), and the congregation read it as "The people is greater and taller than we" (v. 28). The evidence was never absent; what was absent was belief — and so the words with which Moses exhorts them offer no new evidence, but only set the old evidence out again: what He did in Egypt, what He did in the wilderness, how a father bears a son. The lesson of faith is often not to wait for new evidence, but to be willing to draw the conclusion from the evidence already given. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion] On who initiated the proposal of the spies, this chapter and Numbers 13 appear on the surface to differ; the two are set out side by side and not harmonized: Numbers 13:1–2 records that the LORD commanded Moses, "Send thou men, that they may search the land"; verse 22 of this chapter records, "ye came near unto me every one of you, and said, We will send men before us," and verse 23 records that Moses found the saying good — the whole passage has not one word about God commanding the sending. The readings of scholarship and of the commentary tradition are set out side by side: one reading takes the people's initiative as the fact of the matter — the people proposed it, Moses permitted it, and God afterwards took this proposal up into His own command (the command in Numbers 13 being the ratification of the permission); another reading takes God's command as the governing line — Numbers 13 records the source of the command and this chapter the course of its execution, each recording one side; a third reading takes this chapter's omission as rhetoric — in his retelling Moses deliberately does not mention God's command, in order to lay the responsibility for the proposal wholly back on the people, because what he is arguing is the people's unbelief, not the origin of the institution of the spies. At the Fact layer this manuscript registers only the difference between the two texts and the coexistence of the three readings, without adjudication. [Summary] The governing sentence of this part: in the Kadesh scene, the evidence is complete to the uttermost — the land is a good land, the fruit is in hand, God goes before them, bearing them as a father bears a son; and the refusal is also complete to the uttermost — they would not go up, and in their tents they read deliverance as hatred. The end of the eleven days is not a city that cannot be broken through; it is a heart that will not believe.

### Part Four: The Sentence and Three Kinds of Exception (1:34–40; 41:00–51:00)

[Fact] Verse 34, the beginning of the sentence, in the base text: "And the LORD heard the voice of your words, and was wroth, and sware, saying," — Wrath (in the Chinese base text, 發怒, "was angry") occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 34, 37; on the KJV letter the two verses use two different words, "was wroth" and "was angry" — see Endnote 5), and swearing occurs three times in the chapter (vv. 8, 34, 35): at verse 8 the oath is a promise, at verses 34–35 the oath is a sentence; the same God who swears first establishes grace by oath and then establishes judgment by oath. Verse 35 is the body of the sentence, quoted in full in the base text: "Surely there shall not one of these men of this evil generation see that good land, which I sware to give unto your fathers." — "there shall not one": the sentence first falls as a universal, and only then are the exceptions opened; the weight of the exceptions shows precisely because the universal comes first. [Fact] The exceptions are three entries, registered one by one. The first entry, verse 36, quoted in full in the base text: "Save Caleb the son of Jephunneh; he shall see it, and to him will I give the land that he hath trodden upon, and to his children, because he hath wholly followed the LORD." — The reason Caleb would see it is a single sentence: "because he hath wholly followed the LORD." The second entry is not among the exceptions but among those under the same sentence: verse 37, quoted in full in the base text: "Also the LORD was angry with me for your sakes, saying, Thou also shalt not go in thither." — Moses too was shut outside the gate, the cause being given as "for your sakes" (in the Chinese base text the pronoun here is singular, "for thy sake," in the same form as verse 21, addressing the congregation as one man; the KJV reads the plural "your" — registered separately, see Endnote 14); the attribution in this verse differs from the account in Numbers 20, and is left to the Discussion layer. The third entry, verse 38, quoted in full in the base text: "But Joshua the son of Nun, which standeth before thee, he shall go in thither: encourage him: for he shall cause Israel to inherit it." — In this chapter Joshua is not named as an exception in the capacity of a spy (in Numbers 14 he is named together with Caleb; registered from the surrounding context), but is named in the capacity of a successor; the man who has already been prepared receives the charge: "encourage him." [Fact] Verse 39 is the most unexpected entry in the sentence, quoted in full in the base text: "Moreover your little ones, which ye said should be a prey, and your children, which in that day had no knowledge between good and evil, they shall go in thither, and unto them will I give it, and they shall possess it." See the cause and effect clearly: one of the excuses the people gave at the time for refusing was that their wives and little ones would be a prey (the sense of Numbers 14:3, registered as a cross-reference report); the sentence pointedly names this very group of whom it was said they "should be a prey" — they shall go in, and they shall possess it. The children whom the adults used as an excuse for unbelief became the heirs of the promise. The words "the land that he hath trodden upon" in verse 36 have a sequel registered from the surrounding context: Joshua 14:9 records that in his old age Caleb cited Moses' oath of that time, to the effect that the land whereon his feet had trodden would surely be his inheritance and his children's for ever, and at Joshua 14:13–14 he received Hebron for an inheritance (registered as a report) — a promise spoken within the sentence was fulfilled, forty-five years later, when Caleb was eighty-five years old, without one word falling short. This chapter registers only "the land that he hath trodden upon" in this verse; the fulfilment lies in the later text and is not read back. Verse 40 is the sentence's execution clause: "But as for you, turn you, and take your journey into the wilderness by the way of the Red sea." — "Turn you": the people standing at the gate turn round and walk back into the wilderness; the forty years begin to be counted from this turning round. [Argument] Hidden in the structure of the sentence is the shape of mercy: beneath the universal, three entries are opened — Caleb, who wholly followed, shall see it; Joshua, who stood ministering, shall go in; the children, who had no knowledge between good and evil, shall possess it. When God shuts a door, His memory is exact: who wholly followed Him, He remembers; who stood beside Him ministering for many years, He remembers; even the children whom that generation itself cursed as those who "should be a prey," He remembers, and with His own hand turns the words of the curse into a promise. The most despairing words of man, God takes up just as they were spoken and changes one word: not "a prey" — "they shall possess it." [Scholarly and Historical Discussion] On the attribution of Moses' not entering the land, this chapter and Numbers 20 appear on the surface to differ; the two are set out side by side and not harmonized: Numbers 20:12 records the LORD saying to Moses and Aaron, in substance, that because they believed Him not and sanctified Him not in the eyes of the children of Israel, they would not bring the congregation into the land — the attribution lying in the matter of smiting the rock at Meribah (registered as a report of the sense); verse 37 of this chapter has Moses' own account, "the LORD was angry with me for your sakes," the attribution lying on the people (at Deuteronomy 3:26 and 4:21 Moses uses the same form of attribution in his retelling; registered from the surrounding context). Two readings are set out side by side: one takes the two places as two sides of one matter — the sin of smiting the rock and the provocation of the people are the outside and the inside of each other, and Moses, standing in this address in the position of the exhorter, mentions only the side that concerns his hearers; another takes the words of this chapter as Moses' rhetoric of self-defence, to be read by the reader against the narrative of Numbers 20, neither place being taken alone for the whole picture. At the Fact layer this manuscript registers only this: the two forms of attribution coexist in the canon, one in this chapter and one in Numbers 20, without adjudication. One further difference is appended: in the sentence at verse 36 of this chapter Caleb is named alone, and Joshua appears in a separate entry at verse 38 in the context of succession; at Numbers 14:30 Caleb and Joshua are named together as the exceptions. The two registrations differ; they are registered only, and not harmonized. [Summary] The governing sentence of this part: the sentence says "there shall not one… see," yet immediately writes down three "shalls" — Caleb shall see it, Joshua shall go in, and the children shall possess it. God's anger is never boundless: as He shuts the door on one generation, He calls out the names of the next generation one by one.

### Part Five: Belated Bravery — Going Up the Mountain Presumptuously (1:41–46; 51:00–57:00)

[Fact] After the sentence had fallen, there is one last scene. Verse 41, the people's reaction; their confession and their action are quoted in full in the base text: "Then ye answered and said unto me, We have sinned against the LORD, we will go up and fight, according to all that the LORD our God commanded us. And when ye had girded on every man his weapons of war, ye were ready to go up into the hill." — Register the items one by one: the confession was real ("We have sinned against the LORD"), the action was also real (every man had girded on his weapons of war), and the attitude was even eager ("ye were ready to go up"); only one thing was false: the timing. Yesterday, when they were told to go up, they would not; today, when they are told to turn back, they are ready to go first. [Fact] Verse 42 is the warning; God's words are quoted in full in the base text: "And the LORD said unto me, Say unto them. Go not up, neither fight; for I am not among you; lest ye be smitten before your enemies." — The reason for the warning is not "you are not strong enough"; it is "for I am not among you." Verse 43 is the second rebellion, in the base text: "So I spake unto you; and ye would not hear, but rebelled against the commandment of the LORD, and went presumptuously up into the hill." — "Rebelled" occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 26, 43), and the word "presumptuously" is this verse's own name for the action: the first rebellion was refusing to do God's word; the second rebellion was rushing to do God's word with its timing changed by themselves. [Fact] Verse 44 is the defeat, quoted in full in the base text: "And the Amorites, which dwelt in that mountain, came out against you, and chased you, as bees do, and destroyed you in Seir, even unto Hormah." — "As bees do": formerly they feared the Amorites; now the Amorites came out as bees swarm. The end point of the chase is Hormah (on the proposal that its name is connected with "destruction," a reading found in scholarship on its etymology, this manuscript registers it as discussion only and makes no assertion — see Endnote 13). Verse 45: the tears came, quoted in full in the base text: "And ye returned and wept before the LORD; but the LORD would not hearken to your voice, nor give ear unto you." — Set this against the opening of the exodus (registered from the surrounding context): Exodus 2:23–25 records that the groaning of the children of Israel came up unto God, and God heard, and remembered, and looked upon them (registered as a report); the tears of this chapter meet "would not hearken" and "nor give ear." The difference between the two tears lies not in the tears but in the timing and the heart: one was a cry under oppression, the other a remorse after defeat. Verse 46 is the chapter's closing sentence, a single sentence, quoted in full in the base text: "So ye abode in Kadesh many days, according unto the days that ye abode there." — The terminus of the eleven days' journey became a dwelling place of "many days"; Deuteronomy 2:14 registers thirty-eight years from leaving Kadeshbarnea to crossing the brook Zered (registered as a cross-reference report), and the main body of the forty years was spent within these "many days." [Argument] This scene is the sharpest lesson the chapter gives to later generations: obedience has its appointed time. God's word is not only a matter of whether its content is right; it includes when it is to be done. Yesterday, when it was time to go up the mountain and they would not, that was unbelief; today, when it is not time to go up the mountain and they are ready to go first, that is presumption — the former is a passive rebellion, the latter an active rebellion, and the chapter records one "rebelled" for each. Belated bravery is not obedience; it is using action to cover unbelief: the confession was made very quickly, and the going up the mountain was a scramble to be first; the one thing they would not do was the one thing the sentence commanded — turn back into the wilderness and wait quietly. [Argument] The sentence "for I am not among you" is the whole reason of the warning in verse 42, and a key to the theology of the whole chapter: Israel's victories never came because they could fight, but because God was among them. When God is present, the land is "set before" them (vv. 8, 21); when God is not present, the same mountain country is Hormah. The people's weapons, numbers, and courage undergo no change at all in this chapter; the only thing that changes is this — whether God is among them or not. [Summary] The governing sentence of this part: at Kadesh there were two rebellions — when the gate was open they would not enter, and when the gate was shut they insisted on forcing their way in; both times, tears and bravery alike were used at the wrong moment. The whole chapter closes within the words "ye abode… many days": the price of unbelief is never an immediate thunderbolt; it is spending the days away, one day at a time, in a place one step short of the promise.

Conclusion (57:00–60:00). [Argument] Look back over this chapter: an eleven days' journey and a forty years' wilderness, and what lies between them is not mountains and rivers but the one word at verse 32 — unbelief. Moses told this most painful stretch of history, verse by verse, to the second generation, not so that they would be ashamed in their fathers' place, but so that, standing at the same gate, they would make a different choice. After this chapter is written, Deuteronomy's address continues: the law will be repeated, the covenant renewed, the song left behind; but the foundation of all of it is the mirror of this chapter — first see clearly how eleven days became forty years, and only then can one speak of how to finish walking the road that remains. Today this address is spoken also to today's hearers: each of us has had such an eleven days in his hands — God set the land before us, the command was clear, the evidence complete; all that was lacking was the willingness to believe and to act. May we not, inside our tents, interpret deliverance as hatred; may we not wait until the gate is shut to become eager and brave; may we, while the gate is still open, go up just as He has said. [Argument] Finally, set two sentences side by side as the close of the whole chapter: at verse 6 God says, "Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount:"; at verse 46 the narrative says, "So ye abode in Kadesh many days." One "dwelt long enough" is the urging of grace; one "abode… many days" is the bill for delay. Israel's story is not finished in Deuteronomy — the second generation did afterwards cross the Jordan, and between eleven days and forty years there proved, after all, to be no eternal distance. Moses stands in the land of Moab speaking this chapter precisely because he knows: history is told so that it will not be repeated.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotional use)

"And in the wilderness, where thou hast seen how that the LORD thy God bare thee, as a man doth bear his son, in all the way that ye went, until ye came into this place." (Deut 1:31) Before Moses rebukes the people for their unbelief, he first reminds them of the tenderest thing of all: all this way, God had borne them as a father bears his son, and brought them along. A father bears his son not only when the child can walk; he lifts him up when the child cannot walk. He does not go before them only when the road is level; in "that great and terrible wilderness" He both fought for them and searched out a place for them to pitch their tents. When Moses said this, there was heartache inside the rebuke: it is not that you have never been loved; you have been borne all the way here — how is it that, having come to the gate, you do not believe this Father after all? We too often stand at our own "Kadesh": the gate is in sight, yet we dare not enter. In truth, the lesson of faith is sometimes not to ask for a new piece of evidence, but to turn back and recognize the hands that have been bearing us all along — He bore the burden Moses could not bear, and He bears us too, until He brings us into this place. Prayer: Father, thank You for bearing us all the way, as a man bears his son. We confess that we often shrink back at the gate and read Your leading as coldness. Remind us, we pray, to turn back and count: which step was not a step You bore us through? May we today, without delay, just as You have said, be willing to believe and willing to act. Amen.

### Devotional Extract (English, KJV-based)

Read Deuteronomy 1:31 (KJV): "And in the wilderness, where thou sawest how that the LORD thy God bare thee, as a man doth bear his son, in all the way that ye went, until ye came into this place." Before Moses names Israel's unbelief, he names God's fatherly care: this people had been carried, not merely guided. The very word Moses used for the burden he could not bear alone (1:9, 12) is the word used here for God bearing His children (1:31). Faith often grows not by demanding new evidence, but by recognizing the hands that have carried us all the way to this place — and then walking through the door while it is open.

## First-Translation Record (Deuteronomy opening batch, 2026-10-25)

- This manuscript is the English first translation of the finalized Chinese manuscript "Eleven Days' Journey" (Deuteronomy 1), translated in full, paragraph by paragraph, by a single translator from that Chinese manuscript as the sole source. The structure corresponds section by section: title verification / base-text notes / form / main text (introduction, five parts, conclusion) / devotional excerpt / endnotes (14) / bibliography, each complete. The source manuscript's Chinese review records are not carried into this English manuscript; this record stands in their place.
- Scripture quotations: every quotation of Deuteronomy 1 in the main text and the devotional excerpt, quoted in full or in excerpt, is embedded verbatim from the anchor text held for this batch (KJV-申1.txt, KJV, 46 lines, fetched in full from the BibleGateway KJV whole-chapter page) and has been checked against the anchor by the translator, quotation by quotation: the thirty quotation places of the source manuscript's register (Endnote 10) are all present and verbatim, 30/30; with the further verses quoted in the header, the introduction, and the devotional excerpt, forty-one verse-sites were checked in all and forty-one agree with the anchor letter for letter, punctuation included, 41/41. Cross-references outside this chapter are kept at the level of registered report throughout, as in the source manuscript, and are not raised to direct quotation; the one short fragment quoted from Numbers 13:2 follows the KJV letter of that place.
- Counts: all counts have been recounted on the KJV letter itself. Where the KJV counts differ from the Chinese companion manuscript's register, the differences are registered separately in Endnote 5 (eight items: the place names at 1:1; the phrases at 1:7; the noun "burden"; the designations at 1:15; the two words of wrath; the pronoun at 1:37; the groups at 1:39; the persons at 1:31) and are neither harmonized nor conformed to the Chinese counts.
- Strong's numbers are carried over from the source manuscript unchanged, number and word-form pairings unaltered: thirteen verified words (Endnote 8) and five words pending verification, with the number of bēʾēr at 1:5 also pending (Endnote 9); nothing pending has been filled in.
- Devotional excerpt: the Chinese devotional text has been translated into English above, its opening quotation embedded from the KJV anchor (1:31). The source manuscript's own English extract, "Devotional Extract (English, KJV-based)," is reproduced verbatim below the translation, letter for letter as in the source — including its wording "where thou sawest" at 1:31, which differs from the anchor's "where thou hast seen"; the extract is preserved as the source gives it, the difference is registered here, and it is not an error of this translation.
- Status: as declared in the header — first translation, pending proofreading （初译待校）; not yet proofread by a native speaker, unpublished, not committed to any repository.

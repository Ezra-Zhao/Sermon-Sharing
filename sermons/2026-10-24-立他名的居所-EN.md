# The Place Which the Lord Thy God Hath Chosen to Put His Name There — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 12

> **English first translation · 初译待校 (first translation, pending native proofread)** — translated in full from the Chinese fixed text (CUNPSS-based sermon manuscript, strongly rechecked and corrected, finalised 2026-10-25). All Scripture quotations in this English version are taken verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), grabbed first-hand from BibleGateway's KJV text; none is translated from the Chinese. See the Translation Record at the end of this file.

> **Determination of the title**: Five candidates were considered — (1) *The Place Where He Puts His Name*, from v. 5, "to put his name there," and v. 21, "the place which the Lord thy God hath chosen to put his name there"; (2) *That Place Only*, from vv. 13–14; (3) *Whatsoever Thy Soul Lusteth After*, from vv. 15 and 21; (4) *The Blood Is the Life*, from v. 23; (5) *Thou Shalt Not Add Thereto, Nor Diminish from It*, from v. 32. Candidate (1) is adopted: the pivot of the whole chapter is "the place which the Lord thy God shall choose to put his name there" — the chapter opens by destroying "all the places" (v. 2, plural), turns and raises up "the place" (v. 5, singular), and the formula recurs in vv. 11, 14, 18, 21 and 26; even the freedom within the towns (from v. 15 onward) is measured out from this one place. The title therefore takes the axis of the whole chapter, not the colour of a single paragraph. The English title uses the chapter's own wording in the KJV at 12:21: **The Place Which the Lord Thy God Hath Chosen to Put His Name There**.

In this English version the base text for all quotation is the KJV, grabbed first-hand from BibleGateway (KJV); the collation items below are carried over from the Chinese fixed text and registered as they stand. ## Main Body

### Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Factual layer] Deuteronomy 12 is the watershed of the second discourse. Chapters 5–11 lay the foundation of the covenant: the Ten Commandments, the Shema, the law written on the heart. Chapter 12, verse 1, opens with the words "the statutes and judgments" — in the KJV: "These are the statutes and judgments, which ye shall observe to do in the land, which the Lord God of thy fathers giveth thee to possess it, all the days that ye live upon the earth" — and so opens the section of concrete law in chapters 12–26; and the first item in the law section is not ethics, and not the feasts, but the place of worship. The chapter has 32 verses; the voice throughout is still Moses', and the imperative runs from end to end: destroy, you shall not, only, take heed, observe. The two poles of the chapter are set out already in vv. 2 and 5: at one end, "all the places" (plural — on the high mountains, on the hills, under every green tree); at the other end, "the place which the Lord your God shall choose … to put his name there" (singular — this one place only). The whole chapter is a gathering-in from many to one: tear down all the places, raise up the place; and then, under the constraint of this one place, turn back and speak of freedom in every town. This chapter stands at the head of chapters 12–26: every statute that follows unfolds with this one chosen place as its common coordinate.

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: the law opens with worship, because the shape of worship comes before the details of conduct — where a person worships, whom he worships, and in whose manner he worships, decides the shape of everything else in his life. Moses does not speak first of how to treat people; he speaks first of where to offer sacrifice. The order itself is the teaching. This is an inference.

[Outline layer] The gist of the chapter: destroy their names, and set up His name (vv. 3, 5); every man doing what is right in his own eyes is to be exchanged for doing what is right in the sight of the Lord (vv. 8, 25); freedom as wide as "whatsoever thy soul lusteth after," and a boundary as hard as a single drop of blood (vv. 15, 23).

### One: Destroy Them Utterly (12:1–4; 05:00–15:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 1 is the heading of the whole section, and the general heading of chapters 12–26: "These are the statutes and judgments, which ye shall observe to do in the land, which the Lord God of thy fathers giveth thee to possess it, all the days that ye live upon the earth." Four items are registered. First, "the statutes and judgments," ḥuqqîm ûmišpāṭîm, is the law section of this book's own name for itself; every statute that follows falls under these words. Second, the time limit is "all the days that ye live upon the earth," kol-hayyāmîm — not a one-generation agreement, but every day of being alive. Third, the land comes before the law: "the land which the Lord God of thy fathers giveth thee to possess it" — the land is given, it is an inheritance; first there is the inheritance of grace, then the law upon the inheritance; the place from which the law is kept is inside the land already given, not outside it, earning a way in. Fourth, "observe to do," tišmərûn laʿăśôt — the pairing of observing and doing reappears in the last verse of the chapter (v. 32, KJV: "observe to do it"): the head and the tail of the chapter are both locked onto these words.

Verse 2: "Ye shall utterly destroy all the places, wherein the nations which ye shall possess served their gods, upon the high mountains, and upon the hills, and under every green tree:" The Hebrew is ʾabbēd təʾabbədûn, the infinitive absolute with the cognate verb, an intensified form (ʾāḇaḏ H6). Three items are registered. First, "all the places," hamməqōmôt, is plural — the first characteristic of Canaanite worship is that its places are many. Second, the three terrains are named one by one: upon the high mountains, upon the hills, and under every green tree (kol-ʿēṣ raʿănān) — the mountain is height, the tree is shade; Canaanite worship is spread over the whole landscape. Third, the qualifier is places where they "served their gods" — the text does not deny that worship was happening there; the question was never whether there is worship, but whom one worships, where, and with what.

Verse 3 unpacks "destroy" into five actions: "And ye shall overthrow their altars, and break their pillars, and burn their groves with fire; and ye shall hew down the graven images of their gods, and destroy the names of them out of that place." The five verbs are registered one by one: overthrow (nātaṣ H5422), of the altars; break (šāḇar H7665), of the pillars (maṣṣēḇôt; maṣṣēḇâ H4676); burn with fire (śārap H8313), of the groves (ʾăšērîm; ʾăšērâ H842; the 2010 edition reads "Asherahs" — see collation item (xi) above); hew down (gādaʿ H1438), of the graven images (pəsîlîm; pāsîl H6459); and destroy (ʾāḇaḏ), of — the names. The objects of the first four are implements; the object of the fifth is "the names of them," šəmām: the end point of destruction is not a thing but a name — that that place should no longer remember what those gods were called.

Verse 4 closes the section: "Ye shall not do so unto the Lord your God." The Hebrew is lōʾ-taʿăśûn kēn; this "so," kēn, is the first of three occurrences in the chapter (see Note 9). One item is registered: what this verse forbids is not the worship of false gods — that has already been torn down; it forbids serving the true God in their manner. The object may be right, but if the manner is borrowed, it will not do. This verse also faces v. 31 across the chapter — KJV: "Thou shalt not do so unto the Lord thy God" — the same prohibition, set in one place after the list of things to be destroyed, and in the other within the warning against imitation: the chapter's line of defence is posted at both its head and its tail (for the kēn tally, see Note 9).

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: the order of this section is destruction before construction. God did not command the people first to build the sanctuary at the chosen place and then slowly to clear the places of worship round about; the first command is to tear down — tear down the altars, break the stones, burn the wood, hew down the images, destroy the names. The cleansing of worship comes before the fervour of worship: bring the implements of the old places into the new place, and the old places have followed you into the new. A further layer: the destruction falls on implements, but its end point falls on "names" — a place may be used again; a name must be destroyed; because the contest of worship, in the end, is a contest of names. At this end their names are destroyed; in the next section His name is set up; the two sections stand over against each other across a single adversative. This is an inference.

[New Testament reading layer] No direct New Testament quotation of this section is registered. Only a thematic echo is registered: the separation guarded in 1 Corinthians 10, "flee from idolatry" (v. 14; registered as paraphrase, not verified verbatim), runs in the same direction as the destruction motif of this section; the echo of John 4 is registered in Section Two as a whole, and is not retrojected here in advance.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The scope of the command to destroy is set out in two readings, without adjudication: (a) The total-destruction reading: the objects are all the installations and the very landscape of Canaanite worship — high mountains, hills, and under the trees without exception. (b) The delimited reading: bounded by the qualifier in v. 2, the places where they "served their gods," the objects of the command are the places of worship and their implements themselves; what is destroyed is altars, images and names, not the rest of the landscape. The two readings are set side by side; this sermon does not adjudicate between them.

[Outline layer] The gist of Section One: the first lesson of worship is not building but tearing down; and only when even the names are gone is there a place in which to set up His name.


### Two: The Place Where He Puts His Name (12:5–7; 15:00–24:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 5 is the turning point of the whole chapter: "But unto the place which the Lord your God shall choose out of all your tribes to put his name there, even unto his habitation shall ye seek, and thither thou shalt come:" The Hebrew opens with kî ʾim — not "and," but "but," "only." Four items are registered. First, "shall choose," bāḥar H977, six occurrences in this chapter (vv. 5, 11, 14, 18, 21, 26; see Note 2; on the KJV's own forms, see Note 17): the subject of the choice of place is always He — the place is not picked by man; it is chosen by God. Second, the first formula for putting the Name, lāśûm ʾet-šəmô šām ləšiknô — to put His name there, as His habitation; God's association of Himself with one place rests on the "name." Third, "shall ye seek," tidrəšû, dāraš H1875 — man's action toward that place is to seek and enquire; it is not that the place comes to accommodate man, but "thither thou shalt come": worship carries movement — one must rise, set out, and arrive. Fourth, "out of all your tribes" — the chosen place falls within the territory of a tribe (v. 14 confirms, one tribe), yet it is open to all the tribes: the place comes from His choice and is not the private property of the tribe in which it lies; one tribe's land, the home of twelve tribes.

Verse 6, the list of what is brought to that place, seven items: "And thither ye shall bring your burnt offerings, and your sacrifices, and your tithes, and heave offerings of your hand, and your vows, and your freewill offerings, and the firstlings of your herds and of your flocks:" Registered one by one: burnt offerings (ʿōlôt; ʿōlâ H5930), sacrifices (zəḇāḥîm; zeḇaḥ H2077, a general word for sacrificial offerings, rendered "sacrifices" in the KJV; the CUNPSS renders it "peace offerings" — see collation item (iii) above), tithes (maʿăśərōt; maʿăśēr H4643), heave offerings of your hand (tərûmat yedkem; tərûmâ H8641), vows (nidrêkem; neder H5088), freewill offerings (nidḇōtêkem; nəḏāḇâ H5071), and the firstlings of your herds and of your flocks (bəkōrōt; bəkôr H1060). A further item is registered: all seven items carry "your" — your offerings, your tithes, your vows, the firstlings of your herds and of your flocks; the cost of centralised worship is not borne by the sanctuary; the worshipper himself carries it on the road.

Verse 7, life at that place: "And there ye shall eat before the Lord your God, and ye shall rejoice in all that ye put your hand unto, ye and your households, wherein the Lord thy God hath blessed thee." Three items are registered. First, "eat" stands in the list of the verbs of worship — that place is not only an altar for offering; it is also a table for eating. Second, those who sit at the table together are "ye and your households," bāttêkem: worship is by household; it is not a merely individual affair. Third, "rejoice," śāmaḥ H8055, three occurrences in this chapter (vv. 7, 12, 18; see Note 4); the ground of rejoicing lies within the sentence — "wherein the Lord thy God hath blessed thee" in "all that ye put your hand unto" (mišlaḥ yedkem; mišlaḥ H4916): first blessing, then rejoicing; the order may not be reversed.

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: God does not dwell in houses made by hands, yet He is willing to put His name in one place — the name is the warrant of His presence, and the coordinate of His people's gathering. The purpose of centralised worship is not control but unity: when twelve tribes eat at the same table of worship in the same place, they first become one family before God, and only then become one nation on the earth. A fact must further be registered, and then the inference drawn: in the whole chapter the place is never named — Moses leaves the sovereignty over the location to God's "choice," not to man's memory; what the people are to learn is not to memorise a place-name, but to follow His choice all their lives. This is an inference.

[New Testament reading layer] The echo between this chapter and John 4 is registered here. The question the Samaritan woman puts to Jesus is precisely a Deuteronomy 12 kind of question — the dispute over the place of worship. John 4:20, KJV: "Our fathers worshipped in this mountain; and ye say, that in Jerusalem is the place where men ought to worship." Mount Gerizim against Jerusalem — that is, "all the places" against "the place which he shall choose," the Deuteronomic pattern recurring on the scene in the New Testament. Jesus' answer, 4:21: "Jesus saith unto her, Woman, believe me, the hour cometh, when ye shall neither in this mountain, nor yet at Jerusalem, worship the Father." 4:23: "But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him." 4:24: "God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth." (Verse 22, grabbed with the passage and registered for completeness: "Ye worship ye know not what: we know what we worship: for salvation is of the Jews.") Only the echo is registered, with no retrojection: the text of Deuteronomy 12 promulgates the command to go to the place He has chosen; it does not prophesy the abolition of the place of worship. John 4 takes the question of "place" up into "the hour cometh," and the coordinate of worship moves from one place toward spirit and truth. This layer registers only the juxtaposition of the two passages; it does not rewrite Deuteronomy by John, nor flatten John by Deuteronomy.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] That the chosen place is left unnamed is set out in three readings, without adjudication: (a) The Jerusalem reading: what is meant is Jerusalem, where David would later build the temple, only it is not named to the present hearers. (b) The open reading: the formula deliberately leaves the place unnamed, so that "the place which he shall choose" may fall, in succession, on more than one location (Shiloh, Jerusalem, for example), the choice resting wholly with God. (c) The genre reading: this chapter is a legal formula, not a prophecy; the location is made known step by step through the choices made in history, and is not closed in advance within the text. The three readings are set side by side; this sermon does not adjudicate among them.

[Outline layer] The gist of Section Two: when all the places have been torn down, only one place remains that bears His name; and the rule of that place is: bring it there, eat it there, rejoice there — the offerings are brought to that place (v. 6), the feast is eaten at that place (v. 7), the rejoicing overflows at that place (v. 7); under that name man eats, under that name he rejoices, under that name he becomes one family.

### Three: After the Rest, That Place Only (12:8–14; 24:00–35:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 8 first describes the present situation: "Ye shall not do after all the things that we do here this day, every man whatsoever is right in his own eyes." Two items are registered. First, "every man whatsoever is right in his own eyes," ʾîš kol-hayyāšār bəʿênāyw — hayyāšār is "what is right," bəʿênāyw is "in his own eyes"; the phrase is at once a description of the present situation and a verdict upon it. Second, the tenses are clear: "that we do here this day" against "Ye shall not do after" — present practice is expressly marked as transitional, not as the norm.

Verse 9 gives the reason for the transition: "For ye are not as yet come to the rest and to the inheritance, which the Lord your God giveth you." Two items are registered: the rest (hammənûḥâ; mənûḥâ H4496) and the inheritance (hannaḥălâ; naḥălâ H5159) are mentioned together — the rest is not ceasing from work; it is an inheritance in which one can settle; and the form of address at this verse is the singular in the Chinese base text ("thy God" — see collation item (iv) above; the KJV here reads "your God": "which the Lord your God giveth you," a divergence between the two versions' address forms registered in Note 17).

Verse 10, the picture of the rest: "But when ye go over Jordan, and dwell in the land which the Lord your God giveth you to inherit, and when he giveth you rest from all your enemies round about, so that ye dwell in safety;" Three items are registered: "giveth you to inherit" (manḥîl, the hiphil of nāḥal H5157) — the inheritance is one that He causes you to inherit; "he giveth you rest" (wəhēnîaḥ, the hiphil of nûaḥ H5117) — the rest is given by Him, not fought out by Israel itself; "ye dwell in safety" (beṭaḥ H983) — the content of the rest is: undisturbed by enemies without, an inheritance to dwell in within. A further syntactic item is registered: in the CUNPSS, v. 10 closes with a full stop and the verse-sentence is complete in itself (the modern-punctuation edition runs it on to the next verse with a comma); in the original, the rest (v. 10) and the bringing of offerings (v. 11) are carried in one breath by a chain of waw-consecutive verbs — the rest is not the background to the bringing of offerings; it is the tense in which the bringing of offerings takes effect. In the KJV, v. 10 likewise stands as its own sentence-unit, closing with a semicolon.

Verse 11, the command taking effect after the rest: "Then there shall be a place which the Lord your God shall choose to cause his name to dwell there; thither shall ye bring all that I command you; your burnt offerings, and your sacrifices, your tithes, and the heave offering of your hand, and all your choice vows which ye vow unto the Lord:" Two items are registered. First, the list has five items, read against the seven items of v. 6: there are no freewill offerings and no firstlings (see collation item (ii) above); this sermon registers the agreements and differences only, and does not explain the reason for the additions and omissions on the text's behalf. The KJV's "all your choice vows" renders kol-mibḥar nidrêkem — what is vowed is to be picked from the best. Second, the second formula for putting the Name, ləšakkēn šəmô šām — the piel of šākan H7931, causing His name to "dwell" there, a different wording from "to put his name there" in v. 5 (see collation item (i) above).

Verse 12, the list of those who rejoice: "And ye shall rejoice before the Lord your God, ye, and your sons, and your daughters, and your menservants, and your maidservants, and the Levite that is within your gates; forasmuch as he hath no part nor inheritance with you." Two items are registered. First, the list has four groups — you, your sons and daughters, your menservants and maidservants, and the Levite; in v. 7 there were two groups, "ye and your households"; by the time of the land of rest, the circle of the feast has widened to four groups. Second, the Levite's qualifier is "he hath no part nor inheritance with you," ʾên-lô ḥēleq wənaḥălâ — within the inheritance system he has no portion of his own (ḥēleq H2506); therefore at the feast of worship a portion must be counted for him. "That is within your gates" — the word "gates" (šaʿar H8179) occurs five times in this chapter; this is its first occurrence (see Note 12).

From v. 13 the text tightens: "Take heed to thyself that thou offer not thy burnt offerings in every place that thou seest:" Two items are registered. First, "Take heed to thyself," hiššāmer ləkā, three occurrences in this chapter (vv. 13, 19, 30; see Note 5). Second, the edge of the contrast lies in two verbs: the place that man "seest" (māqôm ʾăšer tirʾeh) against "the place which the Lord shall choose" (hammāqôm ʾăšer yiḇḥar) — seeing and choosing are a pair of axes in this chapter: man picks a place by his eyes; God chooses a place by His will; burnt offerings may not be offered in every place that man sees.

Verse 14, the positive command: "But in the place which the Lord shall choose in one of thy tribes, there thou shalt offer thy burnt offerings, and there thou shalt do all that I command thee." Two items are registered. First, "in one of thy tribes," bəʾaḥad šəḇāṭeykā — the choice is made concrete within the territory of a single tribe (see collation item (vii) above). Second, "there" (šām) occurs twice: the offerings are to be offered there, and the commandments done there — offering sacrifice and keeping the commandments are in the same place, not divided between two homes.

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: centralised worship carries a timetable. The logic of vv. 8–11 is: the rest has not yet come, and the present situation may be one of making do; once the rest has come, making do must end. God's commands distinguish clearly between "this day" and "then" — He does not condemn the expedients of the transitional period, yet He does not allow expedients to masquerade as the norm. Further, rest and unity are bound together: only when men are settled in the inheritance can they go up to one place; only when there is no disturbance round about can the twelve tribes walk the same road up to the sanctuary. And a further layer: the word "shall" (in "Ye shall not do after…") leaves this generation room to turn — the people of the transitional period are not called rebellious; they are called only people who have "not as yet come." The tone of the command toward them is waiting, not condemnation. This is an inference.

[New Testament reading layer] No direct New Testament quotation of this section is registered. Only a thematic echo is registered: Hebrews 3–4, on "rest" (registered as paraphrase, not verified verbatim), joins with the mənûḥâ motif of this section — the same structure of "not as yet come" set against "enter"; only the echo is registered, and it is not used as this section's exposition.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] What "every man whatsoever is right in his own eyes" in v. 8 refers to is set out in two readings, without adjudication: (a) The present-situation reading: it refers to the moment of the discourse itself — the transitional period in the wilderness and east of the Jordan, when offerings had not yet been centralised and each man acted according to the present situation. (b) The forward-pointing reading: the phrasing is of the same form as the verdict at the end of the book of Judges (registered as a scholarly observation), pointing forward to the age after entering the land, when centralised worship fell empty. The two readings are set side by side; this sermon does not adjudicate between them.

[Outline layer] The gist of Section Three: what is right in one's own eyes today is the reality of the waiting period; "that place only" then is the duty that follows the rest.


### Four: In All Thy Gates, Whatsoever Thy Soul Lusteth After — Only the Blood Thou Shalt Not Eat (12:15–25; 35:00–48:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 15 is the greatest single turning point of the chapter, and in the CUNPSS the quotation-marked discourse also begins from this verse (see collation item (v) above): "Notwithstanding thou mayest kill and eat flesh in all thy gates, whatsoever thy soul lusteth after, according to the blessing of the Lord thy God which he hath given thee: the unclean and the clean may eat thereof, as of the roebuck, and as of the hart." Six items are registered one by one. First, "Notwithstanding," raq — a limiting particle, four occurrences in this chapter (vv. 15, 16, 23, 26; see Note 8); each time it appears, it draws a boundary at the very point of breadth. In the KJV the particle at this verse is rendered "Notwithstanding," and at vv. 16, 23 and 26, "Only" (see Note 17). Second, "whatsoever thy soul lusteth after," bəkol-ʾawwat napšəkā (ʾawwâ H185), three occurrences in this chapter (vv. 15, 20, 21; see Note 6); the KJV phrase "whatsoever thy soul lusteth after" likewise occurs three times at the same verses, and v. 20 carries one further desire-word, "thy soul longeth to eat flesh" (see Note 17). Third, "kill" uses zāḇaḥ H2076 — the verb of sacrificing, here handed down to everyday slaughtering for food: to kill and eat is not thereby to have offered a sacrifice. Fourth, "according to the blessing of the Lord thy God which he hath given thee" — the measure of "whatsoever thy soul lusteth after" is measured by the blessing. Fifth, "the unclean and the clean may eat thereof," ṭāmēʾ H2931 and ṭāhôr H2889 at the same table: this flesh is not a holy thing, and no threshold of cleanness is set for it. Sixth, the standard of the comparison is "the roebuck, and … the hart" — in the Hebrew ṣəḇî H6643 and ʾayyāl H354, clean game that could nevertheless never be offered in sacrifice; the flesh eaten in the towns is eaten by the standard of game. (The CUNPSS names the animals "gazelle and deer"; the KJV names them "roebuck and hart" — a terminological difference between the versions, registered in Note 17.)

Verse 16, the first boundary within the freedom: "Only ye shall not eat the blood; ye shall pour it upon the earth as water." Two items are registered: "ye shall not eat the blood" is the first of four occurrences of the blood prohibition in this chapter (vv. 16, 23, 24, 25; see Note 7); and the destination of the blood is the earth — "ye shall pour it," tišpəkennû kammāyim, šāpak H8210, pour it out as water: not eaten, not stored, returned to the earth. (In the KJV the prohibition at vv. 24 and 25 is carried by the pronoun "it" — "Thou shalt not eat it" — so the KJV's literal word "blood" tallies differently from the Hebrew/Chinese register; see Notes 7 and 17.)

Verse 17, the other side of the freedom: "Thou mayest not eat within thy gates the tithe of thy corn, or of thy wine, or of thy oil, or the firstlings of thy herds or of thy flock, nor any of thy vows which thou vowest, nor thy freewill offerings, or heave offering of thine hand:" One item is registered: the list has five classes (see collation item (ii) above), all of them things already set apart — the dividing line does not lie in what flesh is eaten, but in whether the thing has already been given over to God: what has been vowed, what has been set apart, what has been tithed, is not within the scope of "whatsoever thy soul lusteth after." "Within thy gates," bišʿārekā, is of the same form as in v. 12.

Verse 18, brought back to that place to be eaten: "But thou must eat them before the Lord thy God in the place which the Lord thy God shall choose, thou, and thy son, and thy daughter, and thy manservant, and thy maidservant, and the Levite that is within thy gates: and thou shalt rejoice before the Lord thy God in all that thou puttest thine hands unto." Two items are registered: the list of four groups recurs — thou, thy son and daughter, thy manservant and maidservant, and the Levite; and two of the chapter's four occurrences of "before the Lord" fall in this verse (see Note 12), and the third occurrence of rejoicing is also closed in this verse's account.

Verse 19, a warning standing alone: "Take heed to thyself that thou forsake not the Levite as long as thou livest upon the earth." Three items are registered: "Take heed to thyself," hiššāmer ləkā, its second occurrence; "forsake," taʿăzōḇ, ʿāzaḇ H5800; and the time limit, kol-yāmekā ʿal-ʾadmātekā — all the days of your life, upon your land. Within the system of centralised worship, the first to be left out is the Levite, who has no inheritance and lives by provision; so the command names him alone.

Verse 20, the case when the border is enlarged (in the CUNPSS the quotation opens a new paragraph here): "When the Lord thy God shall enlarge thy border, as he hath promised thee, and thou shalt say, I will eat flesh, because thy soul longeth to eat flesh; thou mayest eat flesh, whatsoever thy soul lusteth after." Three items are registered: the enlarging of the border (yarḥîḇ ʾet-gəḇûlkā, the hiphil of rāḥaḇ H7337; gəḇûl H1366) has "as he hath promised thee" as its premise; "thy soul longeth to eat flesh," təʾawweh napšəkā — the text quotes the sentence that is in man's heart: "I will eat flesh"; the text does not rebuke this sentence; it gives this sentence a lawful road. A further item on the occasion is registered: this desire appears in the days of enlargement of border and prosperity according to promise, not in days of lack — the appetite of the mouth in abundance, and the plea in lack, are handled in this chapter by the same road: you may eat; eat as commanded.

Verse 21, the case of distance: "If the place which the Lord thy God hath chosen to put his name there be too far from thee, then thou shalt kill of thy herd and of thy flock, which the Lord hath given thee, as I have commanded thee, and thou shalt eat in thy gates whatsoever thy soul lusteth after." Three items are registered: the third formula for putting the Name, lāśûm šəmô šām, recurs here (see collation item (i) above); "be too far from thee," yirḥaq, rāḥaq H7368 — distance is a fact of life, and the law faces it squarely, making it neither an excuse nor a charge; "as I have commanded thee," kaʾăšer ṣiwwîtikā — the killing in the towns is still a killing in the shape of a commandment, and wəzāḇaḥtā, the zāḇaḥ there, is again used of slaughter that is not sacrifice.

Verse 22: "Even as the roebuck and the hart is eaten, so thou shalt eat them: the unclean and the clean shall eat of them alike." One item is registered: the roebuck and the hart, and the unclean and the clean — the two pairs of terms recur for the second time; the Hebrew has yaḥdāw, "alike/together" — eating at the same table is precisely the point of this verse, and the KJV carries it with "alike": "the unclean and the clean shall eat of them alike."

Verse 23, the centre of gravity of the whole section: "Only be sure that thou eat not the blood: for the blood is the life; and thou mayest not eat the life with the flesh." Three items are registered. First, "be sure," raq ḥăzaq — ḥāzaq H2388; the chapter's only command to be strong/steadfast is given to the blood prohibition. Second, the reason is stated in the text: "for the blood is the life," haddām hûʾ hannepeš; nepeš H5315 — the blood is not an appendage of the flesh; the blood is the life itself; in the list of what may be eaten in the towns at the soul's desire, life is not included. Third, the CUNPSS parenthesis, "in the original, 'the life'" (see collation item (x) above): what may not be eaten is life. A further item on the Hebrew word order is registered: lōʾ-tōʾkal hannepeš ʿim-habbāśār — the sentence names first, as what may not be eaten, "the life," and only then "with the flesh": in the original word order, life is guarded first, and flesh spoken of afterwards. The parallel passages, checked directly on their per-verse pages and registered here in the KJV grabbed first-hand: Genesis 9:4: "But flesh with the life thereof, which is the blood thereof, shall ye not eat." — the blood prohibition stands long before the law of Moses; it is a command given to the house of Noah. Leviticus 17:11: "For the life of the flesh is in the blood: and I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls: for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul." — the blood's rightful destination is atonement upon the altar, not food in the mouth.

Verse 24: "Thou shalt not eat it; thou shalt pour it upon the earth as water." This repeats v. 16 almost word for word (omitting only the opening "Only") — the same command, the same manner of pouring, promulgated twice within one section; the repetition is itself its weight. (In the KJV, vv. 16 and 24 share the clause "thou shalt pour it upon the earth as water" word for word; the first halves differ: "Only ye shall not eat the blood" / "Thou shalt not eat it.")

Verse 25, the promise of this section: "Thou shalt not eat it; that it may go well with thee, and with thy children after thee, when thou shalt do that which is right in the sight of the Lord." One item is registered: hayyāšār bəʿênê YHWH — "that which is right in the sight of the Lord," standing in exact counterpoint to "whatsoever is right in his own eyes" in v. 8 (see Note 14): the axis of the whole chapter's ethics lies in this pair of phrases — your eyes, or His eyes. And the well-being extends to "thy children after thee."

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: this section is the Bible's model text on freedom and boundary. How far does the freedom go? Slaughter and eating flesh at the soul's desire, no threshold of cleanness, distance counted as no debt, and that sentence in the heart, "I will eat flesh," need carry no shame. How hard does the boundary stand? One drop of blood. And the principle by which the boundary is drawn is single: whatever has already been set apart to God (v. 17) is not within the freedom; whatever belongs to life (v. 23) is not within the appetite. In this section the eating of flesh leaves the altar and enters the daily life of every town; yet the blood is at the same time set apart more deeply as holy — its destinations are only the altar and the earth. There is also the contrast of two tables: the table of worship in v. 18 has a list — sons and daughters, menservants and maidservants, and the Levite; the table in the towns in v. 15 has no list — the unclean and the clean at the same table. The table of the sanctuary shows the order of the covenant; the table in the towns shows the breadth of grace. The inference reaches this point: the table in the towns is permitted to be free and easy; only toward life must the attitude be everywhere steadfast. This is an inference.

[New Testament reading layer] No direct New Testament quotation of this section is registered. Two echoes are registered (both registered as paraphrase, not verified verbatim): in the decision of the apostolic council in Acts 15, the prohibition of blood is listed among the few boundaries given to Gentile believers (15:20, 29) — the blood prohibition of town slaughter in Deuteronomy 12 is restated when the New Testament church deals with the table of the Gentiles; and Hebrews 9–10, on blood and atonement, joins with the motif of Leviticus 17:11, "it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul." These are registered only, and are not used to alter this section's exposition.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The relation between the slaughter-and-eating in the towns in 12:15 and 21 and centralised worship is set out in three readings, without adjudication: (a) The division-of-labour reading: slaughtering for food and offering sacrifice are two different things — the former was never sacrifice from the start, so eating flesh was never governed by the centralisation command; v. 15 is not a relaxation but a drawing of lines: what is centralised is sacrifice only, and daily meat was always permitted in every town. Centralisation and freedom run in parallel without contradiction. (b) The concession reading: the centralisation command comes first, and town slaughter is the concession that follows from it — if every slaughtering had to be brought to the sanctuary (compare the rule for within the camp in Leviticus 17, registered as a scholarly observation), then once the people were scattered across the land after the rest, going up to the sanctuary for every piece of meat would be impracticable; vv. 15 and 20–21 therefore open a door of concession on the grounds of enlargement of border and distance, with the blood prohibition as the retained bottom line. (c) The setting reading: the difference between the two readings arises in part from the different settings of Leviticus 17 and Deuteronomy 12 — gathered together within the camp, against settled and scattered across one land; this section is the law's adjustment to settled life. The three readings are set side by side; this sermon does not adjudicate among them.

[Outline layer] The gist of Section Four: in all the towns, flesh may be eaten at the soul's desire; but of blood, not one drop may enter the mouth; the boundary of freedom is drawn at life.


### Five: Bring Them to the Place Which He Shall Choose; Thou Shalt Not Add Thereto (12:26–32; 48:00–57:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 26, the hard edge of the freedom: "Only thy holy things which thou hast, and thy vows, thou shalt take, and go unto the place which the Lord shall choose:" Three items are registered: raq makes its fourth appearance here — wherever there is breadth, there is an "Only"; "thy holy things," qodāšekā, qōdeš H6944 — what has been set apart no longer belongs to the towns; "thou shalt take, and go," tiśśāʾ ûḇāʾtā, nāśāʾ H5375 — lift up, carry: the holy things must be carried by one's own hand up to that place; there is no option of dealing with them near at hand. A further pair is registered side by side: "thy holy things" is the holiness of things — what has already been set apart; "thy vows" is the holiness of the mouth — what has already been vowed. The holiness in this chapter has two sources, and both must go up to that place.

Verse 27, the rule upon the altar: "And thou shalt offer thy burnt offerings, the flesh and the blood, upon the altar of the Lord thy God: and the blood of thy sacrifices shall be poured out upon the altar of the Lord thy God, and thou shalt eat the flesh." Three items are registered: the burnt offering is a whole offering — "the flesh and the blood" together go up upon the altar (mizbēaḥ H4196); the blood of the sacrifices, yiššāpēk (the niphal of šāpak), is poured out upon the altar; and the flesh of the sacrifices returns to the offerer to eat. The destinations of the blood are now registered completely across the chapter: the blood of the altar is poured upon the altar (v. 27); the blood in the towns is poured upon the earth (vv. 16, 24) — one place returning to the altar, one place returning to the earth, and no place returning to the mouth. Within this verse there are again two handlings: the burnt offering, flesh and blood together, goes wholly upon the altar, and the offerer has no share; in the sacrifices, the blood goes upon the altar and the flesh returns to the offerer to eat. On the same altar platform, one offering belongs wholly to God, and one offering is shared with man in the eating — the degree of setting apart differs; the ownership of the blood is the same.

Verse 28, the first summing-up of the whole chapter: "Observe and hear all these words which I command thee, that it may go well with thee, and with thy children after thee for ever, when thou doest that which is good and right in the sight of the Lord thy God." Four items are registered: "Observe and hear," šəmōr wəšāmaʿtā — the pair of words, keep and hear, which runs through this book from the Shema onward; "that which is good and right," haṭṭôḇ wəhayyāšār — what is right in His sight is not only "right" but also "good"; "for ever," ʿad-ʿôlām — the time limit of the promise is for ever, and its object is "thee, and … thy children after thee"; and in the CUNPSS the quotation section that began at v. 15 closes at the end of this verse (see collation item (v) above).

From v. 29 the warning turns outward (in the CUNPSS a new quotation opens here): "When the Lord thy God shall cut off the nations from before thee, whither thou goest to possess them, and thou succeedest them, and dwellest in their land;" Two items are registered: "shall cut off," yakrît, the hiphil of kārat H3772 — the gaining of the land is the result of His cutting off; and the point in time at which the warning is set is after "thou succeedest them, and dwellest in their land" — the danger is not in the time of war, but after settling down.

Verse 30, the deepest warning of the chapter: "Take heed to thyself that thou be not snared by following them, after that they be destroyed from before thee; and that thou enquire not after their gods, saying, How did these nations serve their gods? even so will I do likewise." Registered item by item. First, "Take heed to thyself," hiššāmer ləkā, its third occurrence; "be not snared," pen-tinnāqēš, whose root affiliation is pending verification (see the Note on format above); only the word-form is registered. Second, "enquire … after their gods," pen-tidrōš lēʾlōhêhem — the second occurrence of dāraš, standing in exact reverse reflection to v. 5: in v. 5 one goes to the place which He shall choose to "seek" (tidrəšû); in v. 30 one must not "enquire after" their gods (tidrōš). The same verb is used only twice in the whole chapter, once toward Him, once toward them — the direction of the seeking is the direction of the worship. Third, the sentence quoted out: "How did these nations serve their gods? even so will I do likewise." — curiosity begins with "How": first ask about their method, then learn their worship; kēn, "so/likewise," makes its second occurrence here (see Note 9; KJV: "even so will I do likewise").

Verse 31: "Thou shalt not do so unto the Lord thy God: for every abomination to the Lord, which he hateth, have they done unto their gods; for even their sons and their daughters they have burnt in the fire to their gods." Three items are registered: "Thou shalt not do so," lōʾ-taʿăśeh kēn, answering v. 4 from head to tail — the whole chapter is framed by "you shall not do so": v. 4 frames the manner of worship, v. 31 frames the imitation of worship; "every abomination to the Lord, which he hateth," kol-tôʿăḇat YHWH ʾăšer śānēʾ — tôʿēḇâ H8441, śānēʾ H8130; and the extreme example is the burning of sons and daughters (yiśrəpû ḇāʾēš) — the end point of "serving in their manner" can be laying one's own children on the altar.

Verse 32, the seal of the whole chapter: "What thing soever I command you, observe to do it: thou shalt not add thereto, nor diminish from it." Three items are registered. First, "thou shalt not add thereto," lōʾ-tōsēp, yāsap H3254; "nor diminish from it," wəlōʾ-tigraʿ, gāraʿ H1639 — the wholeness of the word is the root of the wholeness of worship. Second, the parallel command, checked directly on its per-verse page and here given in the KJV grabbed first-hand: Deuteronomy 4:2: "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish ought from it, that ye may keep the commandments of the Lord your God which I command you." — the same seal: promulgated in chapter 4 at the end of the introduction to the discourses, and promulgated again in chapter 12 at the end of the law of worship; the two places are registered in cross-reading. Third, the numbering variant: this verse is numbered 13:1 in the Masoretic text (see collation item (xii) above); the KJV numbers it 12:32.

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: the enemies of worship are of two kinds. One looks outward: imitating how they "serve" (v. 30) — curiosity about method is the starting point of departure. The other edits inward: adding and diminishing (v. 32) — reshaping God's word to be more serviceable, more convenient. Deuteronomy 12 guards, at the last, not an altar but a word: altars rise and fall with the ages; the wholeness of the word decides the continuance of worship. A layer deeper: from beginning to end the chapter guards two lines — one is direction: to go and seek Him (v. 5), or to go and enquire after them (v. 30); the other is the word: to observe and hear (v. 28), or to add and diminish (v. 32). The last section draws the two lines together: direction and the word — that is the whole line of defence of worship. This is an inference.

[New Testament reading layer] One echo is registered in this section (registered as paraphrase, not verified verbatim): the warning in Revelation 22:18–19 against adding to and taking from the words is of the same form as v. 32 — the last seal of the whole Bible standing over against the first seal of the law section at a great distance. The echo of John 4 was registered in Section Two and is not repeated here. No retrojection, and this is not used to alter this section's exposition.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] First, the function of the 12:32 / 13:1 numbering is set out in two readings, without adjudication: (a) The seal reading: by the chapter division of the English tradition and the CUNPSS, this verse is the closing seal of chapter 12 — the prohibition of adding and diminishing sets its seal upon the whole law of worship. (b) The threshold reading: by the Masoretic numbering, this verse is the opening of chapter 13 — chapter 13 goes on at once to treat false prophets and false dreams, and the prohibition of adding and diminishing is the threshold for discerning false revelation: whoever leads men to forsake this word is not to be heard, even if there are signs and wonders. The two numberings each make a meaning; they are set side by side and not adjudicated. Second, the alternation of singular and plural address ("your God" and "thy God" coexisting in one chapter — singular at v. 9, plural at v. 12, singular at v. 18) is set out in two readings, without adjudication: (a) The rhetorical reading: collective and individual address alternate — the law is promulgated to the whole congregation, and it also speaks to every single person. (b) The transmission reading: the alternation is a trace of different source layers standing side by side in the editing. The two readings are set side by side; this sermon does not adjudicate between them.

[Outline layer] The gist of Section Five: the holy things must be carried up to that place, and of the word not one syllable may be moved; the wholeness of worship rests, at the last, upon the wholeness of the word.

### Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Factual layer] A review of the whole chapter: the destroying of all the places (vv. 1–4); the raising up of the place where He puts His name (vv. 5–7); after the rest, that place only (vv. 8–14); in all the towns, whatsoever the soul lusteth after — only the blood thou shalt not eat (vv. 15–25); the holy things brought to that place, and the word neither added to nor diminished from (vv. 26–32).

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: Deuteronomy 12 concentrates the lesson of worship into a pair of verbs — He chooses, we seek; a pair of eyes — right in one's own eyes, right in His eyes; and one boundary — flesh at the soul's desire, of blood not one drop into the mouth. Between the table and the altar a man learns one and the same thing: to let His eyes be his own eyes. Moses, standing east of the Jordan and looking toward the land, draws this map of worship in advance for a people who have not yet entered — the earlier the map is drawn, the shorter the drifting of the transitional period. This is an inference.

[Outline layer] The gist of the Conclusion: from all the places to one place, from whatsoever the soul lusteth after to one drop of blood — this is the map of worship that Moses set up for Israel before they entered the land.

## Devotional Extract

**Scripture (Deuteronomy 12:23, KJV)**: "Only be sure that thou eat not the blood: for the blood is the life; and thou mayest not eat the life with the flesh."

(The KJV quotation above is the English devotional text already carried in the Chinese fixed text, here re-grabbed first-hand from BibleGateway's KJV and verified word for word: it agrees in full, including "thou mayest not eat the life with the flesh." See the Translation Record for the one registered variant in another first-hand KJV line noted in the Chinese recheck record.)

**Reflection**: This chapter grants a wide freedom: in all your gates you may slaughter and eat meat according to all the desire of your soul. Yet at the center of that freedom stands one firm word — the blood is the life, and you shall not eat it. God has placed the sign of life in the blood. We may enjoy His blessings every day and eat of His bounty, but before one thing we must stop: life itself. Life is not food; life is His. Our reverence for life is our reverence for the Giver of life. Today many blessings are set before you, and much may be taken up and used at your soul's desire; may you be sure and steadfast before every life: never treating life as mere consumption, never taking grace for granted. Gratitude before enjoyment, reverence before use — this is worship at the tables of our own towns.

**Prayer**: Lord, thank You for setting Your blessings in the midst of my ordinary days, and for letting me enjoy them freely. Teach me to be sure and steadfast, and to hold life in reverence: never to despise what You have made, never to waste what You have given. What is right in Your eyes, make right in mine; and the boundary You have set, make me glad to stand within it. In the name of Jesus Christ, Amen.

The CUNPSS quotation sections: quotation begins at v. 15, a new paragraph begins at v. 20, and the quotation closes at the end of v. 28; a further quotation opens at v. 29, and in the 2010 edition the whole section is closed by a closing quotation mark at the end of v. 32.

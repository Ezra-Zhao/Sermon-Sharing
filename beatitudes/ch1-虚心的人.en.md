## I. The Scripture Text

"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." (Matt. 5:3, NKJV)

This is the first sentence of the Sermon on the Mount, and it is the gate of the entire sermon. When Jesus opened His mouth to speak of the kingdom of heaven, the first sentence was not about conduct, and not about merit; it was about a "having nothing" — poverty in spirit. The Sermon on the Mount is not a moral handbook that gives extra points to "people who are more or less all right"; it is an admission ticket opened to "people who are utterly bankrupt." Without this beatitude, the seven beatitudes that follow are all castles in the air.

## II. Exegesis of the Original Text

The Greek original: Μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.

**1. "Blessed" (μακάριος, makarios, Strong G3107)**

This word does not mean "lucky," nor does it mean "in a good mood." In the Greek world, it described an inward steadiness — however bad the outward circumstances might be, inside there is no wavering. The New Testament reserves this word for those whose relationship with God is right (see Rev. 14:13, "Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord from now on"). So "Blessed are the poor in spirit" is not a consolation prize; it is a verdict: people of this kind are already living in blessedness.

**2. "Poor in spirit" (πτωχὸς τῷ πνεύματι, ptōchos tō pneumati)**

This is the key to the whole verse. The New Testament has two words to describe "poor": πένης (penēs, Strong G3993) refers to the poor person who works with his hands — life is tight, but he can still get by; πτωχός (ptōchos, Strong G4434) is different. Its root πτώσσω means "to cower, to shrink back," and it refers to someone who has absolutely nothing, who can only stretch out his hand and beg for food. Lazarus in Luke chapter 16 — covered with sores, laid at the rich man's gate — is described with this very word.

Matthew adds "in spirit" (τῷ πνεύματι), which moves this utter poverty from the wallet to the soul. The Lü Zhenzhong Translation renders this sentence as "Blessed are the poor in heart and spirit," staying closer to the original: it is not "there is no money in my pocket," but "I have nothing at all in my spirit."

A person who is poor in spirit has an attitude toward God summed up in eight words: I have nothing, I can do nothing. It is not a moment of modesty, not a gesture of politeness; it is seeing clearly — standing before a holy God, I have nothing to bring out with which to bargain.

**3. "Theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν)**

Note that the verb ἐστιν ("is") is in the present tense. It is not "someday in the future the kingdom of heaven will belong to them"; it is now, at this very moment, the kingdom of heaven already belongs to them. The first beatitude is the foundation of the whole passage: a person must first admit his spiritual bankruptcy before he can enter the gate of the kingdom of heaven. At the very moment he admits his bankruptcy, the gate opens, and the kingdom of heaven is already his.

English versions for comparison:
- KJV: "Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."
- NIV: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."
- NRSV: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

The three versions are almost word-for-word identical on this verse, which shows that the meaning of the original is clear enough to leave no room for dispute.

## III. The Place and Logic of This Beatitude in the Sermon on the Mount

The eight beatitudes are not eight parallel maxims; they are a road. The first beatitude is the starting point of this road, and also the foundation of the entire Sermon on the Mount:

- Chapter 5, verses 3–10, first speaks of "who are the citizens of the kingdom of heaven" (the eight beatitudes),
- then speaks of "the life of the citizens of the kingdom of heaven" (salt and light, 5:13–16),
- then speaks of "how the citizens of the kingdom of heaven fulfill the law" (from 5:17 onward).

Without the first beatitude, everything that follows is put on from the outside. The mark of a citizen of the kingdom of heaven does not begin with "meekness," nor does it begin with "mercy"; it begins with "admitting that I cannot do it." Poverty of spirit gives birth to mourning (grieving over sin, 5:4), mourning gives birth to meekness (no longer wrestling with God, 5:5), meekness gives birth to hungering and thirsting for righteousness (5:6)… The eight beatitudes are a road of repentance, and the first step is called "poor in spirit."

The tax collector in Luke chapter 18 is the first beatitude in living form: "God, be merciful to me a sinner!" He would not so much as raise his eyes to heaven, but beat his breast. When he went home, Jesus said, "this man went down to his house justified rather than the other." The Pharisee prayed a long prayer, and God did not listen to a word of it; the tax collector said only one sentence, and God took it all in. The difference was not in how much or how little was said; it was in whether the spirit was rich or poor.

## IV. Cross-References

- Isa. 66:2 — "But on this one will I look: on him who is poor and of a contrite spirit, and who trembles at My word." Whom does God look upon? Not upon the capable, but upon the poor in spirit.
- Isa. 57:15 — The High and Lofty One who inhabits eternity says: "I dwell in the high and holy place, with him who has a contrite and humble spirit." Where does God dwell? He dwells in the heart of the poor in spirit.
- Ps. 51:17 — "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and a contrite heart — these, O God, You will not despise." After David sinned, what he brought out was a bankrupt heart.
- Rev. 3:17 — The church of Laodicea said, "I am rich, have become wealthy, and have need of nothing," but the Lord said, "you are wretched, miserable, poor, blind, and naked." The feeling of spiritual riches is the most dangerous poverty of all.
- 2 Cor. 8:9 — "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor, that you through His poverty might become rich." Christ's poverty has exchanged for our riches. This is the gospel foundation of the first beatitude.

## V. Life Application

**Scene One: No longer putting on an act in prayer.** Many people's prayers are like writing a work report: they tell the good things and hide the bad ones. The prayer of the poor in spirit is different — the project they cannot manage, the family members they have wronged, the temper they cannot hold down, they bring it all before God just as it is. "God, I cannot do it anymore" — these few words carry more power than a beautifully written prayer. God does not lack ears to hear fine words; what God wants is a true heart.

**Scene Two: Admitting fault before one's children.** The hardest thing for a father is to admit he was wrong; he always feels that once he admits fault, his authority is gone. In fact it is the opposite: when a child sees his father dare to admit "I was wrong" before God, only then will he dare to do the same before God when he grows up. Poverty of spirit is not weakness; it is showing the child a road — when you are wrong, admit it; when you have admitted it, change; and what you cannot change, hand over to God.

**Scene Three: Handing over the steering wheel when there is nowhere left to go.** There are moments in everyone's life like this: the future of immigration undecided, work insecure, the body flashing red lights; calculating this way and that with one's own small ability, and the more one calculates, the more panicked one becomes. The first beatitude was prepared precisely for such moments: admit it — you cannot walk on by yourself. Handing over the steering wheel is not admitting defeat; it is acknowledging the Lord. The kingdom of heaven is "theirs" — it belongs to those who dare to admit they have no steering wheel.

## VI. Response Prayer

Lord, I confess that I am poor in spirit. I have put on an act for so many years — "I'm all right," "no problem" — but today, before You, I will not act anymore. I have nothing, I can do nothing; my righteousness is like filthy rags, and my cleverness cannot walk out of its own dead end.

Thank You that Christ became poor for me, so that through His poverty I might become rich. Today I hand the steering wheel over to You. Come and dwell in me, and dwell together with me, a person of a contrite heart. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ I pray, Amen.

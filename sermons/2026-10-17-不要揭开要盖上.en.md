## Summary (approx. 100 words)

In the second half of Genesis 9, Noah became a farmer, planted a vineyard, drank of the wine and became drunk, lying uncovered inside his tent. Ham walked in, saw his father's nakedness, and went outside to tell his brothers; Shem and Japheth took a garment, walked backward into the tent, and covered their father without looking upon his nakedness. When Noah awoke, the curse fell on Canaan, while the blessing rested upon Shem and Japheth. This sermon examines three lessons: even those who receive grace may stumble, and the lesson of the vineyard is not far from the ark; when confronted with another’s shame, one has two paths—to expose and broadcast it, or to cover and honor; Noah died at the age of nine hundred and fifty, closing the flood generation, yet God’s line continued onward through the tents of Shem.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you all, brothers and sisters!

In our previous message, we paused beneath the rainbow. God establishes His covenant, God remembers, and the bow is set in the clouds. Reading up to that point, our hearts find deep security.

Yet the Bible does not stop beneath the rainbow. In the second half of chapter 9, the pen turns abruptly—from the covenant in heaven down into a single tent: Noah is drunk, lying uncovered, and his sons are standing at the doorway.

When the Bible writes of heroes, it never whitewashes their flaws. Noah was a righteous man, a preacher of righteousness (cf. 2 Pet. 2:5); the Book of Hebrews enrolls him in the hall of faith (cf. Heb. 11:7). Yet regarding this very same Noah, the Bible records this scene of drunkenness faithfully, without deletion or embellishment. This chapter is not easy to preach, yet it must be preached: for when those who have stepped out of the ark return to daily ordinary life, the first temptation often does not arise on the battlefield, but at home, in the cup, during days when no one is watching over us.

Let us first read the key passages of Scripture:

"Now the sons of Noah who went out of the ark were Shem, Ham, and Japheth. And Ham was the father of Canaan." (Gen. 9:18)

"And Noah began to be a farmer, and he planted a vineyard. Then he drank of the wine and was drunk, and became uncovered in his tent." (Gen. 9:20-21)

"And Ham, the father of Canaan, saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brothers outside. But Shem and Japheth took a garment, laid it on both their shoulders, and went backward and covered the nakedness of their father. Their faces were turned away, and they did not see their father’s nakedness." (Gen. 9:22-23)

"So Noah awoke from his wine, and knew what his younger son had done to him. Then he said: 'Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants he shall be to his brethren.'" (Gen. 9:24-25)

"And he said: 'Blessed be the LORD, the God of Shem... May God enlarge Japheth, and may he dwell in the tents of Shem.'" (Gen. 9:26-27)

"So all the days of Noah were nine hundred and fifty years; and he died." (Gen. 9:29)

Today we will examine the text in three parts:

First, 9:18-21, Stumbling in the Vineyard—Those who have received grace and left the ark must still guard their boundaries in daily life;
Second, 9:22-23, Two Kinds of Sons—One sees and tells, while two walk backward to cover;
Third, 9:24-29, Words Upon Awakening—Curse and blessing diverge in their directions, and Noah’s generation concludes in this chapter.

Let me clarify one matter at the outset: Historically, this chapter has been taken out of context to defend slavery and racial oppression, which is a gross abuse of the text. The object of the curse in the text is Canaan, not all descendants of Ham, nor does it grant any license for people of any era to oppress others. This sermon addresses only the lessons of family, honor, and covering based on the text itself.

Let us bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. In writing about Noah, You recorded not only his faith, but also his weakness, so that we may not boast in ourselves or look down on those who stumble. We pray that today You will teach us to be people who cover, not people who expose, both at home and in the church; help us also to guard our own boundaries, lest indulgence in the cup or in daily life lead us into shame. We pray in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: Coming Out of the Ark, Stumbling in the Vineyard (9:18-21)

### 1.1 "Noah began to be a farmer": A new life begins in the ordinary, and also slackens in the ordinary

Verse 20: "And Noah began to be a farmer, and he planted a vineyard."

"Began," in Hebrew *vayachel*, comes from the verb *chalal* (H2490, to begin, initiate; depending on the context, it can also mean to profane or defile) [1]. Chapter 5 says that Noah was born to bring "comfort" (Gen. 5:29). The first long-term endeavor he undertook after the flood was tilling the ground and planting a vineyard. There was nothing inherently wrong with this: God had handed the stewardship of the earth back to mankind (Gen. 9:1-3); being a farmer was a proper calling, and tending a vineyard was an honorable labor.

The issue arose in the second half of the sentence. Ordinary life itself is not the temptation; indulgence within ordinary life is. Noah did not stumble on the day he planted the vineyard; it happened when the fruit ripened, the wine was brewed, and, with no ark-building project pressing him and no flood waters threatening him, he let down his guard during those peaceful, secure days.

Brothers and sisters, the most dangerous days are often not the days of affliction. In times of suffering, people pray, remain watchful, and cling to God. But in days of ease, boundaries are moved back inch by inch by our own doing: one more drink won't hurt, one more glance won't matter, one more day of delay is fine. Noah's ark could withstand forty days and forty nights of rain, but his tent could not withstand cup after cup of wine.

### 1.2 "He drank of the wine and was drunk": Wine itself and drunkenness must be clearly distinguished

"Wine," in Hebrew *yayin* (H3196, wine) [2]; "was drunk," *shakar* (H7937, to become drunk) [3].

Our attitude toward wine in Scripture must remain balanced; we must not swing to extremes. Psalm 104:15 says that wine "makes glad the heart of man"; our Lord Jesus turned water into wine at the wedding in Cana (cf. John 2); Paul also advised Timothy to use a little wine for his stomach's sake (cf. 1 Tim. 5:23). What Scripture condemns is not the grape, but drunkenness and indulgence: Proverbs 20:1 says, "Wine is a mocker, strong drink is a brawler, and whoever is led astray by it is not wise." Proverbs 23:29-35 vividly portrays the woe, sorrow, contentions, redness of eyes, and wounds of those who linger long over wine, concluding with what sounds like a drunkard’s confession: "They have struck me, but I was not hurt."

The New Testament draws a definitive line: "And do not be drunk with wine, in which is dissipation; but be filled with the Spirit" (Eph. 5:18). The danger of drunkenness is not merely physical harm; it is that a person surrenders the steering wheel: the tongue that could be guarded when sober, the eyes that could be restrained, the propriety that could be maintained, are all completely lost when intoxicated. Noah lying uncovered in his tent presents a picture of that breach: a man respected by his entire household lies fallen beneath the wine he brewed himself, having lost both his dignity and his covering.

### 1.3 "Became uncovered in his tent": Shame begins when covering is lost

"Uncovered" is related in the original language to *ervah* (H6172, nakedness, shameful exposure) [4]. From chapter 3 onward, nakedness has been linked with shame: the moment man sinned, he first discovered that he was naked (Gen. 3:7); God gave man garments of skin to restore covering to him. This scene with Noah is an echo of chapter 3: as soon as man indulges himself, the covering falls away, and shame enters.

The words of Habakkuk 2:15 speak directly to this kind of matter: "Woe to him who gives drink to his neighbor, pressing him to your bottle, even to make him drunk, that you may look on his nakedness!" The Bible sees through this clearly: causing someone to get drunk and looking upon another's humiliation has never been a small matter—whether the instrument used is wine, words, or a smartphone today.

Warren W. Wiersbe, in his commentary on Genesis discussing Noah’s drunkenness, reminds readers to take note that even those who have received great grace must never slacken in everyday indulgence; the stumbling of great men is recorded in Scripture to serve as a warning for those who come after.

Let us summarize this first part in a single sentence: **The lesson of the ark is obedience; the lesson of the vineyard is temperance. The former can endure great waves, while the latter must guard daily life—people rarely fall in grand crises; they mostly slacken in small matters.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part 2: One Goes Out to Tell, Two Walk Backward to Cover (9:22-23)

### 2.1 Ham: "Saw... and told outside"

Verse 22 describes Ham in just two steps: "And Ham, the father of Canaan, saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brothers outside."

Please notice that the text does not say Ham did anything more outrageous; these two steps alone—seeing, and going out to tell—became the turning point of the entire incident. In Ham’s eyes, his father’s shame became news, became fodder for gossip. He did not cover it, he did not look away, he did not stay inside the tent to deal with it; he carried his family’s shame outside.

Brothers and sisters, this scene is replayed every single day, only the tent has changed its form: a family member’s embarrassment is posted in a group chat, a brother’s fall becomes a snack for idle talk over tea, a colleague’s blunder is screenshot and passed around. Ham’s sin was not only in his eyes, but in his mouth; not only in seeing, but in broadcasting. Proverbs 11:13 says: “A talebearer reveals secrets, but he who is of a faithful spirit conceals a matter.” (Prov. 11:13). Proverbs 17:9 is even more direct: “He who covers a transgression seeks love, but he who repeats a matter separates friends.” (Prov. 17:9).

Before passing words along, ask first: If these words go out, do they cover someone, or do they expose someone; do they edify someone, or do they consume someone?

### 2.2 Shem and Japheth: “Took a Garment… and Went Backward”

Verse 23 is the most beautiful picture in the whole chapter: “And Shem and Japheth took a garment, laid it on both their shoulders, and went backward and covered the nakedness of their father. Their faces were turned away, and they did not see their father’s nakedness.” (Gen. 9:23).

“Covered,” in Hebrew *kasah* (כָּסָה, H3680, to cover, conceal)[5]—this is the exact same covering as in Psalm 32:1: “Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered.” (Ps. 32:1). In what these two sons did, there are three details worth learning:

First, they took the garment before entering the tent. Covering is an action taken with preparation, not standing at the doorway sighing with emotion.
Second, they went backward. There was wisdom in their method: they handled the problem without looking at their father’s embarrassment a second time. Covering is not pretending nothing happened; it is dealing with it without exploiting it.
Third, their faces were turned away. They could not even bear to look. This is honoring: that the father was drunk was a fact, but the father was still the father. Exodus 20:12, “Honor your father and your mother” (Ex. 20:12), is given a visual picture here—honoring is not merely showing respect when a father is dignified; it is still preserving his dignity when he is at his least dignified.

First Peter 4:8 says: “And above all things have fervent love for one another, for ‘love will cover a multitude of sins.’” (1 Pet. 4:8). Galatians 6:1 speaks of the attitude in restoring one who has fallen: “Brethren, if a man is overtaken in any trespass, you who are spiritual restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness” (Gal. 6:1). Covering is not condoning sin; Shem and Japheth did not say their father was right to drink. Covering is a matter of priority—first lifting someone out of their shame before discussing right and wrong.

Let us ignite this point right here.

Brothers and sisters, what this generation lacks least are hands that expose: hands that screenshot, hands that forward, hands that stand at the doorway shouting to others. What is most lacking in the church and in the home are hands that walk in backward to drape a garment.

Right today, not tomorrow—right now in your mind there is someone’s embarrassment: a family member’s, a brother’s, a colleague’s, which you have continually used as fodder for talk, as a laughingstock, as proof that you are better than them. Take up that garment today: stop forwarding that message, leave that sentence unsaid, be the first one in that group chat to stand up and say, “Enough.” Covering someone will not cost you anything; exposing someone chills the heart of the entire family. God remembers those who cover—what later came out of Shem’s tent, we will see in the third part.

### 2.3 The Same Tent, Two Kinds of Hearts

Please notice that the three sons faced the exact same situation: the same father, the same tent, the same shame. The distinction lay not in the circumstances, but in the heart. Ham’s heart treated shame as a resource; Shem’s and Japheth’s hearts treated shame as a responsibility.

When seeing another person’s weakness, a person’s first reaction reveals the condition within him: those who despise first expose themselves; those who cover demonstrate a heart that fears God.

Let us wrap up this second part in one sentence: **When shame falls upon a home, the issue is never whether someone sees it; it is which way the one who sees it walks—broadcast it outward, and the home scatters; walk backward to cover it, and the home remains.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: After Waking Up, Words Divided Direction (9:24–29)

### 3.1 Noah Awoke from His Wine: The Curse Fell on Canaan

“So Noah awoke from his wine, and knew what his younger son had done to him. Then he said: ‘Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants he shall be to his brethren.’” (Gen. 9:24–25).

Here are two points in reading the text that must be addressed honestly. First, who the “younger son” refers to; the context points to the line of Ham, though scholars have various discussions regarding the specific reference, and this manuscript does not draw a forced conclusion. Second, the object of the curse was Canaan, Ham’s son, not Ham himself, and certainly not all of Ham’s descendants. Later, the history of the various peoples of Canaan and Israel (cf. the outcome of the Canaanite peoples in Joshua and Judges) is seen in the biblical narrative as the line of fulfillment for these words; this manuscript makes only a narrative correspondence and does not extend to any subsequent ethnic group.

We must also see a mirror in Noah: the words he spoke after waking up were tinged with anger. It was true that as a father he had been disgraced, but for words spoken in anger, their weight must be left to God to execute; we must not use this chapter to find justification for uttering cursing words toward family members. James chapter 3, speaking of the tongue, says that the tongue is set on fire by hell, and that blessing and cursing proceeding from the same mouth ought not to be so (cf. Jas. 3:6–10). Noah’s words are recorded in Scripture as a narrative, not as a license for us.

### 3.2 “Blessed Be the LORD, the God of Shem”: The Line of Promise Walks through Shem

The blessing contains two sentences, carrying immense weight:

“Blessed be the LORD, the God of Shem.” (Gen. 9:26).

Please notice the phrasing: it does not bless Shem first, but blesses the LORD—the God of Shem. In the entire Bible, God linking His own name with a person’s name happens only along the chosen line: later, “the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob” first surfaces its roots here. From Shem’s descendants later came Abraham, came David, came the Lord Jesus Christ (cf. the genealogy tracing upward in Luke 3). For the son who covered his father, his tent became the tent where God dwelt.

“May God enlarge Japheth, and may he dwell in the tents of Shem.” (Gen. 9:27). Japheth’s blessing was not to fly solo either, but to dwell in the tents of Shem—to have a share in the covenant God made with Shem. This foreshadowed the picture of Gentiles becoming fellow heirs of the promise in Christ (cf. Eph. 3:6). A single tent, first the place where Noah collapsed in drunkenness, later became the sign where God dwelt and where all nations came to seek refuge.

### 3.3 “Noah Lived Nine Hundred and Fifty Years; and He Died”: A Generation Closed, but the Line Did Not Break

Verses 28 and 29 close very quietly: “And Noah lived after the flood three hundred and fifty years. So all the days of Noah were nine hundred and fifty years; and he died.” (Gen. 9:28–29).

That recurring phrase from the genealogy of chapter 5, “and he died,” fell upon Noah. The generation of the flood came to a close here: Noah was the last man of the old world and the first farmer of the new world; he had seen the rainbow and had planted a vineyard; he had preached righteousness and had collapsed drunk in his tent. Scripture neither deletes a stroke from his life nor exaggerates a stroke.

Yet the line did not break. In chapter 10, the nations were to unfold from these three sons; in chapter 11, there was Babel, and there was also the genealogy of Shem, finally pausing upon the calling of Abram. Where Noah’s story ended was precisely where the foreshadowing of Abraham’s story began. God’s work has never depended on one man living forever; He brings about generational transitions, causing the covenant to be passed down from generation to generation.

Psalm 90 is a prayer Moses wrote amidst generational transition: “So teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom.” (Ps. 90:12). Noah’s nine hundred and fifty years sounds long, but cast into eternity, it remains merely a single generation. However long or short a person’s days on earth may be, those who count them clearly spend their days covering, covenanting, and passing on blessing; those who cannot count them clearly squander their days exposing, gossiping, and getting drunk.

Let us wrap up this third part in one sentence: **Noah’s generation closed, yet his words left behind a direction: for those who cover, their tent becomes the place where God dwells; how a generation ends depends on whether what is left behind for the children is more words of cursing, or a clear path of blessing.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

Today, this chapter speaks to six categories of people:

**To seekers:** You might feel that the Bible is full of perfect people, and that someone like yourself could never enter. This chapter of Noah first dismantles that misunderstanding: the Bible records truthfully even the drunkenness of the person it honors most. God accepts people not because they have no embarrassments, but because He Himself covers them. Do not dare not draw near today because of your past; come before Him first—covering begins with Him.

**To new believers:** When you stumble in everyday small matters after believing in the Lord, do not abandon yourself to despair, nor treat it as trivial. Learn two things: First, establish boundaries early—alcohol, eyes, cell phones, money—whichever is your vineyard, settle the boundaries clearly today; second, if you stumble, first find brothers and sisters who will cover you to restore you; do not hide inside your own tent and rot away.

**To mature brothers and sisters who have followed the Lord for many years:** The longer the years, the more you must guard against slackness in security. Noah did not fall in the flood; he fell after the harvest. Take stock: Have your boundaries tightened or loosened in these past few years? Is your first reaction toward someone who falls a Ham-like broadcasting, or a Shem-like covering? The maturity of a long-time believer is seen first in these two things.

**To parents:** This chapter serves as a two-sided mirror for parents: on the one hand, do not let your self-indulgence become a lifelong lesson for your children—children learn more at the entrance of your tent than from what you say behind the pulpit; on the other hand, if your children have seen your weakness, do not suppress them with anger and curses, but steer the course back with repentance and blessing. The words you speak after you awaken, your children will remember for a lifetime.

**To those striving in the workplace:** Every day in the office, there are other people's tents: a colleague's blunder, a supervisor's embarrassment, a client's private matter. Ham-like people rely on peddling gossip to gain position, while Shem-like people are entrusted with great matters because they are trustworthy. In the long run, bosses know in their hearts who they can dare to entrust with crucial matters. Be the one who walks in backward to cover; your integrity is your future.

**To those who are currently in suffering:** You may be lying inside your own tent right now—a shameful thing, a fall, a past you don't want anyone to know about. The word for today is: do not keep lying there waiting for someone to come and uncover it. Come before God first; His covering is not mockery, it is the tunic of skin, it is the rainbow, it is the robe of Christ's righteousness. Those who are covered by God can stand back up and walk out; shame is not your final chapter.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Summary · Prayer of Response

Let us review today's three sentences:

First, the ark can endure massive waves, but the vineyard must be guarded in daily life—for the redeemed, self-control must never be relaxed;
Second, the same tent, two kinds of sons—spread it abroad, and the family falls apart; walk backward to cover, and the family stands secure;
Third, Noah's generation comes to a close, but the tents of Shem march forward—covering and blessing are the most solid inheritance left to future generations.

Now let us bow our heads and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. You did not edit out the page of Noah's drunkenness, nor did You let shame become the final verdict of his life. We pray that You guard our daily lives: in wine, in our eyes, in our speech, and in places where no one sees, give us a heart of self-control. We also pray that You transform our hands and our mouths: no longer uncovering, no longer broadcasting, but learning from Shem and Japheth—taking up the garment, walking in backward, and gently covering the shame of our family and our brethren. For parents, we pray that You refine the words we speak after we wake up, leaving only blessing for our children. May our tents become tents where You delight to dwell. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

[5] kasah: Hebrew כָּסָה (*kāsâ*, H3680), to cover, conceal; see Gen. 9:23 (covered their father); Ps. 32:1 (whose sin is covered); definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

[6] Regarding Gen. 9:23, "went backward and covered the nakedness of their father": Chinese Union Version; English comparison: KJV, NIV, and NRSV render the verse with the same meaning; this manuscript does not directly quote the English translations. [7] Regarding the recipient of the curse in Gen. 9:25: the Chinese Union Version translates it as "Cursed be Canaan," where the recipient is Canaan alone, not all the descendants of Ham; historically, using this verse to justify slavery and racial oppression is a misuse, which this manuscript explicitly rejects.

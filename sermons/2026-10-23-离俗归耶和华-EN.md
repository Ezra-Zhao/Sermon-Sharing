# Separated unto the LORD — A Sermon on Numbers 6

- Text: Numbers 6:1-27
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending cross-review (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Numbers 6; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible and cross-read against the full chapter as carried by the FHL CBOL project; verses not checked word for word are given in summary only, never as quotations.
- Note on the title: The tentative directions, "the vow of the Nazarite" and "the priestly blessing," are summaries of the chapter's content rather than any unbroken wording of the Chinese base text, and each covers only half the chapter. The base text's own recurring unbroken wording is "separate oneself unto the LORD" — at Numbers 6:2, 6:5, 6:6, and 6:12, each checked verse by verse — the charter wording of the vow regulations, which reaches across to the blessing that closes the chapter. The Chinese title was therefore corrected to those characters of the base text. The KJV at those verses reads "to separate themselves unto the LORD" (6:2), "he separateth himself unto the LORD" (6:5), "he separateth himself unto the LORD" (6:6), and speaks in 6:12 of consecrating "unto the LORD the days of his separation." The English title renders the corrected Chinese title: "Separated unto the LORD." See Endnote 2.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Chapter 5 deals with cleanness inside the camp — suspicion, unfaithfulness, and wrongs, each to be dealt with in turn. Chapter 6 turns in an entirely different direction: not the expulsion of the unclean, but the completing of the willing. Any man or woman who makes a special vow may separate himself unto the LORD and live, for a set stretch of days, a priest-like life of holiness: touching nothing that comes of the vine, letting no razor come upon his head, and coming at no dead body — not making himself unclean even for father, mother, brother, or sister. The text then provides for the most discouraging accident of all: the days are not yet fulfilled, a man dies very suddenly beside him, and the head of his consecration is defiled — he shaves his head on the seventh day, brings two turtles or two young pigeons on the eighth for a sin offering and a burnt offering, adds a lamb of the first year for a trespass offering, and the days that were before are lost; he begins again. When the days are at last fulfilled, there is a whole set of completion offerings: three animals, a basket of unleavened bread, with the meat offering and the drink offerings; the hair of his separation is shaved off and put in the fire under the sacrifice of the peace offerings, and only after that may he drink wine. The chapter's second half is only six verses, yet it holds the words all Israel was to hear continually: the LORD Himself teaches Aaron and his sons the words of blessing, to put His name upon the children of Israel — The LORD bless thee, and keep thee: The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee: The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace — and pledges His own word to it: "and I will bless them." This sermon reads the chapter in the text's own order: first the voluntariness of the vow and its charter phrase; then the three separations item by item; then defilement and beginning again, and the full list of the completion day; and finally the threefold structure of the priestly blessing and its close in the putting of the Name. The thread it traces is this: separation is not escape from people, but the handing over of a whole stretch of days; every prohibition of the vow is precious, and the vow's end is not man's achievement but the blessing spoken by God's own mouth.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 Man or woman: a vow taken up willingly

(Fact) Chapter 6 opens with the LORD speaking to Moses, commanding him to speak to the children of Israel; the qualification for the vow stands in the KJV: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, When either man or woman shall separate themselves to vow a vow of a Nazarite, to separate themselves unto the LORD:" (Num 6:2, KJV) Three features of this verse must be set steady first, for they are the chapter's foundation. First, "either man or woman" — this vow is not divided by sex. The offices of the Old Testament are mostly bounded by maleness, by the tribe of Levi, by the sons of Aaron; the Nazarite vow is open to every Israelite: a man may take it, and a woman may take it; a common Israelite, no priest, may live for a season a priest-like life of holiness. It is a rare commoner's door in Israel's faith: holiness is not only an identity fixed by birth; it can be a stretch of days a commoner vows with his own mouth and walks into of his own will. Second, the vow is "special": the Hebrew verb here is the causative form of pālāʾ (Strong's H6381), close in sense to "to make an extraordinary vow." In preparing this sermon, verse 2 was checked in the Hebrew text (BHS) as carried on wd.bible: the verse joins the words neder, "a vow" (Strong's H5088), and nāzîr, "a Nazarite" (Strong's H5139), and the separating is expressed by the causative infinitive of nāzar (Strong's H5144) — nāzîr is precisely the noun of that verb: a Nazarite is "one separated." The Chinese base text itself glosses the word in its note at this verse as "set apart unto the Lord," in exact agreement with the root. Third, the direction: "to separate themselves unto the LORD" — separation is the act, and "unto the LORD" is its direction. This separation is not separation for separation's sake, not a leaving of the crowd for a strange life; it is the marking off of a stretch of days from common use, and the handing of the whole stretch to the LORD. Separated, yet not unto the LORD, is mere eccentricity; unto the LORD is the vow's very nature.

(Fact) One more singularity of this vow must be stated in this section: it has a term. The chapter speaks throughout of "the days of his separation," "all the days of the vow of his separation," and days "fulfilled" — verse 5 speaks of the days "in the which he separateth himself unto the LORD" being fulfilled, and verse 13 of the days of the Nazarite's separation fulfilled (the verse quoted in section III). Nowhere does the chapter give one fixed number of days: not thirty, not a hundred; it speaks only of the stretch the vower himself has vowed (see the discussion-level treatment in Endnote 5). The Nazarite vow is therefore a commoner's vow — voluntary, for a term, and solemn: voluntary, so that man and woman alike may come; for a term, so that it has a beginning, a fulfilment, and a completion offering; solemn, so that defilement along the way carries a real cost, and completion carries a full set of offerings to the door of the tabernacle. It is neither a passing emotion nor a lifelong chain — it is a stretch of days marked off, and counted as they pass.

(Application) Mark where this vow stands: immediately after chapter 5. There, men are examined: the wrongdoer must confess and make restitution, and the woman under suspicion is brought before the priest to be tried. Come to chapter 6, and the picture is wholly changed: no one is examined, no one is compelled; someone stands forward of his own accord and says, I will. In the world of the law, this stretch of text is like a window: drawing near to God is not all obligation and appointment; it can also be love and free will. The Levites' service was assigned; the priests' identity was inborn; the Nazarite's vow alone was vowed — and God, among His people, deliberately left one road by which the most ordinary person could say, in the most solemn manner: these days are Thine. To today's hearers this is comfort and reminder at once. Comfort, because the road of nearness to God stands open to everyone, regardless of sex, tribe, or trade. Reminder, because though the door stands open to all, to walk through it costs — the three prohibitions of the next section cut closer, each one, to the bone. A vow is no light mood; a vow is a covenant spoken by the mouth and kept to the end.

(Application) One more layer, to be preached to those willing to offer themselves: the vow is man's to take, but its regulations are God's to give. A man may decide whether to vow, and for how long; he may not decide how to keep it — what is forbidden, what follows defilement, what is offered at the fulfilment, are all words the LORD spoke to Moses, and none of them is the vower's invention. Voluntariness and obedience are, in this vow, two faces of one thing: to enter is voluntary; having entered, it is obedience. Many today understand "voluntary" as "in my own way," and turn consecration into self-expression. The Nazarite vow says the opposite: the sincerity of your offering shows itself precisely in whether you will keep it by His regulations rather than by your own ideas. How far the vine is to be refused, how long the hair is to grow, how completely the dead are to be avoided — the details are all fixed by Him; yours is only to be faithful in the details, day by day. True consecration unto the LORD was never a grand gesture; it is faithfulness, in details no one sees, one day at a time.

### II. 08:00-24:00 A threefold separation: the vine, the hair, and the dead

(Fact) The one who has vowed separation keeps three prohibitions; this section reads them item by item from the text. The first: abstinence from everything that comes of the vine. Verse 3 stands in the KJV: "He shall separate himself from wine and strong drink, and shall drink no vinegar of wine, or vinegar of strong drink, neither shall he drink any liquor of grapes, nor eat moist grapes, or dried." (Num 6:3, KJV) Verse 4 carries the range to its end: "All the days of his separation shall he eat nothing that is made of the vine tree, from the kernels even to the husk." (Num 6:4, KJV) Count the list of the two verses together, item by item, as this sermon has done: wine and strong drink — two items, the drink itself; vinegar of wine or of strong drink — one item, the drink in its soured form; liquor of grapes — one item, juice not yet become wine; moist grapes and dried — two items, the fruit fresh and dried; and verse 4 adds its summary — nothing made of the vine tree, from the kernels even to the husk, not even the grape's stones and skin excepted. What is forbidden is not drunkenness; it is the entire produce of one plant: fermented and unfermented, liquid and solid, flesh and kernel and husk — in the days of his separation the whole vine tree has nothing to do with him. And this is the most common, most gladdening produce of Israel's daily life — wine that makes glad the heart of man, and the grape as the signature fruit of Canaan — yet the separated one must first say "no" to the most common joy of all.

(Fact) The second prohibition: no razor. Verse 5 stands in the KJV: "All the days of the vow of his separation there shall no razor come upon his head: until the days be fulfilled, in the which he separateth himself unto the LORD, he shall be holy, and shall let the locks of the hair of his head grow." (Num 6:5, KJV) Mark this verse's order: it does not speak first of hair; it speaks first of holiness — "he shall be holy" — and the locks left growing are holiness's visible sign upon the body. Another man looks at his head and knows this man is inside a stretch of days that belong to the LORD; the days pass, the hair lengthens, and the vow's progress is written on his head, with no need for him to explain himself aloud. The third prohibition: no coming at a dead body. Verse 6 stands in the KJV: "All the days that he separateth himself unto the LORD he shall come at no dead body." (Num 6:6, KJV) How strict this is appears only in verse 7 (Summary, the verse's closing words differing slightly between editions of the Chinese base text, and it is therefore not quoted in the Chinese manuscript either): when his father, his mother, his brother, or his sister dies, he shall not make himself unclean for them, because the consecration of his God is upon his head. Verse 8 gathers the three prohibitions up: "All the days of his separation he is holy unto the LORD." (Num 6:8, KJV) The "consecration" upon his head in verse 7 is, in the Hebrew, nēzer (Strong's H5145), whose sense is separation, consecration — and a crown: the long hair on his head is not a mere sign; in the original it carries the image of a crown. A commoner makes a vow, and there is one more crown on his head, an invisible one — the token that he belongs to God (for the occurrences of nēzer checked in this chapter, see Endnote 8; a checked selection, not an exhaustive list).

(Fact) The three prohibitions, set side by side, are not strict to an even degree, and the difference is worth stating. The vine prohibition is strict in range: the whole produce of one tree forbidden, kernel and husk with no margin left — a strictness that fences off danger at a distance, permitting not even an approach to the edge. The razor prohibition is strict in time: from the vow's first day to its last, the razor may never once come upon his head — a strictness of endurance, with not one day's exception. The dead-body prohibition is strict in relation: not even the funerals of his nearest kin may make an exception for him — father, mother, brother, sister; the nearest kin listed in verse 7, counted item by item for this sermon, are four: father, mother, brother, and sister. An ordinary Israelite defiled by the dead was unclean seven days and then clean; a priest was even allowed a measure of exception for near kin (Leviticus 21, Summary); the Nazarite has not even that allowance — in this one point he is stricter than the priest. And the text gives the reason plainly: because the consecration of his God is upon his head. On his head rests the crown of belonging to God; his feet therefore cannot walk into the tent of death. This is not heartlessness; it is the vow's own logic: he stands inside a stretch of days wholly God's, and even the most rightful mourning must give way.

(Application) Together, the three prohibitions draw three lines across a man's life, and each line faces one of the most ordinary human instincts. The vine faces enjoyment: it is not that wine is evil; it is that in days belonging to the LORD, even rightful enjoyment may be laid down first — and the more completely it is laid down, the more it shows that this stretch of days is kept for Him alone. The hair faces respectability: long hair in that world was no fashion; it was a mark — a willingness to live before men wearing the sign "I am keeping a vow," unafraid of being seen and unafraid of being asked. The dead face affection: this is the hardest line — not that he is not to love father and mother, but that in this fixed stretch of days the belonging is so absolute that even filial duty must wait its turn. When the three lines are drawn, this man's whole shape of life is rewritten by the vow — in eating, in appearance, in relationships. That is the weight of "separation": not hiding in the hills, but living, in the middle of ordinary life, a shape distinct at every point. People today vow and consecrate, and commonly consent to be separate only where it does not hurt: not disturbing enjoyment, not costing respectability, not touching affection. The Nazarite shows us another kind of separation: the most painful places are exactly where separation is most real — enjoyment laid down, respectability surrendered, even the great matters of kinship made to yield — and only then has the vow reached the root.

(Application) A fair word must also be said for verse 7, lest hearers read it as coldness. The text does not bid him not love his parents, nor bid him keep away from them while they live; it says that within this fixed stretch of separated days, if near kin should die, he may not break the vow for the funeral. The stretch is limited; when the vow is fulfilled he may go home and render all honour at his parents' grave. What the law shows here is not heartlessness but a sense of order: within these days, the vow to God stands first, and every other rightful duty waits behind it. To today's hearers this is a thoroughly practical lesson: most of us suffer not from a lack of rightful duties, but from a surplus of them — every one rightful, and every one usable as a reason to postpone belonging to God. The Nazarite regulation reminds us coldly: there is a stretch of days in which God's matter must rank before every rightful matter; if the ranking holds even before a parent's funeral, then we know the words are true.

### III. 24:00-42:00 A man dies very suddenly: beginning again; and the days fulfilled: the whole list offered

(Fact) The most human passage of the chapter is its regulation for accidents (vv. 9-12). If any man dies very suddenly by him — mark the "very suddenly": it is not that he went near a dead body, but that a dead body came near him; not that he broke the prohibition, but that an accident reached him — and he has defiled the head of his consecration, he shaves his head in the day of his cleansing, on the seventh day (v. 9, Summary; the Chinese base text's punctuation of this verse varying slightly between sources, the Chinese manuscript likewise gives it in Summary). On the eighth day the procedure stands thus: he brings two turtles, or two young pigeons, to the priest, to the door of the tabernacle of the congregation; the priest offers the one for a sin offering and the other for a burnt offering, makes an atonement for him for that he sinned by the dead, and hallows his head that same day (vv. 10-11, Summary). Count this step's offerings item by item, as this sermon has done: two birds — turtles or young pigeons, a pair made up from either kind; and the two birds divide by offering, one for the sin offering and one for the burnt offering. Then comes the new beginning, in the KJV: "And he shall consecrate unto the LORD the days of his separation, and shall bring a lamb of the first year for a trespass offering: but the days that were before shall be lost, because his separation was defiled." (Num 6:12, KJV) Three figures and facts in this verse must be laid out plainly. First, the trespass offering's lamb is one — of the first year, a male lamb. Second, the cleansing's sequence is the seventh day for the shaving and the eighth for the birds, two days in succession, never confused. Third — and the verse that sinks the heart — "the days that were before shall be lost": the days he has already kept, be they ten or ninety, are struck off, neither credited nor reserved; he counts from the beginning. The accident was not his fault, yet the cost is wholly his: the law here makes no concession to feelings; it only keeps holiness's account with perfect clarity.

(Fact) When the days are at last fulfilled, the completion regulation begins at verse 13, its charter in the KJV: "And this is the law of the Nazarite, when the days of his separation are fulfilled: he shall be brought unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation:" (Num 6:13, KJV) The items follow (vv. 14-15, Summary), copied out and counted item by item in preparing this sermon: he offers his offering unto the LORD — one he lamb of the first year without blemish for a burnt offering; one ewe lamb of the first year without blemish for a sin offering; one ram without blemish for peace offerings; and a basket of unleavened bread — cakes of fine flour mingled with oil, and wafers of unleavened bread anointed with oil; and their meat offering, and their drink offerings. Count the completion list through: the animals are three in all — he lamb, ewe lamb, and ram, one each, each to its own offering, burnt offering, sin offering, peace offerings; all three carry the specification "without blemish," and the first two the further specification "of the first year"; the bread is one basket, containing two kinds — cakes of fine flour mingled with oil, and unleavened wafers anointed with oil; and the meat offering and drink offerings are accompanying offerings, offered together with theirs (the items and their sources are set out in Endnote 6). The priest's order of handling is: first he brings them before the LORD and offers the man's sin offering and burnt offering; then he offers the ram for a sacrifice of peace offerings with the basket of unleavened bread, offering also its meat offering and drink offering (vv. 16-17, Summary). Then comes the chapter's most moving scene, in the KJV: "And the Nazarite shall shave the head of his separation at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, and shall take the hair of the head of his separation, and put it in the fire which is under the sacrifice of the peace offerings." (Num 6:18, KJV) The long hair kept through the whole stretch of days, the locks that were on his head as token and as crown, are shaved off at the door of the tabernacle — not kept as a souvenir, not carried home, but put on the fire of the peace offerings and burned: the token of his separation becomes, at the last, itself an offering to God.

(Fact) The completion procedure's second half (vv. 19-20, Summary): after his shaving, the priest takes the sodden shoulder of the ram, and one unleavened cake out of the basket, and one unleavened wafer, and puts them upon the hands of the Nazarite; the priest waves them for a wave offering before the LORD, and this is holy for the priest, with the wave breast and the heave shoulder. Count the things laid in his hands item by item, as this sermon has done: the sodden shoulder of the ram, one; one unleavened cake, one; one unleavened wafer, one — three things in all, waved through his hands, and then the priest's. And then, at verse 20's end, stands the sense of the sentence the whole chapter has waited for longest: after that, the Nazarite may drink wine. The vine was refused through the whole stretch of days; the first cup of wine returns to his hand only when the whole regulation has been walked through — head shaved, offerings offered, wave offering waved. Verse 21 gathers the whole passage up (Summary): this is the law of the Nazarite who has vowed, and of his offering unto the LORD for his separation, beside that which his hand shall get — beyond the regulation, whatever more he is able to add, he may add; according to the vow which he vowed, so he must do after the law of his separation.

(Application) "The days that were before shall be lost" is the sentence in this chapter that most sinks the heart, and the one that most needs preaching through. A man has kept eighty days; on the eighty-first, a man falls dead suddenly beside him — not his fault — and the eighty days are void. Let his grievance first be spoken aloud for him: the text does not dodge this hardship; it writes the "very suddenly" down in so many words — the thing came suddenly, and was not of his seeking. Yet the regulation's handling has no vagueness: shave, offer, atone, begin again. Why? Because what this vow keeps is not a score but a whole stretch of days belonging to the LORD; a stretch defiled by death cannot be repaired by proportion; it can only be repaired by a new beginning. Holiness's account is never kept by percentages. But look at the handling's other face: for all its strictness, the regulation does not cast the man out — the seventh day has its shaving, the eighth day its birds, the trespass its one lamb at a stated price, and the road of beginning again is laid out with perfect clarity. God did not say, "Your vow is finished"; He said, "Your vow shall be counted again." To those today whose work has been undone by accident, by other men's affairs, by circumstances beyond all control, this passage is a great comfort: what is lost is the former days, not the man himself; days can be counted again, a vow can be taken again, a head can be hallowed anew. The cost of falling is real; and the door of beginning again stands really open.

(Application) The completion scene must also be preached slowly, for those who keep vows. The hair on his head was the most precious thing of his whole stretch of days: his token, his crown, the visible sign of his holiness — and on the day of fulfilment he shaves it off with his own hand and puts it on the fire. There is a deep lesson here: not even the sign of separation itself may be kept as one's own glory. How many, having kept a stretch of consecrated days, make those days into a medal and wear it on the breast for men to see — how I fasted, how I watched, how I offered. The Nazarite burns his token: the evidence of the kept vow is not carried away, the souvenir of consecration is not preserved; the fire burns, and all is clean — as if to say: these days were His, and even the evidence that they were His is His, and not mine. Then he drinks the first cup of wine, and returns to ordinary days, the ordinary table, the ordinary crowd — the end of separation is not separation forever, but a return into the midst of the common world carrying a stretch of days once set apart, a man changed by the days he kept. To be able to keep separation, and able also to lay separation down; to be able to vow greatly, and able to burn the token — that is the whole lesson this vow teaches us.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The priestly blessing: in my name, for Israel

(Fact) The chapter's second half turns with complete abruptness: verse 21 has just gathered up the Nazarite's law, and at verse 22 the LORD speaks to Moses and begins again — and the actors this time are not the vowing commoner but Aaron and his sons. The command stands in the KJV: "Speak unto Aaron and unto his sons, saying, On this wise ye shall bless the children of Israel, saying unto them," (Num 6:23, KJV) Then the blessing's own text, in three movements, set out movement by movement in the KJV, with nothing altered: the first, "The LORD bless thee, and keep thee:" (Num 6:24, KJV) The second, "The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee:" (Num 6:25, KJV) The third, "The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace." (Num 6:26, KJV) The punctuation follows the KJV likewise: the first two movements close with colons, the third with a full stop. Now count the structure plainly, item by item, as this sermon has done: three movements, each with two verbal actions — bless and keep; make His face shine and be gracious; lift up His countenance and give peace — six actions in all, every one with the LORD as its subject, and every one addressed to the singular "thee." This is no priest wishing the people well in fine words: of the six sentences, not one names a thing a priest can do — the blessing is the LORD's, the keeping is the LORD's, the face shining is the LORD's, the grace and the peace are the LORD's; the priest's office is to speak to the people, word for word, the words the LORD Himself has taught him.

(Fact) The structure carries three layers of deepening besides, to be laid out one by one. The first movement (v. 24) speaks of provision and protection: "bless thee" — He gives thee good; "keep thee" — He holds harm away from thee: one giving and one guarding make blessing's outermost layer. The second movement (v. 25) enters into relation: "make his face shine upon thee" — the face is the Old Testament's habitual word for God's presence and favour: His face shining on thee is not His giving thee good from far away; it is His countenance turned toward thee, receiving thee; and "be gracious unto thee" names this shining's concrete content — grace. The third movement (v. 26) enters one layer deeper: "lift up his countenance upon thee" — He raises His face to look on thee with intent attention, as an elder lifts his face to gaze at the child he bends over; and the fruit is "give thee peace" — and peace, šālôm (Strong's H7965), in the Hebrew is not merely the absence of war; it is wholeness, soundness, everything well, a complete good. The three movements walk from gifts to the face, and from the face to peace: God first gives good things, then gives Himself, and last gives a life lit by His countenance. The gathering verse stands in the KJV: "And they shall put my name upon the children of Israel, and I will bless them." (Num 6:27, KJV) Mark this verse's two blessings: the priests bless the children of Israel, and the LORD says, "I will bless them" — man's blessing first, and God's blessing after it; the priest's mouth opens in the Name, and God pledges Himself to accomplish it. The KJV's wording here keeps the Hebrew image: blessing is not the art of speaking; it is the putting of God's name upon a man; and the man on whom the Name has been put is thenceforth a man under that Name. (On a difference between the two base texts at this verse, see Endnote 9.)

(Fact) Two of the blessing's word-anchors were checked for this sermon, and are set out one by one. First, the verb of "bless" throughout is bārak (Strong's H1288): verse 24's "bless thee," and verse 27's blessing of Israel by the priests and the LORD's own "I will bless them," are forms of this one verb, recurring through the four verses — the bārak spoken by men in the Name, and the bārak pledged by God, are the same word: the words the priest speaks and the thing God accomplishes share one verb. Second, "peace" is šālôm (H7965), already set out above. Both pairings of word and number were checked one by one (see Endnote 10). And one further correspondence is worth marking: in the chapter's first half, the Nazarite has nēzer upon his head — the token of belonging to God set upon him; in its second half, God puts His own name upon the children of Israel. In the first half, one man of his own will places himself under God's name; in the second, God of His own initiative places His name upon all the people: one vow and one blessing state the two ends of the holy life between them — man walks one step toward God, and what God says toward man is a whole blessing.

(Application) This blessing is perhaps the most-heard benediction at the close of worship in Chinese churches; precisely because it is so familiar, its weight must be preached afresh. First, its words are taught by God, not composed by men: Moses is not told to have Aaron "think of some fine words" with which to bless the people; the LORD fixes every word and teaches it to the priests — "On this wise ye shall bless the children of Israel, saying unto them." The priest has no authority to alter a word; he has only the office of conveying them. To all who preach and all who bless today, this is a standing reminder: the weight of blessing-words lies never in their eloquence but in their source — in whether the sentence is one you thought of, or one He spoke. Second, its pledge bears God's own signature: the priest finishes speaking, and God says, "and I will bless them" — the priest's mouth is the channel; the blessing's author, from first to last, is God Himself. This passage is called "the priestly blessing," but more exactly it is God's blessing by the priest's mouth. Third, its scope is the whole people: the Nazarite vow was taken by individuals; this blessing is spoken to "the children of Israel" entire — the commoner's voluntary door in the chapter's first half and the blessing in which all have a share in its second half answer one another: a separated life unto the LORD may be lived by few, but the light of God's countenance is to shine on everyone who is His. The vow belongs to the few; the blessing belongs to all — and that is why this chapter binds the two together.

(Application) Mark, too, the deepening face within the blessing. The text does not stop at "bless thee, and keep thee" — and that would already be good enough: a man walking in the wilderness, with provision and protection, what lack has he? But God's words do not stop there: He will "make his face shine upon thee." A man may receive a king's bounty and never in his life see the king's face; what Israel receives is not bounty only — it is the face, His countenance toward them. That means that in God's own heart, blessing's summit is not things but relation; not what He gives thee, but that He is toward thee. And the third movement drives the relation to its most intimate point: "lift up his countenance upon thee" — He looks on thee with intent attention, as on a thing He treasures; and peace flows out of that being-looked-upon. Men today seek peace mostly as circumstances without storms; the Bible's peace comes from a face: His face shines upon thee, His countenance is lifted up toward thee — the storm may still blow, and the peace is already there, because the peace is not that the storm has stopped; it is that His face is toward thee. Before the preacher hands this blessing to the congregation, let him stand a while himself in the light of that face: what you convey is not six sentences of good wishes — it is a face, turned toward men.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: the vow has its fulfilment; the blessing has no end

(Fact) The whole chapter runs from "When either man or woman shall separate themselves" to "and I will bless them": its first twenty-one verses are one vow's law, its last six verses one blessing. Its three lines can now be gathered. First, the vow is voluntary, for a term, and solemn: man and woman alike may take it (v. 2), and what is taken is a separation unto the LORD (vv. 2, 5, 6, 12); its three prohibitions can be counted item by item — nothing made of the vine tree, from the kernels even to the husk, may be eaten (vv. 3-4), no razor may come upon his head all the days of the vow (v. 5), and no dead body may be come at, not even near kin's (vv. 6-7) — gathered up in one sentence, "All the days of his separation he is holy unto the LORD" (v. 8). Second, the vow's road is reckoned with perfect clarity: defiled very suddenly, he shaves on the seventh day and offers two birds on the eighth, one for a sin offering and one for a burnt offering, and brings one lamb of the first year for a trespass offering, and the days before are lost and a new stretch chosen (vv. 9-12); the days fulfilled, three animals without blemish, one basket with two kinds of unleavened bread, and the meat offering and drink offerings are offered in full, the hair of his separation is put in the fire under the peace offerings, and after that he may drink wine (vv. 13-20); beyond the law, he adds according to what his hand shall get, doing according to the vow he vowed (v. 21). Third, the blessing is God's fixed words and God's signed pledge: three movements, six actions, every subject the LORD, every object the singular "thee" (vv. 24-26); the priest's office is to put God's name upon Israel in blessing, and God's pledge is "and I will bless them" (v. 27). The vow has its head and its tail, its fulfilment and its burning in fire; and the One who finished speaking the vow turns His face and teaches the words of blessing — the story of separation unto the LORD ends, at the last, upon words of blessing.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, to be separated unto the LORD is to hand over a whole stretch of days: not to leave God a small corner inside one's life, but to let Him fill, for that stretch, the eating and the appearance and the relationships — the more concrete the handing over, the more real the vow. Second, the days of a kept vow must be reckoned clearly, and laid down freely: defiled, the days are lost and counted again, with no protesting of accounts and no discounting; fulfilled, the token is burned in the fire, not kept as a medal and not carried home — able to begin again, and able to let go, and the vow is kept clean. Third, the end of all separation is to hear His blessing: for however many days thou hast separated for Him, not one word of what He has prepared for thee is lacking — bless thee and keep thee; His face to shine upon thee and grace unto thee; His countenance lifted up upon thee, and peace given thee. May the vows we vow not fall empty, and the days we keep not be in vain; and may every one of us find his own name beneath this blessing that came out of God's own mouth — for that Name has been put upon us, in His name.

(New Testament reading) Two New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. The first is at Revelation's end: there it is said that His servants shall serve Him, and they shall see His face, and His name shall be in their foreheads (Rev 22:3-4, Summary) — this chapter's putting of God's name upon Israel in blessing, and its prayer that His face shine upon them, answer in the New Testament reading to that scene: on one side, the Name put on in blessing and the face besought to shine; on the other, the face seen with one's own eyes and the name in the forehead; and between the two stands the One who Himself became the blessing. The second is Acts 21: there four men are recorded who have a vow on them, and Paul takes them, purifies himself with them, and pays their charges, that they may shave their heads (Acts 21:23-24, Summary) — the ordering of vow-keeping and purification after the Nazarite pattern was still alive in the Jerusalem of the New Testament; this sermon states the correspondence of that record with this chapter's regulations in the matters of shaving and purification, and does not read the sense of one back into the other. (Application) But the layers must be kept straight: Numbers 6 is first of all itself — a vine prohibition countable item by item, locks grown on a head into a token, a cleansing procedure of a seventh day and an eighth, a blessing quotable word by word in three movements; the New Testament reading is the light in which the church, in Christ, looks back on it, and must not be used to dissolve this chapter's regulations. The more solid this chapter's regulations — the prohibition countable verse by verse, the offerings countable animal by animal, the blessing quotable word by word — the more weight the reading layer's correspondence carries: the blessing once put upon the people in the Name has, in the face of that One, been lifted up toward us at last.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's own hands. You may never have thought of separating a stretch of days for God — to you this chapter says first: the door stands open; man or woman, the vow is open to you; to separate a stretch of days for Him is not the privilege of spiritual giants, but a vow common Israelites could take. You may be midway through a vow when an accident has suddenly come, and the former days look lost — to you this chapter says: what is lost is the days, not you; shave on the seventh day, offer on the eighth, count from the beginning — that road He has already laid out for you. You may have kept a vow, and fulfilled it — to you this chapter gives its reminder: burn the token; do not make consecration into a medal; return to the ordinary table and the ordinary crowd, a man changed by the days he kept. And last, may we, when we are dismissed, not merely hear a familiar blessing, but hear afresh the Lord who taught these words speak them Himself: they shall put my name upon the children of Israel, and I will bless them.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 6 first pressed me with its list of prohibitions: he shall eat nothing that is made of the vine tree, from the kernels even to the husk; no razor shall come upon his head all the days of his separation; and he shall not make himself unclean even for his father or his mother, for his brother or his sister, when they die. I asked myself: what have I ever set apart for God? My separations always keep a kernel and a husk of reserve. But the chapter does not stop at the prohibitions. It tells me that when a man dies very suddenly by him and the days that were before are lost, God leaves a way to begin again; and when the days are fulfilled, the consecration on his head is shaved off and put in the fire under the sacrifice of the peace offerings, not kept as his own glory. Then the whole chapter turns, and God Himself teaches the blessing: "The LORD bless thee, and keep thee: The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee: The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace." The days I separate for Him are limited, dated, and soon fulfilled; the blessing He gives me has no list of conditions — only His own face. So I pray today: Lord, teach me to give Thee a whole stretch of days, keeping back no kernel and no husk; and teach me not to lose heart when I fail and must begin again, because Thou hast left a way to begin again. Let Thy face shine upon me, and let Thy name rest upon me, and I shall have peace.

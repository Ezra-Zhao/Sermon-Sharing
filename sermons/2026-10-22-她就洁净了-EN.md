# And She Shall Be Clean: A Sermon on Leviticus 12

**Text**: Leviticus 12:1–8 (KJV)
**Author**: Written by Guangyi Zhao, based on the biblical text and commentaries
**Status**: Draft · Pending cross-review (sermon production batch two, 2026-10-22; unpublished)

**Translation note**: First translation, pending proofreading （初译待校）; translated from the Chinese original

> **Abstract**: Leviticus 12 (eight verses) is the second unit of the purity laws (chs. 11–15), prescribing the mother's purification after childbirth: forty days for a son, eighty for a daughter. This sermon expounds the ritual nature of "uncleanness," the precise limits of the "blood of her purifying," the theology of a sinless woman offering a chattat ("purification offering"), the scholarly proposals on the doubled days for a daughter, and — by way of the poor woman's "two birds" clause — the offering of Mary in Luke 2. The chapter's destination is its closing word: "she shall be clean."

## Sermon Text

### 1. Introduction: Among the Shortest Chapters in the Book (00:00–08:00)

Leviticus 12 has only eight verses. Among the twenty-seven chapters of Leviticus, it stands alongside the very shortest: the fewest words, the most economical speech. Yet what it deals with is not small at all.

First, let us fix its location. **(Fact)** Leviticus 11–15 forms a single whole, usually called the purity laws: chapter 11 concerns clean and unclean food — what may be eaten and what may not; chapter 12, a woman's childbirth; chapters 13–14, skin diseases (the Hebrew צָרַעַת covers a far wider range than the modern medical category of leprosy; the Chinese Union Version renders it "leprosy"); chapter 15, bodily discharges. Chapter 12 is the second unit of this group, immediately after the food laws and immediately before the skin-disease laws. Before it, chapters 1–10 contain the sacrificial laws and the priestly laws; after the purity laws, chapter 16 is the book's summit — the Day of Atonement. Chapter 12 therefore stands at a very specific place: it deals with the most ordinary and most intimate event in Israelite life — a child has been added to the household, and how does the mother come back before God?

The whole chapter treats this one matter. No story, no personal names, no dialogue. Just one regulation, eight verses from beginning to end. Let me lay out its skeleton first, so that we have a map in mind: a woman who bears a son is unclean seven days; on the eighth day the boy is circumcised; the mother then keeps thirty-three days of "the blood of her purifying" — forty days in all — and at their fulfillment offers sacrifice. If she bears a daughter: unclean fourteen days, sixty-six days of purifying — eighty days in all — and then the offering. The offerings are: a year-old lamb for a burnt offering, and a young pigeon or a turtledove for a sin offering; if she cannot afford a lamb, two birds take its place, one for the burnt offering and one for the sin offering. The priest makes atonement for her, and the closing word is a single sentence — "she shall be clean."

Now the question, and it is a sharp one: why should a woman who has just given birth be called "unclean"? To modern ears the word is almost an insult. Childbirth is joy; it is blessing. Psalm 127 says children are a heritage from the LORD, and the fruit of the womb a reward from Him. How is it that, in the next breath, the mother who has borne the pain of labor becomes the "unclean" one, counting days, barred even from the sanctuary?

To answer this, we must first be clear about what "unclean" means in Leviticus. This is the foundation of today's sermon. If the foundation is laid crooked, everything after it goes wrong.

**(Reflection)** Let me put the conclusion first: in Leviticus 11–15, "uncleanness" is a ritual category, not a moral verdict. It does not mean guilty; it does not mean God is displeased; still less does it mean women are demeaned. This conclusion is not reached to spare the feelings of a modern audience; the text itself will prove it. Let us go through it passage by passage and see how the text sets this conclusion on its feet.

### 2. Days and Boundaries: Forty Days and Eighty Days (08:00–24:00)

Verse 2: "Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, If a woman have conceived seed, and born a man child: then she shall be unclean seven days; according to the days of the separation for her infirmity shall she be unclean" (12:2, KJV). "The woman who has given birth" — the Hebrew is יֹלֶדֶת (yoledet), the participle of the verb יָלַד (yalad, "to bear, give birth," H3205), literally "a woman in childbirth, a woman who has just delivered." Notice the tone of the text here: it does not praise her first, and it does not blame her first; it simply gives her a name drawn from what has just happened to her. She is a yoledet; she has just borne a child.

"Then she shall be unclean" (וְטָמְאָה, vetam'ah) — the verb טָמֵא (tame', H2930), "to be unclean." This is the keyword of Leviticus 11–15, occurring more than a hundred times in the book. The key lies in the comparison that follows: "according to the days of the separation for her infirmity" — נִדַּת דְּוֹתָהּ (nidat davotah), "the days of her menstrual impurity." The text hands us a ready-made coordinate: the impurity of the first seven days after childbirth is of the same kind as menstrual impurity. **(Fact)** Menstruation is not a sin. Leviticus 15 places menstruation among the impurities of "discharge" (15:19–24) — a cyclical, involuntary bodily process — without one syllable about wrongdoing. By comparing postpartum impurity to menstrual impurity, the text tells us from the outset: this impurity has nothing to do with morality.

Hold that ruler steady, and the logic of the whole purity system comes into view. **(Fact)** In Leviticus 11–15, most of the things that render a person unclean have nothing to do with the will: touching the carcass of an animal (ch. 11); a man's nocturnal emission (15:16–17 — "he shall be unclean until the even," one night only); and Numbers 19 adds that touching a corpse makes one unclean for seven days — a son who buries his own father, performing the greatest act of filial devotion, must be unclean seven days. What sin has he committed? None at all. So tame' describes a ritual distance between a person and the sanctuary: in what states a person may not, for the time being, approach the holy things. It registers a state, not an offense; it measures distance, not character. To misread "ritual impurity" as "moral guilt" is not merely to misread Leviticus 12; it is to read the entire purity system backwards.

Verse 3: "And in the eighth day the flesh of his foreskin shall be circumcised" (12:3). The verb is מוּל (mul, H4135), "to circumcise." This sentence takes us back to Genesis 17: "And he that is eight days old shall be circumcised among you, every man child in your generations, he that is born in the house, or bought with money of any stranger, which is not of thy seed" (Gen 17:12, KJV). **(Fact)** Two procedures run in parallel here: on one side, the boy is circumcised on the eighth day and enters the covenant; on the other, the mother — on that same eighth day her seven days of impurity are just complete, and she passes into the blood of her purifying, with thirty-three days still to go. The sign of the covenant does not stop and wait for the mother; the mother's restoration is not shortened by a single day on account of the circumcision. Each proceeds on its own track, each in its own time. There is a quiet orderliness in this juxtaposition: the new life is received into the covenant without the mother having first to "qualify"; and the mother returns to the sanctuary without needing the child as her passport.

Verse 4 is the verse most easily misread in the whole chapter, and it must be read precisely: "And she shall then continue in the blood of her purifying three and thirty days; she shall touch no hallowed thing, nor come into the sanctuary, until the days of her purifying be fulfilled" (12:4, KJV). "The blood of her purifying" — the Hebrew is דָּם־טָהֳרָה (dam tohar), "blood of purification," literally "clean blood." Notice: these thirty-three days are of an entirely different nature from the first seven. The first seven days are impurity "as in the days of her separation": according to Leviticus 15, menstrual impurity is contagious — whoever touches her, whatever she sits or lies on, whoever touches those things, is unclean until evening (15:19–24). But in these thirty-three days, the blood is called by the text itself "blood of purification" — which means that the blood of this phase no longer communicates impurity through ordinary contact. **(Fact)** The restrictions of these thirty-three days can be counted on two fingers: she shall touch no hallowed thing, and she shall not come into the sanctuary. There is no third. She is not on a quarantine list. Hartley calls this period an "intermediate state": one step below full cleanness — she may not yet enter the sanctuary; one step above contagious impurity — her daily life goes on entirely as normal.

**(Reflection)** God's system shows a fine delicacy here. In the first seven days after childbirth, the mother is placed in the most complete rest, her hands free of everything. In the thirty-three days that follow, she returns to the midst of ordinary life: she may hold her child, cook the meals, sit at the table with her family — only she does not yet enter the sanctuary or touch the holy things. The regulation does not drive her outside the camp — compare chapter 13, where the person with a skin disease must dwell alone outside the camp (13:46); the new mother does not. She is kept at home, kept at the table, kept beside her child. For her, "uncleanness" comes down to this: one place she enters less, one class of things she touches less, and thirty-three more days of rest. This is not exile; it is rest with a fence around it.

Verse 5: "But if she bear a maid child, then she shall be unclean two weeks, as in her separation: and she shall continue in the blood of her purifying threescore and six days" (12:5, KJV). Fourteen days, sixty-six days, eighty in all — exactly double the son's. **(Fact)** The structure of the numbers is not hard to see: seven is Leviticus' base unit, from the seven days of creation; for a son it is seven plus thirty-three, totaling forty; for a daughter, fourteen plus sixty-six, totaling eighty — every segment exactly doubled. Wenham reminds us that numbers in Leviticus often carry a symbolic use: sevens, and multiples of seven, recur throughout the purity laws as an ordered language. As for the total of forty — forty days and nights of the flood, Moses forty days on the mountain, Israel forty years in the wilderness — Bible readers will not find it unfamiliar. But let me say this with restraint: the text does not tell us why forty and eighty, and still less why the daughter's days are doubled. On the reason for the doubling, the text is silent. And where the text is silent, the sermon must not rush to speak. The "why" of this passage we will take up in the next section, laying out the scholarly proposals one by one, each with its own grounds and its own limits.
### 3. A "Sin Offering" Without Sin, and the Doubled Days (24:00–42:00)

Before we reach verse 6, we must face one problem head-on, because it is the greatest theological stumbling block of the chapter: when the mother's days are fulfilled and she offers sacrifice, the list of offerings contains — plainly — a "sin offering" (so the KJV and the Chinese Union Version alike). She has not stolen, robbed, lied, or worshiped idols — she has only borne a child. What "sin offering" is she offering, exactly?

Let us set the original wording out clearly first. The offering's name is חַטָּאת (chattat), derived from the root for "sin," which is why the traditional rendering is "sin offering." But **(Fact)** nowhere in Leviticus 12 is any sin attributed to the mother. There is no "if she has committed…," no "if she has sinned unintentionally…," no confession, no pronouncement of forgiveness. The same offering-name, in chapters 4 and 5, sits in a completely different setting: chapter 4 treats "sinning unintentionally" (שְׁגָגָה, shegagah, 4:2) — doing unwittingly one of the things the LORD commanded not to be done; chapter 5 treats specific debts and oaths. Every time, the offense is written out plainly. In chapter 12, not one word.

**(Scholarly discussion)** In his Anchor Bible commentary on Leviticus, Milgrom proposed an influential rereading of chattat: this offering should be translated "purification offering" (or "decontamination offering"), not "sin offering." His chain of argument runs roughly as follows. First, the blood of the chattat, in its actual ritual function, is applied to the altar and the furnishings of the sanctuary to decontaminate them — clearest of all on the Day of Atonement in chapter 16, where what the chattat cleanses is precisely the sanctuary, the tent, and the altar themselves, because they abide in the midst of the uncleanness of the children of Israel (16:16). If the object cleansed is the sanctuary, then what this offering deals with is the contamination of impurity, not moral guilt. Second, the postpartum mother is not the only person in Leviticus who offers a chattat without having committed any sin: the person healed of a skin disease (14:19) and the person healed of a discharge (15:15, 30) offer the same sacrifice. Set the three passages side by side and the pattern emerges: the chattat is the purity system's "decontamination procedure"; whoever comes out of a state of impurity passes through it. On this reading, the chattat of chapter 12 is not a problem at all; it fits the system seamlessly.

The grounds for the traditional rendering must also be laid out, to be fair. The root of chattat is, after all, "sin"; and the verb used for what is done for her in chapter 12, כִּפֶּר (kipper), "to make atonement," is in chapters 4–5 entirely about real sins. So the traditional reading says: the function of kipper was never narrow to begin with — it covers both forgiveness for sin and cleansing from impurity, both being the restoration of a person's relation with God to what it should be. What the two readings are really disputing is how wide an umbrella kipper holds. This sermon adopts "purification offering" as the main rendering, with the traditional "sin offering" noted alongside, because the context of chapter 12 — no offense named, a fixed term of days, and a closing word of "clean" rather than "forgiven" — supports Milgrom's reading better. But let me say plainly: this is no settled conclusion. The scholarly discussion continues; we present it, we do not adjudicate it.

**(Reflection)** Whichever rendering is adopted, there is one theological conclusion both readings share, and I ask you to hold it fast: childbirth is not a sin, and God is not punishing the mother. The chattat here is a rite of restoration, not a document of condemnation. Anyone who takes the words "sin offering" and goes home to judge that a woman "sins by bearing a child" has misread not the Greek, but Leviticus.

Before we come to the doubling, one further thread from Wenham deserves inclusion. **(Scholarly discussion)** In Leviticus' symbolic world, cleanness is bound up with life, and uncleanness with the sphere of death: the sanctuary is the dwelling of the God who gives life, and so every state that carries the breath of death — corpses, decay, the loss of blood in menstruation and childbirth — must keep its distance from the sanctuary for a time. Childbirth falls exactly on this line: at the very moment new life is born, the mother loses blood and her body undergoes a real depletion. The regulation keeps her at a distance for a season of rest, not because life is detestable — quite the opposite: because the sanctuary is the source of life, the depleted person is first to be replenished in rest. This thread can explain why the purity corpus binds together food, childbirth, disease, and discharges; but it remains an interpretive framework, not the text's own statement, and when a sermon cites it, its level must be marked, and it must not be preached as the text's own doctrine.

Now the doubled days. Eighty days for a daughter, forty for a son — why? **(Scholarly discussion)** The text gives no reason. The scholarly proposals are mainly three, and I will lay them out one by one, each with its limits of evidence noted.

The first proposal: ancient physiological ideas. Scholars have pointed out that medical literature from the ancient Mediterranean world — such as the Hippocratic writings on postpartum discharge — held that after the birth of a girl, the mother's lochia (postpartum bleeding) lasted longer than after the birth of a boy. If the legislation absorbed the physiological observation of its day, the doubling would have an empirical source. The limit of this proposal: at most it proves that such an idea circulated in the ancient world; it cannot prove that the legislator of Leviticus legislated on this basis. Greek medicine and the formation of the Pentateuch are far apart in background, and no direct documentary chain exists.

The second proposal: Milgrom's inference. In his commentary he suggests that the daughter, when she grows up, will herself become a source of ritual impurity — her menstruation (15:19–24) and her own childbearing (chapter 12 itself). The doubled days the mother keeps for her are, as it were, an "advance payment" of the impurity this daughter will one day bring into the sanctuary's order — hence double. The limit of this proposal: it is Milgrom's own construction; the text does not state it in a single word; and within the purity system of Leviticus there is no parallel instance of "prepaying" a purification period for someone else's future impurity. It explains elegantly, but its evidence is indirect.

The third proposal: custom and structure. The Wenham line of reading tends to treat the doubling as symbolic language: within Leviticus' numerical system, intensification is often expressed by doubling (seven, fourteen, forty, eighty, rising step by step); others hold that it reflects the duration-structure of childbirth customs somewhere in the ancient Near East. The limit of this proposal: the persuasive force of symbolic interpretation lies in the coherence of the system, and its weakness is that it cannot be falsified; the custom proposal lacks direct documentary support.

With the three proposals laid out, my conclusion is a single sentence: where the text is silent, the sermon is silent too — to display the proposals honestly is integrity; to select one and preach it as the text's meaning is overreach. **(Reflection)** There is a popular moralizing reading that interprets the doubling as evidence of "female original sin" or "female second-class status," as though God used a double number of days to stamp the baby girl as inferior. This reading errs by substitution: it swaps the ritual category for a moral one, and the whole system of chapters 11–15 — the son who buries a corpse, the man with a nocturnal emission, the woman with an issue of blood — will not sustain that swap at a single point. Conversely, the opposite reading, which rewrites the text from a modern standpoint and declares the regulation itself discriminatory, is likewise not exegesis but standpoint first. Leviticus 12 is an ancient purity regulation; it prescribes the schedule of sanctuary access, not a ranking of women's worth. On this point, both sides must exercise restraint.

Let me bring this section back to the mother herself. **(Reflection)** The most moving thing in this chapter is that God takes the most fragile stretch of a woman's life — just delivered, her body bleeding, no unbroken sleep at night, her hormones ebbing — and writes it solemnly into a law code concerning His own sanctuary. The tone of the regulation is not "You are dirty; keep away from Me," but "Rest now; I am counting the days; when they are fulfilled, come back, and I will receive you." Uncleanness is a temporary state; cleanness is the appointed end. Eight verses, beginning with "she shall be unclean" and ending with "she shall be clean" — that trajectory is the gospel skeleton of chapter 12.

### 4. Offering and Return: Two Birds and Mary (42:00–56:00)

Verse 6: "And when the days of her purifying are fulfilled, for a son, or for a daughter, she shall bring a lamb of the first year for a burnt offering, and a young pigeon, or a turtledove, for a sin offering, unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, unto the priest" (12:6, KJV). There are two offerings: a burnt offering (עֹלָה, olah) — a year-old lamb, wholly burned and consecrated to God as holy; according to the regulation of chapter 1, the burnt offering is the offering of acceptance (1:3–4), carrying the offerer's wholehearted devotion; and a purification offering (chattat) — a young pigeon or a turtledove. It is worth noticing: the specification of the burnt offering is fixed — a lamb, a year old, no negotiation — while the purification offering is, from the very start, a bird: a dove or a turtledove, something within reach of an ordinary Israelite household. The mother comes back to the door of the tent carrying wholehearted devotion (the burnt offering) together with cleansing (the purification offering).

Verse 7: "Who shall offer it before the LORD, and make an atonement for her; and she shall be cleansed from the issue of her blood. This is the law for her that hath born a male or a female" (12:7, KJV). The KJV's "she shall be cleansed from the issue of her blood" renders the Hebrew וְטָהֵרָה מִמְּקֹר דָּמֶיהָ; translated directly, it reads "and she shall be clean, from the source of her blood" — and the words "she shall be clean" (vetaherah) are precisely this chapter's formulaic closing word (for details, see Note 9). "Make an atonement for her" (וְכִפֶּר עָלֶיהָ, vekhipper aleha) is Leviticus' formula sentence: the priest performs the kipper rite for her, and the outcome is the single sentence "she shall be clean." This construction recurs throughout chapters 4 and 5 — 4:20, 4:26, 4:31, 4:35; 5:10, 5:13, 5:16, 5:18. Read the formula sentences side by side, and the likeness and difference become clear. **(Fact)** In chapters 4–5, kipper treats unintentional sin and indebtedness, and the closing word of the sentence is most often "it shall be forgiven him" (וְנִסְלַח לוֹ, venislach lo); in chapter 12 there is no unintentional sin and no word "forgiven" — the closing word is "she shall be clean" (וְטָהֵרָה, vetaherah). One and the same verb produces "forgiveness" in the context of sin and "cleanness" in the context of impurity — the same word, two doors. The finer comparison I have placed in Note 9 at the end of the sermon; here only the conclusion stands: the kipper of chapter 12 is an atonement that removes ritual impurity and restores access to the sanctuary, not a pardon delivered from a judgment seat.

Verse 8 is where the warmth of the chapter lies: "And if she be not able to bring a lamb, then she shall bring two turtles, or two young pigeons; the one for the burnt offering, and the other for a sin offering: and the priest shall make an atonement for her, and she shall be clean" (12:8, KJV). "If she be not able" — the Hebrew puts it concretely: "if her hand does not find, does not reach, the measure of a lamb" (יָדָהּ דֵּי שֶׂה, yadah day seh; יָד yad is "hand," extended to "means, resources"; דֵּי day is "enough"). This arrangement is not unique in Leviticus: chapter 5, on the trespass offering, has exactly the same graduated clause — for those who cannot reach a lamb, two birds; for those who cannot reach even birds, a tenth of an ephah of fine flour (5:7, 5:11). In God's law code, the threshold of cleanness is adjusted downward according to the depth of the pocket. **(Reflection)** Now notice the closing word of verse 8: it is identical, word for word, with the lamb version of verse 7 — "she shall be clean." The woman with means comes with a lamb, and is clean; the poor woman comes with two birds, and is clean. Cleanness is not discounted. The offering is cheaper; the outcome is not. This is not God making do; this is God's decency: He charges the poor no admission fee.

More than a thousand years later, the two birds flew into the New Testament. Luke chapter 2: "And when the days of her purification according to the law of Moses were accomplished, they brought him to Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord… And to offer a sacrifice according to that which is said in the law of the Lord, A pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons" (Luke 2:22, 24, KJV).

Let us keep the levels distinct. **(NT intertext — narrative fact)** The ritual procedure Luke records matches Leviticus 12 item for item: the days of purification fulfilled, the journey up to Jerusalem, the offering; and the list of offerings he cites — "a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons" — is not the ordinary offering list; it is exactly the poor person's clause of Leviticus 12:8, in nearly its own words. This is narrative fact in Luke's account, and may be quoted as such. **(Inference)** From this, readers through the centuries have inferred that Mary and Joseph were at that time not well off, using the clause meant for the poor. The inference is very reasonable — a carpenter's household, no room at the inn, the child laid in a manger all bear witness alongside — but it must be labeled: it is an inference. Luke does not say outright "they were poor," and in preaching we may not present an inference as the fact of the text. An inference may be preached; its hat must sit straight.

**(Reading tradition)** Take one step further along this intertext, in a layer the Christian reading tradition has long held: Galatians 4 says that God sent forth His Son, "made of a woman, made under the law" (Gal 4:4, KJV). Chapter 12 is the earliest scene of that "under the law": the Son of God entered this world without bypassing the road prepared for the poor — His mother, like countless Israelite mothers, counted forty days, carried two birds, and stood at the door of the temple, while the priest performed the rite for her according to the same regulation and spoke the same closing word. The Son entered this world so completely that even His mother's cleansing after childbirth was walked through step by step, at the poor person's specification.

**(Reflection)** Finally, bring this chapter back among ourselves. In today's church there are not a few sisters sitting in postpartum weakness: bodies recovering, emotions rising and falling, waking countless times in the night. The lesson Leviticus 12 gives the church is a posture: learn to wait until the days are fulfilled. The system did not hurry the new mother to get better quickly; the system counted the days with her, walked with her to the door, and then said: you are clean, come back. If the church's first word is always "Why aren't you better yet?" — that is pressure, not pastoral care. In the posture of this chapter, it should be another sentence: "The days are not yet fulfilled; you just rest. When they are fulfilled, I will go back with you." At the door of the sanctuary God left a specification of two birds for the poor, and for the weak He left time — forty whole days, eighty whole days.
### 5. Conclusion: She Shall Be Clean (58:00–60:00)

Eight verses take two minutes to read aloud, and eighty days to count out. Let us fix our eyes at last on the closing word. "She shall be clean" — once in verse 7, once in verse 8. Leviticus 12 begins with "she shall be unclean" and ends with "she shall be clean"; what lies between is not her striving and effort, but the fulfilling of the days and the rite the priest performs for her at the door of the tent. She has done nothing for herself. She has only waited, waited until the days were full; then she came with her offering and stood at the door. Cleanness is declared, not earned.

**(Reflection)** Notice the order carefully: God did not wait for her first to recover completely, to rally, to prove herself qualified, before declaring her clean. The declaration and the restoration are two faces of one act here — or rather, the declaration itself completes the restoration. A mother walks through forty days, or eighty, and what she hears is not "Be careful next time," but a clean sentence with nothing trailing after it: "You are clean." The door of the sanctuary opens to her again; the holy things she may touch again; she returns into the midst of the worshiping congregation — everything as before, except that there is one more child at home.

Today, let us pray for mothers: for those in their month of confinement, for those who rise countless times in the postpartum night, for sisters whose bodies and emotions have not yet come back. And let us pray for every one of us — we have all stood at a door, feeling not yet qualified. Leviticus 12 tells us: the days are counted by God, the door is kept by God, and cleanness is declared by God. Forty days will pass; eighty days will pass; "she shall be clean" will not pass away.

## Notes

1. **יֹלֶדֶת (yoledet, "the woman who has given birth")** — the subject of 12:2. The Qal active participle, feminine singular, of the verb יָלַד (yalad, "to bear, give birth," H3205). The chapter's subject is never named, only identified by this role. Note also the opening תַזְרִיעַ (tazria, "she conceives seed") of 12:2, a rare expression with the woman as subject, literally "she causes seed to grow." See BDB and HALOT, s.v. ילד.
2. **טָמֵא (tame', H2930, "to be unclean")** — 12:2 וְטָמְאָה (vetam'ah). This root is the core vocabulary of the purity system in Leviticus 11–15, set over against its antonym root טָהֵר (taher, H2891, "to be clean"). It denotes a ritual state, not a moral evaluation: within the same system, uncleanness is contracted by contact with a corpse (Num 19:11–14), skin disease (Lev 13), and discharges (Lev 15), most of them unrelated to the person's will. See BDB and HALOT, s.v. טמא.
3. **נִדַּת דְּוֹתָהּ (nidat davotah, "the days of her menstrual impurity")** — 12:2. The construct form נִדַּת of נִדָּה (niddah, H5079, "impurity; menstrual uncleanness"), with the feminine possessive form of דָּוֶה (daveh, H1740, "unwell, menstruating"), from the root דוה ("to be ill, to menstruate"). The same combination occurs at 15:33 and 20:18. See BDB and HALOT, s.vv. נדה, דוה.
4. **דָּם־טָהֳרָה (dam tohar, "blood of purification")** — at 12:4 in the form בִּדְמֵי טָהֳרָה (bidmei taharah, "in the blood of her purifying"). דָּם (dam, "blood," H1818) + טֹהַר (tohar, "purity, purification," H2893). The blood of this phase is named by the text itself "blood of purification": unlike the first seven days, "as in her menstrual impurity" (12:2), it does not communicate impurity to those in ordinary contact with her (contrast the contagiousness of menstrual impurity at 15:19–24). The restrictions of 12:4 for this phase are exactly two: בְּכָל־קֹדֶשׁ לֹא־תִגָּע, "she shall touch no hallowed thing" (קֹדֶשׁ qodesh, "holy thing," H6944), and וְאֶל־הַמִּקְדָּשׁ לֹא תָבֹא, "nor come into the sanctuary" (מִקְדָּשׁ miqdash, H4720). The limits extend to holy things and the sanctuary only, not to daily life. Hartley calls this phase an "intermediate state." See BDB and HALOT, s.vv. טהר, קדשׁ, מקדשׁ; and Hartley's comment on 12:4.
5. **מוּל (mul, H4135, "to circumcise")** — 12:3 וּבַיּוֹם הַשְּׁמִינִי יִמּוֹל בְּשַׂר עָרְלָתוֹ ("and in the eighth day the flesh of his foreskin shall be circumcised"; עָרְלָה orlah, "foreskin," H6190). Intertext: Gen 17:12, "he that is eight days old shall be circumcised among you." The circumcision's date falls on the eighth day of the mother's purification schedule; the two procedures run in parallel and neither waits for the other. See BDB and HALOT, s.v. מול.
6. **חַטָּאת (chattat, H2403)** — 12:6, 12:8. Rendered "sin offering" in the KJV, the Chinese Union Version, and other traditional translations. Milgrom argues that by its function it should be rendered "purification offering" (decontamination offering): its blood serves to cleanse the sanctuary and its furnishings (cf. 16:16, 19, where on the Day of Atonement the sanctuary, tent, and altar are cleansed), dealing with the contamination of ritual impurity; those who offer it include the person healed of a skin disease (14:19) and the person healed of a discharge (15:15, 30), none of them on account of a sin committed. The traditional rendering rests on its root חטא ("sin") and on the use of כִּפֶּר in the atonement contexts of chs. 4–5. Chapter 12 attributes no sin to the mother; the two readings are presented side by side, without adjudication. See Milgrom, *Leviticus*, Introduction and comments on 12:6–8; BDB and HALOT, s.v. חטאת.
7. **עֹלָה (olah, H5930, "burnt offering")** — 12:6, 12:8. The burnt offering of 12:6 is a כֶּבֶשׂ בֶּן־שְׁנָתוֹ (keves ben-shenato, "a lamb of the first year"; כֶּבֶשׂ keves, "lamb," H3532). According to ch. 1, the burnt offering is wholly burned and renders the offerer accepted (1:3–4). In the poor person's clause of 12:8, birds replace the lamb: one for the burnt offering, one for the purification offering. See BDB and HALOT, s.v. עלה.
8. **תֹּר (tor, H8449, "turtledove") and בֶּן־יוֹנָה (ben yonah, "young pigeon")** — 12:6, 12:8. יוֹנָה (yonah, "dove," H3123) + בֵּן (ben, "son"). The two birds are the fixed offering for the purification sacrifice (cf. 5:7; 14:22; 15:14, 29). The "pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons" (ζεῦγος τρυγόνων ἢ δύο νοσσοὺς περιστερῶν) of Luke 2:24 is quoted from here. See BDB and HALOT, s.vv. תר, יונה.
9. **כִּפֶּר (kipper, H3722, "to make atonement")** — the formula sentence of 12:7, 12:8: וְכִפֶּר עָלֶיהָ הַכֹּהֵן וְטָהֵרָה (vekhipper aleha hakkohen vetaherah, "and the priest shall make an atonement for her, and she shall be clean"; וְטָהֵרָה vetaherah is the perfect form of טָהֵר taher, H2891). Comparison with the atonement language of chs. 4–5: the chattat of ch. 4 addresses unintentional sin (שְׁגָגָה shegagah, 4:2), and its formula closing is וְנִסְלַח לוֹ (venislach lo, "and it shall be forgiven him," at 4:20, 26, 31, 35 and 5:10, 13, 16, 18); ch. 12 has no unintentional sin, and its closing is not "forgiven" but "clean" — the same kipper yields forgiveness in the context of sin, and cleanness with restored sanctuary access in the context of impurity. See also 12:7 מִמְּקֹר דָּמֶיהָ (mimmeqor dameha, "from the source of her blood"). See BDB and HALOT, s.vv. כפר, טהר.
10. **וְאִם־לֹא תִמָּצֵא יָדָהּ דֵּי שֶׂה ("and if she be not able to bring a lamb")** — 12:8. יָד (yad, "hand," H3027) is here the idiom for "means, resources"; דֵּי (day, "enough, sufficiency," H1767) + שֶׂה (seh, "lamb"). Such means-graduated clauses are not isolated in Leviticus: 5:7 and 5:11, on the trespass offering, likewise provide two descending substitutions, birds and then fine flour. See BDB and HALOT, s.vv. יד, די.
11. **The numerical structure** — for a son, 7 + 33 = 40; for a daughter, 14 + 66 = 80. שֶׁבַע (sheva', "seven," H7651) is the base unit of the purity laws in Leviticus (cf. the seven-day structure of creation, Gen 1:1–2:3); אַרְבָּעִים (arba'im, "forty," H705) recurs across the biblical narrative (Gen 7:12; Exod 24:18; Num 14:33). Eighty is the exact double of forty, sixty-six of thirty-three, fourteen of seven — structurally, a doubling of every segment. This observation stays at the level of textual structure; the reason for the doubling is nowhere stated in the text, and the proposals presented in the sermon belong to the level of interpretation and must not be conflated with the structural fact.
12. **A textual note on Luke 2:22** — Luke writes "the days of their purification" (τοῦ καθαρισμοῦ αὐτῶν, "their"); some manuscripts read αὐτῆς ("her"). On the plural reading, Luke's reference may embrace mother and child together, or reflect his summary style. The variant does not affect the intertextual link between Lev 12 and Luke 2; it belongs to the textual level, is recorded here only, and does not enter the sermon text.

## Devotional Excerpt

Leviticus 12 gives a mother time: forty days, or eighty. God does not hurry her. He writes the days, one by one, into the law of His own sanctuary, so that the whole congregation knows: this woman is recovering; no one may rush her — she need not even rush herself. Day by day she has only one task: to rest, to nourish her child, to wait. When the days are fulfilled, she comes to the door of the tent with her offering — a lamb if she has one, two birds if she does not — and in God's mouth the closing word is identical, word for word: "She shall be clean." Many years later, it was with two birds that Mary entered the temple (Luke 2:24). Today, if you too are in a season of recovery — after childbirth, after illness, or learning to walk again after a fall — hear what this chapter says softly: your days are being counted, not forgotten. When they are fulfilled, rise and return to Him. Cleanness was never earned; it is declared — by Him. May what you hear today be not the hurrying question, "Why aren't you better yet?" but the word that waited for you forty days at the door of the tent: the days are fulfilled; you are clean; come back. And if someone beside you is in such a season of recovery, learn this chapter's posture: do not hurry them, do not interrogate them — simply count the days with them, to the end.

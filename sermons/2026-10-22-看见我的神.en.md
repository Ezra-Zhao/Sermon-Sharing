## Summary (approx. 100 words)

Abram had lived in Canaan for ten years, yet the promised son was still not born. Sarai gave her Egyptian maidservant Hagar to her husband as a concubine, hoping to obtain children by her. When Hagar conceived, she despised her mistress; Sarai dealt harshly with her, and she fled into the wilderness. The Angel of the LORD found her by a spring of water on the way to Shur, asked where she came from and where she was going, commanded her to return and submit under her mistress's hand, and promised her a son, Ishmael—because the LORD had heard her affliction. Hagar called the LORD "The-God-Who-Sees," and the well was named Beer Lahai Roi. This message examines three things: the cost when human hands reach out out of impatience; how God asks, hears, and promises first in the wilderness; and how a mistreated maidservant became the first person in Scripture to name God.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Introduction · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

In our last message, we stood under the starry sky: Abram believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness; a burning torch passed between the pieces of meat, and the covenant was cut and sealed. By all rights, what follows ought to be the story of the promise being fulfilled step by step. But the Bible is deeply honest. The very opening of chapter 16 records ten years: "after Abram had dwelt ten years in the land of Canaan." Ten years, not ten days. The promise had not arrived, the child had not arrived, and Sarai's womb remained completely silent. Brothers and sisters, what tests faith most severely is often not violent storms and raging waves, but day after day passing by where every word of God remains on record, yet not a single thing seems to happen. Today's message is about what happens when people can wait no longer, and how God deals with those who have been wounded in the waiting—and the first one He seeks out is not Abram, nor Sarai, but the maidservant Hagar who fled from the tent.

Let us first read the key passages together:

"Now Sarai, Abram’s wife, had borne him no children. And she had an Egyptian maidservant whose name was Hagar. So Sarai said to Abram, 'See now, the LORD has restrained me from bearing children. Please, go in to my maid; perhaps I shall obtain children by her.' And Abram heeded the voice of Sarai." (Gen. 16:1–2, excerpted)

"So he went in to Hagar, and she conceived. And when she saw that she had conceived, her mistress became despised in her eyes." (Gen. 16:4, excerpted)

"And when Sarai dealt harshly with her, she fled from her presence." (Gen. 16:6, excerpted)

"Now the Angel of the LORD found her by a spring of water in the wilderness, by the spring on the way to Shur. And He said, 'Hagar, Sarai’s maid, where have you come from, and where are you going?'" (Gen. 16:7–8, excerpted)

"Behold, you are with child,
And you shall bear a son.
You shall call his name Ishmael,
Because the LORD has heard your affliction." (Gen. 16:11, excerpted)

"Then she called the name of the LORD who spoke to her, You-Are-the-God-Who-Sees;" (Gen. 16:13, excerpted)

Today we will look at this in three parts:

First, 16:1–6, Unable to wait any longer—ten childless years; Sarai proposes a scheme, Abram heeds, and the whole family bears the cost of human hands reaching out.
Second, 16:7–12, Found by the spring—God's Angel first inquires about origin and destination, then gives commands and promises; the name Ishmael records that God heard her affliction.
Third, 16:13–16, The God who sees—Hagar names God, the well becomes a memorial, she returns, the child is born, and Abram confesses the name with his own mouth.

Let us bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. Abram's household waited ten years, and through that waiting came missteps and wounds. Yet You did not cast any of them away; even for the maidservant who fled, You personally went into the wilderness to seek her. We pray that today You will also seek out every one of us who has been hurt in the waiting and wants to run away. Ask us where we have come from and where we are going, and let us personally recognize with our own mouths: You are the God who sees. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Unable to Wait Any Longer—Human Hands Reach Out First, the Promise Is Wounded First (16:1–6)

### 1.1 "Had Dwelt Ten Years in the Land of Canaan": The Trouble Begins with Time (16:1–3)

The opening verse of chapter 16 contains no clashing of swords, only a span of time: "Now Sarai, Abram’s wife, had borne him no children." Verse 3 deals another blow: "after Abram had dwelt ten years in the land of Canaan."

What do ten years mean? It means the promise was truly waited for; it was not mere lip service. In Genesis 12, God said, "I will make you a great nation," and Abram was seventy-five years old then. Now ten years have passed, and he is eighty-five. Sarai's barrenness shifted from "not yet" to "most likely never." In Sarai's words there is a very heavy phrase: "See now, the LORD has restrained me from bearing children." This sentence is half fact and half accusation—she laid her barrenness at the LORD's feet, and then naturally took matters into her own hands: since God has shut the door, I will open a window myself.

Brothers and sisters, the most dangerous moment in waiting is not when you cease to believe in God, but when you believe in God's promise yet no longer believe in God's timetable. You do not deny the promise, but you concoct your own solutions—that is how Hagar entered the story. Hagar was an Egyptian, a maidservant brought out when they went down to Egypt (cf. Gen. 12). Sarai's arrangement was by no means unusual according to the customs of that time; obtaining children through a maidservant and counting them under the mistress's name was common practice in the ancient Near East. What conforms to custom, however, does not necessarily conform to God's will. What custom can provide is a child; what custom cannot provide is the fulfillment of the promise. In chapter 15, God had just crossed out the backup plan involving Eliezer (Gen. 15:4); now in chapter 16, a new contingency plan is placed before the household—this time not a male servant, but a maidservant.

### 1.2 "Abram Heeded the Voice of Sarai": Silence Is Also a Decision (16:2–4)

The text records Abram's reaction in only one phrase: "And Abram heeded the voice of Sarai."

We are no strangers to this phrasing. In Genesis 3, God's words to Adam include the very same stroke: "Because you have heeded the voice of your wife, and have eaten from the tree of which I commanded you..." (Gen. 3:17, paraphrased summary). When the called person stays silent at a critical moment, exercises no discernment, and goes along with the words of those closest to them, the consequences are never small. It was not that Abram did not know the promise—he had just believed under the starry sky and was accounted righteous. But knowing the promise and withstanding pressure at home to hold fast to the promise are two entirely different things.

The outcome came swiftly: "So he went in to Hagar, and she conceived. And when she saw that she had conceived, her mistress became despised in her eyes." Notice the honesty of Scripture: it does not depict Hagar as a purely innocent victim. Conception altered her position in the household, and she began to despise Sarai. Human pride often begins the moment someone turns their fortunes around—the person who bowed her head yesterday holds her head high today with a swollen belly, and the look in her eyes completely changes. This contempt dredged up all ten years of Sarai's bitter anguish.

### 1.3 Sarai's Grievance, Abram's Abdication, and Hagar's Flight (16:5–6)

Sarai said to Abram, "My wrong be upon you! ... May the LORD judge between you and me." Abram's reply was, "Indeed your maid is in your hand; do to her as you please."

Looking at these two statements together, the wounds of an entire family are fully exposed: Sarai felt wronged, so the one who proposed the scheme turned around and became the victim—whenever people reach out their own hands and cause trouble, their first reaction is always to blame others for not stopping them; Abram washed his hands of it entirely, handing all authority back to her so that he remained untouched—following his silence was an evasion of responsibility; and Hagar was caught in the middle: "when Sarai dealt harshly with her, she fled from her presence."

"Dealt harshly," the causative form of the Hebrew *anah*, is related in the original text to עָנָה (*anah*, H6031: to oppress, mistreat, humble)[1]. Remember this word, for in verse 11 God will personally respond to it: the LORD "has heard your affliction." Affliction shares the same root as mistreatment—just as humans mistreated her, so God heard her. Sarai mistreating Hagar was wrong; Hagar fleeing had its own sorrow. The text takes no sides to whitewash either party; it simply lays out the facts: a decision born of impatience caused all three of them to be wounded.

Psalm 27 is David's prayer in the midst of waiting. At the end, he exhorts both himself and us: "Wait on the LORD; be of good courage, and He shall strengthen your heart; wait, I say, on the LORD!" (Ps. 27:14). Waiting requires good courage—waiting, it turns out, is something that takes boldness; reaching out because you cannot wait requires no boldness at all, only a culturally acceptable excuse.

To sum up the first part in a single sentence: **Ten years without a child, Sarai reached out her hand through cultural custom, Abram complied and then shirked responsibility, and Hagar rose in status only to suffer—the price of failing to wait is never paid by one person alone, but by the whole family together; the promise was not canceled, yet a wound was added first.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Found by the Spring—God First Asks, First Listens, Then Gives the Promise (16:7-12)

### 2.1 "The Angel of the LORD Found Her by a Spring of Water": God Goes to Seek the One Who Fled (16:7)

The direction of Hagar's flight was the Way of Shur toward Egypt—the road back to her homeland. Right on this road, "the Angel of the LORD found her by a spring of water in the wilderness, by the spring on the way to Shur."

"Found," in the original text, can also mean "sought out, located": it was not Hagar who found God, but the Angel of God who found Hagar. Please note that this is the very first time in Genesis that the Angel of the LORD appears to anyone, and the recipient was not a patriarch, but a pregnant, runaway Egyptian maidservant. Brothers and sisters, God's seeking never lines up according to status. Inside the tent, Hagar was a maidservant; by the spring in the wilderness, she was the one God personally came to seek. There was a spring in the wilderness, a place that preserves life; the Angel of the LORD was waiting for her beside the spring—before words of judgment were ever spoken, what was first granted was an encounter beside the water.

"Angel," Hebrew מַלְאָךְ (*malakh*, H4397: messenger, angel)[2]. The way this Angel spoke was most unusual: He spoke in the name of the LORD; verse 10 says, "I will multiply your descendants exceedingly," and in verse 13 Hagar directly addressed Him as the LORD. Most commentators believe this Angel is an appearance of the LORD Himself, and Hagar's confession is based precisely on this—she knew that the One speaking to her was not merely a messenger carrying a message.

### 2.2 "Where Have You Come From, and Where Are You Going?": God's Questions Precede God's Commands (16:8-9)

Opening His mouth, the Angel first called her name and identity: "Hagar, Sarai's maid." This call was both an acknowledgment of her and a reminder of who she was and where she had come from. Then came two questions: "Where have you come from, and where are you going?"

Hagar could only answer the first: "I am fleeing from the presence of my mistress Sarai." As to where she was going, she could not answer—those who flee know where they come from, but have no place to go. Brothers and sisters, God's questions are never meant to gather information; they are meant to lead people to articulate their own situation clearly. In Genesis 3, God asked Adam, "Where are you?" and here He asked Hagar where she came from and where she was going—the same God, asking people who have lost their way.

Then the Angel gave the command: "Return to your mistress, and submit yourself under her hand." These words are hard to hear, yet one must hear them in their entirety: God was not endorsing Hagar's flight, nor was He telling her to keep running toward Egypt; He told her to return not because Sarai was in the right, but because the home of promise was there, and the child was to be born there. But notice the order: immediately following the command is the promise—God does not send people back empty-handed to suffer; He cushions every step of the return journey with promises beforehand.

### 2.3 "Because the LORD Has Heard Your Affliction": The Name Ishmael (16:10-12)

The Angel promised Hagar: "I will multiply your descendants exceedingly, so that they shall not be counted for multitude." And He said: "Behold, you are with child, and you shall bear a son. You shall call his name Ishmael, because the LORD has heard your affliction."

"Ishmael," Hebrew יִשְׁמָעֵאל (*Yishmael*, H3458: God hears)[3], with the root שָׁמַע (*shama*, H8085: to hear, listen, obey)[4]. It was God who named the child, and the reason given was "because the LORD has heard your affliction"—affliction shares the same root as the "dealt harshly" in verse 6 (*anah*, H6031)[1]: just as Sarai dealt harshly with her, so God heard her. However loud the sound of human mistreatment, that near was God's listening ear. Before this child was even born, his lifelong name was set: every time someone called him Ishmael, it was a proclamation—God heard that maidservant's affliction.

The Angel also foretold the boy's character: "He shall be a wild man; his hand shall be against every man, and every man's hand against him." God did not sugarcoat it: this child, born through human schemes, would bear the mark of conflict throughout his life and dwell in the presence of all his brethren. The promise is a true promise, and the cost is a true cost—God heals the reaching of human hands, yet He does not pretend that reaching has no consequences.

Here is the first ignition point for today.

Brothers and sisters, have you ever had a season in your life where you fled from somewhere? Fleeing a relationship, fleeing a home, fleeing a church, fleeing a position where you were wronged. Today the Angel of the LORD asks you the very same question beside the spring: Where have you come from, and where are you going? You can name where you came from, but if you cannot name where you are going, then stop running for now—today, not tomorrow, halt beside the spring. He has heard your affliction, more clearly than you yourself could ever put into words; He has not come to seize you and put you on trial; He has come to give you a name, a promise, and then send you back onto that path you thought you could no longer walk.

To sum up the second part in a single sentence: **The Angel of the LORD found the runaway Hagar beside the spring, first asking where she came from and where she was going, then commanding her to return, promising her descendants, and naming the child "God Hears"—however deeply humans mistreated her, that truly did God hear her; the runaway had an origin but no destination, yet God Himself gave her a destination.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The God Who Sees—A Maidservant Names God, and a Well Becomes a Sign (16:13-16)

### 3.1 "Then She Called the Name of the LORD Who Spoke to Her, You-Are-the-God-Who-Sees": The First Person in Scripture to Name God (16:13)

Having heard the promise, Hagar did an astonishing thing: "Then she called the name of the LORD who spoke to her, You-Are-the-God-Who-Sees; for she said, 'Have I also here seen Him who sees me?'"[7]

"The God-Who-Sees," in Hebrew אֵל רֳאִי (*El Roi*, H410 God + H7210 derived from *raah* to see, H7200)[5], can be translated as "the God of seeing" or "the God Who sees." Let us make this clear first: human beings cannot define the Creator or bestow titles upon Him—God's name is revealed by Himself; what Hagar did was cry out and record an attribute of God that she had personally experienced, using a title. Please note: in the entire Bible, among those who gave God a new title in this way and had it recorded, Hagar was one of the very first—not a patriarch, not an Israelite, but an Egyptian maidservant. The basis of her naming was not learning, but experience: I was sought out in the wilderness, my affliction was heard, therefore He is the God who sees me. "Have I also here seen Him who sees me?"—this phrase carries astonishment: that someone like me should actually see the God who sees me, and still be alive.

Brothers and sisters, often the names of God are first cried out by those who suffer. The one in the tent calls Him the God of promise; the one in the wilderness calls Him the God Who sees—the same God, recognized by people under different names in different circumstances. If your knowledge of God today stays only in the doctrines you have heard, it is thin; but when you meet Him by your own spring, you will have a name that grows out of your experience, cried out with tears.

### 3.2 "Beer Lahai Roi": A Well Commemorating an Encounter (16:14)

"Therefore the well was called Beer Lahai Roi; observe, it is between Kadesh and Bered."

"Beer Lahai Roi," in Hebrew בְּאֵר לַחַי רֹאִי (*Beer Lachay Roi*, H883, the well of the One who lives and sees me)[6]. The Israelites loved to name places where they had encountered God as a memorial: Jacob set up a pillar at Bethel (Gen. 28); Moses encountered the burning bush at Mount Horeb (Exod. 3, summary). Hagar, a Gentile maidservant, also left a name in Israel's geography—every time subsequent generations drank the water of that well, they would remember: here, God looked upon a runaway woman. Isaac later even lived by this well (Gen. 24:62, summary), which shows that this place carried no small weight in the memory of the patriarch's family.

### 3.3 She Returned, and Abram Named the Child (16:15-16)

The story ends quietly: "So Hagar bore Abram a son; and Abram named his son, whom Hagar bore, Ishmael."

Do not miss two details: First, Hagar really did return—she returned under the hand of the mistress who had dealt harshly with her, to bear the child within the household of promise; God's word told her to return, and she returned. Sometimes obedience simply means walking back down that difficult road. Second, the one who named the child was Abram—he used the name God had given, "Ishmael," which was equivalent to acknowledging with his own mouth: behind the origin of this child, there is God's hearing. At eighty-six years old, Abram had his first son, but it was not the son of promise. Scripture records the years with utter clarity here, laying the groundwork for the arithmetic of those thirteen years of silence in the next chapter.

Paul in Galatians chapter 4 uses the contrast between Hagar and Sarah to teach an allegorical lesson: the one born according to the flesh versus the one born through promise—different paths, different outcomes (Gal. 4:22-31, summary). But do not misread: Paul is speaking about two paths of receiving an inheritance, not despising Hagar as a person—the God of Genesis 16 personally sought her, heard her, named her child, and promised to multiply her descendants. Paths must be discerned, while people must be pitied; God's care for Hagar is not diminished in the slightest by the subsequent allegorical exposition.

Warren W. Wiersbe, in his Genesis commentary expounding Genesis 16, reminds readers that Hagar's experience demonstrates that God hears and cares for the oppressed; when people use their own methods to force the promise, they only create new pain, and only by returning to God's timing and arrangement can wounds find a way out. (See Warren W. Wiersbe, *The Bible Exposition Commentary: Genesis*.)

Here is today's second spark point, and also the altar call for the entire sermon.

Brothers and sisters, today this chapter reaches out to two kinds of people. One kind is like Sarai and Abram: you have waited a long time, your hand has already reached out, and someone in the household has already been hurt—it is not too late to turn back, the promise has not been revoked; withdraw the outstretched hand, care for those who have been hurt, and wait anew for God's timing. The other kind is like Hagar: you are the one suffering, you are the one fleeing, you feel that no one sees you—I tell you, there is One who sees you, and His name is the God-Who-Sees. Today, pause by your spring, and let Him question you, hear you, give you a new name and the courage to return. He hears affliction; from Hagar's time until today, He has never changed.

The third part in one concluding sentence: **Hagar called the Lord the God-Who-Sees, the well was named Beer Lahai Roi, she obediently returned to bear Ishmael, and Abram acknowledged the child with the name given by God—the wilderness encounter left behind a name and a sign: God sees, God hears, and man can return.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

Today's chapter speaks to six categories of people:

**To seekers:** This chapter introduces you first to a name of God—the God-Who-Sees. The first person He sought out was not a patriarch of status, but a runaway maidservant. Your background, your wounds, the affliction you cannot voice—He hears them all. Today, if you are willing, you do not need to get your life sorted out first; first say a word to Him in your heart: God Who sees, please look upon me too. This is the starting point of your encounter with Him.

**To new believers:** Learn two things from Hagar. First, when you encounter hardship, run before God first, not away from God: she fled to the spring, and God was waiting for her there; your spring is prayer. Second, when you hear God's word, do according to it, even with a word as difficult as "return"—the road of obedience is hard to walk, but it is paved with promises; with every step you take, God's word becomes more real.

**To brothers and sisters who have been in the Lord for many years:** Beware of a Sarai-like impatience: the promise is still on your lips, but you have already taken your own measures. What grows most easily after serving for many years is a set of practices that conform to customs and experience, but simply do not conform to God's timetable. Today, withdraw your outstretched hand and take inventory: which arrangements were commanded by God, and which were added by yourself because you could not wait? Whatever has been added on, stop first, and hand it over to God to clean up.

**To parents:** This chapter is a mirror for parents: one decision by Sarai and Abram caused the child, even before birth, to carry a prophecy of conflict (16:12). For parents' anxiety and reaching out in the midst of waiting, the children will pay the bill for a very long time. Bring your anxiety before God to be dealt with first before arranging your children's affairs; also teach your children to know this God-Who-Sees—when they are in the wilderness in the future, the very first word they cry out will be this name.

**To those striving in the workplace:** In the workplace, you might be that "Hagar": the credit belongs to others, the hardship is yours, you are despised, mistreated, and want to walk away. Do not rush to flee down the way to Shur: that God hears your affliction does not mean God is telling you to leave immediately—He first asks where you have come from and where you are going, and then gives you His arrangement. Pour out your grievances to God first and listen to Him regarding your destination; that is far steadier than walking out in anger. You may also be sitting in Sarai's position: those with power in hand, remember that the cries of mistreated subordinates have all been heard by God.

**To those currently in suffering:** The name Ishmael is for you: God hears. Your affliction has not sunk like a stone into the sea; He has heard that unspoken part of yours. The well of Beer Lahai Roi still stands between Kadesh and Bered as a sign—everyone whom God has seen has their own well. Today, first make it to the spring alive: take a drink of water, and cry out the name—God Who sees, You see me. Crying out like this, you will be able to keep going.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Summary · Responsive Prayer

Let us review today's three sentences:

First, could not wait any longer—ten years without a child, Sarai reached out, Abram obeyed, Hagar suffered, and the whole family paid the bill for being unable to wait;

Second, found by the spring—the Angel of the Lord found Hagar as she fled, first asking where she came from and where she was going, and then giving both a command and a promise; Ishmael serves as a memorial that God heard her affliction;
Third, the God who sees—Hagar named God, the spring was made a sign, she returned to bear a son, and into the family of promise came one more child heard by God.

Now let us bow our heads and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. When Sarai could not wait, You did not nullify Your promise; when Hagar fled, You came in person to the spring to find her. Forgive us our sin of reaching out our hands to force matters, and heal the wounds we have suffered when others reached out their hands against us. Teach us to call You by Your new name: the God who sees—because You see, and because You hear, we are able to go back, and we are able to keep on waiting. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

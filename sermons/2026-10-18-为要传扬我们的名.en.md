## Summary (approx. 100 words)

In Genesis 11, all humanity had one language and one speech. What was meant to be the ideal raw material for unity was instead used to build a city with a tower reaching into the heavens. Scripture plainly reveals their motive: to make a name for themselves, lest they be scattered. The LORD came down to see, confused their language, and scattered them over the face of all the earth; the city was named Babel. This sermon explores three realities: unity without godly direction is mere collective pride; man sought to climb to heaven via a tower, yet God Himself came down—His descent was both judgment and restraining mercy; and the dispersion man feared most was precisely God’s original blessing. In the very next chapter, God would personally grant Abram a great name—there was no need for man to seize it for himself.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Introduction · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

In the previous message, we finished reading the genealogies in chapter 10. The nations were dispersed, establishing nations according to their tongues and lands—that was the path of blessing.

Yet we must first clarify how to read this: chronologically, Babel in chapter 11 is not a brand-new event that took place only after chapter 10. The name Peleg in chapter 10 already told us that "in his days the earth was divided." Chapter 11 rewinds the tape to show us the reason behind that great dispersion: Why were people separated? Why could they no longer understand each other's speech? The camera zooms in from the map of the whole world to a single construction site on the plain of Shinar.

On this construction site, all humanity was working together. There were no foreign enemies, no natural disasters; they shared one language and one speech, perfectly unified. They wanted to do something great—something that, once achieved, would make their name famous throughout the world.

Scripture records their motive without hiding a single thing; it lays it bare right in the text. Today, we will look intently at this motive to examine ourselves—because Babel is far closer to us than we think.

Let us first read the key passages together (quoted from the Chinese Union Version / NKJV):

"Now the whole earth had one language and one speech. And it came to pass, as they journeyed from the east, that they found a plain in the land of Shinar, and they dwelt there." (Gen. 11:1-2)

"And they said, 'Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth.'" (Gen. 11:4)

"But the LORD came down to see the city and the tower which the sons of men had built." (Gen. 11:5)

"And the LORD said, 'Indeed the people are one and they all have one language, and this is what they begin to do; now nothing that they propose to do will be withheld from them.'" (Gen. 11:6)

"So the LORD scattered them abroad from there over the face of all the earth, and they ceased building the city. Therefore its name is called Babel, because there the LORD confused the language of all the earth; and from there the LORD scattered them abroad over the face of all the earth." (Gen. 11:8-9, excerpt)

Today, we will divide our study into three parts:

First, 11:1-4: One language, building a tower in the wrong direction—unity turned the wrong way, motivated by fame and driven by fear underneath;  
Second, 11:5-7: The LORD came down—man wanted to climb up, but God came down; the confusion of languages was judgment, yet also mercy that restrained humanity from plunging into greater evil;  
Third, 11:8-9: Dispersion accomplished, the tower halted—what man feared most became the path of blessing; after Babel, God Himself would give man a name.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. Your Word does not cover up human sin; it shows us plain and unvarnished the words hidden in the hearts of the people of Babel: 'let us make a name for ourselves.' You show this to us so that we might recognize the towers erected in our own hearts early on. We pray that You would tear down today every project by which we try to make a name for ourselves through our unity, our talents, and our busy striving. Teach us to surrender our name back into Your hands. We also thank You that when You restrain man, mercy is often hidden underneath; and the path of dispersion on which You send people ultimately turns out to be the path of blessing. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: One Language, Building a Tower in the Wrong Direction (11:1-4)

### 1.1 "One language and one speech": Unity is good material, but pointed in the wrong direction it becomes a grave problem

Verse 1: "Now the whole earth had one language and one speech."

"Speech," in Hebrew *saphah* (H8193, lip, speech) [1], is literally "one lip." There was zero cost of communication; all humanity was on the exact same wavelength. Such unity was in itself a gift—at Pentecost, the Holy Spirit would take up this gift once again. Yet the very first thing the people of Babel did with this gift was to confer with one another to build a tower.

Please notice the recurring phrase in verse 3: "Then they said to one another, 'Come...'" And again in verse 4, there is another "Come..." Two "Comes"—one directed toward technology, the other toward ambition. There is nothing inherently wrong with "Come"; when God created man, He also said, "Let Us make man in Our image, according to Our likeness" (Gen. 1:26, excerpt). The difference lies not in being unified, but in what direction that unified effort is aimed: toward God’s blessing, or toward making a name for oneself?

Brothers and sisters, unity is never automatically righteous. A family, a team, or a church unified in doing the wrong thing is far more dangerous than a scattered, disorganized group—a scattered group cannot accomplish grand feats, but a unified Babel dares to build any tower imaginable. When evaluating unity, do not first ask how aligned people are; first ask where they are heading.

### 1.2 Bricks and asphalt: Technology is not wrong; the trouble begins when ambition commandeers it

Verse 3 describes this in great detail: "'Come, let us make bricks and bake them thoroughly.' They had brick for stone, and they had asphalt for mortar."

The Mesopotamian plain lacked stone, so people baked bricks; it lacked mortar, so people used asphalt, or bitumen. This was genuine technological progress: standardized materials, uniform specifications, capable of being stacked infinitely upward. Scripture records this not to oppose technology. A brick is neutral, and the problem of the tower did not begin with the first brick laid.

In his commentary on Genesis, Warren W. Wiersbe, in explaining Babel, reminds readers that human ingenuity and collaboration are themselves gifts from God; the sin of Babel lay not in building, but in the pride and rebellion behind the building.

These words pierce our hearts even more deeply today. Our "bricks" are infinitely superior to those of the people of Babel: platforms, traffic, algorithms, artificial intelligence—are not all of these thoroughly baked, high-grade bricks? The question is always the same ancient question: In whose hands are the bricks, and in what direction are they being stacked? Technology magnifies the human heart—if blessing is in the heart, technology magnifies blessing; if making a name is in the heart, technology will build a tower faster than Babel ever could.

### 1.3 "Let us make a name for ourselves": The motive is laid bare; the fear is hidden beneath

Verse 4 is the key to the entire chapter; humanity speaks out its own motive:

"And they said, 'Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth.'"

"Tower," Hebrew *migdal* (H4026, tower, elevated platform) [2]; "whose top is in the heavens," literally "its head in the heavens," reflecting the rhetorical style of the Mesopotamian ziggurats of that era (provided here merely as background). "Name," Hebrew *shem* (H8034, name, renown) [3]—take note of this word, for it is the very same word as the name "Shem": Shem, *Shem*, the exact same word. In the previous message, God’s line of promise ran through the line of "Shem" (the Name); in this chapter, man rushes to make a "name" (*shem*) for himself. The tension of the entire Bible collides on this single word: Is a name something we wait for God to give, or something we seize for ourselves?

We must also look at the layer beneath this motive: "lest we be scattered." Underneath pride lies fear. Fear of being scattered, fear of being forgotten, fear of fading into obscurity among the masses. They wanted to make a name for themselves half out of ambition, and half out of terror—hoping to buy out security and immortality all at once with a single tower.

This is precisely where Babel collides head-on with blessing. God’s way was: Be dispersed across the whole earth, fill the earth, and in that dispersion be fruitful, establish families, and be blessed. Man’s way was: Let us huddle tightly together, build high, and pin ourselves in place with a name. Brothers and sisters, many towers are not built by wicked people; they are built by fearful people. Fear that one's children will lose, fear of being made redundant, fear that no one will remember us when we grow old—fear pushes people up the tower, layer after layer.

Whatever is driven by a "lest we..." kind of fear, no matter how high it is built, can never truly preserve what people are terrified of losing.

To wrap up Part One in a single sentence: **The problem with Babel was not that people were too united or too capable; it was that both their unity and their capability were aimed at their own name—the motive was to make a name, the underlying driver was fear, and the direction faced completely opposite to God's blessing.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Man Wants to Go Up, but God Comes Down (11:5–7)

### 2.1 "The Lord Came Down to See": A Tower Touching Heaven, Yet God Had to Come Down to Even See It

Verse 5 contains some of the quietest, yet sharpest irony in the Bible: "But the Lord came down to see the city and the tower which the sons of men had built."

Man said the top of the tower would reach to the heavens. But what about God? God had to "come down" just to see that city and tower. The implication could not be clearer: a project that in human eyes reached the heavens was, in God's eyes, so small that He had to stoop down to inspect it. With this single action, Scripture measures the entire gap between man's self-perception and his actual size.

"Came down" is the Hebrew *yarad* (H3381, to go down, descend) [4]. This word appears again later: in chapter 18, God comes down to inspect Sodom (cf. Gen. 18:21); in Exodus, God comes down upon Mount Sinai (cf. Ex. 19). In Scripture, God coming down is always a momentous event: He does not merely hear reports from afar; He comes down in person, sees clearly, and only then speaks and acts. Inspecting before judging—this is the righteousness of how God works. He does not condemn based on hearsay; He sees for Himself.

The words of Psalm 127:1 seem like a footnote written specifically for Babel: "Unless the Lord builds the house, They labor in vain who build it; Unless the Lord guards the city, The watchman stays awake in vain." The people of Babel both built a house and guarded a city; they had everything covered, except for one thing: this city and tower were not in the hands of the Lord.

### 2.2 "Nothing... Will Be Withheld from Them": What God Restrains Is Not Human Capability, but Its Direction

In verse 6, God says something weighty: "now nothing that they propose to do will be withheld from them."

This statement must not be read as God being afraid of man. This is the God who had just made the flood recede and hung the rainbow with His own hand; He is not afraid of a tower of bricks. This statement is a diagnosis: when a unified humanity resolves to walk in rebellion, the greater their ability to execute, the more thorough and swift their corruption becomes. The generation before the flood in chapter 6 had already demonstrated this outcome: mighty men ran rampant, and every intent of the thoughts of their heart was only evil continually (cf. Gen. 6:5). What God does here is to step on the brakes before the tower is finished and before evil reaches its full maturity.

The order in Proverbs 16:18 is half-fulfilled here: "Pride goes before destruction, And a haughty spirit before a fall." By confusing their language, God was dismantling the scaffolding of "pride" before "destruction" could land. Brothers and sisters, when God hinders someone, in the moment they usually just feel disappointed, feeling their plans have been disrupted. Only years later, looking back, do they realize that being interrupted was often His way of preserving them. That matter you prayed for that didn't happen, that deal that fell through, that social circle you couldn't break into—don't be in a rush to complain; ask yourself: is He dismantling the scaffolding on my tower?

### 2.3 "Let Us Go Down and There Confuse Their Language": Judgment in the Shape of Mercy

Verse 7: "Come, let Us go down and there confuse their language, that they may not understand one another's speech."

"Confuse" is the Hebrew *balal* (H1101, to mix, confuse, confound) [5]—and "Babel" (*Babel*, H894) [6] derives its name from the sound of this word. Scripture itself provides the explanatory note in verse 9: "Therefore its name is called Babel, because there the Lord confused the language of all the earth." The name man tried to make for himself did not endure; what endured is the name God gave to the city. A project meant to make a name ended up making this name known: Babel, confusion. This is the irony of Scripture, and the solemnity within God's humor.

We must also see another thing: God did not use a flood again. The covenant of the rainbow stood, and He keeps covenant. The judgment He used was just enough to dismantle the tower—when communication broke down, the work stopped, and the people scattered. His judgment is measured, and His mercy has a distinct shape: He did not wipe out mankind; He shut down the construction project. Many years later, on the Day of Pentecost, when the Holy Spirit descended, the disciples spoke in other tongues, and people from every nation understood them (cf. Acts 2). Notice that at Pentecost, God did not eliminate the languages of the nations; He enabled people across different languages to understand one another. The curse of Babel was not diversity, but incomprehension; the reversal at Pentecost was not uniformity into a single tongue, but the Holy Spirit Himself acting as the translator. God's way has never been to melt everyone into a single mold, but to bring all nations into mutual fellowship in Him.

Let me drive this home with fire.

Brothers and sisters, someone sitting here today is hurting over a tower whose construction has ground to a halt: a project that fell through, a team that disbanded, a reputation that failed to materialize, a dead-end road. You feel that God is not helping you. Today, on behalf of this chapter on Babel, let me tell you this: some work is stopped not because God doesn't love you, but because He loves you too much to let you finish the tower. The day that tower is finished would be the real disaster—you would stand at the top thinking you reached heaven, when in reality you would be far from the earth, far from people, and far from God.

Right now today, not tomorrow, name that tower in your heart: Is it prestige? Is it security? Is it "I can't lose to so-and-so"? Name it, and surrender it. Don't wait until your language is confused and the construction site is scattered to be forced to halt. For whoever willingly steps down from the tower today, the place where your feet hit the ground will be the starting point of your next calling—Abram didn't build any tower, yet God Himself made his name great. Coming down is not defeat; coming down is returning the business of making a name to the One who alone can truly give a name.

To wrap up Part Two in a single sentence: **Man wanted to climb up by means of a tower, but God came down in person; He confused their language, dismantling the scaffolding of pride while keeping the covenant of the rainbow—His judgment is measured, upheld beneath by mercy.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The Tower Stopped, but the Way Opened (11:8–9)

### 3.1 "They Ceased Building the City": What Man Feared Most Happened Just Like That

Verse 8: "So the Lord scattered them abroad from there over the face of all the earth, and they ceased building the city."

Man built the tower "lest we be scattered abroad"; God scattered them abroad, and the tower stopped. What man feared most happened after all. But please read this verse together with chapter 10: what happened after they were scattered? A cataclysm like the flood? No. What happened was the entire table of nations in chapter 10—bearing children, establishing nations, and filling the whole earth, each according to their language. Man thought being scattered meant utter ruin, but being scattered turned out to be the way of life. When fear points in the wrong direction, all your resistance is wasted energy.

Brothers and sisters, this lesson applies to our lives every single day: fear of our children leaving home, fear of changing jobs, fear of moving, fear of church planting, fear of familiar circles dissolving. The tighter we huddle and the higher we build our towers, the emptier we feel inside. God's way is often the reverse: He sends people out, and along the way out, He pours out His blessing. When Abram was called, the very first lesson was, "Get out of your country, From your family And from your father's house" (Gen. 12:1). The very place the people of Babel clung to, Abram left behind in obedience—the same lesson, walked out in two opposite ways.

### 3.2 Babel and Abram: A Name Snatched for Oneself Shatters; A Name Given by God Endures

In the second half of chapter 11, Scripture immediately follows with the genealogy of Shem (11:10–32), tracing all the way down to Abram. Then in chapter 12, verse 2, God says to Abram: "I will make you a great nation... And make your name great."

Please put these two side by side; Scripture deliberately juxtaposes them:

The people of Babel said: Let us make a name for ourselves. The result: their name was never made great; the city was called Babel, meaning confusion.
Abram snatched for nothing. God said: "I will make your name great." The result: that name is so great that people across the whole earth are still speaking it today.

This is the economy of "names" throughout the entire Bible: a name is fruit, not prey; it is God's reward to the obedient, not a trophy for the ambitious. The Lord Jesus articulated this law most thoroughly: "For whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted" (Luke 14:11). In the kingdom of God, the switch that raises or lowers someone is not found in the height of their tower, but in the height of their knees.

Zephaniah 3:9 also leaves us with an enduring hope: that God will restore to the peoples a pure language, that they all may call on the name of the Lord, to serve Him with one accord. The languages confused at Babel will one day be reunited in worship—not by reverting to a single tongue, but by all peoples serving with one accord. The table of nations in chapter 10, Babel in chapter 11, the hope of Zephaniah, the mutual understanding at Pentecost, and every tribe and tongue in Revelation form a single line: from beginning to end, what God desires is for all nations to share fellowship in Him, not for all nations to be swallowed up by a single tower.

The people of Babel sought unity through a human project; the disciples at Pentecost received unity through the Holy Spirit. The former ended in dispersal; the latter spread from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth.

### 3.3 Shinar Has Not Disappeared: Babel Is a Test Every Generation Must Retake

One final reminder. Chapter 10 says that the beginning of Nimrod's kingdom was in Shinar; in chapter 11, the tower was built in Shinar, and later this land was called Babylon. In the Book of Daniel, Nebuchadnezzar walked on the roof in Babylon and said: "Is not this great Babylon, that I have built for a royal dwelling by my mighty power and for the honor of my majesty?" (Dan. 4:30). Before the words were out of his mouth, the king was driven out to eat grass like oxen. In the Book of Revelation, "Babylon the great" becomes the collective name for the city that opposes God (cf. Rev. 18).

In other words, Babel is not a piece of ancient news; it is a recurring illness: power, technology, and united effort, paired with a heart set on making a name for itself. Every generation, every person on an upward climb, must retake this very test. Reading it today is not about laughing at the ancients; it is looking into a mirror for ourselves.

To sum up the third part in one sentence: **The tower halted, yet the path opened: the scattering that man feared most became the blessing of filling the earth; the stolen name shattered into Babel, while the name given by God stands to this day—from now on, entrust the matter of your name to God.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

Today's chapter speaks to six groups of people:

**To seekers:** You may not feel like you are building a tower. But please ask honestly: has what you have fought desperately to protect all these years also been a "lest"—lest you be looked down upon, lest you have no one to rely on in old age, lest no one remember your name? Babel tells you that this tower cannot protect any of those things. God's way is entirely different: He Himself comes down to seek man; the name He gives requires no striving on your part. Today, pause the construction of your tower, and listen to what He has to say.

**To new believers:** The easiest Babel-illness to fall into after coming to faith is turning faith itself into a tower: chasing after a spiritual reputation, comparing who is used more mightily, and using ministry to make a name for yourself. Establish rules early: whenever a ministry makes you crave being seen, pause and examine your motives; whatever small task goes unnoticed, do it faithfully. When God gave Abram a name, Abram was walking—he wasn't building a tower.

**To longtime brothers and sisters in the Lord:** The towers of seasoned believers are often built inside the church: our building is the biggest, our tradition is the purest, our circle is the most authentic. If unity becomes nothing more than circling the wagons to protect what we have, it is simply the church version of Babel. Use this chapter to examine yourself: this past year, did we disperse outward to bless others more, or did we huddle inward to protect our turf more?

**To parents:** How many parents harbor that phrase in their hearts: "lest we be scattered"? Lest our children move far away, lest our children suffer hardship, lest our children fall behind others. And so, layer upon layer is added to the tower: packed schedules, calculated career paths, plans that allow no deviation. Look into the mirror of Babel: what are you building for your child—the blessing of filling the earth, or a tower built out of fear that they might leave your sight? If you need to let go and allow God to lead them forth, do it without delay.

**To those striving in the workplace:** The workplace is the modern plain of Shinar: brickmaking technology upgrades daily, and the competition to make a name escalates constantly. Check two things: first, what is the skill in your hands magnifying—blessing, or ambition? Second, is that "lest" you fear making decisions for you? When a deal is blocked by God or a partnership falls through, don't rush to label it a failure; perhaps God is taking down the scaffolding, not undermining your foundation.

**To those in the midst of suffering:** You may currently be standing on an abandoned construction site: your career has stalled, relationships have unraveled, and plans are completely overturned. The word for you today is: the disbanding is not the end. After the people of Babel were scattered, Scripture goes on to record the genealogy of Shem and the call of Abram—God’s greatest story begins precisely after the dispersing. Don't rush to rebuild the tower; remain where you are and listen: the next chapter might just be yours.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion & Response Prayer

Let us review today's three key statements:

First, one language built a tower in the wrong direction—the motive was to make a name for themselves, driven underneath by the fear of being scattered;
Second, man sought to go up, but God came down—confusing the language was judgment, yet it was also a restraining mercy, as He kept His rainbow covenant;
Third, the tower halted, yet the path opened—there is no need to seize a name, for God will make the name of the obedient great.

Let us bow our heads now and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. You did not let us finish our tower, but stopped us midway; today we acknowledge this restraint as grace. We pray that You show us the name of the tower in our hearts: is it reputation, is it security, is it the fear of being forgotten? Today we surrender it, seeking no longer to make a name for ourselves. Teach us to follow Abram's example—to leave where we are, to walk the path You point out, and to place the making of a name entirely into Your hands. May Your name be exalted, and may the languages of all peoples be reunited in You, until that day when every tribe and tongue worship together in one voice. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

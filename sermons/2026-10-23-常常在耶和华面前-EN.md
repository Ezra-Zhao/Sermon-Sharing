## Abstract

Leviticus 24 has only twenty-three verses, yet it carries two genres. The first nine verses are the regular service of the sanctuary — pure olive oil for the lamp, and twelve cakes of bread renewed every sabbath. The last fourteen verses are a case in the camp: the son of an Israelite woman and an Egyptian father blasphemes the Name in a fight, is held in custody, waits for the LORD's direction, is judged, and brings forth in his train a whole section of "eye for eye" legislation. This sermon does not expound every verse; it follows the chapter's structure and theological thread. Sanctuary and camp are two faces of one holiness: the lamp and the bread that stand continually before the LORD, and the Name that may not be blasphemed in the camp, belong to the holiness of one and the same God. The order of worship and the order of justice are not two systems but one. "Continually" is the keyword of the first half; "one manner of law" is the keyword of the second; and the chapter closes with "did as the LORD commanded," nailing the two halves together.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 Introduction: sanctuary and camp, two faces of one holiness

(Fact) The chapter's position must be seen first. Leviticus 23 has just laid out the year — Sabbath, Passover, the fiftieth day, trumpets, atonement, booths: time sanctified. Chapter 24 follows at once: its first half moves the camera inside the sanctuary, to watch how the lamp is kept and the bread set out; its second half pulls the camera back into the middle of the camp, to watch a quarrel become a case of blasphemy, and how that case is judged and closed. From chapter 25 the text returns to the land and the jubilee. Chapter 24 is thus a rivet driven between the festival calendar and the land laws: the feasts govern days; this chapter governs a place and a people — the sanctuary as a place, the camp as a people. In Leviticus, holiness is never an abstract quality-word; it comes to rest in three things: time has feasts, space has a sanctuary, and a people has statutes. Chapter 24 sets the last two side by side.

(Fact) In genre, the two halves are sharply distinct. The first half (vv. 1-9) is pure legislation: it opens with the LORD speaking to Moses (v. 1, Summary) and specifies oil, lamp, bread, table, and frankincense, with measures and order all written out — no persons, no plot. The second half (vv. 10-23) is narrative: there are persons — the son of an Israelite woman, his mother Shelomith, and an Israelite who strives with him; there is a place — in the camp; there is conflict, custody, waiting, verdict, and execution. Such a direct splicing of statute and story is not common in Leviticus; comparable cases are the episode of Nadab and Abihu set among the priestly ordinances in chapter 10, and the case of the wood-gatherer on the Sabbath set among the statutes in Numbers 15 (Summary, by way of comparison). (Application) The splice itself is a sermon: the law is not a disembodied code-book. It walks into the daily life of the camp, into a quarrel. Sometimes it is promulgated by direct command; sometimes a real case forces the principle out of it. Every statute in the second half of this chapter — the penalty for blaspheming the Name, the proportion of eye for eye, the application of one law to all — is not deduced in a study; it grows out of a case that had already happened.

(Fact) Between the halves there is a deeper seam: a counterpoint of theme-words. The phrase that recurs in the first half is "before the LORD" — the lamp before the LORD (vv. 3, 4), the bread set before the LORD (vv. 6, 8, Summary of their wording). The word that recurs in the second half is "the name" — he blasphemed "the name" (v. 11, Summary of the Hebrew idiom, on which see note 9 of the Chinese original), and the statute treats of him that "blasphemeth the name of the LORD" (v. 16). The first half asks: what is fit to stand continually before the LORD? The second half asks: how is the LORD's name to be treated in the camp? (Application) Read the two halves as one chapter, and the theological thread emerges: holiness has a positive face and a boundary. The positive face is offering — the finest oil, the finest flour, an unextinguished lamp, bread always set out. The boundary is judgment — the Name not to be blasphemed, life not to be taken lightly, injury answerable in measured proportion. Offering without boundary turns the sanctuary into decoration; boundary without offering turns the camp into an execution ground. Leviticus 24 puts the two in a single chapter so that Israel learns nearness and reverence together: the people at the lamp and the people in the camp are the same people; the hand that tends the lamp in the morning must not curse by night; the worship that sets out bread on the Sabbath and the speech of ordinary lips are watched by one and the same God.

(Fact) One more structural detail must not be missed: the whole chapter, head and tail, is framed as command and execution. It opens with the LORD speaking to Moses and commanding him to command Israel (vv. 1-2, Summary); it closes at verse 23 with Moses speaking to Israel and the people doing as commanded (quotation in section V). In between, the ordinances of lamp and bread carry no recorded receipt of execution, while the case runs through the whole course of execution without a single step missing. (Application) The asymmetry is eloquent: obedience in a regular ordinance is silent and day-by-day; it needs no dispatch of victory in every chapter. Obedience in a case is public and once-for-all; the whole camp must see it. Most of the life of faith is the first half's unapplauded "continually"; but when the Name is blasphemed and life is taken lightly, there must be the second half's camp-wide "did as commanded." Of the four sections that follow, the first two treat the regular service of the sanctuary, the third the case in the camp, and the last gathers the whole: the sanctity of the Name is protected alike in worship and in justice.

### II. 08:00-24:00 Lamp and oil: pure, beaten, continual

(Fact) The first item of the sanctuary's regular service is the lamp, and the command begins with the oil: "Command the children of Israel, that they bring unto thee pure oil olive beaten for the light, to cause the lamps to burn continually." (Lev 24:2, KJV) Notice the chain of supply: the oil is not provided by the priest out of his own store; it is brought by the children of Israel — supplied by all the people, tended by the priest. Here is worship's first lesson: the light in the sanctuary is kept burning by what the congregation brings from its own land. (Fact) The oil's three qualifiers must all be handled according to the original text. The Hebrew phrase is šemen zayit zākh kāṯîṯ: šemen is oil; zayit (Strong's H2132) is the olive, the olive tree; zākh (H2134) is pure, clear; kāṯîṯ (H3795) is "beaten, crushed," marking that this oil is got by beating and pressing the olives, not left to settle turbid. (Application) The three qualifiers together make a doctrine of worship: what is offered to God is to be pure, to have passed through crushing, and to be made expressly for the light. Not any oil will do — not the turbid, not the convenient, not the leftover. "Beaten" means breaking comes first: the olive uncrushed yields no oil; this is not an allegory pasted on by the preacher but a fact of the process itself — the best portion always comes out of the pressing.

(Fact) The man who tends the lamp, and his hours, are specified with the same concreteness: "Without the vail of the testimony, in the tabernacle of the congregation, shall Aaron order it from the evening unto the morning before the LORD continually: it shall be a statute for ever in your generations." (Lev 24:3, KJV) The place is outside the veil of the testimony — inside the sanctuary, outside the Holy of Holies, where the lampstand stood; the hours are "from the evening unto the morning" — the lamp's shift is the night shift; the tender is Aaron himself. Verse 4 gathers it up once more: "He shall order the lamps upon the pure candlestick before the LORD continually." (KJV) (Fact) The keyword here is "continually": verses 2, 3, and 4 all hang on the Hebrew tāmîd (Strong's H8548), "continually, regularly, without interruption." The word is a hidden thread through the tabernacle ordinances: the bread is to be set before the LORD "always" (Exod 25:30, Summary), the daily burnt offering is called the continual burnt offering (Exod 29:42, Summary), and the incense on the altar is likewise perpetual (Exod 30:8, Summary). Tāmîd marks those parts of worship that do not ride on festival peaks but only on day-by-day keeping. (Application) Note the lamp's timetable: it burns at night. From evening to morning is the stretch when the camp is asleep and the world is darkest; and precisely in that stretch there is a lamp in the sanctuary that does not go out. Whoever preaches this chapter should be seized by this first: Israel's faith is not a faith that holds only at feasts, in daylight, when all are gathered; in the unseen night it is kept by one man who, at the set time, adds oil, trims the wick, and orders the lamps. The things that matter most in a church are often night-shift things — unexciting, off-stage, and instantly missed when absent. A lamp does not burn forever because it was lit once; tāmîd is not a single resolve but an everlasting statute come down into a daily act.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) This lamp ordinance runs almost word for word parallel with Exodus 27:20-21 (Summary): the same pure olive oil, the same evening-to-morning hours, the same statute for ever. Scholarship generally reads the two as two placements of one tradition — in Exodus within the blueprint for building the tabernacle, in Leviticus within the living ordinances of the holiness legislation. This sermon records the intertextual observation only: the same lamp is a design in the blueprint and a routine in Leviticus. A blueprint can be drawn once; a routine must be repeated every night. (Fact) One more layer is often overlooked: the lampstand's light does not shine outward; it shines inside the sanctuary — onto the table of the bread opposite it (Summary of the arrangement of lampstand and table facing one another within the sanctuary). (Application) The sanctuary's light, then, is not first a display to the world; it is light that illumines the offering itself: the bread that stands before God stands always in the light. Worship is lit first, and witness comes after. And that leads us naturally across to the table — for what the lamp shines on is the bread of the next section.

### III. 24:00-42:00 The bread of the Presence: twelve cakes, two rows, frankincense, an everlasting covenant

(Fact) After the lamp ordinance comes the bread ordinance: "And thou shalt take fine flour, and bake twelve cakes thereof: two tenth deals shall be in one cake." (Lev 24:5, KJV) The numbers are listed as the text gives them: fine flour, twelve cakes, two tenth deals in each. Verse 6 directs that they be set in two rows, six on a row, upon the pure table before the LORD (Summary). This bread is traditionally called the shewbread; its Hebrew name is leḥem happānîm, literally "the bread of the Face, the bread of the Presence" — leḥem (Strong's H3899) is bread, food, and pānîm is face, presence. The bread is "shewed," then, not to men but to God; it is set out as an offering for God to "see." (Fact) The word for the rows also deserves attention: verse 6's arrangement is maʿăreḵeṯ (Strong's H4635), "a row, an ordered pile" — the twelve cakes are not heaped; they are marshalled. Two rows of six, in perfect order. (Application) The aesthetic of the sanctuary is an aesthetic of order: the lamp tended, the bread in rows, the oil pure, the flour fine. Leviticus shows no mercy to the word "careless," because it knows that how a man arranges an offering before God reflects where he has placed God in his heart. Of the number twelve the preacher must speak carefully: this passage itself says only "twelve cakes"; it does not here expressly name the twelve tribes (Fact). To read the twelve cakes as representing the twelve tribes is an association as ancient as it is natural, widely taken up in tradition and scholarship (Scholarly and historical discussion); this sermon takes up that reading but labels its level. The text's own emphasis lies not in the symbolism behind the number but in the three actions of the next verses: frankincense laid on, renewal every sabbath, and the priests eating the bread.

(Fact) The first action is the frankincense: "And thou shalt put pure frankincense upon each row, that it may be on the bread for a memorial, even an offering made by fire unto the LORD." (Lev 24:7, KJV) Frankincense, Hebrew ləḇōnāh (Strong's H3828), is a precious aromatic resin, used also in the grain offerings (Summary, referring to its use in Leviticus 2). It is laid on each row to serve "for a memorial" — the term for that portion of an offering which is burned and so brings the whole before God in remembrance (Summary). The bread itself is not burned; the frankincense is offered by fire in its stead: the twelve cakes stand for a week, and what ascends for them as an offering is that pinch of frankincense taken by the fire. (Application) "Memorial," in worship, is never nostalgia. The fragrance of the frankincense "signs" the week's bread: this is no ordinary flour stored in a sanctuary, but an offering that carries fragrance, counts as a sacrifice, and is remembered by God. It is laid on every row — not one row with and one without: the remembrance must be complete, as the two rows must be complete.

(Fact) The second action is the renewal, and the third is the eating of the bread; two verses state them together: "Every sabbath he shall set it in order before the LORD continually, being taken from the children of Israel by an everlasting covenant." (Lev 24:8, KJV) Verse 9 says the bread shall be Aaron's and his sons', and they shall eat it in the holy place, for it is most holy to him of the LORD's offerings made by fire, by a perpetual statute (Summary). Look closely at the logic of these three verses: the bread comes from the children of Israel (v. 8, "being taken from the children of Israel" — the supply of the bread comes from the people, exactly parallel to the supply of the oil); the bread stands before the LORD for a whole week, from one sabbath to the next; at the term it is renewed — new cakes go up, the old come down and belong to the priests, to be eaten in the holy place. (Application) This is a complete cycle: the people offer, the bread stands before God, the priests receive. No link may be pulled out — if the people do not offer, the table stands empty; if the bread is not set out, the offering shrinks to a momentary gesture; if the priests do not eat, the cycle breaks halfway and the offering becomes a museum piece. And the words "an everlasting covenant" sit at the very centre of verse 8: a weekly changing of bread is called a covenant. Here a covenant is not a document signed once, but a relationship maintained by fifty-two repetitions a year. The "continually" of tāmîd here takes a different rhythm from the lamp's: the lamp is nightly, the bread weekly — one by the day, one by the sabbath. The keeping of worship never runs at a single speed: some lessons are done daily, some weekly; break the rhythm, and the "continually" breaks.

(Fact) Now look back at the pattern of the whole passage: the oil is brought by the people (v. 2), and the bread's material is the people's too (v. 8, Summary); the lamp is before the LORD (vv. 3-4), and the bread is before the LORD as well (vv. 6, 8); the lamp's rule is "a statute for ever in your generations" (v. 3), and the bread's rule is "an everlasting covenant" and "a perpetual statute" (vv. 8-9). (Application) When the first nine verses are done, the picture inside the sanctuary is this: a lamp by night, bread by day; light and food alike supplied by all the people, tended by the priests, standing continually before God. Israel sleeps in tents and cannot see inside the sanctuary, but they know: the oil they sent is burning in the lamp, the flour they sent is lying on the table — their whole people stands, continually, before the LORD. That is the theology of the first half: a representative standing-before. The people cannot all enter the sanctuary, but their oil and their bread stand there in their place. A sermon might well stop at this warm picture — but the text does not stop. At verse 10 the noisy camp bursts in: striving, cursing, blasphemy. As quiet as the sanctuary is, so real is the camp; in the next section we watch what treatment the Name receives in the camp.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The case of the blasphemed Name: waiting for direction, one manner of law, eye for eye

(Fact) The narrative of the case begins with the man's identity (v. 10, Summary): the son of an Israelite woman, whose father was an Egyptian, went out among the children of Israel, and in the camp he and a man of Israel strove together. Notice first what the text calls him: throughout, he is given no name; he is called only by his mother's and father's peoples — the Israelitish woman's son, the Egyptian's son. (Fact) Verse 11 records the act, and his mother's name: "And the Israelitish woman's son blasphemed the name of the LORD, and cursed. And they brought him unto Moses: (and his mother's name was Shelomith, the daughter of Dibri, of the tribe of Dan:)" (Lev 24:11, KJV) The narrative here sets up a sharp contrast: the offender is nameless, but his mother has name, father's name, and tribe — Shelomith, daughter of Dibri, of the tribe of Dan. (Scholarly and historical discussion) Why the mother's lineage alone is recorded, scholarship discusses without agreement: perhaps because the question of jurisdiction turns precisely on his maternal descent — his mother an Israelite, his father an Egyptian, his own belonging in the camp was ambiguous, and the striving may have arisen from that (the text does not say so, and this sermon makes no determination); or because the narrative means to show that, ambiguous as his descent was, he is nevertheless tried as one of the camp, under one law. This sermon sets the observations side by side and supplies no motive the text has not given.

(Fact) His offence is built of two verbs. The first is "blasphemed," Hebrew nāqaḇ (Strong's H5344): the word's root sense is to pierce, to bore through, and by extension to specify, to name distinctly — in this chapter's usage, to pronounce the Name expressly and blaspheme it (Summary, combining the word's sense with its use in this passage). The second verb is "cursed," Hebrew qālal (Strong's H7043), whose root sense is to make light of, to hold in contempt, and by extension to curse — to speak of its object as worthless and wish it ill. The two words stand together: he pointed out the Name that may not be taken in vain, and with contempt he cursed. (Fact) "The name" itself is the theme-word of the passage: Hebrew haššēm (šēm, Strong's H8034, with the definite article), literally "the Name" — not any name, but the Name of that One. Just before this chapter, Leviticus has spent a whole chapter (ch. 22, Summary) commanding that the name of the LORD not be profaned; the case of chapter 24 is that commandment's first head-on collision in the camp. (Application) And note the place of the offence: not in the sanctuary, not beside the altar, but in a fight in the camp. The place where the Name is most threatened is usually not the place of worship, but the mouth of a man who has lost his temper. In the first nine verses the Lord of the Name receives the most meticulous service in the sanctuary; at the opening of the second half the Name is dragged out and blasphemed in a camp quarrel — the contrast between the chapter's two halves is sharp enough to need no added sentence from the preacher.

(Fact) The procedure of the case is the part of this section most worth reading slowly. The people do not deal with him on the spot: they bring him to Moses (v. 11), and then they put him in ward, until the mind of the LORD should be shewed them (v. 12, Summary — the KJV at v. 12 reads, "And they put him in ward, that the mind of the LORD might be shewed them."). This step is the hinge of the whole passage: the camp faces a case it has never judged; the congregation does not lynch him in anger, Moses does not decide alone out of experience; the whole camp holds the man in custody, shelves the case, and waits for God to speak. (Application) This is the quietest, and the weightiest, moment in Leviticus's theology of justice: waiting. A holy society is not the society that reacts fastest, but the one most willing to wait for direction. In verses 13-14 the direction comes: the LORD speaks to Moses — the one who cursed is to be brought forth outside the camp; all who heard him are to lay their hands on his head; and all the congregation is to stone him (Summary). Each detail carries weight: the execution is outside the camp — the blasphemous words are not dealt with inside the camp; the sin is carried out of it, as the scapegoat of the Day of Atonement carries sin into the wilderness (Summary, by way of comparison); those who heard lay on their hands — the witnesses declare publicly: we heard it, we testify, we have no part in this sin; responsibility for the judgment begins with testimony; and the whole congregation acts — the verb is rāḡam (Strong's H7275), to stone, to kill by stoning: the penalty is not the business of an execution squad but of the whole camp, because the Name blasphemed is the whole camp's Name. (Fact) Then, in verses 15-16, the case rises into a general statute (Summary): whoever curses his God shall bear his sin; and he that blasphemeth the name of the LORD shall surely be put to death, and all the congregation shall stone him — with this express addition: as well the stranger, as he that is born in the land, when he blasphemes the Name, shall be put to death.

(Fact) Immediately after this general statute, the text inserts a whole section of legislation (vv. 17-22) — the famous "eye for eye" passage. By this sermon's stated discipline, it is set out here at the (Fact) level only, in its own wording and scope, without exposition. Its content is: he that killeth any man shall surely be put to death (v. 17, Summary); he that killeth a beast shall make it good, life for life (v. 18, Summary); if a man causes a blemish in his neighbour, as he has done, so shall it be done to him (v. 19, Summary); and its formula sentence runs: "Breach for breach, eye for eye, tooth for tooth: as he hath caused a blemish in a man, so shall it be done to him again." (Lev 24:20, KJV) He that killeth a beast shall restore it, and he that killeth a man shall be put to death (v. 21, Summary). The section's general rule stands at verse 22: "Ye shall have one manner of law, as well for the stranger, as for one of your own country: for I am the LORD your God." (Lev 24:22, KJV) The "one manner of law" is in Hebrew mišpāṭ ʾeḥād — mišpāṭ (Strong's H4941) is judgment, statute, a just determination, and ʾeḥād is one, a single one: one standard of judgment, not two. The two personal terms are the skeleton of the passage: "the stranger" is gēr (Strong's H1616), the sojourner, the resident outsider; "one of your own country" is ʾezrāḥ (Strong's H249), the native-born, one sprung from the land itself. (Fact) In the text's own order of statement, this section's sense of distinction is unmistakable: injury to man and injury to beast are legislated separately — a beast's life is closed by restitution (vv. 18, 21), while a man's life and a man's body are dealt with in their own proportion (vv. 17, 19-21); and every one of these rules is capped, as to its scope, by verse 22: one manner of law for stranger and native alike. In other words, within this text the proportion principle occupies the place of a measure for sentencing, and the "one law" principle the place of scope of application: measure in the penalty, no distinction in the persons.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) This "eye for eye" section is known in scholarship as the lex talionis; formulae of the same type appear also in Exodus 21 and Deuteronomy 19 (Summary, by way of comparison), differing in detail and setting: Exodus places it within a collection of injury cases, Deuteronomy within the law of the false witness, and this chapter sets it after the verdict in a case of blasphemy. Whether it was executed literally, or whether it established a ceiling of proportion under which compensation was in practice substituted, ancient interpreters and modern scholarship have disputed for centuries; this sermon sets the dispute out as undecided and determines neither side — a sermon that dogmatically pronounced on its history of execution here would exceed what this text can bear. (Fact) One point, however, is expressly carried by this text itself and belongs to no dispute: this legislation is spoken inside the verdict of the blasphemy case (the address of vv. 13-23 opens with the LORD speaking to Moses, Summary), and it is sealed with "for I am the LORD your God" — its source of authority and its scope of application are both written within the text itself.

(New Testament reading) The New Testament has an explicit corresponding reading of "eye for eye," set out, as throughout, only at this level — not read back into this text, and not harmonised with it: in Matthew 5, Jesus cites the old saying, "An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth," and then, with "resist not evil" and the turning of the other cheek, re-sites the disciple's response when he is wronged (Matt 5:38-42, Summary); and Romans 12 commands, "Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord." (Rom 12:19, KJV), handing the sovereignty of vengeance back into God's hand. (Application, within this level) These two New Testament readings address the individual disciple's revenge and right of retaliation when wronged; they are not a repeal pronounced upon the text of Leviticus. At its own level, this passage remains itself: the camp's judicial measure of proportion, and its principle of one law. Read the two levels separately, and neither is wronged.

(Fact) The case is closed at verse 23: "And Moses spake to the children of Israel, that they should bring forth him that had cursed out of the camp, and stone him with stones. And the children of Israel did as the LORD commanded Moses." (Lev 24:23, KJV) Note the narrative's wording: the executors are not Moses but the children of Israel; the place is out of the camp; the manner is the very manner the verdict prescribed. (Application) From the striving "in the camp" of verse 10 to the bringing forth "out of the camp" of verse 23, the case runs its full course: occurrence, referral, custody, waiting, direction, promulgation of the general rule, execution. Not one step is private; not one step is emotional. This sermon asks its hearers to remember especially the step in the middle — custody and waiting. The mistake a camp most easily makes is to seize the right of condemnation in anger; the camp of Leviticus first learned something else: hold the man, close the mouth, hand the case upward, and wait until the LORD shews His mind. That is not slowness; that is holiness. A community unwilling to wait for direction can keep neither the lamp nor the Name.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: the sanctity of the Name, protected in worship and in justice alike

(Fact) The chapter's seal is the last sentence of verse 23: "And the children of Israel did as the LORD commanded Moses." (Lev 24:23, KJV) This sentence-form is the standard receipt of completed execution in Leviticus and Numbers: the word went out from the LORD, was spoken through Moses, was done by the people — the circuit closed. Does the chapter's opening carry such a receipt for the first half's ordinances? It does not — lamp and bread are regular ordinances, and a regular ordinance's receipt is not in any one verse but in the nights and sabbaths of generation after generation. At the chapter's end the case has a receipt, because a case is once-for-all: it happened, and there must be an accounting; it was judged, and the judgment must be executed; it was executed, and the record notes, "did as commanded." (Application) Two obediences, two receipts: one written in days, one written in the case-file. A life of faith that has only case-file obedience — moving only when something happens — will let lamp and bread fall into neglect; a life that has only the obedience of days — keeping on with bowed head and asking no questions of right and wrong — will find no witness in the camp when the Name is blasphemed. Leviticus 24 binds the two obediences into one chapter because a man needs them both.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, holiness is "continually": pure oil, fine flour, a lamp night by night, bread week by week — in Leviticus, holiness is first of all not a peak experience but tāmîd: continual, regular, unbroken. What a man's relation to God is truly worth is seen not in his fervour on the feast days, but in whether the lamp still burns in his ordinary nights. Second, holiness is "before": the lamp is before the LORD, the bread is before the LORD, and a man lives before the LORD too. The sense of "before" is the sense of presence: knowing that nothing one does escapes that One's watching, the offering dare not be mixed with turbid oil, and speech dare not make light of that One's Name. Third, holiness is "one manner of law": one law for the stranger and for him that is born in the land; priests and people under the same scrutiny; sanctuary and camp belonging to one God. If holiness is graded — by rank, by inside and outside, by near and far — it is no longer the holiness of Leviticus. As that One's Name is honoured in the sanctuary, so it is to be honoured in the camp's quarrels, in a neighbour's injuries, and in a sojourner's case — for I am the LORD your God: in this chapter that sentence is both the reason for the offering and the signature on the verdict.

(New Testament reading) The New Testament takes up this chapter at several further points, stated, as throughout, only as a layer of reading: in John 8 Jesus calls Himself the light of the world (John 8:12, Summary), and the church's memory of the sanctuary's lamp is in the New Testament directed toward Christ Himself; First Corinthians 10 reads Israel's wilderness history as an example written for later generations (1 Cor 10:11, Summary); and Hebrews 12 reminds its readers that they are come unto mount Sion and the city of the living God, and warns them not to refuse Him that speaketh (Heb 12:22-25, Summary). (Application) What these readings mean for today can be gathered into one sentence: the questions of Leviticus 24 have not expired — Is your lamp still burning? Where does God's Name stand in your reckoning? Do you keep one manner of law for the stranger and for the native? Every generation must stand before this chapter and answer those three questions again.

(Application) One last word on the difficulty of preaching this chapter. Its first half is quiet as the sanctuary at midnight; its second half is heavy as the stones of an execution ground. A preacher is tempted to take only one half: lamp and bread only, making a gentle doctrine of worship and skirting the blood of the case; or the case only, making a stern word of warning and skirting the tenderness of lamp and bread. Either way, the text is wronged. The chapter's editor has deliberately nailed the two into one chapter: because in God's sight, the people who offer to Him and the people who blaspheme His Name may be people of one and the same camp; the hand that tends the lamp and the hand that casts the stone are the hands of one and the same company. To preach this whole chapter is to preach one whole fact: God's Name is honoured both in the lamp-fire of the sanctuary and protected in the justice of the camp; it deserves pure olive oil and fine flour, and it deserves the whole camp's quiet waiting for direction before Him. May we learn from this chapter its two lessons: tend the lamp well in the night when no one sees, and guard the Name well in the camp where everyone stands — continually, before the LORD.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Leviticus 24 sets me in two places: the sanctuary by night, and the camp by day. In the sanctuary Aaron orders the lamp from the evening unto the morning before the LORD continually — no audience, no applause, only pure oil olive beaten for the light, supplied little by little, or the lamp goes out. I see that what my life of faith usually lacks is not a summit but this night shift: whether prayer continues when no one watches, whether the bread is still set in order when the days are plain. The twelve cakes on the table, renewed every sabbath, are called an everlasting covenant: a covenant, it turns out, can be kept as concretely as once a week, on time. Then the text leads me into the camp: a striving, a curse, and the whole camp stops — they put the man in ward, waiting until the mind of the LORD should be shewed them. What moves me most is that waiting: they neither seized the right to condemn in anger, nor let the matter drop, but handed the case upward. How many of my own words each day are spoken unwaited, unthought, uncommitted to Him? May I learn first the sanctuary's "continually," and then the camp's waiting: fill the lamp at night, guard the mouth by day; keep more reverence for the name of the LORD, more honest measure toward a neighbour's hurt, and one manner of law for the stranger and for him that is born in the land. My smallest step this morning: tonight, before sleep, take ten minutes in silence, and let tomorrow's words pass first before Him.

8. nāqaḇ (H5344): root sense, to pierce, to bore; it also carries the sense of specifying, naming distinctly; BDB sets out a distinct branch of usage, "to curse, blaspheme," at Leviticus 24. 12. The lex talionis in its three parallels: Exodus 21:23-25; Leviticus 24:19-20; Deuteronomy 19:21 — the formula is the same, the settings differ.

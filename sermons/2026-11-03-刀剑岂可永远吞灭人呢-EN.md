> Reading guide — five things must be fixed before teaching this chapter. First, David went up to Hebron because he asked, not because he seized: after the lament (2 Samuel 1), he did not march north to grab the throne or coerce anyone; he asked, and asked even which city. Do not turn this chapter into a success manual of clever power-taking: its first key is inquiry — anointing and entry come after asking, and the order itself is theology. Second, two kingdoms side by side is the chapter's reality; do not paint it as unity: Judah anointed David, Abner made Ish-bosheth king, and God's promised kingship for David first landed on one tribe, one city, seven years and six months. Do not skip those seven and a half years and jump straight to king over all Israel; God's promise in time often lands first on one corner, then reaches the whole. Third, the contest at the pool of Gibeon was no friendly match gone wrong: Abner proposed that the young men "compete" (the original word carries both play and combat; commentators' readings are registered, not adjudicated), and twenty-four men died together, one against one — a commander spending young men's lives as a prelude. The pulpit must condemn a leadership culture that treats subordinates and the young as chess pieces, not romanticize the contest as heroic excitement. Fourth, before recording Asahel's death, the text records that Abner twice urged him back, saying plainly that he did not want to kill him and afterward could not face his brother Joab: the killer had his reluctance, the victim his stubbornness; this sermon tells both sides honestly and does not stir one-sided emotion. Still less should Asahel's death be preached as a simple hymn to heroic martyrdom — swift feet are no sin; refusing counsel and chasing the wrong target are the lesson. Fifth, register, without developing, the root of disaster at the chapter's end concerning Joab: Joab recorded his brother's death, and Abner's spear henceforth hung between the two houses; Joab's later killing of Abner to avenge his brother (see 2 Samuel 3, registered) has its root at this pool. Today's hearers must not use this chapter's ending to endorse any revenge in advance; what the chapter teaches is Abner's line — "Shall the sword devour forever?" — and the side of Joab that answered by blowing the trumpet to stop the war.

## Synopsis

2 Samuel 2 is the ugliest possible opening of a new era: not acclamation by all, but north–south division. On one side, David inquires of God, is directed up to Hebron, moves with his whole camp's families, and the men of Judah anoint him king over the house of Judah — a kingdom as small as one tribe, whose first royal act is to send messengers thanking the men of Jabesh who buried Saul. On the other side, Saul's old commander Abner crosses the river to Mahanaim and makes Saul's remaining son Ish-bosheth king; his listed realm is large, but its foundation is one commander alone. The forty-year-old king reigns two years, while David must sit in Hebron for seven years and six months: on which side time stands, the chapter gives half its answer early. In the second half, the two armies meet at the pool of Gibeon; a contest turns into a melee; young Asahel, swift as a gazelle, chases Abner and will not stop, refuses two warnings, and dies under the butt of a spear. At sunset Abner stands on a hill and cries, "Shall the sword devour forever?" — and Joab blows the trumpet and calls off the pursuit. The battlefield is counted: David's side has lost twenty men; Abner's side, 360 have fallen. This sermon treats three things: when a man who has waited many years receives his place, he first inquires and first thanks his benefactors — kingship begins with gratitude; a puppet's crown, however large, cannot outlast one commander's arm; and once the sword is drawn within one people, the most important lesson is that before dark someone is willing to cry stop, and someone is willing to blow the trumpet.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, after the lament is sung, life must still move forward. Today we stand at Hebron's gate and at Gibeon's pool: teach those who have waited many years, when the opportunity truly comes, not to reach out and grab, but first to open their mouths and ask; teach the newly lifted up to make their first act not a banquet of celebration, but thanks to those who kept loyalty in defeat. Stop, we pray, the Gibeons among us: how many families, teams, and churches sit down across a pool, and one word about a contest is spoken and can never be taken back. Raise up those willing to cry, "Shall the sword devour forever?", and give us hearts that can hear the trumpet and stop our feet. For the young among us who run fast yet are chasing the wrong direction, send someone to turn back twice and urge them, and give them ears willing to heed counsel. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: The First Step after the Lament

Join chapter 1 to this chapter. Chapter 1 ends in tears: David tears his clothes, fasts, and composes the Song of the Bow; this chapter opens with a question: "Shall I go up into one of the cities of Judah — may I?" A man who has just sung a lament takes as his first step not enthronement but a question — the steadiness of that step is the steadiness of the next seven and a half years. Note how hard the timing of his inquiry was: Saul dead, the throne empty, Jonathan also dead; his anointing (1 Samuel 16) had waited more than ten years by some reckonings, and by human calculation this was precisely the moment to race for time — in the north Abner might set up a new king at any moment, and division might harden at any moment. Exactly at the moment when he should have sprinted, David slowed down and asked two questions. Waiting is not a virtue that wastes time; it is the discipline of one who, at the critical moment, still lets God arrange the first step. The chapter has three parts. Part One, inquiry and anointing (vv. 1–7): two questions, up to Hebron, the whole household moves north, Judah anoints, and the first dispatch is thanks to the men of Jabesh. Part Two, two kingdoms (vv. 8–11): Abner crosses the river and makes Ish-bosheth king; forty years old, reigning two years; David in Hebron seven years and six months. Part Three, at the pool of Gibeon (vv. 12–32): sitting across the pool, a contest turning into a melee, Asahel chasing to his death, Abner crying out, Joab blowing the trumpet, the count and the burial. The chapter has three inner threads. The thread of asking: in 1 Samuel 30 David asked whether he would overtake; here he asks whether he may go up, and to which city — David's schooling as king grew out of the inquiry he learned as a fugitive; his identity changed, his way of asking did not. The thread of thanks: his first royal document goes not to his own men but to the men of Jabesh — as far as gratitude is remembered, so rightly does kingship walk. The thread of the sword: the chapter's second half moves from the contest's swords, to the butt of a spear, to the sword that devours, tightening step by step, until a cry and a trumpet call it back; this chapter is a miniature of all the internal war in 2 Samuel: how the sword is drawn, and how it must be called back into its sheath.

In reading this chapter, set three measures. First, do not write Abner off in one stroke as a treacherous minister: in this chapter he sets up a young lord and proposes the contest — both open to judgment — but he also twice urges Asahel back and is the first to cry for the fighting to stop; his later negotiation with David and his killing by Joab carry their own verdicts (see 2 Samuel 3, registered), and this sermon argues only from this chapter. Second, do not preach Ish-bosheth as a joke: the text gives him almost not one word of his own; he is forty, lifted onto a throne by a commander, widest in listed realm, and his silence itself is the footnote of his tragedy; preach him with the weight due a man manipulated by others, without mockery. Third, do not miss the line in vv. 5–6, "May the LORD show steadfast love and faithfulness to you": steadfast love (hesed) and faithfulness (emet) are the last warmth in the web of loyalty around Saul's house; David's words to the men of Jabesh will later become the measure by which the whole book measures David himself — how he treats Saul's house, this chapter first sets the vow, and the pulpit should hold up this measure first (see the line through 2 Samuel 9 and 21, registered).

## Part One: Inquiry and Anointing (2 Samuel 2:1–7)

### 1. "Shall I go up into one of the cities of Judah?" (2 Sam 2:1)

After this, David inquired of the LORD: "Shall I go up into one of the cities of Judah?" The LORD said, "Go up." David said, "To which shall I go up?" He said, "To Hebron" (see 2 Sam 2:1, condensed rendering of the gist).

To inquire: the original שָׁאַל (šāʾal, H7592), to ask, to inquire. According to BDB, s.v. שאל. [1] To go up: עָלָה (ʿālâ, H5927), to go up, to set out. According to BDB, s.v. עלה. [2] Hebron: חֶבְרוֹן (Ḥeḇrôn, H2275), a city name in the hill country of Judah. According to BDB, s.v. חברון. [3] Look first at the shape of the question: David does not ask, "May I be king now?" — anointed for years, he does not demand that crown from God; he asks about one very concrete small thing: whether to go up to a city of Judah, and to which city. God's answer also comes in two steps: first permission to go, then the direction. Hidden in this exchange is a basic skill in discerning God's will: the great direction He had long fixed (the anointing of 1 Samuel 16); the concrete steps must still be asked one by one. Those who take the great direction and then arrange all the details themselves usually die in the details. Look also at the weight of the name Hebron: it is where Abraham bought the cave of Machpelah to bury Sarah (see the line of Genesis 23), the city Caleb received as his inheritance in old age (see the line of Joshua 14); the patriarchs' tombs are all there. God did not send him into Jerusalem — that city was still in Jebusite hands (see 2 Samuel 5, registered); He settled him beside the patriarchs' tombs to begin. A dynasty's starting point is not the future capital but the family graveside: only one who knows where he comes from walks steadily toward where he must go. Preach this verse to everyone standing at the door of a new opportunity: do not ask the biggest question first; ask about this step before your eyes; do not rush to pick the most glamorous landing place; hear clearly which city He points to.

### 2. "With the men who were with him, everyone with his household" (2 Sam 2:2–3)

So David went up there, and his two wives also, Ahinoam and Abigail; and David brought up the men who were with him, everyone with his household, and they lived in the towns of Hebron (see 2 Sam 2:2–3, condensed rendering of the gist).

The men who were with him are the six hundred; the households are the wives and children carried off at Ziklag in 1 Samuel 30 and recovered. David's move north is not one man going to sit on a throne: he moves the whole camp, families in tow, into the towns around Hebron. This seemingly domestic stroke is in fact the new regime's first roster: those who wept with him, pursued with him, and shared the spoil with him enter the new reign without one missing. How a man who receives a place settles his old companions is the first public display of his character — some, once elevated, first replace everyone with new faces; David, once elevated, first gives his old people homes. The names of the two wives are also on the list: Abigail, the one who stopped him at Carmel and urged him not to shed blood (see the line of 1 Samuel 25, treated in an earlier sermon in this series). Writing her in the first row of the enthronement list is like setting a warning permanently at the king's bedside: at the king's most triumphant moment, someone must still be able to stop his hand. Note the text's restraint: it records no procession into Hebron, only that they lived in the towns. Before he was anointed, he first became captain of a migrating company and neighbor to many households; kingship's first lesson is not command, but settling people.

### 3. "The men of Judah came to Hebron and anointed David king" (2 Sam 2:4a)

The men of Judah came to Hebron, and there they anointed David king over the house of Judah (see 2 Sam 2:4a, condensed rendering of the gist).

To anoint: מָשַׁח (māšaḥ, H4886), to smear with oil, to anoint. According to BDB, s.v. משח. [4] This is David's second anointing: the first was Samuel's secret anointing in his father's house at Bethlehem (see the line of 1 Samuel 16) — God's choice before God; this one is the tribe of Judah's public acclamation — human recognition before men. Years separate the two anointings — after Samuel's oil was poured on his head, he went back to the fields to shepherd, played the lyre in the palace, was hunted, and lived under another's roof; the oil skipped no stretch of road for him. Fix this line of waiting for your hearers: God's anointing never cashes out as a shortcut; the first anointing is promise, the second is the promise's first station as it lands in time, and the wilderness and Ziklag in between are part of the anointing, not an interlude outside it. See also the scope: the house of Judah — one tribe only. The promise was all Israel; the kingdom first reached only Judah. How many, because the first version of a promise's fulfillment is smaller than they imagined, suspect the original oil was false; this chapter states it here: small is not false; one corner is not the whole. Only one who can keep a small kingdom for seven and a half years can receive the all-Israel that comes later.

### 4. The First Document: Thanking the Men of Jabesh (2 Sam 2:4b–7)

When they told David that the men of Jabesh-gilead had buried Saul, David sent messengers to the men of Jabesh-gilead: "You showed this loyalty to Saul your lord and buried him; may the LORD bless you. May the LORD show steadfast love and faithfulness to you, and I will do good to you because you have done this thing. Let your hands be strong and be valiant, for Saul your lord is dead, and the house of Judah has anointed me king over them" (see 2 Sam 2:4b–7, condensed rendering of the gist).

Steadfast love: חֶסֶד (ḥesed, Strong H2617), covenant love, kindness, loyal treatment. According to BDB, s.v. חסד. [5] Faithfulness: אֱמֶת (ʾĕmet, H571), truth, faithfulness. According to BDB, s.v. אמת. [6] Be strong: חָזַק (ḥāzaq, H2388), firm, strong. According to BDB, s.v. חזק. [7] Notice how surprising this document's destination is: the men of Jabesh are east of the Jordan, Saul's old troops and old beneficiaries, by geography the northern camp's territory; David's first act on the throne is to thank and bless a group of corpse-buriers in the opposing camp. See the structure of the thanks: he first calls their deed a good thing done to Saul, addressing Saul with a subject's courtesy as "your lord" — no grabbing, no belittling; then he promises a double recompense — "May the LORD show steadfast love and faithfulness to you" is God's share, "I will do good to you" is his; only at the very end does he announce his own anointing and urge their hands to be strong: the announcement takes one sentence, placed last. This is thanks first, proclamation afterward. Politically this is of course also conciliation, but per the reading guide, the pulpit's argument rests not on calculation but on order: David first publicly acknowledges that Saul deserved this burial and that the righteous men of Jabesh deserve blessing, and only then does his own throne come up. A new leader's character is visible in whom his inaugural document mentions first: benefactors first, or himself first. This first document also plants a foreshadowing for the whole book: as Saul's house later withers, how David redeems his own words about treating Saul's house with steadfast love and faithfulness — the story of Mephibosheth (see 2 Samuel 9, registered) comes to settle accounts with this first document.

### Ignition Point One: Ask to the End, Then Go Up

Here I stop and speak to one kind of person: the door you have waited for seems to be opening — the position waited on for years has loosened, the opportunity has softened, the one blocking the road is gone. Such moments are the most dangerous: how many fall in a sprint at the last step. The Amalekite young man who brought the news rushed to claim credit (2 Samuel 1): he delivered the crown and received a sentence. Saul rushed (1 Samuel 13): he would not wait for Samuel, offered the sacrifice himself, and lost the kingdom. David, standing on the same threshold, became a third kind of man: he asked. One question was not enough; he asked a second: "To which city?" Today — not after everyone else has moved — take that great matter you think is settled, break it into step after step, and ask God again. You may long have known the direction; the place and the timing He must still point out Himself. If you are willing to ask to the end, Hebron opens its gate for you; sprint one step, and Ziklag's fire is still ahead. The line between those who receive a place and those who lose it often lies between these two questions.

## Part Two: Two Kingdoms (2 Samuel 2:8–11)

### 1. Abner Brings Ish-bosheth across the River (2 Sam 2:8)

The scene shifts east of the river: the anointing at Hebron has just been completed, and the remnant of Saul's house is also being arranged.

But Abner son of Ner, commander of Saul's army, took Ish-bosheth son of Saul and brought him over to Mahanaim (see 2 Sam 2:8, condensed rendering of the gist).

Abner: אַבְנֵר (ʾAḇnēr, H74), son of Ner, Saul's cousin, commander of the whole army. According to BDB, s.v. אבנר. [8] Commander of the army: the original שַׂר־צָבָא (śar-ṣāḇāʾ), chief of the host — śar (H8269) plus ṣāḇāʾ (H6635). According to BDB, s.v. שר, s.v. צבא. [9] Ish-bosheth: אִישׁ־בֹּשֶׁת (ʾÎš-bōšet, H378), Saul's youngest son. According to BDB, s.v. איש־בשת. [10] Mahanaim: מַחֲנַיִם (Maḥănayim, H4266), "two camps," a place east of the Jordan. According to BDB, s.v. מחנים. [11] Crossing the river is a retreat eastward: Philistine pressure made the west bank untenable, so Abner moved the surviving royal house east of the river for safety. The place was not chosen at random: Mahanaim is where Jacob, returning home, met God's messengers and called the place God's camp (see the line of Genesis 32, cross-read below); years later it became the capital of a regime propped up by human hands. The earlier Mahanaim was God's camp; the later Mahanaim has only Abner's one camp of men. See also who the subject of the king-making is: it is Abner who makes him king over the territories — the next verse says so plainly. Ish-bosheth is made king; he does not make himself king, still less is he chosen by the people. If a king is only a flag in a commander's hand, whose regime this really is stands plain on the ledger. Preach this layer to all who are puppets and chess pieces: a title can be borrowed, a foundation cannot; the day the one who lifted you lets go is your day of falling (see the line of 2 Samuel 4, registered).

### 2. Ruling Gilead, the Ashurites, to All Israel (2 Sam 2:9)

Abner made him king over Gilead, the Ashurites, Jezreel, Ephraim, Benjamin, and all Israel (see 2 Sam 2:9, condensed rendering of the gist).

Set the two realm-lists side by side: David — the house of Judah, one tribe; Ish-bosheth — Gilead, the Ashurites, Jezreel, Ephraim, Benjamin, plus the words "all Israel," nominally almost Saul's whole realm. On the ledger the north has won: far more territory, and legitimacy as Saul's own son. Yet Scripture's narrative eye is never on acreage but on foundation: the longer the north's list, the more single its source — all conquered by Abner, guarded by Abner, reported by Abner; David's realm is one tribe only, yet beneath it stand inquiry, anointing, and the people's hearts, all three. Here overturn for your hearers a common way of reckoning: in assessing a situation do not first count how many squares it occupies; ask first what it stands on. As for Abner's list, this sermon registers without adjudicating: the exact referent of "the Ashurites" (the territory of the tribe of Asher, or a place name) is disputed among commentators, registered at cross-review; the list's rhetorical effect is clear enough — the less stable a regime's foundation, the fuller it needs its titles written.

### 3. Forty Years Old, Reigning Two Years; Seven Years and Six Months (2 Sam 2:10–11)

Ish-bosheth son of Saul was forty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned over Israel two years; but the house of Judah followed David. The time David was king in Hebron over the house of Judah was seven years and six months (see 2 Sam 2:10–11, condensed rendering of the gist).

Two numbers side by side are the chapter's quietest verdict. Forty years old — no longer a youth, in life's strongest years — and he reigns two years, and those two years propped up by another. David was thirty when he began to reign (see 2 Sam 5:4, registered), and on one tribe in Hebron he first sat seven years and six months. Seven and a half years against two: God's timetable and man's propping-up timetable settle their accounts in numbers. How the two years and the seven and a half fit together — whether Ish-bosheth came to the throne only in the later part of David's Hebron period, whether some years in between had no king in the north — commentators read differently; this sermon only registers, and does not adjudicate. [25] What the pulpit must actually preach is this question: do you envy the two years that first filled the whole map, or the seven and a half that sat steady on one corner? Rushed glory has one common disease: it comes whole and goes whole. Abner could in one night put Ish-bosheth on the throne of the whole realm precisely because that throne never grew from Ish-bosheth's own root. David's seven and a half years are anxiously slow, yet year by year they count: thanking the men of Jabesh, marrying and fathering sons (the line of 2 Samuel 3, registered), the tug-of-war with the north — all slowly growing, in those seven and a half years, into the later king of the whole nation. Slowness is no virtue in itself; slow with roots — that is the thing.

## Part Three: At the Pool of Gibeon (2 Samuel 2:12–32)

### 1. Sitting across the Pool (2 Sam 2:12–13)

Abner and the servants of Ish-bosheth went out from Mahanaim to Gibeon; Joab and David's servants went out and met them at the pool of Gibeon, and they sat down there, one group on this side of the pool and the other on that side (see 2 Sam 2:12–13, condensed rendering of the gist).

Gibeon: גִּבְעוֹן (Giḇʿôn, H1391), an important town in Benjamin's territory. According to BDB, s.v. גבעון. [12] Pool: בְּרֵכָה (bərēkhâ, H1295), a pool. According to BDB, s.v. ברכה. [13] Joab: יוֹאָב (Yôʾāḇ, H3097), David's nephew, chief of his army. According to BDB, s.v. יואב. [14] Look first at the restraint of the scene: the two armies meet and do not immediately slaughter; they first sit down across the pool. A pool of water lies between them; the two groups look at each other — they are not foreigners but one people, children of the same patriarch; not a few may well know each other. Gibeon carries an older account as well: once the Gibeonites by a ruse made a covenant with Joshua, and the Israelites let them live without inquiring (see the line of Joshua 9, treated in an earlier sermon in this series); now at Gibeon's pool Israel again moves the sword on a mere proposal — again without inquiring. Preach this sitting-down to your hearers: civil war is usually ignited not on the battlefield but in such a sit-able moment, lit by one light word. Sitting across the pool is itself an opportunity: they could talk, withdraw, each go home. While the pool is still between them, everything is still in time; sadly, the man who opens his mouth is Abner.

### 2. "Let the young men arise and compete" (2 Sam 2:14–17)

Abner said to Joab, "Let the young men arise and compete before us." Joab said, "Let them arise." So twelve arose on each side and stood opposite; each seized his opponent by the head and thrust his sword into his opponent's side, and they fell together; therefore that place was called Helkath-hazzurim, in Gibeon. The battle was very fierce that day, and Abner and the men of Israel were beaten before David's servants (see 2 Sam 2:14–17, condensed rendering of the gist).

To compete / play: the original שָׂחַק (śāḥaq, H7832), to play, to wrestle, to fight — both ends of the meaning coexist. According to BDB, s.v. שחק. [15] Helkath-hazzurim: חֶלְקַת הַצֻּרִים (Ḥelqat-haṣṣurîm, H2521), a place name whose meaning commentators read differently ("field of sword-edges," "slippery field," etc.); registered, not adjudicated. According to BDB, s.v. חלקת הצרים. [16] Take this passage apart sentence by sentence. First, the proposer is the commander, the dead are the young: Abner says "before us" — he and Joab are the audience, twenty-four young men are the show. A leader's momentary temper is billed to the youngest heads; that is this passage's first charge. Second, Joab agrees too fast: "Let them arise" — a taunt from the other side, an echo from this side. How many battles are not necessary at all, but happen because neither leader will be the first to lose face. Deliver this lesson to everyone sitting in Joab's seat: to be provoked into battle is not courage; it is being led by the nose. Third, the outcome is twenty-four dying together: each seizes the other's head, sword in the other's side — at that closeness it is no longer victory or defeat, it is dying together; the place name nails the event to the map, and later passers-by at Gibeon must read the name aloud. Fourth, the contest's end is not the end but a general assault: "The battle was very fierce that day." One-to-one deaths ignite the whole army's deaths. Set a boundary here: there is no such thing as violence as entertainment; watch a fight as a show, and the show escalates into war. The moment the twenty-four young men fall, there are no more spectators at the pool — both sides have become actors.

### 3. The Man Swift of Foot as a Wild Gazelle (2 Sam 2:18–21)

Joab's two brothers Abishai and Asahel were also there; Asahel was swift of foot, like a gazelle of the field. Asahel pursued Abner and did not turn right or left. Abner looked back and said, "Is it you, Asahel?" He said, "It is I" (see 2 Sam 2:18–21a, condensed rendering of the gist).

Asahel: עֲשָׂהאֵל (ʿĂśâ-ʾēl, H6214), Joab's brother. According to BDB, s.v. עשהאל. [17] Gazelle: צְבִי (ṣəḇî, H6643), a gazelle. According to BDB, s.v. צבי. [18] The three sons of Zeruiah — Joab, Abishai, and Asahel — are the bravest and hardest-to-manage household in David's army (their mother Zeruiah was David's sister; see the line of 1 Chronicles 2, registered). This passage first states Asahel's capital: swift feet like a gazelle. That is real ability: in a routed army, the only man who can stay on the enemy commander's heels is he. Yet what the text next teaches is precisely how capital becomes a death warrant: he pursued Abner and did not turn right or left — at first the line praises his focus; read on, and it becomes a warning: he chases too single-mindedly, so single-mindedly that he cannot hear words spoken back to him. Abner recognizes him and first calls his name: "Is it you, Asahel?" To confirm who the other is before killing, in a fleeing commander, is already a rare measure of clarity. Preach this verse to all young people who live by speed: speed is a gift, but the swift most easily stake their whole lives on a target that should not be chased; the faster you run, the higher the price of a wrong direction, because by the time you hear the counsel, you are closer than anyone to that spear.

### 4. Abner Twice Urges Him to Turn Aside (2 Sam 2:22–23)

Abner said again, "Turn to your right or your left, seize one of the young men and take his spoil." But Asahel would not turn aside. Abner said to him again, "Turn aside; why should I strike you down? If I struck you down, how could I face your brother Joab?" He still would not turn aside, so Abner struck him down with the butt of his spear, and the spear came out at his back; he fell and died there, and everyone who came to the place where Asahel had fallen and died stood still (see 2 Sam 2:22–23, condensed rendering of the gist).

Spear: חֲנִית (ḥănît, H2595), a spear. According to BDB, s.v. חנית. [19] Read Abner's two urgings in full: the first gives a way down — go catch a young soldier, take a set of spoil; honor is still available. The second appeals to relationship — if I kill you, afterward I cannot face Joab. This is a fleeing general's last restraint toward his pursuer: he knows clearly that killing this man makes a mortal feud with Joab, and once that feud is tied, the words he himself cries at this chapter's end — "Shall the sword devour forever?" — can hardly be taken back. Asahel heeds neither urging. This sermon does not beautify the stubbornness: he may have taken the commander's urging as cowardice, stopping as shame, or in youthful temper had only one thought — capture the commander and win fame in one battle. The most expensive tuition on a battlefield is here: a step down offered by an enemy is sometimes a real step down, not all of them are snares; one who cannot tell counsel from bait will die of his own not-turning-back. Note also the butt of the spear: Abner uses not the spearhead but the tail end (the exact construction of this verse and the precise entry point are registered among commentators' details, retained as registered at cross-review) — as if at the last moment still trying to drive the man back rather than stab him, yet the force still kills. Restraint that at the final step does not hold the hand back is, on the moral ledger, still killing; this sermon spares neither side: the one who was urged and would not listen paid with his life, the one who killed with incomplete restraint owes a blood debt, and that debt will be collected in the next chapter in another way (see 2 Samuel 3, registered). The last line is very quiet: everyone who came to that place stood still. A young man's corpse stops pursuers better than any order — only death can make a man stand still when he runs hardest; may God teach us to hear counsel before that stop, without waiting for it.

### 5. "Shall the sword devour forever?" (2 Sam 2:24–28)

Joab and Abishai pursued Abner; at sunset they came to the hill of Ammah, beside Giah on the way to the wilderness of Gibeon. The Benjaminites gathered behind Abner into one company and stood on a hilltop. Abner called to Joab: "Shall the sword devour forever? Do you not know that the end will be bitter? Why do you not tell the people to turn back from pursuing their brothers?" Joab said, "As God lives, if you had not spoken, the people would not have stopped pursuing their brothers until morning." So Joab blew the trumpet, and all the people stood still and pursued Israel no more, and fought no more (see 2 Sam 2:24–28, condensed rendering of the gist).

To devour: the original אָכַל (ʾākhal, H398), to eat, to devour. According to BDB, s.v. אכל. [20] Forever: נֶצַח (neṣaḥ, H5331), long duration, perpetuity. According to BDB, s.v. נצח. [21] Trumpet: שׁוֹפָר (šôpār, H7782), a horn trumpet. According to BDB, s.v. שופר. [22] Hill of Ammah: אַמָּה (ʾAmmâ, H522), a place name. According to BDB, s.v. אמה. [23] The man who speaks these words is Abner — the same man who earlier proposed the contest. Within one day the same person first lights the fire, then cries stop: this sermon therefore neither preaches him as a prophet nor erases the words; truth is truth from whosever mouth it comes, and before these words were spoken, hundreds had already fallen at the pool and on the hillside road (for the count, see next section) — it is truth with a price in blood. Savor his three sentences: "Shall the sword devour forever?" — a question to the sword's endlessness; "Do you not know that the end will be bitter?" — a prophecy of vengeance recoiling; "Why do you not tell the people to turn back?" — he points the brake's location to Joab: only the one leading the troops can order a stop. Joab's answer has a rare honesty: "If you had not spoken, we would not have stopped until morning" — he admits the pursuers had lost their brake, and admits this cry saved both sides. Then, the trumpet. At the trumpet's sound, the whole army stands still. This passage is where the chapter's theme is most concentrated; preach the order clearly: first someone willing to cry stop, then someone willing to hear the cry, finally the trumpet's sound carried out as an order. Missing any one of the three, more would die tonight. How many families and groups, worn down to white heat, lack not the truth but one of these three: no one cries, or cries and is not heard, or is heard and never becomes a rule. This chapter gives a complete ceremony of stopping a war; one ring short is not a stop.

### 6. The Count, Burying a Brother, Marching All Night (2 Sam 2:29–32)

Abner and his men went all night through the Arabah, crossed the Jordan, passed through all Bithron, and came to Mahanaim. Joab returned from the pursuit and gathered the people; counting David's servants, besides Asahel nineteen men were missing; but David's servants had struck down 360 of the Benjaminites under Abner. They carried Asahel and buried him in his father's tomb at Bethlehem; Joab and his men marched all night and arrived at Hebron at daybreak (see 2 Sam 2:29–32, condensed rendering of the gist).

Bithron: בִּתְרוֹן (Bitrôn, H1338), a district east of the Jordan. According to BDB, s.v. בתרון. [24] Read the battle report's numbers aloud as they stand: David's side, nineteen men plus Asahel, twenty lives in all; Abner's side, 360 men. [26] Victory and defeat look plain, yet Scripture does not let the chapter stop at numbers: its last two strokes are burial and a night march. David's side has no music for its triumph: they carry the commander's brother back to Bethlehem into the family tomb, then the whole army marches all night and reports at Hebron at daybreak. "Marched all night" echoes across the pages the night journey of the men of Jabesh who recovered the bodies in the previous book (see the line of 1 Samuel 31, treated in an earlier sermon in this series): in this book, wherever there is great compassion and great responsibility, people walk night roads. Settle the chapter's emotion precisely here: this is not a night of victory; it is a night of burial. And leave one registered line for the next chapter, undeveloped: Joab buried his brother with his own hands, and the account of Abner's spear is already recorded in Joab's heart; in this chapter Abner asked whether the sword should devour forever, and Joab answered by blowing the trumpet — whether he can keep that trumpet's sound for a lifetime is one of the most gripping questions in the rest of 2 Samuel. Our lesson today lies exactly here: one who has promised to stop a war must prove with the rest of his life that the trumpet was not one night's emotion.

### Ignition Point Two: Today, Blow the Trumpet

Here I stop, speaking not to armies but to those still trapped inside: in your family, your team, your church, is there a sword that has already devoured too many? One sentence dug up and it is another battle; one old account raised and it is another night; passed down a generation, even the children can recite the ledger of grudges by heart. Abner's question today takes its turn asking you: shall the sword devour forever? Must you really wait until "the end will be bitter" — until someone falls, someone leaves, someone never comes back in this lifetime — before you believe this sentence? Do not wait. Today, not waiting for the other side to bow first: if you lead the team, you give the order to turn back first; if you are the elder, you close your mouth and do not answer back first; if you are the side being chased, learn from Abner and cry the sentence aloud from the hilltop. The one who blows the trumpet loses no face; shame belongs to the one who knows they are brothers and still chases until dark. Today God puts this trumpet into your hand — blow it. The first sound of a ceasefire always comes from the one willing to lay down the sword first.

## Cross-Reading: Four Passages Read Together

This chapter's events have echoes elsewhere in Scripture; four cross-readings nail it down. Old Testament one: Mahanaim's two camps — Jacob, returning home after parting from Laban, met God's messengers and said, "This is God's camp," and called the place Mahanaim, meaning two camps (see Genesis 32:1–2, condensed rendering; key sentences checked against the Chinese Union Version, registered at cross-review). Abner retreats the royal house to Mahanaim, borrowing the old name's dignity to prop up appearances; read together, the question emerges: if the camp in a place name is no longer God's camp and only one camp of human remnants remains, no name however resounding can save a regime. Old Testament two: Hebron's beginning — what Caleb asked Joshua for forty-five years later was precisely Hebron; he said, see, I am still strong — "give me this hill country" (see Joshua 14:10–12, condensed rendering). Read together: David's going up to Hebron is not taking advantage; it is receiving a city that only faith and years can hold — the ground of a beginning the forebears waited for and asked for, never grabbed. Psalms one: brothers dwelling in unity — "Behold, how good and pleasant it is when brothers dwell in unity!" (Ps 133:1, Chinese Union Version). Psalm 133's traditional superscription is David's (psalm title registered); set this psalm back into this chapter and it stings all the more: the man who sang of brothers in unity is the very man who watched brothers kill each other across a pool — unity is not his daydream; it is the state policy he set standing beside civil war's pile of corpses. Proverbs one: strife and its stopping — "The beginning of strife is like letting out water, so quit before the quarrel breaks out" (Prov 17:14, Chinese Union Version); and the warning against avenging oneself: "Do not say, 'I will repay evil'" (see Prov 20:22, condensed rendering). Read both with this chapter: the contest at the pool is the first crack of water let out; Abner's belated cry and Joab's trumpet are the late practice of quitting a quarrel. New Testament one: all who take the sword — the Lord Jesus, stopping Peter's sword in Gethsemane, said, "Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword" (Matt 26:52, Chinese Union Version); and the teaching on living with others: "If possible, so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all; do not avenge yourselves" (see Rom 12:18–19, condensed rendering). Read together, the lesson of Gibeon's pool is gathered by the Lord's words into a New Testament rule: the sword's account only ever snowballs; the first sound of stopping a war always belongs to the one who hears the trumpet first.

## Application: Six Kinds of People

First, to those who have waited many years and whose opportunity has just loosened. David's two questions are your homework this season: do not walk the last step with a grabbing heart. Break that great matter into which step goes where and when the next step comes, ask each clearly, then act; the nearer the door, the slower the heart must be. Concrete homework: this week, set aside a whole period of prayer for that matter at the threshold, and ask only two things — whether to go up, and to which city — not when it will be your turn to be greatest.

Second, to those newly lifted up and newly taking office. Learn first from David's first document: in your first week in office, first publicly thank the most loyal people of the old team, first settle the old companions who shared hardship with you, first keep a decent remembrance of your predecessor — then talk about your new policies. Order is caliber: thanks first, proclamation afterward, and your seat will be upright.

Third, to Abner-type figures — capable people propping up a wreck, holding up a situation that cannot be held up. In this chapter you lit a fire and also cried stop; count honestly: is the foundation of the situation you prop up God's promise, or only your own arm left? If the latter, the best thing you can do is not to write the titles fuller, but to bring the situation back to the right road early, even at the cost of face. The sequel is on record: Abner in the end goes to negotiate with David (see 2 Samuel 3, registered) — the road back has always been there; walking it early kills fewer people than walking it late.

Fourth, to Asahel-type young people — fast runners, tight trackers, refusers of counsel. Your speed is a gift; your refusal of counsel is a risk. Two concrete rules: designate in advance two people whose turning-back words you are willing to hear, fix their names, and call them first when things happen; when an elder or an opponent offers a step down, stop three minutes first, tell whether it is bait or a step, then decide whether to run. A fast runner's first lesson is being able to brake.

Fifth, to those trapped in attrition within family, team, and church. Learn this chapter's complete three steps of stopping a war: someone willing to cry, someone willing to hear, and making it a rule. Tonight do one thing: write "Shall the sword devour forever?" in your own words and hand it to the side facing you; or set one new rule for this month — old accounts may be discussed only at one fixed time and one fixed place each month, and when the talk ends, blow the trumpet and dismiss; no digging up accounts at midnight.

Sixth, to parents and mentors. In the audience seats at the pool sat commanders; those who fell were young men: how adults talk about competition and how they use the young determines on which battlefield the next generation dies. This week's concrete homework: in front of your children, call a stop to one pointless contest — one quarrel, one comparison, one conversation pitting your child against another's in a contest — stop it on the spot and say why; children learn to stop wars from watching adults blow the trumpet.

## Closing Prayer of Response

Lord, we gather 2 Samuel 2 into response. Teach the waiting to ask to the end, then go up; teach those taking office to write "thanks" first in their first document; teach those propping up wrecks to recognize their arm's limit and be willing to turn back to the right road; teach swift young people ears that can hear counsel, and a turning-aside before the spear's point; teach families and churches trapped in attrition to blow today the trumpet that stops the war, and not let the sword devour another year. For the twenty-four young men of the contest in this chapter, for Asahel, for the 360 fallen Benjaminites, we mourn before You and resolve before You: our generation will be trumpet-blowers, not spectators sitting across the pool. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Extract (for daily devotion, 300–500 words in the original)

In 2 Samuel 2, David did not grab the kingship. He asked the LORD: May I go up? To which city? Only after receiving the answer did he go up to Hebron, and the men of Judah anointed him king over the house of Judah — the promise was all Israel, but its landing was first one tribe, seven years and six months. His first act on the throne was to send messengers thanking the men of Jabesh who buried Saul, promising steadfast love and faithfulness (see 2 Sam 2:1–7, condensed rendering). In the same chapter's second half, Abner and Joab's two armies met at the pool of Gibeon; one word about a contest killed twenty-four young men; Asahel chased Abner and would not stop, heeded neither of two urgings, and died under the spear; at sunset Abner cried, "Shall the sword devour forever?" and Joab blew the trumpet and called off the troops (see 2 Sam 2:14–28, condensed rendering). Three questions for meditation today: the step at my threshold — have I asked it to the end? In my first document in office, do I name benefactors first, or myself first? In the tug-of-war I am in, who will blow the trumpet — could it be me? Prayer: Lord, teach me to ask to the end before going up, to thank before proclaiming, and to be willing, before dark, to blow the trumpet that stops the war.

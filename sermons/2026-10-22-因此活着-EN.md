# He Shall Live by Them — A Sermon on Leviticus 18

**Textual basis statement:** English Scripture quotations are taken verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), checked verse by verse against the published KJV text of Leviticus 18 (checked 2026-10-23). The Chinese original quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation edition, verified verse by verse; this English version is a full translation of that Chinese sermon, but its Scripture quotations are the KJV text itself, never a re-translation from the Chinese. Verses not quoted verbatim are presented in clearly marked summaries.
**Approach:** This sermon teaches the chapter's structure and theological framework rather than rehearsing the list item by item; disputed questions are presented with the positions on each side and the limits of the evidence stated, without a verdict.

**Abstract:** This chapter stands at the core of the Holiness Code: the prologue first declares the identity of the redeeming God and only then issues ethical boundaries; verse 5, “he shall live in them,” shows that the statutes aim at life; the central list expands in concentric circles from near kin outward; and the closing section warns in the language of land theology — Canaan was vomited out for defiling the land, and Israel, if it imitates Canaan, will meet the same end. Six occurrences of “I am the LORD” frame the whole chapter.

## I. 00:00–08:00 Prologue: Redemption First, Ethics Second (18:1–5, Introduction)

Brothers and sisters, as we open Leviticus 18, let us not hurry into the list at the chapter's centre. (Fact) Its location in Leviticus must first be made clear: scholars commonly call Leviticus 17–26 the “Holiness Code,” whose keynote — you shall be holy, for the LORD your God is holy — recurs across its chapters. Chapter 18 stands near the front of that code as one of its core chapters, treating the boundaries of family and sexual ethics and the relation of those boundaries to the fate of the land. (Application) But note the order in which the code speaks: it does not first lay down rules and then speak of identity; it first declares identity and then issues boundaries. That order itself is the first preaching point of this sermon.

(Fact) Verse 1 is the formulaic revelation heading — the LORD speaks to Moses and charges him to address Israel — a heading that recurs throughout Leviticus and marks what follows as delivered revelation, not Moses' opinion. Verse 2 then gives the chapter's first self-declaration, in the KJV: “Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, I am the LORD your God” (18:2). Here “I am the LORD” renders the Hebrew 'ani YHWH, the most distinctive formula of the Holiness Code, often called the divine self-declaration formula (see Note 1).

(Fact) How often this formula occurs in the chapter must be counted exactly, not estimated. Verse by verse: verse 2, “I am the LORD your God”; verse 4, “I am the LORD your God”; verse 5, “I am the LORD”; verse 6, “I am the LORD”; verse 21, “I am the LORD”; verse 30, “I am the LORD your God” — six occurrences in all, three in the full form “I am the LORD your God” and three in the short form “I am the LORD.” Their placement also matters: three cluster in the prologue, one stands at the head of the list, one near its end, and one at the close of the warning section, forming an inclusio that wraps the entire list inside the identity of the covenant God. (Application) That means the first ground of every prohibition in this chapter is neither “society has always done so” nor “such things are improper,” but “because I am the LORD your God.” The root of ethics is theology: first who this God is, then how His people ought to live.

(Fact) The prologue next closes two directions of imitation. The KJV of verse 3 reads: “After the doings of the land of Egypt, wherein ye dwelt, shall ye not do: and after the doings of the land of Canaan, whither I bring you, shall ye not do: neither shall ye walk in their ordinances” (18:3). Egypt is where Israel has been; Canaan is where Israel is going — one behind, one ahead. The text shuts both doors at once: Israel's ethical reference point lies neither in the land of its past slavery nor in the land of its future residence, but in the God who has just declared Himself. (Fact) The word rendered “ordinances” at the end of verse 3 is chuqqot (statutes, Strong H2708, plural) used pejoratively — the very same word that verses 4 and 5 use for God's own “statutes.” Other nations have their “statutes,” and God has His; the two sets stand in deliberate verbal contrast (see Note 2).

(Fact) Verse 4 gives the positive command: “Ye shall do my judgments, and keep mine ordinances, to walk therein: I am the LORD your God” (18:4). Three verbs tighten in sequence: do the judgments, keep the ordinances, walk in them. (Application) Read verses 2 to 4 together and the chapter's logic is plain: first the fact of redemption — this God is the God who brought Israel out of Egypt, and “your God” is a possessive Israel did not earn but received in an accomplished deliverance; then, and only then, the ethical demand. Redemption precedes ethics; identity precedes behaviour. That is the consistent order of Old Testament law, and the preface to the Ten Commandments follows it too. This chapter is not a freestanding anthology of customs detached from salvation; it is the instruction of a God who has already saved, addressed to the people He saved, about how they are now to live.

## II. 08:00–24:00 “He Shall Live in Them”: Verse 5 and the Direction of the Statutes (18:5)

(Fact) The prologue reaches its peak in verse 5, from which this sermon's title is taken. The KJV reads: “Ye shall therefore keep my statutes, and my judgments: which if a man do, he shall live in them: I am the LORD” (18:5). Note the actual wording of the standard Chinese text at this point — “he shall live by them” — from which the Chinese title of this sermon is drawn directly, not substituted from another version. In the Hebrew, the key clause reads literally, “the man who does them shall live in them”: the verb of “living” shares its root with “life,” and its primary reference is flourishing, enduring, blessed existence in the promised land within the covenant, not an abstract concept detached from the body (see Note 3).

(Fact) The history of this verse's citation inside Scripture must likewise be told in layers, not flattened into one. (Fact — textual layer) Ezekiel 20 recalls this saying and notes that Israel already broke it in the wilderness; the confession in Nehemiah 9 likewise recalls that the law had shown that a person who obeys would live by it. These are inner-Old Testament echoes, showing that the verse was always remembered in Israel's faith as a real and solemn promise. (New Testament reading layer) In the New Testament, Paul cites this verse in Romans 10:5 and Galatians 3:12, using it to articulate a principle of “living by doing” and setting it in contrast with the principle of “living by faith.” That is the New Testament writers' theological reading of the verse, belonging to the New Testament layer of its interpretation history. This sermon marks that layer honestly: it neither reads the later contrast straight back as the verse's only meaning in Leviticus, nor denies the legitimacy of the New Testament citation. (Application) In its own Levitical setting, the verse's immediate function is to orient the whole list that follows: the prohibitions ahead are not given to crush life but to preserve it. Here the boundary is not the destination; it is the road — the one who walks in it “shall live in them.”

(Fact) The verse's wording holds one more detail that must not be passed over: it pairs “statutes” and “judgments,” a pair already used in verse 4. In the Hebrew, “statutes” is chuqqot (plural of H2708), enacted and decreed regulations; “judgments” is mishpatim (judgments, rulings, Strong H4941), norms established by adjudication. The pair recurs throughout the Holiness Code and together covers both written statute and case-based norm — the whole of God's revealed order for life. (Fact) Set this beside verse 3: Canaan's customs are also called chuqqot. The same class of word is used on both sides — for the nations' “abominable customs” and for God's “statutes.” The contrast amounts to a declaration: every community lives under some set of “statutes”; the question is never whether there will be norms, but which norms will be obeyed, and under whom as covenant Lord. (Application) Read verse 5 within that contrast and its weight grows clearer: the verse is not neutral between two ways of life; it asserts that life is found in God's set of statutes. The nations' chuqqot lead toward being “cut off” and “vomited out”; God's chuqqot lead toward “living.” The chapter's whole tension is already embedded in the vocabulary of its prologue.

(Fact) Finally, note the correspondence between this verse and the closing section: the prologue says the one who obeys “shall live,” while the conclusion says the one who commits these abominations “shall be cut off from among their people” (18:29, summary). “Live” and “cut off” are the chapter's two end-point words, one at its head and one at its tail, with the entire list set between them. (Application) That framing is not rhetorical decoration; it tells the hearer that this chapter was never about matters of taste, but about the survival of the community. In this chapter's vision, how a person treats near kin, neighbour, and child is directly bound to whether this people continues to “live” in the land. In the next section we enter the list itself, and watch it unfold layer by layer, from the inside outward.

## III. 24:00–42:00 The Concentric Circles of the List: From Near Kin Outward (18:6–23)

(Fact) The list opens with a general heading. The KJV of verse 6 reads: “None of you shall approach to any that is near of kin to him, to uncover their nakedness: I am the LORD” (18:6). This heading rests on two Hebrew anchors. First, “near of kin” renders she'er besaro, literally “the flesh-kin of his flesh,” the circle of closest blood relations. Second, the fixed idiom repeated through the whole list, “to uncover the nakedness of…,” combines the verb gillah (to uncover, Strong H1540) with the noun 'ervah (nakedness, shame, Strong H6172). (Fact) In the Old Testament 'ervah can denote physical nakedness or the state of exposed shame; in this fixed idiom it is a restrained, dignified legal euphemism for sexual relations, and Leviticus 20 restates the same idiom with penalties attached (see Note 4). The text uses this measured legal language item by item in order to draw boundaries, not to dwell on detail; this sermon keeps the same restraint, treating structure and category rather than rehearsing each item.

(Fact) Structurally, verses 6–18 form the list's first ring, dealing with boundaries inside the kinship circle: first direct and closest relatives, then paternal and maternal collateral lines, then relations by marriage. Scholars organise these verses differently — some by degree of kinship from nearest outward, others by category of household relation — but both readings agree on one point: the list begins with the closest, most everyday, least guarded circle of relationships. (Application) That beginning is itself an ethical judgment: boundaries are not drawn first for strangers, but first for the nearest relationships. The closer the relationship, the more it needs clear definition to protect it, rather than licence to overstep in the name of closeness. The Holiness Code puts ethics' first lesson inside the home — a pointed contrast with the modern instinct to concentrate moral attention on the public sphere.

(Fact) Verses 19–23 form the list's second ring, pushing outward from kin: the prohibition concerning a woman in her menstrual impurity, the prohibition concerning a neighbour's wife, the prohibition of giving one's offspring to Molech, the prohibition of male-male intercourse, and the prohibition of bestiality (the foregoing is a category summary of these verses, not verbatim quotation). The internal order of this ring deserves notice: it is no longer confined to kin, but enters neighbour relations and worship relations; the Molech item does not even concern sexual ethics as such, but the object of worship and the treatment of children. Scholars widely observe that inserting this item into a list of sexual ethics shows the chapter's concern is broader than any single domain: household order, neighbour order, and worship order are treated in this chapter as one fabric, and disorder in any one of them defiles the whole land (see Note 5).

(Fact) At the level of vocabulary, this ring introduces three evaluative nouns that are the key to the chapter's ethical tone. First, verse 17 uses zimmah (lewdness, Strong H2154) for the case of a woman and her daughter; in the Old Testament the word denotes deliberate, corrupting sexual wrongdoing, heavier in tone than a general offence. Second, verse 22 and verses 24–30 repeatedly use to'evah (abomination, Strong H8441), what is adjudged abhorrent before God; Deuteronomy uses the same word for idolatry and for the customs of the nations. Third, verse 23 closes with tevel (confusion, Strong H8397), literally “mixing, disorder” — bestiality is adjudged, in this chapter's language, precisely as a confusion of the created order (see Note 6). (Application) These three words name the same reality from three angles: from the nature of the act, zimmah; from God's evaluation of it, to'evah; from its effect on order, tevel. The list's language is therefore not merely a list of “thou shalt nots” but a naming system: it teaches the community what to call these acts, and that teaching function is part of what the law is for.

(Fact) In terms of literary form, one further point must be made, or readers will mistake chapter 18 for a complete legal code. The prohibitions of this chapter are cast almost entirely as absolute commands — direct second-person “you shall not” sentences, without casuistic “if a man…” clauses and without attached penalties. Chapter 20 stands in deliberate contrast: it restates many of the same matters and attaches specific penalties, up to death and cutting off. The standard scholarly explanation of the two chapters' relation is that chapter 18 is ethical proclamation and instruction, while chapter 20 supplies the judicial enforcement provisions; the former answers “how shall we live?”, the latter “what is done with the offender?” (Application) That division of labour governs how this chapter should be preached: its tone is pastoral exhortation, not a sentencing code, and its stated purpose, given in the warning function of verses 24–30, is to turn people back before the act, not merely to punish after it. To read this chapter as a catalogue of penalties is to misread both its form and its intent.

(Fact — scholarly discussion) Concerning Molech (Molek, Strong H4432), it must be stated plainly that this remains a live scholarly question with real limits to the evidence. At the textual level, verse 21 (summary) forbids letting one's offspring pass through fire to Molech and forbids profaning the name of God; Kings and Jeremiah likewise speak of children being burned as offerings in the Valley of Hinnom. At the level of archaeological and textual discussion, scholars have reached no consensus on whether “Molech” is the name of a deity or the name of a type of offering: one reading, appealing to the word mlk in Carthaginian and Phoenician inscriptions, holds that it can denote a class of sacrificial act rather than a god's name; another, following the Old Testament's narrative usage and the Septuagint tradition, maintains it as a divine name. Whether the Masoretic vocalisation of the name carries a deliberately disparaging revocalisation is also part of the debate. This sermon simply presents the state of the discussion: Israel was explicitly forbidden to take part in such offerings — that much the text makes clear; as to the exact referent of “Molech,” scholarship has no settled conclusion, and this sermon offers none (see Note 7).

(Fact — scholarly discussion) The semantic scope of verse 22 is the verse on which contemporary scholarly discussion concentrates most heavily; this sermon presents the sides at the level of academic discussion and reaches no verdict. The verse's Hebrew contains the rare phrase mishkevei 'ishah, literally, “the lyings of a woman.” The discussion turns on three points. First, syntax: some scholars argue that the phrase delimits the prohibition's scope, holding that it must be read within the chapter's kinship context and the household structures of the ancient Near East; others argue that the phrase is a fixed idiom meaning “as with a woman,” and that the prohibition's scope is not narrowed by the syntactic analysis. Second, the category of to'evah: one side notes that to'evah is sometimes used in the Old Testament for ritual or cultural categories of abhorrence and argues that the verse's classification within the legal system should be reassessed accordingly; the other side notes that to'evah in Leviticus and Deuteronomy also covers unmistakably ethical matters, and that this chapter's closing section ties these acts directly to the defilement of the land, arguing that its ethical weight should not be reduced by appeal to a ritual category. Third, the history of translation: the Septuagint and the later versions agree in broad terms on this verse, while differing understandings of its wording and scope have persisted throughout its interpretation history. (Statement of evidential limits) All sides work from the same Hebrew text and a limited body of ancient Near Eastern material, and that material is insufficient for any side to declare the discussion closed. This sermon's duty is to lay out the structure and limits of the debate honestly — not to decide it for the hearer, and still less to convert an academic discussion directly into claims about present-day policy (see Note 8).

## IV. 42:00–56:00 The Warning of the Land: Land Theology and the Precedent of Canaan (18:24–30)

(Fact) With the list complete, the chapter's tone widens abruptly at verse 24: from acts one by one, to the accumulated consequence of acts. (Summary) Verse 24 warns Israel not to defile itself in any of these things, because the nations that God casts out before Israel defiled themselves in all of them. Verse 25 then gives the chapter's most distinctive image. The KJV reads: “And the land is defiled: therefore I do visit the iniquity thereof upon it, and the land itself vomiteth out her inhabitants” (18:25). Note the three steps of the verse's narrative logic: first, human acts defile not only the actors but the very land they live in; second, God “visits” the land's iniquity — the verb is the judicial language of God personally intervening to call to account; third, the land performs an almost bodily act of rejection: it vomits out its inhabitants. (Fact) The verb rendered “vomit out” occurs once in verse 25 and twice in verse 28 — three times in this chapter — and Leviticus 20:22 warns Israel with the same verb, forming an inner echo within the Holiness Code (see Note 9).

(Fact) This personification of the land follows a clear trajectory in biblical land theology. Genesis says of Cain's shedding of Abel's blood that the ground opened its mouth to receive his brother's blood from his hand; Numbers says that blood defiles the land; Leviticus 25 says of the sabbath year that the land shall enjoy its sabbaths. In these texts the land is no passive stage but a bearer and witness within the covenant relationship. (Application) The land theology of chapter 18 carries at least three layers of meaning. First, the land is a gift, not a possession: the land Israel is about to enter is not private property it has won, but the place God gives it to dwell in; Israel is sojourner and steward on it, and God is the land's true owner — “the land shall not be sold for ever: for the land is mine” in Leviticus 25 is the same conviction stated outright. Second, ethical acts have public, environmental consequences: in this chapter's vision, private transgressions accumulate into the defilement of the whole land; no one's conduct is ever merely his own affair. Third, judgment has a checkable historical precedent: the expulsion of the Canaanites is not an abstract threat but a historical fact lying directly before this chapter's first hearers.

(Fact) How the closing section deploys that precedent deserves to be traced layer by layer. (Summary) Verse 26 commands Israel to keep the statutes and judgments and to commit none of these abominations, stating expressly that the requirement applies equally to the native-born and to the stranger who sojourns among them — the statutes' constraint is bounded by the land, not by bloodline: whoever lives in this land lives under its ethical order. (Summary) Verse 27 adds a parenthetical retrospect: the people who lived in the land before did all these abominations, and the land became defiled. Verse 28 then turns the warning directly on Israel. The KJV reads: “That the land spue not you out also, when ye defile it, as it spued out the nations that were before you” (18:28). The words “as… also” are the hinge of the chapter's whole warning function: Canaan's fate is not material for Israel's schadenfreude but a mirror — the same land, faced with the same defilement, will give the same response. Election never functions in this chapter as exemption; on the contrary, identity brings a clearer warning.

(Fact — inner-biblical echoes) This chapter's warning left clear echoes in the later historical books — a matter of intertextual fact, not of this sermon's conjecture. When 2 Kings 17 explains why the northern kingdom of Israel was carried into exile, it enumerates, item by item, how Israel imitated the customs of the nations and did what was evil in God's sight; the narrative logic is of one piece with this chapter's closing section: Israel was in fact driven from the land just as Canaan had been. Leviticus 26:33–35 carries the same theme one step further: after the people are scattered, the land will lie desolate and make up the sabbaths of which it was deprived — the land there not only “vomits out” its inhabitants but receives, after their departure, the rest it had been denied. (Application) Set these echoes together and the chapter's warning function is complete: it looks backward to Canaan's accomplished precedent, and forward as the lens through which later generations would interpret a fallen kingdom's history. How a community reads its own history depends on whether it still remembers the words of this chapter.

(Fact) The warning's last layer falls on the individual. (Summary) Verse 29 declares that whoever commits any of these abominations shall be cut off from among his people. “Cut off” renders the karet sanction, a term recurring in the Pentateuch and the Holiness Code for a person's excision from the covenant community; whether in concrete execution it means capital punishment, exile, childlessness, or a penalty God Himself imposes has been variously argued by scholars, and the text here does not say — this sermon presents its gravity without adjudicating the scholarship (see Note 10). (Fact) Verse 30 closes the whole chapter with the self-declaration. The KJV reads: “Therefore shall ye keep mine ordinance, that ye commit not any one of these abominable customs, which were committed before you, and that ye defile not yourselves therein: I am the LORD your God” (18:30). From “I am the LORD your God” in verse 2 to the same sentence in verse 30, the chapter forms a complete frame: however long the list, it stands throughout inside that declaration. (Application) Review the closing section's rhetorical order — first history, then possibility, then the individual — and its warning function becomes clear: it is not intimidation but a chance to see the shape of the precipice before falling over it. The precedent of Canaan is the viewing angle this chapter leaves to every later reader: when you read the list, do not read only other people's sins; read the memory of the land.

## V. 58:00–60:00 Conclusion: Living Within “I Am the LORD”

Brothers and sisters, let us gather this chapter into our hearts in two minutes. (Fact) Its structure can be summed up so: the prologue declares God's identity and the priority of redemption; verse 5 shows that the statutes point toward “living in them”; the central list extends in layers from the inner circle of near kin to the outer circles of neighbour and worship; and the closing section, in the warning of the land, shows that ethical collapse is finally a collapse of survival itself. Six occurrences of “I am the LORD” stand like six pillars holding up the roof of the chapter. (Application) The most searching reminder of this chapter for today's hearer lies not in any single prohibition but in its order of seeing: first who God is, then how people should live; first receive the accomplished deliverance, then speak of the boundaries to be kept; first remember the precedent of Canaan, then examine the ground beneath your own feet. May we keep that order, walking in His statutes — and so live in them. Amen.

## Endnotes

Note 1 (Fact): For 'ani YHWH (“I am the LORD”), Milgrom observes that the formula is the Holiness Code's authentication device, grounding each norm directly in the covenant God Himself rather than in tradition or royal authority. The count of six occurrences in this chapter (18:2, 4, 5, 6, 21, 30) was made by direct enumeration of the text; the full form adds 'eloheikhem, “your God.”

Note 2 (Fact): Chuqqot is the plural of choq / chuqqah (H2706 / H2708), glossed in BDB and HALOT as “enacted statute, established rule.” Wenham's commentary likewise notes the deliberate positive–negative contrast between 18:3 and 18:4–5 in the use of this word.

Note 3 (Fact): In 18:5, “live” renders the verb chayah (to live, H2421), and the prepositional phrase bahem means “in them / by them.” Milgrom reads this “life” as long and flourishing existence in the land; reading it as post-mortem eternal life is a later development in interpretation history, not attested by the verse's own context.

Note 4 (Fact): For the idiom gillah 'ervah, see the HALOT entries on galah (H1540) and 'ervah (H6172); the latter is also used for nakedness and shame in Genesis 9 and elsewhere. Leviticus 20:11–21 restates many of this chapter's prohibitions in the same idiom with penalties attached — the principal textual basis for the two chapters' division of labour.

Note 5 (Fact): Both Milgrom and Hartley note the striking placement of the Molech item (18:21) among sexual-ethics provisions, and, on the basis of its second half (“neither shalt thou profane the name of thy God,” KJV), observe that the verse places loyalty in worship and ethics in the family within a single framework of defilement.

Note 6 (Fact): Zimmah (H2154) is glossed by BDB as “plan, lewdness”; tevel (H8397) as “confusion, unnatural mixing”; to'evah (H8441) occurs over a hundred times in the Old Testament, with objects spanning idolatry, the customs of the nations, and ethical matters — a distribution that itself shows no single category can summarise the word.

Note 7 (Scholarly discussion): For the principal positions and sources on the Molech question, see Day's compilation and study of the human-sacrifice texts and Milgrom's critique of the reading of mlk as a sacrificial term. The interpretation of the Carthage tophet material is likewise unsettled among archaeologists; this sermon therefore records discussion without conclusion.

Note 8 (Scholarly discussion): The representative literature on the syntax of mishkevei 'ishah in 18:22 is Olyan's 1994 article, which argues that the phrase delimits the prohibition's scope; the commentaries of Milgrom and Wenham maintain the broader reading. Both positions and their grounds are presented in the sermon; in view of the limits of the evidence, this sermon adjudicates neither.

Note 9 (Fact): The verb rendered “vomit out / spue out” is qo' (H6958), occurring once in 18:25 and twice in 18:28 — three times in the chapter in all — and again in 20:22; HALOT lists its usage here as a personifying metaphor of rejection. The verb for the land's being “defiled,” tame' (H2930), shares its root with the vocabulary of ritual impurity, showing that this chapter incorporates ethical offences into its purity lexicon.

Note 10 (Scholarly discussion): The concrete execution of karet (cutting off) in the Pentateuch has been variously held to be capital punishment, childlessness, a penalty imposed by God Himself, or expulsion from the community; Wenham and Milgrom do not agree. The sermon states only its core sense — being cut off from among the people — and adopts no one theory as a conclusion.

Note 11 (Fact): The dating and composition of the Holiness Code (Lev 17–26) are argued variously — pre-exilic, exilic, and post-exilic proposals all persist; Hartley's introduction surveys them systematically. This sermon uses the conventional label “Holiness Code” without presupposing any chronological conclusion.

Note 12 (Fact — textual layer): Romans 10:5 introduces its citation of Lev 18:5 with “Moses describeth”; Galatians 3:12 cites the same sentence and sets it over against “faith.” These are the New Testament's two most direct citations of 18:5; Luke 10:28, “this do, and thou shalt live,” is also widely regarded as an echo of the verse. This sermon locates them at the “New Testament reading layer,” stated separately from the Levitical context.

## Devotional Extract

Reading Leviticus 18, the first thing that strikes the eye is not a prohibition but an identity. God's first sentence is: “I am the LORD your God” (KJV, Lev 18:2). He first reminds His people that they are already a delivered people, and only then says how delivered people are to live. The boundaries were never a ticket by which salvation is earned; they are the response of a people already brought out of Egypt to the deliverance they have received. So it is with us today: it is not that God accepts us once we have lived well enough; it is that He has already accepted us, and so His word becomes the road beneath our feet. The warning at the chapter's end is just as searching: Canaan's end is not a story given us for pointing at others, but a reminder that the same God, the same land, and the same standard also stand over us. Do not wait until everything is lost to remember that the boundary was always a protection. The text promises, in the KJV's words: “Ye shall therefore keep my statutes, and my judgments: which if a man do, he shall live in them: I am the LORD” (Lev 18:5). May we take one concrete boundary seriously today — not to appear devout before others, but out of loyalty to Him, for we have never forgotten who it is that commands us. May He keep our homes and our steps, that we remain in the land He has given, living in His grace.

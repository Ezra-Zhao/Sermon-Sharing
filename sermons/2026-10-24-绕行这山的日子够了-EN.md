> Title verification: Five candidates were considered — (1) "Long Enough Around This Mountain," from 2:3 in the base text, "Ye have compassed this mountain long enough: turn you northward"; (2) "Thirty-Eight Years," from 2:14, which covers only the reckoning of years in the middle of the chapter, not the prohibitions on war or the victory; (3) "Not So Much as a Foot Breadth," from 2:5 (Chinese base: "not even ground for a foot to tread on"), which covers only restraint toward the brother peoples, not the battle against Sihon; (4) "Not One City Too Strong for Us," from 2:36 (Chinese base: "there was no city too high for us to take"), which covers only the victory, not the circling or the prohibitions; and (5) "Thou Hast Lacked Nothing," from 2:7, which covers only the presence in the wilderness, not the whole journey of the chapter. Candidate (1) is adopted: the whole of this chapter's thirty-seven verses — turning back and circling (vv. 1–8), crossing Moab and the brook Zered (vv. 9–15), drawing near Ammon with the parenthetical notes on the giants (vv. 16–23), crossing the Arnon and the hardening of Sihon (vv. 24–31), and the taking of land and its boundary (vv. 32–37) — all turn on that one sentence in verse 3: the days of circling are at an end, and the direction from here is northward. This chapter locks together with chapter 1, end to end: in chapter 1 God says at Horeb, "Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount" (1:6); in this chapter God says at Mount Seir, "Ye have compassed this mountain long enough" — between the two "long enough"s lie thirty-eight years. English title as settled: Long Enough Around This Mountain.

## Main Body

Introduction (00:00–05:00). [Fact layer] Deuteronomy 2 is the second movement of Moses' first discourse (1:1–4:43), immediately following chapter 1's old story of Kadesh. Chapter 1 ends its account of the defeat that followed the presumptuous ascent of the hill with "So ye abode in Kadesh many days" (1:46); this chapter opens by telling how those "many days" were spent and finished: turning back from Kadesh into the wilderness, circling Mount Seir, until the men of war of that generation had wasted away, and the company turned northward, passed through the coasts of three brother peoples and took not an inch, and at last, beside the Arnon valley, fought the first victorious battle for land since the Exodus. [Fact layer] The chapter has thirty-seven verses in all, and its speech structure is registered first: the whole chapter is Moses' retelling in the first person, within which the words of God form four segments — vv. 2–7, 9–13, 17–25, and v. 31 (for the reckoning see Endnote 3); outside the words of God stand Moses' itinerary narrative and two parenthetical registers of peoples' origins (vv. 10–12, 20–23). [Theological-inference layer] The position of this chapter is worth seeing clearly first: chapter 1 is a "looking back" — why they could not go in; this chapter is a "turning around" — how they set out again on the road in. Moses tells the second generation this history and is at pains to record which piece of land might not be touched, which brook was crossed at what point, and how a certain king fell, because the road the second generation is about to walk is the continuation of this very road: taking the land west of the Jordan must be learned from this lesson east of the Jordan — first learn not to take what must not be taken, and only then learn to take what must be taken. [Summary layer] The governing sentence of this chapter can be put like this: the circling has a term, the restraint has a boundary, the victory has a Lord. The thirty-eight years were not walked in vain: God sustained them through the circling, taught them in the restraint to recognize their brothers' inheritance, and taught them in the first victory to recognize the source of victory. What this chapter sets out, section by section, is how these three lessons are laid out one after another. [Fact layer] One thread running through the whole chapter is registered here in advance: the ownership of land. This chapter says the lands of three peoples may not be taken (Edom, Moab, Ammon), and the reason is the same sentence in each case: that land was given to them by God; and it says the land of Sihon must be taken, and the reason is also the same sentence: that land God has given into your hand. Whether to take or not to take — the dividing line lies not in Israel's strength but in God's giving. This is the master switch of every journey and every battle in this chapter, and every section below returns to this one sentence.

### I. Turning Back and Circling: First Pass Through the Brothers' Land, and Take Not an Inch (2:1–8; 05:00–17:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 1 is the first step after the sentence was pronounced; the base text, quoted in full: "Then we turned, and took our journey into the wilderness by the way of the Red sea, as the Lord spake unto me: and we compassed mount Seir many days." Register the items one by one: first, the direction is a "turning back" — at 1:40 God commands a turning back, "take your journey into the wilderness by the way of the Red sea," and v. 1 of this chapter carries it out to the letter (registered paraphrase, mutually confirming); second, the place of the circling is Mount Seir — the hill country of the Edomites, beside the mountain of a hostile kinsman people Israel circled for "many days"; third, how long those "many days" were this verse does not say — the account is not settled until v. 14: thirty-eight years. Moses' retelling deliberately holds the number back: first how they circled, then how long they circled. [Fact layer] In vv. 2–3 God speaks in the midst of the circling; v. 3 is quoted in full from the base text: "Ye have compassed this mountain long enough: turn you northward." — The word "compassed" in the original is sābab (H5437), seen once at v. 1 and once at v. 3: v. 1 records the fact (they compassed many days), v. 3 pronounces the verdict (the days of compassing are long enough — "enough"). The "long enough" stands in the same form as the "long enough" at Horeb in 1:6, "Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount" (registered paraphrase): the "enough" at Horeb commands them to set out and enter the land; the "enough" at Seir commands them to turn northward — to the north lie Moab, Ammon, and the Arnon valley, the direction of the promised land. The circling was not aimless: at the end of the circle a sentence was waiting, waiting for God to say "enough." [Fact layer] Verses 4–6 give the first prohibition, and its object is Edom. Verse 4, quoted in full from the base text: "And command thou the people, saying, Ye are to pass through the coast of your brethren the children of Esau, which dwell in Seir; and they shall be afraid of you: take ye good heed unto yourselves therefore:" Note the form of address: the children of Esau are "your brethren." The old story of Esau and Jacob (Genesis 25, 27, 33; registered as surrounding context) need not be retold here; register only this chapter's form of address: God does not call them enemies; He calls them brethren. Verse 5 is the body of the prohibition, quoted in full from the base text: "Meddle not with them; for I will not give you of their land, no, not so much as a foot breadth; because I have given mount Seir unto Esau for a possession." — The prohibition on striving with them is seen three times in the chapter as a whole (vv. 5, 9, 19); this is the first occurrence. In the KJV the three prohibitions are not worded as one fixed formula; their forms are registered separately in Endnote 4, and at this verse the KJV form is "Meddle not with them." The ground of the prohibition is not that Edom is too strong; it is that Seir has already been given to Esau "for a possession" (yəruššâ, H3425): a brother's inheritance, given by the same God, is not for the covenant people to reach for. Verse 6 gives the rule for passing through, quoted in full from the base text: "Ye shall buy meat of them for money, that ye may eat; and ye shall also buy water of them for money, that ye may drink." — Food must be bought, water must also be bought; even the water they drink may not be taken for nothing: passing through a brother's land, they must not only refrain from seizing, they must pay. [Fact layer] Verse 7 gives the ground of this prohibition, as God accounts for the thirty-eight years of provision in the same breath; quoted in full from the base text: "For the Lord thy God hath blessed thee in all the works of thy hand: he knoweth thy walking through this great wilderness: these forty years the Lord thy God hath been with thee; thou hast lacked nothing." Register item by item: first, "all the works of thy hand" were blessed — the years of circling were not years of standing still; the work of their hands went on, and the blessing kept being given; second, "he knoweth thy walking through this great wilderness" — every step of the circling lay within God's knowing ("know," yādaʿ, H3045, taken up in Endnote 8); third, "these forty years" — the chapter's reckoning of years is given here first as a round number (forty years), and settled as an exact number at v. 14 (thirty-eight years); the relation of the two numbers is left to the discussion layer; fourth, the conclusion is "thou hast lacked nothing": a people lacking land lacked no food, and a company on a circling road lacked no presence. Verse 8 records the execution (registered paraphrase): the company passed by from their brethren the children of Esau who dwelt in Seir, through the way of the plain from Elath and from Eziongaber, and turned and passed by the way of the wilderness of Moab — the whole territory of Edom skirted and passed, not one city taken, not one battle opened; the prohibition was carried out in full. (In the KJV v. 8 the Chinese base's "the way of the Arabah" appears as "the way of the plain"; the difference in the place-name reckoning is registered in Endnote 6.) [Theological-inference layer] This section establishes an ethic of taking land: a promise is not a licence. God's promise that Israel would take land for a possession does not mean Israel may take any piece of land it can see; on the contrary, the first thing to be learned is not taking but drawing back the hand — that the brother's land would not be given, "no, not so much as a foot breadth," blocks off even the smallest unit of greed: not "take no more than a little," but "take not an inch." If a people under promise does not first learn the boundary, it will read the promise as an excuse for plunder; Moses sets this lesson before the battles for the land, and the order itself is the teaching. [Theological-inference layer] The stroke "buy meat, buy water" makes the restraint concrete: restraint is not keeping a wide distance and having no dealings; it is trading as usual and paying a fair price. This matter of paying money for water was close to humiliation in the wilderness — a great host at the border, yet buying drinking water from the brother people; but it was precisely this "humiliation" that preserved the name "brethren." Relationship comes before interest, and the form of address before the sword: Israel's later generations of feud with Edom (registered as surrounding context: Obadiah and others) only bring out the weight of this chapter's lesson. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The crossing of Edom and the parallel account in Numbers 20 differ markedly on the surface; they are set side by side only, not harmonized: Numbers 20 records that Moses sent messengers from Kadesh to the king of Edom, asking to pass along the king's high way, promising not to enter field or vineyard and to pay for water (registered paraphrase); Edom refused and came out with force against them, and Israel turned away from him. This chapter, by contrast, records that God first issued the prohibition, that Israel bought food and water, and that they went around Edom's coast southward past Elath and Eziongaber, with no record anywhere in the section of Edom's refusal or of troops coming out. The readings of scholarship and the commentary tradition are set side by side: one reading takes this chapter as an abridged retelling — Moses' purpose in the discourse is to teach the second generation restraint, so he records only God's prohibition and the people's obedience, omitting the incident of Edom's refusal; another reading takes the two passages as crossings at different stages — what Numbers 20 records belongs to the second year of the Exodus (the Kadesh period), while what this chapter records belongs to the end of the wilderness period, the company travelling northward along Edom's eastern border, the places passed and the circumstances differing; a third reading takes the two passages as two faces of one event — "buying food and water" is precisely the practical form in which the agreement sought in Numbers 20 was carried out, refusal and detour being cause and effect of the same event, this chapter recording it from the side of God's command and Numbers 20 from the side of the negotiation. The fact layer of this sermon registers only this: the two texts differ in detail and angle, while the three elements — asking the way, paying money, going around — appear in both; no verdict is given. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the first lesson of this chapter is not how to fight a battle but how to pass along a road — toward brethren: no striving, nothing taken for nothing, money paid for food and water. The company that circled for thirty-eight years first learned beside Edom's border to draw back its hand, and only then was it fit, beside the Arnon valley, to stretch that hand out.

### II. Moab and the Brook Zered: The Account of Thirty-Eight Years (2:9–15; 17:00–28:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 9 gives the second prohibition, and its object is Moab; quoted in full from the base text: "And the Lord said unto me, Distress not the Moabites, neither contend with them in battle: for I will not give thee of their land for a possession; because I have given Ar unto the children of Lot for a possession." Read against the Edom prohibition, three points are registered separately: first, the Moabites are not "brethren" but "the children of Lot" — Lot's relation to Abraham (Genesis 12–13, 19; registered as surrounding context) places Moab and Ammon alike among the kin peoples, and the nearness of the form of address differs from Edom's; registered separately. Second, the prohibition adds a first half that Edom's lacked: "Distress not" (ṣûr, H6696) — not only may no battle be opened; distressing, siege-like oppression is also forbidden. Third, the ground stands in the same form: Ar has already been given to the children of Lot for a possession. The same God gave land to Esau and gave it also to Lot — Israel's view of inheritance, from this chapter onward, can never be "whatever is not of our people may be taken." [Fact layer] Verses 10–12 form the first parenthetical group, registering the earlier inhabitants of Moab's land (registered paraphrase): formerly the Emims dwelt there, a people great and many and tall, like the Anakims; these Emims also were accounted giants (Rephaim), and the Moabites called them Emims. Formerly the Horims also dwelt in Seir, and the children of Esau destroyed them, succeeded them, and dwelt in their stead — and the parenthesis closes by turning the sentence onto Israel: as Israel did unto the land of his possession, which the Lord gave unto them (registered paraphrase; the quoted analogy sentence as verified in the Chinese base is taken up at the head of Section III and in Endnote 13). This analogy is registered here and held; it will be discussed together with the third section. [Fact layer] At v. 13 the circling reaches its end (registered paraphrase): God commands them to rise up and get over the brook Zered, and they went over the brook Zered. The brook Zered is a valley on Moab's southern border (registered as surrounding context: Numbers 21:12 likewise records the company pitching in the valley of Zered); the act of "getting over" occurs twice in this chapter — getting over the brook Zered (v. 13) and passing over the river Arnon (commanded at v. 24 and carried out thereafter): two valleys, one the end of the circling, the other the start of the taking. (In the KJV v. 13, the command to rise and cross is voiced with "said I" — the speaker on the surface is Moses relaying the charge within the discourse; the Chinese base places v. 13 within the second segment of God's speech. The difference in speech attribution is registered in Endnote 3 and not harmonized.) [Fact layer] Verse 14 is the chapter's only reckoning of years; quoted in full from the base text: "And the space in which we came from Kadeshbarnea, until we were come over the brook Zered, was thirty and eight years; until all the generation of the men of war were wasted out from among the host, as the Lord sware unto them." Recheck item by item: first, the two endpoints — from leaving Kadeshbarnea (the second year, after the events of Deuteronomy 1) until they were come over the brook Zered (the fortieth year); second, the number of years, thirty-eight; third, the content of those thirty-eight years is one thing only: waiting until the men of war of that generation had wasted out from among the host — the "men of war" (in the original, "men of war" as a body; registered paraphrase) are those numbered as soldiers among "the men of this evil generation" of the sentence in 1:35, disappearing from the camp day after day; fourth, "as the Lord sware unto them": the wasting away was not natural attrition; it was the execution of an oath — at 1:34 God "sware" in His wrath, and this verse registers the oath falling due. Verse 15 (registered paraphrase): the hand of the Lord was also against them, to destroy them from among the host, until they were consumed — the narrator adds this stroke so that it be known that the deaths of thirty-eight years were not the climate of the wilderness but the hand of God; the executor of the sentence, from first to last, was God Himself. [Theological-inference layer] The theological centre of this section is the word "until" — the waiting: for thirty-eight years God was waiting for a generation to die out, Moses was waiting, and the children were waiting. The days of the sentence were not blank days: in one and the same stretch of years, God's hand executed judgment in the camp (v. 15), and God's presence also made them lack nothing (v. 7) — judgment and provision walked together for thirty-eight years; this is the deepest layer of the chapter: God's wrath never cancels His care. He can, upon one and the same company, execute an oath on the one hand and give daily food on the other. The reader of this section today has to learn not only reverence but also patience: God's timetable sometimes takes "a generation" as its unit; He is not in a hurry; He lets His oath be fulfilled slowly, and without one omission. [New Testament reading layer] The echoes of this chapter's wilderness generation in the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only: 1 Corinthians 10 says that with many of that generation God was not well pleased and they were overthrown in the wilderness, and that these things were written for the admonition of those who came after (registered paraphrase); Hebrews 3, citing Psalm 95, looks back on the forty wilderness years, attributes them to unbelief and disobedience, and exhorts its readers not to follow that example (registered paraphrase). How the New Testament uses this history is registered at this layer only and is not read back as the original meaning of this chapter of Deuteronomy; this chapter's own attribution is a single sentence: as the Lord sware unto them. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The relation of the two numbers, thirty-eight years and forty years, is set out side by side, with no settled conclusion: one reading takes forty years as the total of the whole journey (reckoned from the Exodus: the first year at Sinai, the second year reaching Kadesh, then thirty-eight years of circling until the crossing of Zered, making forty years in all), so that "these forty years" at 2:7 is the round total of the whole journey and the thirty-eight years at 2:14 the exact stretch within it, the two numbers agreeing; another reading takes forty years as an integer of rhetoric (Scripture often uses forty years for the full span of a generation), the thirty-eight years being the exact record, the one figure rhetorical and the other factual, not needing to be added together; a third reading takes the "forty years" of Numbers 14:34 as the fixed number of the sentence itself (the spies searched the land forty days, a day for a year), the thirty-eight years of Deuteronomy 2:14 being the remainder after deducting the years already passed before and after the Kadesh events. The three readings stand together. The fact layer of this sermon registers only the two numbers' places of occurrence in this chapter and their endpoints: forty years at v. 7 (no endpoints given), thirty-eight years at v. 14 (beginning at the leaving of Kadesh, ending at the crossing of Zered). [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the brook Zered is a dividing line: north of the brook stands the new generation; south of the brook the old generation is buried. Moses has the second generation remember this brook not so that they may congratulate themselves on having survived, but so that they may know: their standing here is not because they are stronger than their fathers; it is because God's oath has been fulfilled to the end, and the page of grace has been turned.

### III. Ammon and the Parenthetical Notes on the Giants: God's Hand in the Histories of Other Peoples (2:16–23; 28:00–39:00)

[Fact layer] Verses 16–17 register the moment of a turning (registered paraphrase): not until all the men of war of that generation were consumed and dead from among the people did the Lord speak to Moses again — note this order: the wasting away of vv. 14–15 had to be complete before the word of v. 17 came. God's words in this chapter form four segments (Endnote 3), and the timing of the third segment's opening (vv. 17–25) is pinned to "after the men of war were dead." The old generation's account was settled; only then did the new generation's road open. [Fact layer] Verses 18–19 give the third prohibition, and the last, its object being Ammon. Verse 18 (registered paraphrase): God charges them that this day they are to pass over through Ar, the coast of Moab; v. 19, the body of the prohibition, is quoted in full from the base text: "And when thou comest nigh over against the children of Ammon, distress them not, nor meddle with them: for I will not give thee of the land of the children of Ammon any possession; because I have given it unto the children of Lot for a possession." The three prohibitions are now complete, and a single reckoning is made: Edom — brethren; Mount Seir given to Esau for a possession (v. 5). Moab — children of Lot; Ar given to them for a possession (v. 9). Ammon — children of Lot; that land given to them for a possession (v. 19). Three peoples, two sources: Esau from one source, Lot from one source. Three prohibitions, one reason: the sovereignty over land lies with God; whom God gives it to, that one has it. [Fact layer] Verses 20–23 form the second parenthetical group, longer than the first (registered paraphrase): the land of Ammon also was accounted a land of giants; giants dwelt there in old time, and the Ammonites call them Zamzummims; that people was great and many and tall, as the Anakims, but the Lord destroyed them before the Ammonites, and the Ammonites succeeded them and dwelt in their stead — as He did for the children of Esau who dwelt in Seir, when He destroyed the Horims from before them, and they succeeded them and dwelt in their stead even unto this day; and the Avims, who dwelt in Hazerim even unto Azzah, were destroyed by the Caphtorims, who came forth out of Caphtor, and the Caphtorims dwelt in their stead. Register group by group: the earlier inhabitants who were replaced are four groups — the Emims (Moab's land), the Horims (Seir), the giants, that is, the Zamzummims (Ammon's land), and the Avims (the region of Azzah/Gaza); those who received land are four parties — Moab, Edom, Ammon, and Caphtor; among them the Caphtorims have no kinship with Israel at all (registered as surrounding context: some scholarship associates the Caphtorims with the later Philistines, taken up in the discussion layer), yet they too stand within the parenthesis. [Fact layer] What recurs within the parentheses is one and the same sentence form: God destroyed the earlier inhabitants before a certain people, and that people took the land and dwelt there. This form occurs in full three times in this chapter (vv. 12, 21–22, 23; registered paraphrase), and at the end of v. 12 Moses applies this very form directly to Israel as an analogy: the children of Esau destroyed the Horims — the Chinese base's analogy sentence, as verified, reads "just as Israel did unto the land which the Lord gave him for a possession" (the KJV at 2:12 reads, in the same place, "as Israel did unto the land of his possession, which the Lord gave unto them"; the analogy sentence is registered here as a text fact, with the exact verified wording carried in Endnote 13) — note the time point: Moses who speaks this stands east of the Jordan; of the land west of the Jordan Israel had not yet taken an inch (the lands of the two kings east of the river are gained only at the end of this chapter, see Section V); this analogy states a thing not yet completed as though it were already an established case. It is registered as a fact of the text; its readings are left to the discussion layer. [Theological-inference layer] These two parenthetical groups are the part of the chapter most easily skipped, and the part that least ought to be skipped. They set Israel's taking of land back into a larger picture: God is not King only within Israel's history — in the histories of Edom, Moab, Ammon, and Caphtor He likewise apportioned land, likewise destroyed giants, likewise settled the peoples. Israel's distinctiveness never lay in "only their God apportions land," but in this: they knew the Lord who apportions the land, and they had received His word. The second generation is about to take Canaan; Moses first lets them see clearly: the land you are going to, its inhabitants too were settled there by God; your taking of land is not because your nation's god is greater than others', but because the God who governs the lands of all peoples has now pointed this piece of land out to you. Such a knowledge is the first lesson in keeping those who take land from arrogance. [Theological-inference layer] The parentheses have a further purpose aimed at fear: in chapter 1, what made the congregation's heart melt was "the Anakims" and "cities … fenced up to heaven" (1:28). The parentheses in this chapter four times mention peoples of giant stature (the Emims as the Anakims; the giants/Rephaim great in stature), and four times the outcome is the same sentence: they were destroyed, and the land changed masters. Moses is in effect saying to the second generation: the kind of people your fathers feared — in these thirty-eight years God has already dealt with them three times over on the territories of three peoples. Giants are not a new problem; they are an old answer. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three matters are set side by side. First, the tense of the analogy sentence at v. 12: this verse speaks of Israel's taking of land as an accomplished fact of the same kind as Edom's taking. The scholarly readings stand side by side — one reads it as "prophetic certainty": Moses treats what God has promised as already accomplished, speaking of a future thing in the past tense; this is faith and rhetoric. Another reads it as "already accomplished east of the Jordan": at the time of the discourse Israel had already gained the lands of the two kings east of the river (chapter 3 will register the apportioning), and "the land of his possession, which the Lord gave unto them" points to the east bank as an actual reference, the analogy being a fact at that very time. A third reads it as "editorial retrospect": the book's tradition of composition looks back from a vantage after the land was taken, and the analogy sentence is a smooth formulation in hindsight. The three readings stand together; no verdict is given. Second, the identity and origin of the Caphtorims: some readings associate them with Crete and Aegean migration and connect them with the later Philistines; others register only the place and people names. This sermon registers only what this chapter records (they came forth out of Caphtor, destroyed the Avims, and dwelt in the region of Azzah), and lays down no pronouncement. Third, the genealogy of the Rephaim (giants): in this chapter both the Emims and the Zamzummims are "accounted" Rephaim/giants, while the Anakims appear as the standard of comparison for stature (vv. 10, 11, 21); the relation of the three groups to the Rephaim and Anakim peoples recorded in Genesis 14 and 15 is arranged differently in different scholarly schemes. This sermon registers only the classification of this chapter's text and erects no genealogy of its own. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the prohibitions speak of "what may not be taken"; the parentheses speak of "who is doing the giving" — the same God gave to Edom, gave to Moab, gave to Ammon, gave to Caphtor, and is now about to give to Israel. Reading the land register of all the peoples before taking land is Moses' lesson in humility for the second generation: land was never seized; it was apportioned.

### IV. Passing Over the Arnon: The First Battle, and a Hardened King (2:24–31; 39:00–50:00)

[Fact layer] At v. 24 the prohibitions end and the command turns about; quoted in full from the base text: "Rise ye up, take your journey, and pass over the river Arnon: behold, I have given into thine hand Sihon the Amorite, king of Heshbon, and his land: begin to possess it, and contend with him in battle." Read it item by item against what went before: it comes from the same divine mouth as the three prohibitions, yet it reverses them word for word — toward the brethren, "distress them not, nor meddle with them"; toward Sihon, "contend with him in battle." Toward the brethren, "I will not give you of their land"; toward Sihon, "I have given into thine hand … his land," and "begin to possess it." The same God, the same company, one valley of Arnon between them, two sets of orders. The dividing line lies not in the valley but in God's giving: what He has given to another people, take not an inch; what He has given into your hand, you must take. The shape of obedience, accordingly, is never uniformly peace and never uniformly war; it is uniformly heeding orders. And "rise up" is seen twice in the chapter (vv. 13, 24): at v. 13, rise up and get over the brook Zered — the end of the circling; at v. 24, rise up and pass over the river Arnon — the start of the taking. One and the same command: one closes off judgment, the other opens up inheritance. [Fact layer] Verse 25 is the theological prelude to this war; quoted in full from the base text: "This day will I begin to put the dread of thee and the fear of thee upon the nations that are under the whole heaven, who shall hear report of thee, and shall tremble, and be in anguish because of thee." Register two points: first, "This day" — the days of the nations' dread begin at the Arnon, not at the Jordan; this battle east of the river is the source of the name that precedes all the battles west of it (registered as surrounding context: Rahab's words in Joshua 2 and the Gibeonites' fear in Joshua 9 both trace back to the matter of the two kings east of the river; registered paraphrase). Second, the cause of the dread is "hear report of thee" — the battle not yet fought, the report already on its way; half of victory's effect lies off the battlefield: God puts fear in the hearts of the nations, just as He had earlier put His presence in the camp of Israel. [Fact layer] Verses 26–29 record diplomacy first (registered paraphrase): Moses sent messengers out of the wilderness of Kedemoth unto Sihon king of Heshbon with words of peace — rehearsing Israel's coming out of Egypt, their journey through the wilderness, and their present position beside the Arnon valley, and asking the king to let them pass through his land: to go only along the high way, turning neither to the right hand nor to the left, entering no field or vineyard, drinking no well water; the meat they ate and the water they drank they were willing to buy for money, just as the children of Esau and the Moabites had done unto them, asking only to pass through on their feet until they had passed over Jordan into the land God had given them. (This paraphrase follows the Chinese manuscript's registered paraphrase exactly as it stands, including its elements drawn from the parallel embassy in Numbers 20; the KJV text of vv. 26–29 itself, checked against the anchor, stipulates the high way without turning, meat sold and water given for money, passing through on their feet, the precedent of Esau's children and the Moabites, and passing over Jordan into the given land — the source manuscript's registered paraphrase-layer divergences are registered in the Chinese manuscript and are carried here without alteration, not upgraded to quotation.) The terms of this speech answer, in three places, to the rule for Edom at v. 6 and to the speech asking the king of Edom in Numbers 20 (registered paraphrase): toward the Amorite king, Israel observed courtesy before arms, and the courtesy differed in nothing from that shown to the brethren. [Fact layer] At v. 30 the refusal comes; quoted in full from the base text: "But Sihon king of Heshbon would not let us pass by him: for the Lord thy God hardened his spirit, and made his heart obstinate, that he might deliver him into thy hand, as appeareth this day." Register item by item: first, Sihon's refusal and the king of Edom's refusal (Numbers 20; registered as surrounding context) are the same in fact and different in the manner of recording — this chapter states plainly the divine intent behind the refusal: "the Lord thy God hardened his spirit." The two Hebrew words are registered separately — "hardened," qāšâ (H7185, to make hard), is applied to the "spirit" (rûaḥ), and "obstinate," ʾāmaṣ (H553, to make strong), is applied to the "heart" (lēbāb); the division is set out in Endnote 9, and the KJV, unlike the Chinese base, keeps the two objects, spirit and heart, distinct on the surface of the text. Second, the purpose of the hardening is written plainly: "that he might deliver him into thy hand" — the purpose-clause form is of one structure with the hardening narratives of Pharaoh in Exodus (registered as surrounding context); its theological divergences are left to the discussion layer. Third, the four words "as appeareth this day" are the tone of Moses' discourse: the outcome is already accomplished, and in retrospect everything is plain. [Fact layer] Verse 31 is a fourth, short word of God beyond the third segment (registered paraphrase): the Lord said to Moses that He had now begun to give Sihon and his land before Israel, and that they were to begin to possess and inherit his land — the chapter's reckoning of the handing over: at v. 24, "I have given into thine hand"; at v. 30, "that he might deliver him into thy hand"; at v. 31, "I have begun to give Sihon and his land before thee"; at v. 33, "the Lord our God delivered him before us"; at v. 36, "the Lord our God delivered all unto us" — five occurrences, one line: the promise before the battle (24), the explanation within the battle (30), the reaffirmation before the battle (31), the execution within the battle (33), the summary after the battle (36). In the KJV the five points of the line are carried by different verb forms (given, deliver, give, delivered, delivered); the line is a semantic reckoning, registered in Endnote 5, not a single-word count. The word "begin" at v. 31 is registered here: taking land is not finished in one battle; it "begins" with this battle. [Theological-inference layer] The most important dividing line in this section is the difference between Sihon and the three brother kings: it was not nearness or distance of kinship that decided war or no war — Edom are brethren and of course were not fought; Moab and Ammon are merely kin and were also not fought; Sihon is an Amorite, no kin to Israel, yet it was not for lack of kinship that he was fought. The one deciding thing is this: which piece of land God has given to whom. To read the war as ethnic hatred is to misread this chapter; the war in this chapter is "the execution of a land title": the land is God's. What He gave to Edom, Israel passed through, buying water. What He gave into Israel's hand, Israel took under orders. Man's place in this order is to heed orders, not to choose enemies. [Theological-inference layer] Of the stroke concerning Sihon's hardening, this sermon makes only a limited statement at the inference layer: the text itself says the refusal came from God's hardening him, and states plainly that its purpose lay in the delivering over; and this forms an inner-Old-Testament intertext with the Pharaoh event. Two boundaries are to be kept in the inference: first, the text never uses the hardening to lessen the attribution of responsibility to Sihon's side — after v. 31 the account of the war continues to record him as the party who came out to battle (v. 32; registered paraphrase); second, the text also does not generalize the hardening into a universal law — within the same chapter, the three brother kings were not hardened, and Israel's courtesy toward them was not discounted because "God had not delivered them over." In this chapter the hardening is a theological explanation of a single case, not a formula for reading every refusal. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three matters are set side by side. First, the theological readings of the hardening: the commentary tradition diverges markedly — one reading takes the hardening as a direct act of God's sovereignty (God hardened Sihon, as in those verses of the Pharaoh event where "the Lord hardened Pharaoh's heart"), man's refusal accomplishing its end within the purpose of God's delivering over; another reading takes the hardening as God's giving over and confirming (Sihon first hardened his own heart, and God let it go to the end, as in those verses of the Pharaoh event where "Pharaoh hardened his own heart") — the two readings share the same body of verses and differ in the order of attribution; a third reading takes this verse as the theological interpretation layer of Moses' retrospect, differently employed from the narrative layer of Numbers 21, which records only the refusal and not the hardening, the two layers not cancelling each other. Which is right this sermon does not decide; it registers only this: the attribution at v. 30 of this chapter is a formulation unique to this event within the Pentateuch. Second, the difference of detail between the Sihon event and Numbers 21: Numbers 21 records the messengers, the refusal, the battle at Jahaz, and the taking of the land, and appends the old taunt-song of Heshbon (21:27–30) and the name of Chemosh, god of Moab; this chapter's messenger speech is longer and fuller (vv. 26–29, including the rehearsal and the terms), has no taunt-song and no Chemosh, while its treatment after the battle (vv. 33–35) is finer than Numbers 21 (the scope of the utter destruction and the ownership of the spoil). One reading takes the two as the same source differently told, each taking what it needs; another takes this chapter of Deuteronomy as Moses' discursive reworking and retelling, its centre of gravity lying from first to last in instruction, not in archival completeness. The differences are registered only, not harmonized. Third, the self-description in the messengers' speech, "as the children of Esau … and the Moabites … did unto me" (vv. 28–29; registered paraphrase): buying food and water within Edom's territory is seen at vv. 6 and 8 of this chapter; an express text for trade within Moab's territory is separately recorded neither in this chapter nor in Numbers — one reading holds that since the company travelled northward along Moab's eastern border (v. 18), buying water and food along the way was a matter of ordinary supply, not needing to be recorded item by item; another reading takes the analogy in the messengers' speech as a rhetorical seeking of common ground, not needing to be substantiated point by point. The divergence is registered; no verdict is given. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the two banks of the Arnon are two kinds of obedience: on one bank, toward brethren, sheathing the sword and buying food; on the other bank, toward Sihon, going to war under orders; what lies between them is not courage but one sentence of God — what He says He will not give, take not even a foot breadth of it; what He says He has given into your hand, fear not even a hardened king.

### V. Taking and Boundary: No City Too Strong, and Not an Inch of Boundary Crossed (2:32–37; 50:00–57:00)

[Fact layer] Verses 32–35 record the war itself (registered paraphrase): Sihon came out against Israel, he and all his people, to fight at Jahaz; the Lord delivered him before Israel, and Israel smote him, and his sons, and all his people (the handing-over language reaches its execution here; see the reckoning in Section IV); at that time Israel took all his cities, and utterly destroyed the men, and the women, and the little ones, of every city, leaving none to remain, taking only the cattle for a prey to themselves, and the spoil of the cities. (This paraphrase follows the Chinese manuscript's registered paraphrase exactly as it stands; the KJV text of vv. 33–35, checked against the anchor, reads "utterly destroyed the men, and the women, and the little ones, of every city, we left none to remain" at v. 34 and restricts the spoil to cattle and the spoil of the cities at v. 35 — the source manuscript's registered paraphrase-layer wording is carried without alteration and is not upgraded to quotation.) Register the text's account of the treatment item by item: first, persons: utterly destroyed, none left remaining — the stated scope of the wording is the people of the cities (vv. 33–34; registered paraphrase), standing in exact contrast to the "not an inch taken" from the three prohibited peoples: what was taken under orders was treated thoroughly; what was forbidden to be taken was not touched by a fraction. Second, goods: the cattle and the spoil were left (v. 35; registered paraphrase) — the first material substance of the land-taking was livestock and city goods, not deeds of fields. Third, the root of the word for "utterly destroyed" here (of the ḥērem family) has its number still pending verification (Endnote 10); this sermon registers only the base text's wording and scope and lays down no pronouncement on the word's meaning. [Fact layer] Verse 36 is the summary after the war; quoted in full from the base text: "From Aroer, which is by the brink of the river of Arnon, and from the city that is by the river, even unto Gilead, there was not one city too strong for us: the Lord our God delivered all unto us:" Read it item by item against chapter 1: first, the geographical account — from the brink of the Arnon (south) unto Gilead (north), the whole territory of Sihon runs through in one line. Second, "there was not one city too strong for us" — set this sentence beside 1:28: the first generation at Kadesh heard the spies say that the land's cities were great and fenced up to heaven, and their hearts melted; in this chapter the second generation has fought its first battle, and Moses' summary is "there was not one city too strong for us." (In the Chinese base v. 36 is worded by height — "there was no city too high for us to take" — and the contrast is drawn between the same kind of city "high, up to heaven" and "no city too high"; the KJV words the verdict by strength, "too strong," while 1:28 in the KJV likewise speaks of cities "great and fenced up to heaven." The two base texts' wordings are registered separately here and not harmonized; the contrast itself — the same kind of city, read two ways by two generations — stands in both.) The same kind of city: one generation read it as "fenced up to heaven," one generation fought it into "not one too strong." What changed was not the city but man's relation to God: at 1:42 God says, in effect, I am not among you; at v. 36 of this chapter, "the Lord our God delivered all unto us." Third, "delivered all unto us" answers, head to tail, to "I have given into thine hand" at v. 24: the verb of the promise, by the time of the summary, has been honoured without the change of a word in substance. [Fact layer] Verse 37 is the close of the whole chapter; quoted in full from the base text: "Only unto the land of the children of Ammon thou camest not, nor unto any place of the river Jabbok, nor unto the cities in the mountains, nor unto whatsoever the Lord our God forbad us." — The victory won, Moses' last sentence is not a boast but a boundary: the victorious company fought along Ammon's northern border as far as the bank of the Jabbok, and stopped. The Jabbok is the northern border of Sihon's land and the southern edge of Ammon's land (registered paraphrase); the one sentence — that toward everything "the Lord our God forbad us" they "camest not" — settles in one account the execution, within this chapter, of the prohibitions of the preceding sections: Edom and Moab skirted and passed, Ammon approached to its border and there stopped. Victory did not throw the boundary into disorder; this is the last line the chapter leaves to the second generation. (In the KJV v. 37 the verb stands in the second person singular, "thou camest not," where the Chinese base reads the company collectively; registered here, not harmonized.) [Theological-inference layer] The chapter's ending teaches a rare view of victory: true victory is that after winning, the boundary is still kept. A man whose eyes have gone red in plundering, having beaten Sihon, would take Ammon on the side — the land is right next door, the troops are in hand, the excuse is ready-made; Israel did not. This chapter sets "thou camest not" after "there was not one city too strong for us," which is as much as to say: taking every city is a matter of ability; not crossing the boundary is a matter of character; the first rests on God's delivering over, the second on man's heeding orders. The whole later history of the second generation west of the river (registered as surrounding context: the fortunes of Judges and Kings) can be called a footnote to these two sentences: the times when cities could be taken were many; the times when the boundary could be kept were few. [New Testament reading layer] Beyond the traditional echoes of Sihon's name after the New Testament, this layer registers one textual echo only: when Hebrews 11 sets out its list concerning faith, it mentions a summary of the kind "subdued kingdoms" in war (registered paraphrase; Sihon is not named), and this sermon does not pin a summary statement down as a footnote to this chapter. The New Testament makes no verse-by-verse citation of this chapter's events; this layer registers the absence as it stands and forces no correspondences. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters are set side by side. First, the treatment in the war (the "utter destruction" of vv. 33–35) has been read ethically and historically with divergence since ancient times: one reading takes it as the hyperbolic idiom customary in ancient Near Eastern warfare (the "total annihilation" of a city's people being a rhetorical formula, frequently seen in inscriptions of the same age), the facts of the war and the wording to be read apart; another reading takes it as a constituent part of this chapter's theological order (those who took under orders treated thoroughly, and those of the three prohibited peoples not touched by a fraction, the two being two sides of one whole), to be read as the text states it; a third reading takes it as an idealized retrospect in later editing, to be read alongside the actual records of the wars west of the river (Joshua and Judges often record that inhabitants remained). The three readings stand together; this sermon gives no verdict, and the fact layer registers only the scope stated by this chapter's own text. Second, the determination of the borders of Sihon's kingdom: the line Aroer — Gilead — Jabbok at vv. 36–37 agrees in the main with the land-apportioning records of Joshua 12 and 13 (registered paraphrase) with small differences (such as the halving of Gilead north and south), and scholarship has to this day not reached one scheme for the borders of the two kings' lands; this sermon registers only this chapter's own place-name account (Endnote 6) and lays down no pronouncement on the borders. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: the two summary sentences of the first victory, one speaking of ability and one of boundary — "there was not one city too strong for us" speaks of God's delivering over; "thou camest not" speaks of man's heeding orders. The chapter's story stops at the bank of the Jabbok and takes not one step more: this is exactly why it qualifies as the first lesson in taking land.

Conclusion (57:00–60:00). [Theological-inference layer] Look back over this chapter: it begins with "turned" and ends with "camest not"; between them stand one "long enough," three prohibitions, two parenthetical groups, one brook, one valley, and one battle. Chapter 1 asked: why did an eleven days' journey become forty years? This chapter answers: after forty years, God, using the children of the same company, first taught them beside the brethren's border to draw back their hand, beside the brook Zered to settle the account, and before the land register of the other peoples to be humble; and at last beside the Arnon valley to win the first battle — and, having won it, to stop at the border and take not one step more. This word is spoken today to today's hearers as well: our circling may not yet have heard "long enough"; we may be standing between our own Zered and our own Arnon. The order of this chapter of Moses is the road-order of faith: first learn not to take — not to take the portion that is not mine, not to take the step God has not given; then learn to take — what God has given into the hand, do not retreat from it because the king is hardened, and do not fear it because the city is strong; last, learn to stop — having won, still stand only on one's own side. May we, when our days of circling come to an end, hear that "turn you northward"; and may we, when our days of victory come, keep that "thou camest not."

## Devotional Extract (for daily devotional use)

"These forty years the LORD thy God hath been with thee; thou hast lacked nothing." (Deut 2:7) The thirty-eight wilderness years were years of judgment: the men of war of that generation wasted away from among the camp day after day, and the backs of a whole generation disappeared into the circling around Mount Seir. Yet when Moses looks back and reckons up those days, the first thing out of his mouth is not the wasting away but "thou hast lacked nothing" — in the years under the sentence, God's hand was not shortened: He blessed all the works of their hand, He knew their walking through this great wilderness, He was with them continually, and not one day was broken off. We easily suppose that God's presence belongs only to the smooth years; once we have taken a wrong road and come under discipline, He withdraws to a distance and coldly watches us live the days out. But this chapter of Scripture says: even the years of circling He counted step by step; even the thirty-eight years in which a generation wasted away, He dwelt in the camp and made them lack nothing. Circling does not mean He was not there; circling means He was still waiting — waiting for us to hear that "Ye have compassed this mountain long enough," and to rise and turn northward. If today you are standing beside your own brook Zered, and looking back you see nothing but years that circled away, do not begin by sentencing yourself only: count up, through these years, which day's food was not given by Him? Which step was not known by Him? If He did not leave you while you circled, He will not be absent when you turn. Prayer: Lord, thank You that in our years of circling You never walked away. You know every step we have walked, and You have made us lack nothing. Make us able to hear "long enough," and to rise and turn toward the direction You point to; whether what lies ahead is a brook or a valley, we will go over with You. Amen.

### Devotional Extract (English, KJV-based)

Read Deuteronomy 2:7 (KJV): "For the LORD thy God hath blessed thee in all the works of thy hand: he knoweth thy walking through this great wilderness: these forty years the LORD thy God hath been with thee; thou hast lacked nothing." The thirty-eight years were years of judgment — a whole generation was passing away in the camp. Yet when Moses sums them up, the word he reaches for is not "wasted" but "lacked nothing." God was present in the circling years too: He knew every step of the walking, blessed the work of their hands, and never left. Wandering is not the same as abandonment. Sometimes God lets us circle the mountain until we are finally ready to hear, "Ye have compassed this mountain long enough: turn you northward" (2:3, KJV). Count the circling years again today: if He fed you through all of them, He will not forsake you on the road north.

The noun "possession" is seen 6 times in the KJV chapter (v. 5 once, v. 9 twice, v. 12 once, v. 19 twice), of which the three positive declarations of gift in the prohibition contexts (vv. 5, 9, 19) correspond to the Chinese base's count of 3.

The reckoning of phrase occurrences, counted by hand: "compassed" 2 times (2:1, 3); "long enough" once in this chapter (2:3; registered as an intertext with 1:6, "Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount"); the "rise up" command to set out 2 times (2:13, 24); the handing-over line 5 times (2:24 "I have given into thine hand," 2:30 "that he might deliver him into thy hand," 2:31 "I have begun to give Sihon and his land before thee," 2:33 "the Lord our God delivered him before us," 2:36 "the Lord our God delivered all unto us"; the "fight" of 2:32, "to fight at Jahaz," is not counted); "buy" of food and water at 2:6, once for meat and once for water within the verse; the numbers of years 2 entries (forty years, 2:7; thirty and eight years, 2:14); the land-taking language "for a possession / inherit" in the Sihon context 2 times (2:24 "begin to possess it," 2:31 "begin to possess, that thou mayest inherit his land"; v. 31 reaches this sermon as registered paraphrase). KJV register, set down separately: the five points of the handing-over line are carried in the KJV by four different verb forms — given (24), deliver (30), give (31), delivered (33, 36) — so the count of 5 is a reckoning of the handing-over sense, not the occurrence count of any single KJV word; "delivered" itself is seen 2 times (33, 36). The KJV's "possess" is seen 2 times (24, 31), answering to the Chinese base's Sihon-context "for a possession" count of 2.
6. The reckoning of place names, counted by hand: the place names of this chapter in the Chinese base (proper place names occurring in the whole chapter's text, including those reached by the year reckoning, the parentheses, and the messengers' speech) are nineteen — Mount Seir, the Red sea (way), the Arabah (way), Elath, Eziongaber, Moab, Ar, the brook Zered, Kadeshbarnea, Azzah (Gaza), Caphtor, the river Arnon (valley), Heshbon, Kedemoth (wilderness), the Jordan, Jahaz, Aroer, Gilead, and the river Jabbok. The peoples: Edom (the children of Esau), Moab, Ammon (the children of Lot), and the Amorites (Sihon); the four earlier-inhabitant groups: the Emims, the Horims, the Rephaim/giants (the Zamzummims), and the Avims; and one people as the standard of comparison: the Anakims. KJV register, set down separately and not harmonized: counted on the same basis, the KJV chapter also yields nineteen proper place names, but the membership differs at one point — the KJV names Hazerim (2:23, "the Avims which dwelt in Hazerim, even unto Azzah"), where the Chinese base reads "villages" (乡村) and carries no proper name, while the Arabah of the Chinese base's v. 8 appears in the KJV only as the common noun "the plain" ("through the way of the plain") and is not counted as a proper name. The two totals of 19 are therefore not the same nineteen, and the register is kept separate.
7. The reckoning of the parentheses, counted by hand: the parentheses form two groups — the first at vv. 10–12 (the Emims, the Horims), the second at vv. 20–23 (the Zamzummims, the Avims); the formula "earlier inhabitants destroyed, land taken and dwelt in" is seen in full 3 times (v. 12, Esau/the Horims; vv. 21–22, Ammon/the giants, with Esau restated; v. 23, Caphtor/the Avims); the Israel analogy at the end of v. 12 is seen once, registered as the formula's extension to Israel.

8. On "he knoweth" at 2:7 (in the Chinese base, 他都知道了, "he has known it all"): the original word is yādaʿ (H3045, to know) — a word whose range in the Old Testament includes "to know, to care for, to know by experience" (the senses as registered in BDB and HALOT). The Chinese base reads "他都知道了"; the 2010 Union Version reads "他都知道" ("he knows"); the New Chinese Version reads "他也知道你怎样走这大旷野" ("he also knows how you walked through this great wilderness"). The KJV reads "he knoweth thy walking through this great wilderness." The theological inference of this sermon speaks only from the base text's "know" and does not enlarge its assertions on the basis of the word's semantic range.
9. The two hardening words at 2:30, registered separately: in the original, hiqšâ (qāšâ, H7185) is applied to rûaḥ (spirit, H7307), and ʾimmēṣ (ʾāmaṣ, H553) is applied to lēbāb (heart, H3824). The Chinese base reads "使他心中刚硬，性情顽梗" ("hardened his heart, and made his temperament obstinate"), rendering "spirit" into the phrase "temperament/nature"; the two forms are registered separately. The 2010 Union Version reads "使他性情顽梗，内心刚硬" ("made his temperament obstinate and his heart hardened"), with the order reversed; registered separately, neither altered to match the other. Verified directly against the BHS original-language line on the wd.bible verse page for 2:30. KJV register: the KJV preserves the original's two objects on the surface — "hardened his spirit, and made his heart obstinate" — spirit first, heart second, agreeing with the Hebrew division registered here; this is one of the points at which the KJV stands closer to the original's wording than the Chinese base, and it is registered as such, not used to alter the Chinese base's record.

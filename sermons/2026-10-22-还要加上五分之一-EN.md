# And He Shall Add the Fifth Part Thereto — The Trespass Offering, Restitution, and the Order of Man Before God

## Abstract

The last sermon showed how the unintentional sinner is forgiven; this sermon takes one step further: beyond forgiveness there is restitution. Holy things misused must be repaid at the priest's estimation, with a fifth part added. A neighbor wronged must receive the principal in full, with a fifth part added — and only then is the ram brought for a trespass offering. Leviticus lays down a plain and searching rule here: confession cannot replace restitution, and an apology cannot cancel a debt. Whom you have wronged, repay that person first.

## 00:00-08:00 Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all.

In the last sermon we read Leviticus 4:1 to 5:13 and saw that the person who sins through ignorance may bring a sin offering and hear the sentence spoken six times over: it shall be forgiven him. Today we read on, from Leviticus 5:14 to 6:7, and the text changes to a different offering, called the trespass offering. One small note on chapter divisions: the second half of today's text, chapter 6 verses 1 to 7, stands in the Hebrew Bible as chapter 5 verses 20 to 26. Chapter divisions were added later; the content is identical, and the endnotes give the details. These two passages together are only thirteen verses, yet they pause again and again on one detail, until we cannot help making it the title of this sermon: and he shall add the fifth part thereto.

First, the outline of the two passages. The first, Leviticus 5:14-19, gives the basic law of the trespass offering and covers two kinds of case. The first is trespass against the holy things — misusing or withholding objects and offerings that belong to the sanctuary. The second is stranger still: a person sins in one of the things the LORD commanded not to be done, yet does not know it; there is only suspicion, only an uneasy conscience. The second passage, 6:1-7, turns from holy things to people: wrongs against a neighbor. A deposit denied, a bargain cheated, a thing taken by violence, a lost thing found and disowned, even a false oath sworn in God's name. Both passages end with the same sequence of actions: make restitution, add the fifth part, then bring a ram as a trespass offering; the priest makes atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him.

As we read, hold one contrast in mind. The sin offering says: bring the offering, let the blood be applied at the altar, and the sin is forgiven. The trespass offering adds a step before the sacrifice: first there is an account to settle and a payment to make. Whatever of the holy things was lost is valued by the priest, converted into silver, repaid in full, with a fifth part added, and given to the priest. Whatever a neighbor lost is restored to its owner in full, with a fifth part added. In these two passages God seems to write an order with His own hand: first repay what is owed to man and to the sanctuary, and only then lead the ram to the altar. The offering is not a cover for an unpaid debt; the offering is what a person brings with a clear heart after the debt is paid.

Let us read several verses together:

"If a soul commit a trespass, and sin through ignorance, in the holy things of the LORD; then he shall bring for his trespass unto the LORD a ram without blemish out of the flocks, with thy estimation by shekels of silver, after the shekel of the sanctuary, for a trespass offering." (Leviticus 5:15, KJV, excerpted)

"And he shall make amends for the harm that he hath done in the holy thing, and shall add the fifth part thereto, and give it unto the priest: and the priest shall make an atonement for him with the ram of the trespass offering, and it shall be forgiven him." (Leviticus 5:16, KJV)

"And if a soul sin, and commit any of these things which are forbidden to be done by the commandments of the LORD; though he wist it not, yet is he guilty, and shall bear his iniquity." (Leviticus 5:17, KJV)

"He shall even restore it in the principal, and shall add the fifth part more thereto, and give it unto him to whom it appertaineth, in the day of his trespass offering." (Leviticus 6:5, KJV, excerpted)

"And the priest shall make an atonement for him before the LORD: and it shall be forgiven him, for any thing of all that he hath done in trespassing therein." (Leviticus 6:7, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, we say our confessions earnestly and postpone our restitution indefinitely. Teach us through this passage full of accounts: You value not only the sorry on our lips but also the restoring in our hands. Show us today, one by one, what we owe You and what we owe others, and give us the courage and the action to repay. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Today's route has three stops. First, trespass against the holy things: how restitution is valued, converted to silver, increased, and to whom it is given. Second, suspected and unknown sin: how the law receives a person who cannot even be sure he has sinned. Third, wrongs against a neighbor: a list of offenses, one account of restitution, and an order that cannot be reversed. Let it be said in advance: the factual level states only the text and the discussion in the commentaries; how the New Testament takes up this law belongs to the level of New Testament reading and homiletic use, marked separately at the end of the third part, never read back into the plain sense of the original.

## 08:00-24:00 Part One: Trespass Against the Holy Things — Estimation, Silver, and the Fifth Part (5:14-16)

The first stop is Leviticus 5:14-16 — only three verses, yet they set up the whole frame of the trespass offering.

The LORD speaks to Moses: if a soul commits a trespass and sins through ignorance in the holy things of the LORD, he is guilty. The word rendered "trespass" carries a particular weight, treated in its own endnote; here, hold onto its usage in the text. It is not ordinary carelessness but a violation of ownership. Something that belongs to God and to the sanctuary has been used, withheld, or kept back. Whether by intent or by mistake, the ownership has been breached and the trust broken — which is why this sermon speaks of the same root in chapter 6 both as a trespass against the LORD and as a wrong against a neighbor; the accent falls on unfaithfulness and encroachment. What counts as holy things? Across Leviticus, they include offerings presented to the sanctuary, the priest's due portions, firstfruits and tithes, and things vowed or devoted. A person may misuse one of them through a genuine mistake — a confusion, a mix-up, a miscalculation — and the text calls it sin through ignorance. But ignorance does not mean nothing happened: the thing is diminished, and the deficit stands in the sanctuary's account.

The law prescribes three actions, in a clear order. First, bring the sacrificial animal: a ram without blemish out of the flocks, for a trespass offering. Note that the trespass offering's animal never steps down. The sin offering may descend by a person's means from a lamb to birds to fine flour; the trespass offering speaks of a ram from first to last, with no cheaper grade for the poor and no substitute for the ram itself. This is stated as fact, without further inference: the law leaves no room for discount here. Second, restitution in silver: the ram's value is set "with thy estimation by shekels of silver, after the shekel of the sanctuary." The priest estimates, and the standard is the shekel of the sanctuary, the sanctuary's own standard weight. The deficit in the holy things is not repaid by feel, nor at a figure named by the offender, but estimated by the priest by the sanctuary standard and repaid in silver. Third, the addition: "he shall make amends for the harm that he hath done in the holy thing, and shall add the fifth part thereto, and give it unto the priest." Repaying the principal is not enough; a fifth — twenty percent — is added on top. This addition has its own Hebrew name, treated in the endnotes. The fifth part appears more than once in Israel's law: one who eats a holy thing unwittingly adds a fifth; the laws for redeeming fields and houses also add a fifth; and in these two trespass-offering passages it appears twice more, once toward the holy things and once toward a neighbor. The proportion belongs to the law itself; this sermon states only where it appears, and does not expand it into a theory of fines from later jurisprudence.

Only when the three actions are complete does the word of atonement fall: the priest makes atonement for him with the ram of the trespass offering, and it shall be forgiven him. Look at the order: first estimation, first restitution, first the fifth part — then the priest makes atonement with the ram, and the sentence of forgiveness comes last. Offering and restitution are not alternatives, and the offering does not cancel the restitution: the ram is offered and the money is paid, two lines running in parallel, one toward the altar and one toward the account. Whoever thinks that leading in a ram erases the sanctuary's deficit — the text disagrees. Whoever thinks that paying in full makes the offering of confession unnecessary — the text disagrees again. The deficit must be filled and the guilt must be dealt with; this is the most visible functional difference between the trespass offering and the sin offering. On how the two offerings differ in Hebrew usage and function, scholarship holds more than one account; the endnotes set them side by side without deciding. The sermon text establishes only one textual fact: the law of the trespass offering expressly includes a requirement of restitution, and the law of the sin offering does not state one. Keep the levels distinct.

One detail is often missed: the repaid silver and the addition are given to the priest. Why? Because the holy things were administered and enjoyed by the priests in the ordinary course; damage to the holy things falls, at the level of actual administration, on the sanctuary and the priestly office. Paying the priest is paying it back into the sanctuary's account, not into a private purse. That too is a factual-level reading: the text says only "unto the priest," and this sermon adds no speculation about motives. This stop in three sentences: holy things breached — first an estimation, by the sanctuary's scales; restitution in full, the addition a fifth part; restitution first, atoning sacrifice after, the word of forgiveness as the seal. The account settled, we move to the second stop, where there is no concrete account at all — only an uneasy heart.

A further word on the estimation system, so that "converted to silver" is not read too lightly. Estimation by the shekel of the sanctuary is no isolated case in Leviticus: the valuation of persons under a vow and the redemption prices of fields and houses all use the same sanctuary scales and the same priestly estimation. Why is restitution not left to the market price, nor to bargaining between the parties? The structure the text gives is clear: the deficit occurred in the holy things, so the authority to estimate returns to the sanctuary, and the standard does not float with the offender's eloquence or wealth. The rich cannot buy off their guilt cheaply; the poor will not be frightened off by an inflated figure. The estimation is with the priest, the scales are in the sanctuary, and the rule is the same for all. Note also: the silver repays the value of the deficit in the holy things; the ram is offered as the price of atonement for the guilt. The two accounts are kept separate and run separately, and neither can offset the other. Pay the money, and the ram must still be brought; offer the ram, and not a penny less may be paid. This double track is the most distinctive feature of the trespass offering in the whole sacrificial system, and it is the prelude to the third stop's restitution to a neighbor: wherever there is an actually injured party, the law requires actual payment, and no ritual action may wave it away.

## 24:00-42:00 Part Two: Though He Wist It Not, Yet Is He Guilty — Suspected Sin and Bearing Iniquity (5:17-19)

The second stop is the strangest passage in the whole text: Leviticus 5:17-19, three verses about a sin with no specific occasion.

The text says: "And if a soul sin, and commit any of these things which are forbidden to be done by the commandments of the LORD; though he wist it not, yet is he guilty, and shall bear his iniquity." Read it slowly. This is not the unintentional sin of the last sermon, discovered after the fact. At the very moment of offering, this person still does not know whether he has sinned, or in what. He knows only one thing: the list of things the LORD commanded not to be done is long, his own heart has been unsettled of late, he suspects he has crossed a line, yet he cannot name the item. Common sense says: no evidence, no charge — release him. The text gives a startling verdict: though he wist it not, yet is he guilty, and shall bear his iniquity. Guilt is here separated from knowledge: knowledge may be absent, yet guilt is not thereby annulled. This is the plain statement of the text, and this sermon marks it as the factual level. As for the theological reasoning behind this rule, the commentaries discuss it at length: some stress the objectivity of sin — its consequences do not vanish with human ignorance; some stress that the purity of the sanctuary cannot hang on human memory; some observe that the passage's pastoral function outweighs its juridical one, preparing a way out for the troubled conscience. The accounts stand side by side, undecided; and this sermon draws no analogy to any modern legal system, for such an extension goes beyond the text's own scope, and this sermon does not make it.

See then how the law receives such a person. He brings a ram without blemish for a trespass offering, its value likewise by the priest's estimation. The priest makes atonement for him concerning his ignorance wherein he erred unwittingly, and it shall be forgiven him. Note the object of the atonement, stated with great precision: not atonement for the man in general, but for that unwitting error of his. The sin may have no name on the man's lips, but before God it is not a blurred account: there is a ram, an estimation, and a sentence of forgiveness. Verse 19 seals the passage: "It is a trespass offering: he hath certainly trespassed against the LORD." The KJV's "certainly" removes the word "suspected" from the offerer's heart: do not keep guessing. Guilt is dealt with as real, and forgiveness is spoken as real.

This stop speaks to people today in a particular place. Few of us lie awake over ritual errors, but that vague, unnameable sense of indebtedness — everyone knows it. Was that remark a wound? Did I take an unfair advantage in that matter? Is there an account with God or with someone else that I myself cannot reconstruct? The answer of Leviticus is not to sink into endless self-interrogation, nor to wave it away with "it's nothing," but to give an executable exit: treat the suspicion as real, offer what must be offered, confess what must be confessed, then receive the sentence of forgiveness and stop the endless self-examination. Bearing iniquity and being forgiven stand in the same passage, one after the other: the iniquity must be borne, therefore the offering; the offering made, forgiveness truly arrives, therefore it need not be borne forever. The two halves cannot be separated. Bearing without forgiveness crushes a person under guilt; forgiveness without bearing makes sin too light a thing. The law puts both halves within three verses, and that is precisely its pastoral care. This paragraph is homiletic use — ancient law addressed to today's conscience — not a rewriting of the plain sense, and the levels are distinguished as usual.

One more textual fact, against misreading: the offering for suspected sin is still a trespass offering, not a sin offering. In the text's own classification, this guilt that even the person involved cannot specify is placed on the side of "trespass" rather than ordinary ignorance. Why the classification falls this way, the commentaries speculate differently — some from the consistency of the sacrificial animal, some from the word-family linking guilt and restitution — and this sermon presents them without adjudicating. Remember only the classification itself: from Leviticus 5:14 onward, the trespass offering takes the stage, and even the vaguest category of guilt is placed within its range. With that in mind, enter the third stop, where we will see the sharpest edge of the trespass offering: it walks out of the sanctuary, into the neighbor's courtyard, into the tangled accounts between one person and another.

Note also the weight of the phrase "bear his iniquity" in Leviticus. It is no emotional flourish but a formula this book uses again and again: the one who touches uncleanness unknowingly bears iniquity; the one who hears the summons to testify and stays silent bears iniquity. It means the guilt rests on the person involved, to be carried by him himself until the prescribed remedy is complete. In the passage on suspected sin, this formula lands in the most delicate place: the person cannot even name the charge, yet the formula falls all the same, and does not fall empty for want of a name. This shows the order in which Leviticus handles guilt: first confirm the bearing, then give the way out — not comfort first and treatment later. A person who skips the bearing and takes forgiveness directly will experience forgiveness only as relief; one who passes through bearing and then hears forgiveness experiences it as release. So the most pastoral feature of this passage is not that it permits suspicion, but that it forbids vagueness: what is suspected must still be dealt with, and what is dealt with receives forgiveness; the bearing is serious, and the forgiveness is real.

## 42:00-58:00 Part Three: Wronging the Neighbor — Restore in Full First, Then Bring the Ram (6:1-7)

The third stop is Leviticus 6:1-7. The opening, "And the LORD spake unto Moses," is identical to the previous passage, but the definition of the sin changes its wording: if a soul sin and commit a trespass against the LORD, and lie unto his neighbor in that which was delivered him to keep. Notice the sentence structure: wrongs against a neighbor are first defined as a trespass against the LORD. A sin against a person is at the same time a sin against God; God enters the neighbor's account under His own name. That is the factual premise of the whole passage. Then comes a list; this sermon takes its main items without expanding each one.

First, deposits and bargains, handled with denial: a neighbor delivers something to him to keep, or the two are in fellowship or trade together, and he lies about it — there was no such thing. A deposit denied is almost impossible for the owner to prove a second time, so denial nearly always wins — which is why the text expressly names such denial a sin, using the word for lying denial, treated in its own endnote. Second, things taken by violence or gotten by deceit — robbed or extorted, all count. Third, a lost thing found and disowned: he finds what another lost, keeps it, and denies that he found it. Fourth, swearing falsely: over any of these matters, he swears by God's name to a lie, wrapping the most dishonest content in the most sacred form. The list moves from hidden to open: a disputed deposit is hidden, denial is spoken, and a false oath drags God in as a false witness. The four share one feature: beyond the act itself there is a layer of covering. The trespass offering here confronts precisely the person who has taken an advantage and refuses to admit the account.

The order of the remedy is the heart of the passage; follow the text step by step. Verse 4: when his sin is made known, or he acknowledges it himself, he shall restore. The objects of restoration are named one by one: restore what he took by violence, restore what he got by deceit, restore what was delivered him to keep, restore the lost thing he found. Verse 5 settles the account to the bottom: all that about which he has sworn falsely, he shall restore in the principal, add the fifth part more, and give it to him to whom it appertains — in the day of his trespass offering. Mark the timing: not slowly after the sacrifice, not in installments, not when funds loosen, but settled on the day of his trespass offering. The owner first receives principal and addition; then, verse 6, the sacrificial animal takes the stage: he brings his trespass offering to the LORD, a ram without blemish out of the flock, with thy estimation, for a trespass offering, unto the priest. Verse 7 closes: "And the priest shall make an atonement for him before the LORD: and it shall be forgiven him, for any thing of all that he hath done in trespassing therein."

Here this sermon makes one layer of structural observation, marked as the level of observation and not as a theological assertion stated in the text: in the narrative order of the law, restitution comes before sacrifice; man first, then God. God seems to set the entrance to the offering at the neighbor's door: until the neighbor's account is settled, the ram has no standing to be led before the priest. This order is both satisfying and uncomfortable. Satisfying, because it closes the door on piety used to cover indebtedness: no lifting of hands in the sanctuary while a neighbor's deposit sits withheld at home. Uncomfortable, because most of us would rather sacrifice first, be moved first, weep first, and leave restitution until the feeling passes. The law refuses the delay: the day of restitution is the day of the trespass offering; the two are locked to the same day.

See also where the fifth part stands here. One hundred taken from a neighbor is repaid as one hundred and ten. The extra fifth is neither interest nor a shaming penalty under another name; it is the addition fixed by the law itself, the same fifth as in the passage on holy things. The fact it conveys is simple: restitution may not be discounted at one's convenience, nor closed by returning the bare object. The injured party's lost time, lost use, and the wrong suffered during the denial — the law acknowledges all of it in this fixed addition, without requiring the injured party to haggle for it. This sermon makes only that textual-level point; it does not expand the proportion into a jurisprudence of damages. The proportion is the law's, and so is its weight.

Note, finally, the moment that triggers restitution. The text's condition is this: his sin is made known, or he comes to acknowledge it and feels its guilt — and restoration starts at once, without waiting for the owner to come demanding, or for a court to rule. In other words, the engine of restitution is the wrongdoer's own conscience, not the injured party's pursuit. This matches the first passage exactly: there, the priest's estimation drove the process; here, the sense of guilt drives it — one external, one internal — and together the two passages leave no gap for delay. The owner may not yet know where his property is; God has already opened the account in the wrongdoer's heart. In homiletic use, this is today's point of self-examination: do not wait to be confronted. The account that has already risen in your heart is the first one to settle. Restitution made only when exposed is passive; restitution made first, out of a sense of guilt, is the road the law envisions.

Lastly, in this sermon's stated format, the New Testament reading is marked as its own level. New Testament reading and homiletic use: in Luke 19, the tax collector Zacchaeus stands and says to the Lord, half of my goods I give to the poor, and if I have taken anything from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold. Fourfold exceeds the fifth part; it is a measure the repentant man adds himself, not a new rule of law. In homiletic use this sermon values the same direction: genuine turning walks toward restitution on its own, and often beyond the minimum required. In Matthew 5:23-24 the Lord says: if you bring your gift to the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, first be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift. The order — reconcile first, offer afterward — answers across the centuries to this passage's order of restoring first and bringing the ram after. This too is a correspondence at the level of New Testament reading: Matthew speaks of a brother holding a grievance, Leviticus of an owner suffering loss; the contexts differ, and this sermon notes only the correspondence in homiletic use, without reading one passage's words back into the other's plain sense. Distinguishing the levels is what makes the correspondence real: Leviticus itself says restitution plus a fifth part, a ram offered, forgiveness granted; the New Testament, beyond that, shows today's believer what a turned heart looks like. Put the two levels together and the application lands: if a name and a specific account rise in your heart right now, today's homework is not one more song, but to go and settle that account first. Principal and addition — Scripture has already given the proportion. One thing remains: go and repay.

## 58:00-60:00 Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In these thirteen verses we have made three stops. Holy things trespassed against: estimation in silver, restitution in full, a fifth part added, given to the priest. Guilt suspected and unknown: though he wist it not, yet is he guilty and shall bear his iniquity — yet a ram and a sentence of forgiveness receive him. A neighbor wronged: the list made plain, the principal restored in full, a fifth part added, settled on the day of the offering — then the ram is led to the altar, the priest makes atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him. One sentence gathers the whole passage: a trespass is not settled by confession alone but must be carried out as restitution; devotion does not replace repayment, and the altar does not cover an unpaid debt. God set the order clearly: repay man first, then offer; settle the account, then hear the forgiveness. "Add the fifth part thereto" — these words are the honest exercise Leviticus leaves us: owe this much, repay this much, and take the added step as well.

Let us pray: Lord, we lay before You every account that has risen in our hearts: things misused, debts left unpaid, truths we denied. Give us the honesty to reckon clearly, the action to restore, and a generosity beyond the minimum. And let us hear the old sentence at the end of the law: the priest shall make an atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him. The one who has settled the account can hear that sentence with an untroubled heart. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[10] No stepping down of the trespass offering's animal: in Leviticus 5:15, 5:18, and 6:6 the trespass offering is in every case a ram without blemish, tied in every case to the priest's estimation; there is no provision stepping the offering down by the offerer's means, as there is for the sin offering (Leviticus 4; 5:7-13). This is a textual fact; its reasons — means, category of sin, the restitutive character — are variously conjectured in the commentaries, and this sermon states the fact without adjudicating the reason.

[11] The order of restitution before sacrifice: in Leviticus 5:16 restitution and the addition are stated before the priest makes atonement with the ram; in 6:4-6 the principal with the fifth part must be given to its owner "in the day of his trespass offering," and only afterward is the bringing of the ram described. The phrase "man first, then God" in the sermon text is a structural observation drawn from the narrative order, marked as such in the text; it is not a theological assertion expressly stated in the Scripture.

[12] New Testament reading, sources: Luke 19:8, Zacchaeus restoring fourfold; Matthew 5:23-24, be reconciled first, then offer. Both are treated throughout this sermon at the level of New Testament reading / homiletic use: Zacchaeus's fourfold is a measure the repentant man adds voluntarily, not a new rule of law; Matthew 5 concerns a brother's grievance, a different context from an owner's loss in Leviticus 6. Neither is read back into the plain sense of Leviticus, in keeping with the format of this series.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And he shall make amends for the harm that he hath done in the holy thing, and shall add the fifth part thereto, and give it unto the priest: and the priest shall make an atonement for him with the ram of the trespass offering, and it shall be forgiven him." (Leviticus 5:16, KJV)

An apology is easy; restitution is hard. Saying sorry costs nothing — no reckoning, no facing the person wronged. Restitution means counting the principal, adding the fifth part, and putting it into the owner's hand on the very day of the offering. As you read this passage today, do not stop at being moved. Do the reckoning: Whom have I wronged? A sum of money, a trust betrayed, a truth denied? Write down the name and the amount — the principal, and the fifth part more. God set the order: restore to the person first, then bring the offering; settle the account, then hear the forgiveness. The one who has made restitution can stand before the altar with a clear heart. Take that step today: confess what must be confessed, restore what must be restored, in full — and go the extra fifth as well. Then come and hear the old sentence again: it shall be forgiven him.

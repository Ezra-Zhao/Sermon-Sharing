# The God of Mercy and Grace

Preacher: Written by Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and commentaries

Status: Reviewed and published (passed cross-review on 2026-10-23: Gemini independent review + two self-review rounds; see review record, group X)
Review (2026-10-23): Passed cross-review (group X); the 34:9 quotation was restored to KJV wording and "[and that]" supplied at 34:7b during review.

Scripture: Exodus 34:1-35 (KJV)

Continuity: In the previous chapter (Exodus 33), Moses interceded for the people after the golden calf, asking that God's presence not depart and that God show him His glory; this chapter is the answer to that intercession — God gives the tablets again and proclaims His own name.

Series: Sermons on Exodus

## Summary

After the sin of the golden calf, the covenant seemed broken beyond repair. In this chapter God commands Moses to hew new tablets of stone and ascend the mountain alone in the early morning; He descends in the cloud and proclaims His name — merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness. Moses bows and intercedes, God renews the covenant, and Moses comes down with the skin of his face shining. Grace is not indulgence, and holiness is not coldness: this God tells us Himself who He is.

## Sermon

### I. After the Breaking, Up the Mountain Again at Dawn (00:00-08:00)

Brothers and sisters, the chapter we read today stands against the backdrop of one of the most painful scenes in the whole book. Below, the people have made a golden calf and are eating, drinking, and playing around an idol; above, Moses has dashed the two tablets to pieces, because the covenant had already been broken by the people first. The tablets of chapter 32 lie shattered at the foot of the mountain, and in chapter 33 Moses pleads bitterly in the tabernacle: if God's presence does not go with them, they will not go up from there; he even says, if You do not go with us personally, do not bring us up from here. At the end of chapter 33 Moses asks for something greater still: show me Your glory, I beseech You. God promises to let him see the beauty of His back, but not His face, for no one can see His face and live. It is in this atmosphere that chapter 34 opens.

God's words to Moses are unexpectedly calm, and unexpectedly concrete: "Hew thee two tables of stone like unto the first" (34:1, KJV). Notice: this is not a negotiation, not a session of blame, but a command to get to work. Grace here is not a soothing sentence; it is an action by which things can begin again. The tablets are broken — hew new ones. The words are gone — God says, "I will write upon these tables the words that were in the first tables, which thou brakest." The relationship has been severed, yet God Himself puts the rewriting on the agenda. If anyone among us feels that his life is like those two broken tablets — words spoken that cannot be taken back, deeds done that cannot be undone, a covenant made and then destroyed — hear this verse first: the one who opens His mouth and proposes a new beginning is God, not Moses. In chapter 33 Moses asked for presence and for glory, but he did not dare ask for new tablets. The new tablets were God's own idea.

Here is a structural observation worth examining slowly. The first tablets, Scripture says, were the work of God, the writing of God (cf. 32:16); Moses only went up to receive them. This time, the tablets are to be hewn by Moses himself. The tablets are hewn by Moses; the words are still written by God — verse 1 says "I will write," while verses 27-28 say "he wrote" the words on the tablets, and whether that "he" refers to God or to Moses is a question commentators discuss to this day; we present the discussion without settling it, and the notes below give the details. From the surface of the narrative alone, the division of labor is already clear: man prepares the stone, God writes the words. What man can do is cut the stone, clear the early morning, and set his feet on the mountain path; what man cannot do is make those words the words of God. The rebuilding of a relationship works the same way: we are responsible for the preparation, God is responsible for the writing. If a person will not even hew the stone, but sits weeping beside the fragments, God's words have nowhere to land; if a person imagines that he writes the words too, that becomes a self-made religion. Hewing the stone is the plainest form of repentance — unglamorous, laborious, and best done early.

Early is exactly right. Verse 4 says Moses rose up early in the morning and went up Mount Sinai, as the LORD had commanded him, taking the two tablets of stone in his hand. Why early morning? The text does not explain, and we will not invent a reason for it; but the rhythm of the narrative itself speaks: the man who interceded through the night climbs the mountain at daybreak. And he climbs alone. Verse 3 says no man shall come up with you, and no man shall be seen anywhere on the mountain; not even the flocks and herds may feed before that mountain. At the first giving of the law the people could still watch from afar at the foot of the mountain, and Moses, Aaron, and others approached by degrees; this time, even the livestock are cleared away. The mountain is emptied not for spectacle, but so that this encounter allows no spectators: no crowd, no atmosphere, only one man and one God. Many true repairs of a relationship happen where there is no audience. We are used to making declarations in public and being moved in a group; God calls Moses into the early morning of one man alone. No bleating sheep, no human voices — only stone and footsteps. Brothers and sisters, if God leads you into a season of quiet, even of loneliness, do not rush to treat it as abandonment; sometimes it is a clearing of the ground, the prelude to His speaking to you alone. The hewn tablets in hand, the mountain path step by step — this is the first picture this chapter gives us: the breaking is not the final chapter; the final chapter begins with climbing the mountain alone at dawn.

One more detail deserves attention: the place. God commands Moses to present himself in the morning on the top of the mountain (34:2). The one who stands on the summit is not a victor, but the intercessor fresh from the pain of a whole nation's covenant-breaking. And what he holds is not a finished product but two freshly hewn, still blank stones. Blankness here is not a disgrace but a preparation: the first pair of tablets, full of the words of the covenant, were shattered in anger; this pair is empty, waiting for God to speak again. What is hardest for people coming before God is often not bringing many achievements, but being willing to come empty — admitting that we cannot write a new page ourselves, and asking Him to write it. The person who climbs the mountain carrying blank stones is the one who, coming down, holds in his hands nothing but His words.

### II. He Stood There and Proclaimed His Name (08:00-24:00)

After Moses climbed the mountain, one of the most unusual scenes in the Old Testament took place. Verse 5: "And the LORD descended in the cloud, and stood with him there, and proclaimed the name of the LORD." Notice the positions in this picture: Moses is not kneeling at a distance listening to a voice; God descends, stands, is present with Moses, and then speaks. Moses had asked in chapter 33 to see glory; God's answer was not a visual spectacle, but a proclamation about who God is. God's deepest self-revelation is not color and shape, but character and name. A name, in Hebrew thought, is not a label but the sum of who a person is. God proclaimed: "The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth, Keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (34:6-7, KJV).

The weight of this passage can hardly be overstated. It is the most quoted self-proclamation of God in the Old Testament: Moses cites it in Numbers 14 when interceding for the people after the spies' report; the Levites cite it in Nehemiah 9 in their prayer of confession; David cites it in Psalm 86 as he cries out; Joel cites it in Joel 2 when calling the people to rend their hearts and return to God; and in Jonah 4, Jonah is actually angry because this word came true for the people of Nineveh — he says, in effect, I knew all along that You are a merciful and gracious God, and that is why I ran away. One passage, cited by Moses, cited by psalmists, cited by prophets, and cited by a runaway prophet as evidence for his complaint — there is no second example of this in the Old Testament. It became, almost, the identity card of Israel's faith: whenever the people did not know whether God would still have them, they took out this passage and read it once more. Today we too should read it this way, word by word.

First, "merciful." The Hebrew word is rachum, from the same root as the word for "womb" (see Note 1). Commentators, following the lexicons, point out that this word carries an almost instinctive stirring deep in the inward parts, like a mother's unreasoning ache for the infant in her arms. God's mercy is not pity from a height; it is being moved in Himself by the suffering of the other. Next, "gracious" — channun: one who has standing and rank willingly stoops down and gives freely to those who do not deserve it. Mercy is spoken toward suffering; grace is spoken toward unworthiness: we are not only pitiable, we are also undeserving, and He has given us both. Then comes "slow to anger," literally "long of nostrils," long in holding back breath (see Note 3). Notice: the text does not say God does not become angry — that would be cheap comfort — it says He is slow to it. Anger in Him has a very long fuse, because what He has first is abundance: "abundant in goodness and truth." Goodness — chesed — is covenant love, love with commitment and loyalty, not withdrawn because the other party changes; truth — 'emet — is reliability, reality, doing what He says (see Note 4). Steadfast love without faithfulness becomes indulgence; faithfulness without steadfast love becomes a cold accounting office. God places these two words side by side in His proclamation: My love is covenant-keeping love, and My faithfulness is faithfulness with warmth. "Keeping mercy for thousands" — literally, to a thousand generations. "Forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin": three different words in a row, naming nearly every form of human wrongdoing — the deliberate, the rebellious, the straying. Jewish tradition counts thirteen attributes in this proclamation, known as the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy; the ways of counting vary slightly from one reckoning to another, and that is the tradition's way of counting, which we report faithfully without treating it as the text's own divisions (see Note 2).

But if we stop at forgiveness, we have read only half the sentence. The second half of verse 7 says He "[and that] will by no means clear the guilty; visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children, and upon the children's children, unto the third and to the fourth generation." In one and the same proclamation, the first clause keeps mercy to a thousand generations and the second clause visits sin to three or four; set the two numbers side by side: a thousand, against three or four — out of all proportion. The proportion God Himself sets already says everything: His steadfast love is the main theme; His visitation is real, but limited. Yet we do not soften this half of the sentence. The word for "visiting," paqod, at root means to attend to, to remember, to muster and inspect; used of grace it is "visiting in favor," used of sin it is "visiting in judgment" — several readings stand side by side (see Note 5 for the word anchor and the discussion). Between this verse and Deuteronomy 7, where God keeps covenant and mercy to a thousand generations for those who love Him and repays those who hate Him to their face, and Ezekiel 18, where the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father and each dies for his own sin, there is a real tension. Commentators across the ages have reconciled it in different ways — some distinguish collective consequences from individual liability, others distinguish the narrative setting of the Old Testament from the prophet's individualized proclamation — and we present these views side by side without forcing a conclusion here. This text asks us to hold two things at once: God's grace is great enough to be kept for a thousand generations, and God's holiness is real enough that He never calls the guilty innocent. Lose the first, and faith becomes fear; lose the second, and grace becomes license. At the foot of the mountain where the golden calf has just been made, both sentences must be proclaimed together, because the people need to know both that God still wants them and that God is not to be trifled with. Jonah resented the first half of this sentence; the rebellious fear the second half; Moses, standing on the mountain, took in both halves.

One further layer must not be missed: this proclamation is the fulfillment of the promise of chapter 33. When Moses asked for glory, God said, "I will make all my goodness pass before thee, and I will proclaim the name of the LORD before thee" (33:19, KJV). Those who heard at the time may have assumed that goodness was something to be seen with the eyes; only on the mountain did it become clear that goodness is received with the ears — all of God's goodness passed before Moses concentrated in a string of names. That is why, when Scripture speaks of knowing God, it never begins with a theory about God; it begins with hearing Him say, with His own mouth, who He is. The order of faith is set out clearly here: we do not first define God and then decide whether to trust Him; He proclaims first, and we spend the days of our whole lives digesting that proclamation. Every later time Israel weakened, was exiled, or turned back, priests and prophets did the same thing: they read this passage aloud once more. In Nehemiah's day, as the people wept beneath the rebuilt wall, the Levites were still citing it in prayer; the psalmist, sleepless at night, was still citing it as he wrote. This passage is like a well: the drier the season, the more one must return to the well. Our reading of Scripture today should keep the same order: let God's word define God first, and then understand our experiences under that definition, rather than using a passing circumstance to rewrite His name. Circumstances change; the name does not change. The tablets break; the One who writes the words does not change.

### III. The Man Who Bowed Down, and the Covenant Made Again (24:00-42:00)

As soon as God's voice ceased, Moses' response consisted of only two movements: verse 8, "And Moses made haste, and bowed his head toward the earth, and worshipped." Notice the haste. When glory is proclaimed, the most natural human response is not analysis but prostration. Moses did not first offer his impressions, nor first discuss what "to the third and fourth generation" might mean; he first put his face to the ground. Worship always runs ahead of theological discussion: bow first, and think slowly afterward. Then he spoke his intercession — very short words, and very bold: "let my Lord, I pray thee, go among us" (34:9). This is precisely the theme of that long conversation in chapter 33: presence. Moses did not ask God for blessing, for victory, or for land; he asked for one thing only: go Yourself. The land can be traveled slowly; the presence cannot be done without.

Even more surprising is the reason he gives for his plea for grace: "for it is a stiffnecked people." By our logic, that sentence belongs in a confession: Lord, we were wrong, because we are stiff-necked. Moses instead places it inside the plea for grace: Lord, precisely because this people is stiff-necked, I ask You to go Yourself, and to pardon our iniquity and our sin, and take us for Your inheritance. There is a narrative irony here, which we label a structural observation rather than something the author states outright: the very blemish that could not serve even as an excuse in human eyes becomes, in Moses' prayer, the ground of the plea for grace — not, "we have reformed, so please come back," but, "we simply cannot reform ourselves, so You cannot fail to go." This is the difference between an intercessor and a defense attorney: the attorney looks for evidence of innocence; the intercessor takes hold of the sinner's true condition and carries it into God's mercy. Moses stands in the middle of the broken covenant, holding the people's stiff neck in one hand and the name God has just proclaimed in the other: are You not the God who is merciful and gracious? Then treat, according to Your name, this people who by their own nature do not deserve it. This prayer was already prayed once in chapter 33 and comes again in chapter 34, which shows that intercession is not a one-time emotion, but carrying God's words back before God one sentence at a time.

God's answer is: "Behold, I make a covenant" (34:10). And so, after the broken tablets, the covenant is made again. God promises to do marvels before all the people, such as have not been done in all the earth or in any nation; He Himself will cast out the inhabitants of the land — the Amorites, the Canaanites, the Hittites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites, six peoples named one by one (34:11). Notice the order: first God does the marvels, first God drives out, and only then does human responsibility come. The renewal of the covenant is not a negotiation between equals; God sets grace out first, and the people receive their instructions within grace.

For the instructions that follow (34:12-26), we follow the usual approach for legal chapters: grasp the structure and the theological anchors, without expounding every clause in full. The structure of this section is in fact quite clear: first a warning, then the festivals. The heart of the warning is a single sentence: take heed not to make a covenant with the inhabitants of the land, lest it become a snare in your midst. Concretely: do not go after them to sacrifice to their gods, do not eat of their sacrifices, do not take their daughters for your sons, lest their daughters lead your sons astray after their gods; and tear down their altars, break their pillars, and cut down their Asherim (34:12-17). Why demolish so thoroughly? Because at the foot of the mountain the people have just proved with a golden calf that their worship is the thing most easily rewritten by their surroundings. The demand for separation is not God being petty; it is a physician's caution — a person just dragged out of idolatry cannot, for the time being, return to the table of idols. "Thou shalt make thee no molten gods" (34:17): read on its own this sentence is short; read after the golden calf, it weighs a ton. "Molten" — that is exactly what Aaron did.

The festival section (34:18-26) we take only in its main lines. It restates the Feast of Unleavened Bread, in memory of the exodus; it restates the Sabbath, and pointedly adds that rest is required even in plowing time and in harvest — the busy season is no reason for an exception; the busier the season, the more people need to be reminded that the land does not turn by their hand alone. It restates the Feast of Weeks and the Feast of Ingathering, and that three times a year all males shall appear before the Lord GOD. It also restates several details: the blood of the sacrifice shall not be offered with anything leavened, the Passover sacrifice shall not be left until the morning, the first of the firstfruits shall be brought to the house of God, and "thou shalt not seethe a kid in his mother's milk." On this last clause the scholarship holds several views side by side — a remnant of a Canaanite fertility rite, a principle of humane treatment of animals, or simply a requirement of separation — and we present them without settling on one (see Note 8). Read together, the anchor is the same: after the covenant is renewed, what God cares about most is not the people's emotions, but their calendar and their table. How the days are spent, and with whom the meals are eaten, will decide whether this people still knows this God ten years from now. The rebuilding of faith is never completed on the summit; it is completed, day by day, in the festivals, the Sabbaths, and the daily separations at the foot of the mountain.

Looked at one layer deeper, throughout these instructions for the renewed covenant God never bypasses Moses to contract directly with the people; He still speaks through the man who has just bowed down and interceded. This is a profound settling for Moses himself: in the golden calf incident the people had bypassed him and pressed Aaron into making them another god; now God recommissions this same Moses as mediator, declaring before the whole congregation, in effect, that this man still stands between Me and you. Those who intercede for others often carry a particular grievance: I kneel for you, and behind my back you make idols. God's way of handling that grievance is not a few words of comfort, but putting the intercessor back in his place. Notice too that when Moses asked for God's presence, he asked for nothing for himself — not for his reputation, not for his lifespan — only that God go among the people, pardon the people, and take the people as His inheritance. An intercessor's heart is stretched wide in exactly this kind of prayer: one's own matters grow fewer the more one prays; other people's matters grow heavier. If the church today is to see renewal, the first thing to recover is probably not the programs, but this kind of person standing in the breach — not standing aside commenting on how stiff-necked the people are, but standing between God and man, confessing sin with one hand and confessing the Name with the other.

### IV. After Forty Days and Nights, a Shining Face (42:00-56:00)

With the covenant made, Moses remained on the mountain. Verses 27-28 say he was there with the LORD forty days and forty nights; he neither ate bread nor drank water, and the words of the covenant, the ten commandments, were written on the tablets. One word here deserves a note: the "ten commandments" in the Hebrew text are not called ten commands but ten words — devarim means "words" (see Note 7). The covenant God made again with man takes, on the tablets, the form of words — words that can be read, handed on, and taught to children. Moses neither ate nor drank for forty days; this time no one at the foot of the mountain was hurrying him, and no golden calf was waiting for him. The first forty days on the mountain ended in disaster; the second forty days on the mountain ended in glory. The same length of time, the same mountain, entirely different endings — and the only difference was this: this time, the man on the mountain had first heard God's name, and the people at the foot of the mountain had learned, in waiting, to be quiet.

Then comes the most moving picture in the chapter. Verse 29: Moses came down holding the two tablets, but he himself did not know that the skin of his face shone. The word for "shone" is qaran in Hebrew, from the same root as the word for "horn" (see Note 10 for the word anchor). There is a famous case in the history of translation: the Latin Vulgate rendered this word cornutam, "horned," and so the image of Moses in Western art for a time grew horns; the two small horns on Michelangelo's famous statue of Moses come from that rendering. We recount this as a fact of translation history: most modern commentators, following the lexicons and the context, read the word as shining, radiating light; the "horn" reading remains in the history of translation and is not read back into the plain sense of the text. Moses' light was not something he cultivated in himself; he did not even know about it. The mark of a person who has truly been with God is often seen by others first and known by himself last — self-awareness here gives way to something deeper: he was so taken up with the words he had heard on the mountain that, coming down, he never thought to look in a mirror.

But this light frightened the people. Verse 30: Aaron and all the people saw that the skin of his face shone, and they were afraid to come near him. Glory is in itself a good thing, yet falling among sinful people, its first effect is that they hide. What Moses did is worth learning: he neither rebuked them nor put the light away; he called them back — first Aaron and the rulers of the congregation, who came near and spoke with him; then all the people drew near as well, and he gave them in commandment everything the LORD had spoken with him on the mountain (34:31-33). The purpose of the light was never to frighten people, but to draw them before the words: first the light, then the words; the light makes people solemn, the words make it possible for them to live.

Finally, the veil. Verses 33-35 say that when Moses had finished speaking with the people, he put a veil over his face; when he went in before God to speak with Him, he took the veil off, and after coming out he told the people God's words, and then veiled himself again until the next time he went in. This is a most orderly usage: before God, the face is uncovered; before the people, he veils himself, so that the people will concentrate on the words and not keep staring at the light on that face. What a messenger must guard against most is that his hearers remember only the messenger's halo and forget the message itself; with a single veil Moses covered his own glory and let God's words stand out. In the New Testament, the apostle Paul rereads this passage in 2 Corinthians 3, saying that Moses veiled himself so that the Israelites would not gaze at the glory that was fading, and that the veil on people's hearts is taken away in Christ — that is the New Testament author's reading, which we receive as a New Testament layer of reading and do not read back into the plain historical sense of Exodus (see Note 9). Within this chapter alone, the meaning of the veil is already clear enough: take it off when speaking with God, put it on when conveying the words to the people; be open when drawing near to God, exercise restraint when serving the crowd. That order has not expired to this day.

One last detail is placed at the end of the text, but it is not slight. Verse 29 says that when Moses came down, in his hand were the two tablets — the text pointedly reminds us that these same hands had once shattered a pair of tablets, and now steadily held a pair again. The same person's hands can shatter, and can also hold fast; the difference is not in the hands, but in what those hands have been through in between: they have been through intercession, through the early morning, through standing before God and hearing Him proclaim His name. Deuteronomy later recounts that Moses placed this second pair of tablets in the ark (Deut 10:5); of the fragments of the broken pair nothing more is said, but the new pair was treasured away and traveled with the people through the wilderness and into the promised land. What God keeps is never our fragments, but the covenant He has written anew. Of a human life, what can be stored in the ark is not our most respectable page, but the page He rewrote after we were broken.

### V. In His Name, Begin Again (58:00-60:00)

Brothers and sisters, in this chapter today we have walked from the broken tablets to the shining face, and standing in the middle is the name God proclaimed Himself. He is a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abundant in steadfast love and faithfulness; He will by no means clear the guilty, yet He keeps steadfast love for thousands of generations. These two halves of one sentence — neither can be dispensed with. If you are afraid to return to God because of what you have broken, hear the first half: the new tablets were His idea first, and the mountain path at dawn is still open. If you treat grace as license, hear the second half: His holiness is never discounted, and tearing down altars, keeping the Sabbath, and keeping a separate table — none of these can be omitted. If you feel your face is too ordinary and your life too plain, remember Moses coming down the mountain: the light is not cultivated; it comes from having stayed in His presence. First, time spent with the face uncovered; afterward, a glory one does not know of oneself. May our generation not be a stiff-necked people, but a people who climb the mountain at dawn; not a people who stare at the messenger's face, but who listen carefully to God's words. In His name, let us begin again. Amen.

## Notes

Note 1 (rachum and channun): רַחוּם, transliterated rachum, Strong's number H7349, an adjective meaning compassionate, merciful. According to BDB, the word shares a root with rechem ("womb," H7358), and HALOT likewise lists it under the same root, glossing it "compassionate." Commentators commonly note that its flavor carries an almost inborn stirring, like that of one's own flesh and blood; the sermon text draws its popular explanation from this, though the cognate relationship is an etymological observation and does not by itself exhaust the word's meaning in context. Paired with it stands חַנּוּן, transliterated channun, Strong's number H2604, an adjective from the verb chanan ("to be gracious," H2603); BDB glosses it "gracious," the favor shown by one of standing toward the undeserving, and HALOT likewise gives "gracious, merciful." In the Old Testament this word is used almost exclusively of God (e.g., Ps 86:15; 103:8); in 34:6 the two words stand together as the first two attributes of this chapter's proclamation of grace.

Note 2 (The Thirteen Attributes of Mercy): Jewish tradition counts Exodus 34:6-7 as thirteen attributes of mercy (middot), and since the Talmud (Rosh Hashanah 17b) this passage has been a central text of Jewish prayer, especially on the Day of Atonement and in the Selichot services. The reckoning varies slightly within the tradition: some count each of the two occurrences of the divine name as a separate attribute, others begin counting from "merciful," and the thirteen items are divided differently by different authorities. This sermon reports that traditional counting faithfully, without treating it as the text's own division.

Note 3 ('erekh 'appayim): אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם, transliterated 'erekh 'appayim; 'erekh is H750 ("long, slow"), and 'appayim is the dual of 'aph (H639, "nose, anger"), literally "long of nostrils," that is, long in holding back one's breath. BDB glosses it "long-suffering, slow to anger"; HALOT agrees. The phrase also appears in Numbers 14:18, Psalm 86:15, Proverbs 14:29, and elsewhere.

Note 4 (chesed ve'emet): חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת, transliterated chesed ve'emet. Chesed is H2617, glossed by BDB as loyal love and kindness within a covenant relationship; 'emet is H571, from 'aman ("to be firm, reliable"), glossed by BDB as faithfulness, truth. In 34:6 the text reads "abundant in steadfast love and faithfulness" (rav-chesed ve'emet); the pairing of the two words forms the core combination of God's self-proclamation.

Note 5 (paqod): פָּקַד, transliterated paqad / paqod (in its participial form), Strong's number H6485. BDB lists a wide range of senses: to number, attend to, remember, visit in favor, inspect, punish, visit in judgment; HALOT likewise divides it into several senses. In Exodus 20:5 and 34:7 it is usually rendered "visiting," while in Genesis 21:1 and 50:24 it is rendered as visiting in grace. That the same verb serves both grace and judgment — some scholars stress a neutral core sense of attending and dealing with, others stress that context determines the favorable or unfavorable sense — is presented here in parallel, without a forced conclusion. On the tension between 34:7 and Deuteronomy 7:9-10 and Ezekiel 18, both Durham and Childs offer extended discussions, with differing approaches: one stresses the cumulative effect of collective consequences, the other the later setting of the prophets' individualized proclamation; readers may compare them.

Note 6 (The subject of "he wrote"): In 34:1 God expressly says "I will write"; in 34:27 God commands Moses, "Write thou these words," and 34:28 then says "he wrote the words of the covenant on the tablets," with the subject left unspecified. Childs holds that the theological reading of the final form of the text takes God as the writer and Moses as the agent; Durham points out that the two statements coexist at the narrative level and may reflect the editorial traces of different traditions. There is no scholarly consensus to this day. The sermon text states only the division of labor on the narrative surface (the wording of 34:1 is explicit: man hews the tablets, God writes the words) and records the discussion here.

Note 7 (devarim): The "ten commandments" of 34:28 are in the original 'aseret ha-devarim; devarim is the plural of davar (H1697, "word, matter"), literally "the ten words," consistent with the usage in Deuteronomy 4:13 and 10:4. The Chinese Union Version's rendering "the ten commandments" and the KJV's "ten commandments" are both interpretive translations; the original wording highlights the character of the covenant as given in the form of words.

Note 8 (Seething a kid): "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in his mother's milk" (34:26) appears three times, with Exodus 23:19 and Deuteronomy 14:21. The views stand side by side: one takes it as directed against a Canaanite fertility rite involving cooking a kid in milk (a view popular since medieval commentators, though the archaeological evidence is weak and many modern scholars are skeptical); one takes it as a principle of humane treatment, that mother and offspring are not to be used in the same pot; one takes it simply as a boundary law separating the holy from the common, grouped with the prohibitions concerning blood and leaven. Both Childs and Durham list the views without settling on one.

Note 9 (The veil and the New Testament reading): Paul's rereading of Moses' veil in 2 Corinthians 3:7-18 (the veil as symbol of the fading glory of the old covenant and of the covering still on human hearts) is the New Testament author's theological interpretation after the event of Christ. This sermon labels it a New Testament layer of reading: it may be received in faith and cited in preaching, but is not read back as the historical plain sense of Exodus 34, where the narrative itself records only the alternation of veiling and unveiling as Moses entered the tent, spoke with God, and delivered the words (34:33-35).

Note 10 (qaran): קָרַן, transliterated qaran, Strong's number H7160, a verb sharing a root with the noun qeren ("horn," H7161). BDB lists two senses: to send out rays, and to grow horns; HALOT, at this verse, favors the sense of rays streaming out. The Vulgate rendered it cornutam ("horned"), from which arose the tradition in Western art of a horned Moses, of which Michelangelo's statue of Moses is the most famous example; most modern commentators (Childs, Durham, Propp, and others), on the basis of the parallel use of qarnayim ("rays") in Habakkuk 3:4, read it as the skin of his face shining. The sermon text follows the majority reading; the "horn" reading is presented only as translation history.

## Devotional Excerpt

The tablets were broken, yet God spoke first: hew two new tablets, and I will write again. Many assume we must repair ourselves before returning to God; Moses' morning teaches the opposite order — God renews the covenant first, and we only hew the stone, climb the mountain, and make the time. He proclaimed His name: merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness. This is no mere comfort; it is His identity. He will by no means clear the guilty, so we need not pretend we were never broken; He keeps steadfast love for thousands of generations, so we need not doubt that He still wants us. Today, be a hewer of tablets: give Him your early morning, hand Him the fragments, and let Him write His words again on the stone you have prepared. Hewing takes no talent — only a willingness to rise early, to get to work, and to climb the mountain alone; the writing needs no worry from us, for that is His part, and having written once, He will take responsibility for writing the second time. When you come down, you may not know your face is shining — but those around you will see: this is a person who has come back from the mountain; his temper has slowed, his love has lengthened, and there is a weight from the mountain in his words. May today's devotion end not merely in being moved, but in one concrete early morning: put down the phone, open the Scriptures, and bring before Him, in all honesty, your stiffest neck and the broken thing you least dare to mention — His name has been proclaimed beforehand, and He will treat you according to His name.

## Summary (about 100 words)

Exodus chapter 12 is the origin point of the calendar and worship in the Bible: God redefined time by the Passover, commanded each household to take a lamb without blemish and put its blood on the doorposts and the lintel, and said, when I see the blood, I will pass over you. That night judgment went through Egypt; those who trusted the token and obeyed the details went out fully dressed and ready, four hundred and thirty years of slavery ended within a single day, and the story of salvation has been remembered generation after generation ever since.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter we read that the words about disaster had all been spoken. Nine of the ten plagues had passed; Pharaoh said again and again, you may go, and again and again went back on his word. By chapter 11 Moses walked out of the palace with the final announcement: at midnight, all the firstborn in the land of Egypt would die, and even the firstborn on Pharaoh's throne could not be exempted. That was the heaviest chapter in the whole book, and the air was full of the smell of judgment. Yet at the very beginning of chapter 12 today, God did not immediately let judgment fall. He stopped and first did one thing: He taught His people to keep a festival. The night of judgment had not yet arrived, and the calendar of salvation had already turned to a new page.

I love this order very much. God does not strike first and then tell you how to hide; God first points out the way of escape, first prepares the lamb, first brings the basin for the blood to your door, and only then lets that night come. Brothers and sisters, this is the character of our God: His judgment is real, and His mercy is also real, and mercy always runs ahead of judgment and gives the token to people first.

This chapter today is very long, fifty-one verses, and we cannot examine every verse in detail. Our approach is this: we will preach mainly three sections — the command instituting the Passover, the people doing as they were commanded, and what actually happened that night; as for the final ordinance section about how outsiders are to keep the rule, we will cover it in one passage and place the details in the endnotes, which brothers and sisters can study at home. But rest assured, not one bone of this chapter will be missing: time, the lamb, the blood, the manner of eating, that night, and how a saved people walked out of Egypt — we will look at them one by one.

Let us read several key passages together:

"Your lamb shall be without blemish, a male of the first year: ye shall take it out from the sheep, or from the goats: and ye shall keep it up until the fourteenth day of the same month: and the whole assembly of the congregation of Israel shall kill it in the evening. And they shall take of the blood, and strike it on the two side posts and on the upper door post of the houses, wherein they shall eat it." (Exodus 12:5-7, excerpt, KJV; see also 12:2-3 on this month being the beginning of months)

"For I will pass through the land of Egypt this night, and will smite all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, both man and beast; and against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment: I am the LORD. And the blood shall be to you for a token upon the houses where ye are: and when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and the plague shall not be upon you to destroy you, when I smite the land of Egypt." (Exodus 12:12-13, excerpt, KJV)

"And ye shall eat it in haste... And thus shall ye eat it; with your loins girded, your shoes on your feet, and your staff in your hand; and ye shall eat it in haste: it is the LORD's passover." (Exodus 12:8, 11, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, thank You that before the night of judgment arrived, You first prepared for us the lamb and the blood. Open our eyes today to see that the blood on the door is not an ancient ritual but an open door; and give us obedient ears to hear clearly every detail of Your command, and to be ready, on the day You have appointed, to go out with You fully dressed and ready. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The First Month as the Beginning of the Year and the Choosing of the Lamb (12:1-14)

First look at something we easily read past, though its weight is very great: God changed the calendar. "This month shall be unto you the beginning of months: it shall be the first month of the year to you." The Israelites had lived in Egypt for more than four hundred years, and their time was Egypt's time, a slave's time: when to make bricks, when to hand over the work, when to be beaten — all decided by others. How their year passed and how their months were counted had nothing to do with themselves. Now God said, from this month on, your time is counted anew. Not the year of Pharaoh's accession, not the year of the harvest, but the year of your salvation. That redemption redefines time is no exaggeration at all: after a person is truly saved, his chronology is divided into two parts, before salvation and after salvation; his days are no longer numbered by the one who oppressed him, but by the God who saved him.

Brothers and sisters, this is equally true for us today. Some people's year is numbered by debt, by illness, by their children's school grade, by company assessments, by mortgage installments. It is not that these things do not matter; it is that when God enters a person's life, the first thing He takes back is sovereignty over time: your beginning of the year is no longer the day suffering began, but the day grace arrived. Every time the Israelites later kept the Passover, they were resetting the calendar once more: Who are we? We are the people who came out of Egypt. From where do we count our year? From the month in which God saved us. Remembrance is never merely nostalgia; remembrance is recalibrating the clock for identity.

Next look at the choosing of the lamb. The Scripture speaks in great detail, as detailed as a checklist, and every item on the checklist carries intention.

First, in time it is done in two steps: take the lamb on the tenth day of this month, and keep it until the fourteenth day at evening before killing it. Why not kill it as soon as it is taken, but keep it in the home for four days? In those four days, this lamb lives in your courtyard, eats the grass you feed it, and has its head touched by your children. After four days, what you kill is no longer a strange animal, but a lamb that has already entered your home. Salvation is never a cold transaction; the One who dies in your place is the One who first dwells with you and is first known by you. And those four days are also four days of examination: whether there is a blemish, whether there is sickness, must be seen clearly before the killing. God wants His people to be serious; in the matter of salvation there is no "close enough."

Second, in qualification it must be without blemish, a male of the first year. "Without blemish" in the original means complete, whole; we will place the word anchor in the notes and discuss it slowly there. Here first remember one principle: what is offered to God and substitutes for a person cannot be the leftover, the second-rate, the lame. What is worthless in human hands cannot be used to ransom a human life. God wants the best one; this is not God being picky — this is God teaching His people to recognize the cost: a life ransoming a life must be a wholly unblemished life. As for kind, it may be taken from the sheep or from the goats; there is flexibility in kind, but no flexibility in wholeness. This too is worth our learning: God gives people room in form, but never discounts holiness.

Third, in amount it is taken by household, and the lamb is reckoned according to the number of people. "Every man a lamb, according to the house of their fathers, a lamb for an house"; if a household is too small to eat a lamb, it shall join with its next-door neighbor, reckoned according to the number of souls. Brothers and sisters, please note: here God with His own hand dismantles two extremes. On one side is going it alone: salvation is not something a person receives shut up alone in a room; the lamb is received together by household and by congregation, and a small household must combine with a neighbor to form one table — the table of salvation is by nature a shared table. On the other side is waste and show: one must not take more because one is rich, nor be careless because one has many people; reckoning according to the number is reverence, and it is also restraint. Still more important is the "house" in "a lamb for an house": the unit saved that night was not an individual's index of piety, but household after household with blood on its door. Parents, whether there is blood on the door of your house tonight is a question God asks before He asks about your personal spiritual condition.

Fourth, there are also commands about the manner of eating, which we will discuss in detail in the second part; here only one sentence in advance: this lamb is to be roasted whole — head, legs, and inward parts together — its bones are not to be broken, and none of it is to remain until the morning. This is not fastidiousness about a meal; this is a picture: one whole lamb, wholly set before one household. That God handed down such a detailed checklist itself says one thing: obedience has details. Many people believe in God in a very grand way and love God in a very abstract way, yet God in this very chapter measures inch by inch: take it on the tenth, kill it on the fourteenth, where to put the blood, how to roast the meat, whether the bread has leaven, what shoes the people wear. It is not that God is fussy; it is that He wants us to understand: a saved person shows, on a concrete day, in a concrete place, in a concrete manner, that he believes His word. Tonight let us first look clearly at the lamb; in the next section, we will look at that basin of blood.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Blood on the Door and the Judgment of That Night (12:21-42)

Moses called all the elders and passed God's word on exactly as it was, and the people did according to it. The lamb was killed, the blood was caught in a basin, and with a bunch of hyssop dipped in the blood, it was struck on the lintel and on the two side posts [7][8]. Please picture this scene in your heart: it was not struck in the middle of the door panel, nor was a large mark painted on the door for people to see; it was struck on the top and the two sides of the doorframe. A person entering or leaving that door passed under the blood; a household sitting inside eating the lamb had blood above its head and on both sides. The very position of this blood is a sentence of theology: a saved person lives under the covering of the blood.

Then God spoke the central sentence of the whole chapter: "And the blood shall be to you for a token upon the houses where ye are: and when I see the blood, I will pass over you." [1] Brothers and sisters, please bite down on every word of this sentence. First, the blood is a token, not a talisman. The logic of a talisman is: the object itself has magic power; if you hang it in the right place and recite the right spell, disaster automatically retreats. That is not the Bible's logic. The Bible's logic is: the blood is a token, a proof; it does not itself frighten anything away; it is placed before God as a memorial. Disaster does not come, not because the blood is magical, but because God saw the blood, God remembers His own word, and God passes over according to His promise. Second, please see who the subject is: "when I see the blood, I will pass over you." The center is not in the human technique of applying it, not in whether you applied it evenly or thickly or in the right posture. The hyssop shook, palms were full of sweat, and that night many a father's hand trembled as he finished those three strokes. God did not say, I will see who applied it most up to standard, and pass over that one; God said, when I see the blood, I will pass over. The assurance of salvation is not in how steady the hand of your faith is, but in how accurate God's eye is and how firm God's remembrance is. This one sentence is for everyone who doubts himself amid fear: the people inside the door were not necessarily all strong; people inside the door also feared, also wept, also lay awake all night listening to the sounds outside — yet they were saved, because what was decisive that night was not the performance of the people inside the door, but the token of blood on the door and the seeing of God outside the door.

Third, we must also honestly speak of the other side: the token had to be applied as commanded. God said, when I see the blood I will pass over — the premise is that the blood is on the door. If a person said, I believe God in my heart, why must I apply those external things; God looks at the heart, not at ritual — that night, such a person did not obtain the protection of the promise. It is not that God is bound by ritual; it is that a person, by the act of obedience, shows that he has truly taken God's word seriously. If faith is unwilling to walk to the doorway, unwilling to dip that bunch of hyssop, unwilling to put the blood on, that faith is still only on the lips. The way of salvation, from this chapter on, laid down its rule: trust the token, plus obey the details. Neither can be lacking: with only details and no trust, the blood becomes talisman superstition; with only trust and unwillingness in the details, faith becomes empty talk.

Next look at the manner of eating; this likewise is not a minor matter of daily life, but a declaration of faith. The lamb's flesh was to be roasted with fire, not eaten raw and not sodden with water, roasted whole with its head, legs, and inward parts. Roasting means wholly passing through the fire, with nothing held back; it was eaten with unleavened bread and bitter herbs. The bitter herbs were to make them remember with their tongues the bitterness of Egypt: the bitterness you ate in Egypt — do not pretend to forget it just because you are saved tonight; the unleavened bread was because they left in such haste that the dough had no time to be leavened, and this haste itself became a memorial; later, eating unleavened bread for seven days of the feast, with no leaven to be seen in the house, all became an echo of this night [9]. When eating, loins girded, shoes on feet, staff in hand, eaten in haste. Brothers and sisters, this is a strange meal: it is not a victory banquet — a victory banquet is sat down at and enjoyed slowly; this is a marching meal, a group of slaves dressed in full like a group of people about to set out on campaign. A saved person must be fully dressed and ready to depart: God says we leave tonight, so you cannot eat this meal in your nightclothes. Salvation has a kind of urgency: it does not mean pack your bags tomorrow, nor wait until I have settled this bit of business in Egypt before following God. Girded loins, shoes on, staff in hand, eaten in haste — the posture of the body is the posture of faith. Today we should also ask ourselves: in my relationship with God, am I already fully dressed, ready to go the moment He calls, or are my shoes still under the bed, my staff lost who knows where, and my belt never girded at all? When God's time arrives, preparation must be finished on the day the lamb is killed; the night of the Passover waits for no one to make up the lesson.

Then, that night truly came. "And it came to pass, that at midnight the LORD smote all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, from the firstborn of Pharaoh that sat on his throne unto the firstborn of the captive that was in the dungeon; and all the firstborn of cattle." Pharaoh and all his servants rose up in the night, and there was a great cry in Egypt, for there was not a house where there was not one dead. Here we must stop and solemnly face two matters.

First, this is judgment, not merely disaster. In the preceding passage God had already said plainly: that night He would pass through the land of Egypt, smite all the firstborn, and execute judgment against all the gods of Egypt [11]. Many of the gods the Egyptians worshiped claimed to protect children, some claimed to watch over cattle, some claimed to give life; that night, not one of these gods protected its own people. Judgment came upon Egypt's idol system, and also upon Pharaoh's theology of kingship: Pharaoh called himself a god, the lord of life, yet he could not even protect the firstborn on his own throne.

Second, the contrast of the firstborn is the deepest-buried line in the whole book. Do you remember chapter 4? God said to Moses: Israel is My son, even My firstborn; if you refuse to let him go, I will slay thy son, even thy firstborn [12]. Pharaoh held God's firstborn and would not let go; plague after plague, God gave ample opportunity, and Pharaoh's heart grew harder each time. By this night, the one who held another's firstborn had his own firstborn taken away. This is not emotional revenge; this is the echo of God's righteousness: with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again. Every person listening to this passage should record this in his heart: God's patience is long, but it does not drag on endlessly; opportunities are given many times, but the one who refuses to the end must bear the outcome of refusal. Those with blood on the door rested that night; those with no blood on the door wailed that night; the distinction was not in race, nor in a score of good and evil, but only in whether one stood, in the manner God appointed, under the covering of the token.

That night one beautiful thing also happened: Pharaoh called Moses and Aaron by night and said, rise up, get out, take even your flocks and herds, only bless me also. The Egyptians also urged the people to leave quickly, and as the people asked, they gave them jewels of gold, jewels of silver, and raiment, and the Scripture says the people spoiled the Egyptians [13]. The prophecy of chapter 11 was fulfilled here: a group of people who had been slaves for more than four hundred years, empty-handed, walked out of Egypt holding gold, silver, and clothing in their arms. This is not the pride of robbery; this is the back payment of righteousness: more than four hundred years of labor without wages — God settled it for them on the final night. Brothers and sisters, our God keeps accounts, and He never shortchanges anyone; what people owe you, what Egypt owes you — you do not need to stretch out your own hand to seize it; when the day God has appointed arrives, He Himself will cause those who shortchanged you to deliver the goods into your hand.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: On the Very Day the Four Hundred and Thirty Years Ended, the Hosts Went Out (12:29-51) and the New Testament Reading

The Scripture sums up the scale of the Exodus in one sentence: "Now the sojourning of the children of Israel, who dwelt in Egypt, was four hundred and thirty years. And it came to pass at the end of the four hundred and thirty years, even the selfsame day it came to pass, that all the hosts of the LORD went out from the land of Egypt." [10] Four hundred and thirty years — how long is that? So long that not one Israelite had seen Canaan with his own eyes, so long that the language they spoke, the days they lived, and the things they feared had all taken on the color of Egypt. Yet God said: on that very day, not a day early, not a day late, on the selfsame day the years were full, the hosts went out. Please note this name: hosts. When they went out, they were holding children in their arms, driving flocks and herds, carrying unbaked dough and gold and silver on their backs — by any appearance a crowd of refugees; yet God called them hosts, because in God's eyes their identity had already changed: a company of slaves, once named by God, became His hosts. We who are saved are also like this: others look at your past, God looks at the company into which He has mustered you; others address you according to your origin, God addresses you according to His redemption.

Among those who went together there is one detail many people skip over when reading the Scripture; I ask you not to skip it: "And a mixed multitude went up also with them; and flocks, and herds, even very much cattle." [14] The mixed multitude were people of mixed tribes — there were Egyptians, and there were people of other foreign peoples; that night they saw God's acts, their hearts steeled themselves, and they followed out. The boundary of the saved community, from the first day, was drawn very clearly: it is not bloodline, it is covenant. It is not where you were born or who your ancestors were, but in which door you stand tonight, and whether you will go with this people. Later the ordinance section supplies the other side: a foreigner, a sojourner, and a hired servant shall not eat of the Passover; but if a foreigner is willing to have all his males circumcised and enter the covenant, he may keep the feast and eat, with no difference from one born in the land [15]. Strictness and openness wonderfully coexist in the same ordinance section: the door is not entered casually — one who has not entered the covenant cannot sit at the Passover table; and the door is not shut dead — anyone willing to enter according to God's covenant has his place at the table. The church today should also hold these two sides: on one side, the boundary of faith cannot be blurred, and entering the covenant has its appointed way; on the other side, a person of any background, so long as he is willing to enter in the manner God has appointed, we must not bar him from the door because of bloodline, origin, or past. That a mixed multitude followed out of Egypt is the first crack through which the gospel opens to the nations.

The institution of remembrance is also established in this chapter. After the Exodus, God commanded: this day shall be remembered; the Passover shall be kept generation after generation as an ordinance forever; when your children ask, what does this mean, you shall tell them: it is the sacrifice of the LORD's passover, who passed over the houses of the children of Israel in Egypt [1]. Brothers and sisters, how does a single event become an ever-present identity? By remembrance. By eating this meal again year after year, by telling the story to the children generation after generation, by every generation standing again under the doorframe and saying: those who were passed over that night — that was us. People are forgetful; the days of slavery will be beautified, and God's acts will grow dim; therefore, on the very day of salvation, God nailed down the calendar of remembrance. Our life of faith is likewise: breaking bread, keeping feasts, reading Scripture, bearing witness are all medicine God gives to forgetful people. Do not despise repetition; identity is carved deeper, again and again, in repeated remembrance.

There is one more thing worth our pausing over: in this chapter, God's command to the people to explain the meaning of this feast to their descendants is given with more care than many other commands. In that day, when your son asks you, what is this, you shall answer: it is the sacrifice of the LORD's passover, because He passed over the houses of the children of Israel in Egypt, smote the Egyptians, and delivered our houses. The people heard this word and bowed their heads and worshiped. Brothers and sisters, please note this order: the story of salvation had not yet fully passed, and God was already teaching this generation how to tell it to the next. If faith does not enter the telling within the family, it remains only one generation's excitement; if faith is told, generation after generation, as one's own family's affair, it becomes the bones of a nation. Those among us who are parents and grandparents — have your children asked you what your faith actually means? Have you prepared your answer? The answer Exodus chapter 12 gives is not a long treatise of doctrine, but one night, one lamb, one door with blood on it: if only you tell that night clearly, the child will know that our family is a family that was passed over by God and brought out by God. When you tell it, do not embellish how brave you were in the story, because the people inside the door were in fact trembling; what is to be told is how God saw, how God remembered, and how, on the day the four hundred and thirty years were full, without a single day's delay, He brought His people out.

Finally, we read this chapter against the New Testament according to the Christian way of reading. Please note, what I am speaking of is the later Christian interpretive reading, not a claim that the original text of Exodus was narrating Christ about itself; the original text was first spoken to the Israelites of that night, and on this point we must keep our measure. Within that measure, the New Testament itself opens the way for us: Paul says, purge out the old leaven, for even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us [16]; John the Baptist saw Jesus coming and said, Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world [17]; Peter says we were redeemed not with corruptible things such as silver and gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot [18]; the Book of Hebrews also says that through faith Moses kept the passover and the sprinkling of blood, lest he that destroyed the firstborn should touch them [19]. A Christian reading Exodus chapter 12 cannot help thinking of the Lord Jesus: the Lamb who first dwelt with us, who was examined and found without spot, who was slain at Passover — His blood is placed on the door of our lives, and God, seeing it, passes over us. But as we read, let us humbly say: this is the New Testament shedding light on the Old, not our forcing the New back into the Old. The glory of the original text needs no exaggeration from us: in a single night, a nation was separated out of slavery by the token of blood — this in itself is already one of the greatest scenes in the history of salvation; the New Testament reading makes us recognize that the Lamb whom this scene prepared finally came in person.

Applied to ourselves, I leave only three sentences. First, the way of salvation is to trust the token plus obey the details: do not alter God's plan, do not seek a covering apart from the blood, and do not be clever on your own in the details. Second, salvation has the timeliness of a "night": the plague on Egypt's firstborn had its appointed time, and the decisions of our lives also have their windows; preparation must be finished on the day the lamb is killed — do not drag obedience to tomorrow. Third, remembrance makes salvation into identity: write down what God has done for you, speak it out, pass it to your children, and let a single event become the first month of your whole family, generation after generation.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through one long chapter: God set the first month as the beginning of the year, because redemption redefines time; God commanded that a lamb without blemish be taken, because substitution admits no "close enough"; the blood was struck on the doorposts and the lintel, and God said, when I see the blood, I will pass over you, because the center of salvation is not in our hands but in God's eye and God's remembrance; that night, judgment went through Egypt, and the one who had held God's firstborn lost his own firstborn, while the people inside the door — girded, shod, and staff in hand — finished eating fully ready, and on the very day the four hundred and thirty years were full, went out of Egypt as hosts, and even the mixed multitude followed up with them.

Brothers and sisters, when you go home after the meeting tonight, please stop for a moment before your own door. No blood can be seen on the door, but if you trust the Lord Jesus, His blood has already become the token of your life. You need no longer fear the night that goes through the land; what you are to do is gird your belt, put on your shoes, be ready to go with Him at any time, and tell the story of this night to your children.

Let us pray together: LORD our God, thank You that on a night four thousand years ago, You saw the blood on the door and passed over Your people; thank You that at Golgotha You prepared for us that Lamb without spot, whose blood is placed upon the door of our hearts, and judgment passes over us. Forgive the carelessness of our faith and the delay of our obedience; teach us to trust the token and also to obey the details; teach us to be fully dressed and ready to depart, and to remember generation after generation. In the name of the Passover Lamb, the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] pesach and pasach: Passover, Hebrew פֶּסַח (pesach, H6453), Exodus 12:11 "it is the LORD's passover," and 12:27, where the people explain the origin of the feast's name to their children; its verb is pasach, פָּסַח (H6452), Exodus 12:13, 23, 27, "the LORD will pass over." The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Scholarship holds two views on the verb's etymology, presented side by side: one view renders it "pass over, leap over," meaning that God passed over the houses bearing the token of blood; the other view, based on the use of the same word in Isaiah 31:5, "the LORD of hosts [will] defend Jerusalem," holds that it contains the sense of "protect, hover in defense," as a mother bird spreads her wings over her young. This manuscript preaches according to the KJV's rendering "pass over"; both views are presented side by side and no conclusion is forced. "The blood is a token, not a talisman" is a homiletical exposition based on 12:13, "when I see the blood," and is marked as exposition in the text.

[2] seh: lamb, Hebrew שֶׂה (H7716), Exodus 12:3, "every man a lamb (seh), according to the house of their fathers," and 12:4-5; the word can refer generically to one head of sheep or goats, hence 12:5 states plainly that it may be taken from the sheep or from the goats; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[3] tamim: without blemish, Hebrew תָּמִים (H8549), Exodus 12:5, "Your lamb shall be without blemish, a male of the first year"; the word means complete, whole, blameless, and is also used to describe a person's completeness (e.g., Genesis 17:1); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The text's "substitution must be wholly unblemished" is a homiletical exposition based on this word.

[4] mezuzot: doorposts, Hebrew מְזוּזֹת (H4201, plural), Exodus 12:7, "strike it on the two side posts," and 12:22-23; it refers to the two upright posts of a door; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[5] mashqof: lintel, Hebrew מַשְׁקוֹף (H4947), Exodus 12:7, 22-23, listed together with the doorposts; it refers to the crossbeam above the door; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note. The text's "passing under the blood, living under the covering of the blood" is a homiletical exposition based on the position of the application.

[6] matstsot: unleavened bread, Hebrew מַצּוֹת (H4682, plural), Exodus 12:8, "with unleavened bread and bitter herbs," 12:15-20 on the ordinances of the Feast of Unleavened Bread, and 12:39, where unleavened cakes were baked from the unbaked dough; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[7] Hyssop and basin: Exodus 12:22 commands taking a bunch of hyssop, dipping it in the blood in the basin, and striking the lintel and the doorposts, and commands that no one go out of the door of his house until the morning; the narration of the act of application in Part Two of the text is based on this verse.

[8] The order of application: Exodus 12:7 names the doorposts and the lintel first, while 12:22 names the lintel first and then the doorposts; the order differs, and this manuscript makes no doctrinal exposition on the order, stating only that the positions are the top and the two sides.

[9] Unleavened bread and the removal of leaven: Exodus 12:15-19, keeping the Feast of Unleavened Bread for seven days, with no leaven to be seen in the house, the same rule applying to outsiders who keep the ordinance; in 12:34 and 39 the people carried the unbaked dough and went out of Egypt with unleavened cakes, in mutual causation with the "haste"; the text preaches on this basis.

[10] tsiv'ot / hosts: Exodus 12:41, "all the hosts (tsiv'ot, צִבְאוֹת, H6635 plural) of the LORD went out from the land of Egypt," and 12:51 with the same wording; the word often refers to armies, to the multitude of a congregation; the text's identity exposition based on God's calling those who came out of Egypt "hosts" is marked as exposition.

[11] Executing judgment against all the gods of Egypt: Exodus 12:12, "and against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment: I am the LORD"; see also Numbers 33:4. Part Two of this manuscript, "judgment came upon Egypt's idol system," is based on this.

[12] The contrast of the firstborn: Exodus 4:22-23, where God calls Israel "my son, even my firstborn," and foretells that if Pharaoh refuses to let Israel go, God will slay Pharaoh's firstborn; Exodus 11:4-5 and 12:29-30 are its fulfillment. The text's "the one who held God's firstborn lost his own firstborn" is a homiletical exposition based on this contrast.

[13] Spoiling the Egyptians of their goods: Exodus 12:35-36, where the people, according to Moses' word, asked the Egyptians for jewels of gold, jewels of silver, and raiment, and the LORD gave the people favor in the sight of the Egyptians; this fulfills the prophecies of Exodus 3:21-22 and 11:2-3. The text's "the back payment of righteousness / settled on the final night" is a homiletical exposition, not the literal wording of the Scripture, and is specially marked as such.

[14] 'erev rav: mixed multitude, Hebrew עֵרֶב רַב (H6154), Exodus 12:38, "And a mixed multitude went up also with them"; it refers to a mixed crowd of people, whose composition the Scripture does not specify one by one; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The text's "the boundary of the saved community is covenant, not bloodline" is a homiletical exposition based on this verse and the ordinance section.

[15] Summary of the ordinance section (12:43-51): a foreigner, a sojourner, and a hired servant shall not eat of the Passover (12:43, 45); a bought servant, after circumcision, may eat of it (12:44); if a foreigner has all his males circumcised, he may keep the Passover, the same as one born in the land (12:48); it shall be eaten in one house, none of it carried outside, and no bone of it broken (12:46); Part Three of the text, "strictness and openness coexisting in keeping the ordinance," covers this in one passage on this basis.

[16] 1 Corinthians 5:7-8: Paul, saying "even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us," exhorts the church to purge out the old leaven; this manuscript lists this as a New Testament Christian interpretive reading, expressly noting that it is not to be read back as the Old Testament text narrating itself.

[17] John 1:29: John the Baptist pointed to Jesus as "the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world"; its correspondence with the lamb theme of Exodus 12 belongs to the New Testament reading, as in note [16].

[18] 1 Peter 1:18-19: redemption is by the precious blood of Christ, "as of a lamb without blemish and without spot," corresponding to the tamim (without blemish) of 12:5; this belongs to the New Testament reading.

[19] Hebrews 11:28: through faith Moses kept the passover and the sprinkling of blood, lest the destroyer of the firstborn should touch them; see also the slain Lamb worshiped in Revelation chapter 5; included as a self-selected cross-reference of the New Testament reading.

[20] hamashchit: the destroyer, Hebrew הַמַּשְׁחִית (H4889, article + participle), Exodus 12:23, "will not suffer the destroyer to come in unto your houses"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And the blood shall be to you for a token upon the houses where ye are: and when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and the plague shall not be upon you to destroy you, when I smite the land of Egypt." (Exodus 12:13, KJV) "And thus shall ye eat it; with your loins girded, your shoes on your feet, and your staff in your hand; and ye shall eat it in haste: it is the LORD's passover." (Exodus 12:11, KJV)

That night Israel was saved not because they were better than the Egyptians, and not because their hands painted the blood more skillfully than anyone else. God said it plainly: when I see the blood, I will pass over you. What decided that night was God's seeing and God's remembering. Our assurance today likewise does not rest on how steady the hand of our faith is or how well we perform, but on the blood of the Lamb already placed upon the door of our hearts. People forget their own promises; God never forgets His own token. Yet the people that night were not saved lying down: they ate girded, shod, and staff in hand, and when God called, they went. Trust gives our hearts rest, and obedience keeps our feet moving. Do two things today: first, hand your fear over, and believe that when God sees the blood, He keeps His word; second, put your shoes on — if God is leading you out of Egypt today, do not still be looking for your staff. Preparation must be finished today, because the night of the Passover never waits for anyone.

## Abstract

Leviticus 26 is the closing chapter of the Holiness Code (Lev 17-26) and the covenant sanctions of the whole book: the holiness demanded in the preceding twenty-five chapters is here set inside a solemn frame — obedience brings blessing, rebellion brings discipline. This sermon does not expound the catalogue of calamities verse by verse; it follows the chapter's structure and theological thread. The chapter is framed by the prohibition of idols and the keeping of the Sabbath at its head and by the colophon of "statutes and judgments and laws" at its tail. The blessing section (vv. 3-13) climbs from rain and grain to peace, and its climax is not the harvest but God walking among His people and being their God. The curse section (vv. 14-39) is written as a fivefold escalation, and at every step a door back is left open; within it, the land enjoying its Sabbaths while desolate interlocks directly with the Sabbatical year of chapter 25. The chapter's final turn (vv. 40-45) rests on confession and remembrance: man confesses, and God remembers the covenant. The last stroke of the sanctions is not destruction but remembrance; the end of discipline is return, not rejection.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 Prologue and colophon answering each other: the sanctions are terms of the covenant, not a later appendix

(Fact) The chapter opens not with blessing or curse but with two commands. Verse 1 forbids idols: no carved image or pillar is to be set up, and no figured stone placed in the land to bow down to (Summary, with the verse's closing clause quoted below). Verse 2 completes the word: "Ye shall keep my sabbaths, and reverence my sanctuary: I am the Lord." (Lev 26:2, KJV) Notice where these two verses stand: they are not inside the lists of blessing and curse; they stand before the lists. Before any consequence is mentioned, the text fixes two things — no other god, and the Sabbath kept, the sanctuary reverenced. (Application) That order is itself a sermon: the sanctions are not a free-standing machinery of reward and punishment; they grow inside a relationship. First "I am the LORD," first no idols and the Sabbath, and only then can consequences of obedience and rebellion be spoken of. Remove verses 1-2 and chapter 26 becomes a cold table of retribution; restore them, and the whole chapter is the speech of a covenant God to His people: know first who I am, and then hear Me set both roads before you.

(Fact) The closing clause of verse 1 breathes the same air as verse 2: "for I am the Lord your God." (Lev 26:1, KJV) This self-declaration is the door-frame of the chapter. On the other side of the frame stands the last verse, the colophon of verse 46: "These are the statutes and judgments and laws, which the Lord made between him and the children of Israel in mount Sinai by the hand of Moses." (Lev 26:46, KJV) The opening tells who God is; the close tells where these words were made, and through whom — Mount Sinai, Moses. Frame closed, everything between is fixed inside one fact: this is no prophet's improvised warning and no later appendix to the law, but terms declared at the making of the covenant itself. (Application) That matters greatly for today's hearers. We tend to read the blessing as the promise and the discipline as the exception, as though God first gave grace and only later, reluctantly, added punishment. Leviticus 26 is not framed that way: blessing and curse are the two faces of one covenant, declared once at Sinai. The covenant sets both roads out clearly from the beginning — and that clarity is itself part of the grace, for man does not discover the rules only when he reaches the precipice; he hears the end while standing at the start.

(Fact) Structurally the chapter falls into four great blocks: the prologue (vv. 1-2); the blessing (vv. 3-13); the curse (vv. 14-39); repentance and remembrance (vv. 40-45); and the colophon (v. 46). (Scholarly and historical discussion) This arrangement may be compared with the form of ancient Near Eastern covenant documents, which commonly close with blessings and curses as terms acknowledged by both parties; commentators discuss the comparison at length, and this sermon notes it at the level of form only, without resting any dating conclusion on it. (Fact) One verbal thread must be flagged at the outset: "walking." The blessing opens, "If ye walk in my statutes" (v. 3); the blessing's climax says God will "walk among you" (v. 12); and the curse section repeats that the people "walk contrary" — in every case the underlying verb is the same, to walk. (Application) Faith in this chapter is not a static creed but a road; the only question is whether man and God walk together, or walk toward each other and collide. The four sections that follow trace that walking line: first the blessing of walking together, then how the curse deepens step by step when men walk contrary, then the land resting in desolation, and finally how the road is rejoined when man confesses and God remembers the covenant.

(Fact) One further point belongs at the head: by the text's own account, the scene of promulgation is at Mount Sinai, before Israel has entered the promised land (v. 46, quoted above). Almost everything the blessing and curse describe has therefore not yet happened: the land is not yet entered, no timely rain has yet fallen, not one Sabbatical year has yet been kept, and desolation and exile lie far in the future. (Application) This is a view of history announced outside the gates of the promised land: before Israel possesses the land, they are told — the land is not yours unconditionally; the way you live on the land decides whether you can remain on it. A gracious inheritance is delivered together with its instructions for living, and the first page of those instructions is this twenty-sixth chapter.

### II. 08:00-24:00 The blessing: from timely rain to God walking among them

(Fact) The blessing opens with a conditional sentence, the sentence from which this sermon's title is taken: "If ye walk in my statutes, and keep my commandments, and do them;" (Lev 26:3, KJV) In the Chinese base text the verse ends at "my commandments" with a comma — the condition is left hanging, and the consequence does not begin until the next verse. That is no grammatical accident: the text suspends the "if" in the air and lets the hearer pause on the condition. Walk, and keep — the blessing is not distributed automatically and unconditionally; it hangs on a road walked out. (Fact) The promise that follows begins in heaven (v. 4): "Then I will give you rain in due season, and the land shall yield her increase, and the trees of the field shall yield their fruit." (Lev 26:4, KJV) "In due season" is the operative phrase: not the greatest quantity of rain, but rain on time. The agriculture of Canaan hangs on seasonal rain; rain too early or too late is disaster. The first stroke of the promised blessing falls on rhythm — God restores the seasons to their order. (Application) That cuts today's hearer just as sharply. When we ask for blessing we usually ask for "more": more yield, more opportunity, more income. The blessing of Leviticus 26 asks first for "in season": what comes, coming when it ought to come. Rain in due season is more like blessing than a flood; a life with rhythm and season is itself the shape of being blessed.

(Fact) Verse 5 writes the harvest as a chain that never breaks: "And your threshing shall reach unto the vintage, and the vintage shall reach unto the sowing time: and ye shall eat your bread to the full, and dwell in your land safely." (Lev 26:5, KJV) Threshing runs into grape harvest, grape harvest into sowing — the seasons' work overlaps, meaning the previous season's labour is not finished before the next season's produce is at the door. Verse 10 adds (Summary) that they shall eat old store, and bring forth the old because of the new: the barn is still feeding them from last year's grain when the new crop is so abundant the old must be moved out to make room. (Application) Scripture paints abundance not with swollen adjectives but with a crowded timetable and a barn too small. Abundance here is concrete: work that joins up, grain that cannot be eaten through, a storehouse that must be cleared. A sermon that turns this verse into an abstract "prosperity mindset" dissolves the most solid thing in the text; the blessing spoken of is first of all in the plate, in the barn, in the days.

(Fact) After abundance comes peace (v. 6): "And I will give peace in the land, and ye shall lie down, and none shall make you afraid: and I will rid evil beasts out of the land, neither shall the sword go through your land." (Lev 26:6, KJV) Mark the picture: lying down, with none to make afraid. The first image of peace is not triumph on a battlefield but sleeping through the night. Evil beasts removed, the sword not passing through — the threats from wild country and from the border are lifted at both ends. Only then comes warfare (v. 7, Summary: you shall chase your enemies, and they shall fall before you by the sword), with its famous proportion: "And five of you shall chase an hundred, and an hundred of you shall put ten thousand to flight: and your enemies shall fall before you by the sword." (Lev 26:8, KJV) Five against a hundred, a hundred against ten thousand — the ratio stretches wider and wider, and the text's point is not Israel's valour but that victory is not reckoned by numbers. (Application) The order of the blessing deserves slow reading here: rain and grain first, then sleeping unafraid, and only then the battlefield. The text puts peace before victory — first lie down in safety, then chase enemies. A nation that cannot sleep at night is not at peace however many battles it wins; the blessing God gives settles a man's nights before it settles his borders.

(Fact) Verse 9 gathers the blessing onto the covenant: "For I will have respect unto you, and make you fruitful, and multiply you, and establish my covenant with you." (Lev 26:9, KJV) Fruitfulness and multiplication echo the blessing vocabulary running from Genesis onward, but this verse's landing point is "establish my covenant with you" — the increase of the people is not an end in itself but the outward form of the covenant being established. Then the section drives toward its climax (v. 11): "And I set my tabernacle among you: and my soul shall not abhor you." (Lev 26:11, KJV) The tabernacle set among them means God's dwelling is not on some distant mountain but in the very middle of the camp. (Fact) Verse 12 is the summit of the blessing section, and arguably of the whole chapter: "And I will walk among you, and will be your God, and ye shall be my people." (Lev 26:12, KJV) The verb rendered "walk" is hithallēkh, the reflexive (Hithpael) form of hālak (Strong's H1980), carrying the sense of walking to and fro, moving about in the midst — not passing by, not watching from afar, but dwelling within and walking back and forth. (Application) Read the blessing list from its beginning to this point and watch its trajectory: rain, produce, fruit, fullness, safe sleep, victory, increase — every item a good thing, and not one of them the summit. The list's peak is not a thing but a Companion who walks with them. God does not sum up the blessing as "you shall have everything," but as "I will walk among you." For today's blessing-list collectors, that is a correction of order: the centre of blessing is not the blessing but the Blesser dwelling in the midst. If God walks in the midst, rain and grain are grace; if God is not in the midst, the fullest barn is only inventory.

(Fact) The blessing section closes at verse 13 by looking back to the exodus: "I am the Lord your God, which brought you forth out of the land of Egypt, that ye should not be their bondmen; and I have broken the bands of your yoke, and made you go upright." (Lev 26:13, KJV) Two expressions must be handled by the original text. The first is "the bands of your yoke": Hebrew mōṭōṯ ʿōl, where ʿōl (Strong's H5923) is "yoke"; the "bands" are the bars of the yoke, the very timber pressing on the neck, and it is precisely that part God has broken. The second is "go upright": qôməmiyyûṯ (Strong's H6968), a word occurring only here in the Old Testament, denoting the posture of standing erect, head raised. (Application) When the blessing list is finished, the text does not leave the hearer sitting on the list; it carries him back to Egypt: you were once slaves bent double, the bar of the yoke on your necks; the LORD brought you out and broke that bar. "Go upright" is therefore more than posture; it is identity — the way delivered people walk. Read verses 12 and 13 together and the whole blessing stands clear: God walking in the midst, and a people walking upright; a walking God, keeping company with an upright-walking people. That picture is the terminus of the blessing section — and the measuring line for the curse that follows, for the curse dismantles, item by item, exactly the things in this picture.

### III. 24:00-42:00 The curse: a fivefold escalation, and the land enjoying its Sabbaths

(Fact) The curse turns at verse 14: "But if ye will not hearken unto me, and will not do all these commandments;" (Lev 26:14, KJV) Verse 15 then spells out what rebellion is (Summary): despising the statutes, the soul abhorring the judgments, refusing to do all the commandments, breaking the covenant. Notice the text's own explanation of "not doing": it is not weakness, not oversight, but a string of active verbs — despise, abhor, break. The curse section addresses not what man cannot do, but man turning his back. (Application) Fix that point first, and the catalogue that follows cannot be read as a temperamental deity losing His temper: what the text presents is one party to the covenant tearing it up, and the other party executing the terms written into the covenant from the start. Every term was read aloud at Sinai; not one was added afterwards.

(Fact) This sermon reads verses 14-39 as a fivefold escalation, following the markers the text itself supplies. The divisions are not imposed by the preacher; the text nails them out with a repeated formula — each new stage reopens with an "if ye will not yet hearken" and answers with heavier punishment. The first stage (vv. 14-17): terror appointed over them, consumption and burning ague consuming the eyes, seed sown in vain, defeat before enemies (vv. 16-17, Summary) — body and field are struck first, and the victory ratio of the blessing is inverted: there, five chased a hundred; here, they flee when none pursues. The second stage (v. 18) opens with its headline: "And if ye will not yet for all this hearken unto me, then I will punish you seven times more for your sins." (Lev 26:18, KJV) There follow heaven as iron and earth as brass, strength spent in vain, the land yielding no increase (vv. 19-20, Summary) — the timely rain of the blessing is withdrawn, and heaven and earth are sealed like metal. The third stage (vv. 21-22) opens: "And if ye walk contrary unto me, and will not hearken unto me; I will bring seven times more plagues upon you according to your sins." (Lev 26:21, KJV) Its content is wild beasts sent among them, robbing them of their children, destroying their cattle, making them few in number, their ways desolate (v. 22, Summary) — the evil beasts removed in the blessing are now sent in. The fourth stage (vv. 23-26): "Then will I also walk contrary unto you, and will punish you yet seven times for your sins." (Lev 26:24, KJV) A sword is brought that avenges the quarrel of the covenant; when they gather inside their cities, pestilence follows them in; the staff of bread is broken, and they eat and are not satisfied (vv. 25-26, Summary). The fifth stage (vv. 27-39) opens with the sternest formula: "And if ye will not for all this hearken unto me, but walk contrary unto me; Then I will walk contrary unto you also in fury; and I, even I, will chastise you seven times for your sins." (Lev 26:27-28, KJV) There follow the horrors of siege, high places destroyed, cities laid waste, and the people scattered among the nations (vv. 29-39, Summary). This sermon keeps the fifth stage at the level of summary by design: the text wrote these words as warning, not as material for spectacle; to state their structure and direction calmly, at the (Fact) level, is weight enough for the hearer.

(Fact) Two counts in this section must be counted correctly before they are preached. The first is the count of "seven." The punishment is marked as intensified with "seven" four times in this section — at verses 18, 21, 24, and 28 (in the Chinese base text, verses 18 and 21 read "sevenfold," verses 24 and 28 "seven times"; the four occurrences together are the count, counted verse by verse in this sermon). "Seven" here is not an arithmetical multiplier table but Scripture's customary number of completeness: the discipline is made, each time, more complete and more inexcusable. The second is the count of "walking contrary." The word behind it, qerî (Strong's H7147; with the preposition, bĕqerî), occurs seven times in this chapter — repeatedly in the curse section at verses 21, 23, 24, 27, and 28, and twice more in the confession section at verses 40 and 41 (checked occurrence by occurrence against the Hebrew text in preparing this sermon). (Application) The literal flavour of qerî is of meeting head-on, coming face to face: man does not walk away with his back to God; he walks toward God and collides with Him. The escalation of the curse is, at bottom, the escalation of that head-on walk — each time man walks contrary, God answers according to the covenant in mirror image: "if ye walk contrary unto me… then will I also walk contrary unto you" (vv. 21, 24, quoted above). The mirrored sentence is meant to let the people recognise their own gait inside the discipline.

(Fact) Yet inside the fivefold escalation there runs a line most easily missed: the land. Verse 34: "Then shall the land enjoy her sabbaths, as long as it lieth desolate, and ye be in your enemies' land; even then shall the land rest, and enjoy her sabbaths." (Lev 26:34, KJV) Verse 35 continues (Summary): as long as it lies desolate it shall rest, because it did not rest in their Sabbaths while they dwelt upon it. Verse 43 says it once more (Summary): the land, left of them and desolate without them, shall enjoy her Sabbaths. (Fact) These three verses interlock directly with chapter 25, which establishes the Sabbatical year — every seventh year the land is to keep a Sabbath, and the people are not to sow it. Chapter 26 writes in advance what happens if they refuse: if man will not let the land rest, the land will in the end recover its owed Sabbaths all at once, in desolation. The Sabbath account is not cancelled by man's refusal; it is only repaid in another manner. (Application) This is the quietest and sharpest stroke in the chapter. At the worst point of the curse catalogue, the text suddenly moves the camera off the people and onto the land: the people are driven away, and the land, at last, rests. Rest, it turns out, is not a grace for man only; it is also the land's right. Men who overdraw land and rhythm for generations, treating the Sabbath as indefinitely postponable, are told by this text that the account has been kept all along. A sermon need not elaborate far here; set the intertext plainly: the Sabbatical year of chapter 25 and the desolate rest of chapter 26 are two faces of one law — in obedience, land and people rest together, and it is blessing; in rebellion, the land rests alone while the people languish abroad, and it is the curse within the curse.

(Fact) The curse section closes at verses 36-39 (Summary): a faintness is sent into the hearts of those who remain, in the lands of their enemies; the sound of a shaken leaf chases them; they flee as fleeing from a sword and fall when none pursues, falling one upon another; they have no power to stand before their enemies; they perish among the nations and pine away in their iniquity, and in the iniquities of their fathers, in their enemies' lands. (Application) Set this picture beside the blessing and read them together: in the blessing, "ye shall lie down, and none shall make you afraid" (v. 6); in the curse, the sound of a leaf makes them afraid. In the blessing, five chase a hundred; in the curse, they flee when none pursues. In the blessing, God walks in the midst; in the curse, men pine away in the land of their enemies. The text tells us in perfectly symmetrical strokes that the curse is not some second, alien thing: the curse is the blessing, withdrawn item by item. Whoever grasps that symmetry will not read verses 14-39 as another side of God's character, but as the other face of the same God and the same covenant — as He gave, clearly and item by item, so He withdraws, clearly and without ambiguity.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) Several images in the curse catalogue — siege, famine, scattering among the nations — have counterparts in the curse sections of ancient Near Eastern covenant documents, much discussed by commentators; and this chapter stands in obvious parallel structure with the blessings and curses of Deuteronomy 28 (Summary). This sermon sets the observation side by side and decides nothing about the relative order or sources of the two chapters on the basis of the parallel. The argument the sermon rests on is not a question of composition but the theological structure the text itself has written out: escalation, a door left open, and an account kept. It should also be said honestly that the fivefold division is a reading common in the commentary tradition, and commentators differ slightly at the seams; but the repeated conditional openings and the four occurrences of the "seven" intensification are markers countable in the text itself, and do not change with the seams.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 Repentance and remembrance: the end of discipline is return

(Fact) Verse 39 brings the people to their lowest point; then verse 40 turns, and the air of the whole chapter changes: "If they shall confess their iniquity, and the iniquity of their fathers, with their trespass which they trespassed against me, and that also they have walked contrary unto me;" (Lev 26:40, KJV) Note the shape of confession in this verse: they confess their own iniquity and their fathers' iniquity; they confess the deeds done and also the posture — "they have walked contrary unto me," the very phrase that recurred through the curse, now spoken by the people themselves as part of their confession. (Fact) The verb "confess" is hithwaddû, from yādāh (Strong's H3034), in its reflexive form; its semantic field includes confessing, giving thanks, and declaring — confession here is not private regret but open acknowledgement, spoken aloud: this is what happened; we trespassed against Him. Verse 41 then traces the depth of the confession (Summary): their uncircumcised hearts are humbled, and they accept the punishment of their iniquity. The "uncircumcised heart" is lēḇ ʿārēl, ʿārēl (Strong's H6189) meaning "uncircumcised" — the body bore the covenant's mark, the heart did not yet: the outward sign was present, the inward obstinacy remained. The text says the turning comes when that heart is humbled. (Application) This verse gets repentance exactly right. Its first step is not emotion but acknowledgement of the account: confessing one's own sin, and confessing one's contrary way of walking. Its second step is the humbling of the heart — not being beaten into submission, but the uncircumcised heart finally bowing. Its third step is accepting the punishment — no longer calling the discipline unjust, no longer pleading one's own case. Acknowledgement, humbling, acceptance: when the three are together, the door of verse 42 opens.

(Fact) Verse 42 is the turning point of the whole chapter; hear it in full: "Then will I remember my covenant with Jacob, and also my covenant with Isaac, and also my covenant with Abraham will I remember; and I will remember the land." (Lev 26:42, KJV) The verb "remember" is zāḵar (Strong's H2142), and in Scripture it never means merely bringing to mind — when God remembers the covenant, He acts on it. At the opening of Exodus, God hears the groaning of Israel and remembers His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Exod 2:24, Summary), and the exodus follows. In this chapter's Hebrew text, verse 42 alone uses zāḵar three times within one verse (wəzāḵartî / ʾezkōr / ʾezkōr): the covenants with the three patriarchs are remembered, and the land is remembered. Verse 45 uses it once more (Summary): for their sakes He will remember the covenant of their ancestors, whom He brought out of Egypt in the sight of the nations. So within verses 40-45 the Hebrew has four occurrences in all — three in verse 42, one in verse 45 — counted verse by verse against the Hebrew text. (The Chinese base text, gathering the three covenants under a single verb, reads two occurrences in verse 42 and three in all; an earlier form of this sermon followed that surface count, which does not match the Hebrew, and it is corrected here.) (Fact) The order of the names also deserves a pause: verse 42 lists Jacob, Isaac, Abraham — from nearest to furthest, counting backward up the generations, the reverse of the Genesis narrative order. (Application) That small reversal preaches silently: when God remembers the covenant, He begins counting from the generation presently alive — first Jacob, nearest to them, then Isaac, then Abraham. The covenant is no ancient document in a museum; the starting point of the remembering is this very generation, scattered in the enemies' land, just now opening its mouth to confess. God remembers the patriarchs not by bypassing this generation in nostalgia, but for this generation's sake, taking up the whole chain of the covenant again.

(Fact) Verse 43 gathers up the land's rest once more (Summary): the land is left of them, and while it lies desolate without them it enjoys her Sabbaths; and they accept the punishment of their iniquity, because they despised the judgments and their soul abhorred the statutes. Then comes verse 44, the weightiest sentence of the section (Summary): yet for all that, when they are in the land of their enemies, He will not cast them away, neither will He abhor them, to destroy them utterly, nor break His covenant with them, for He is the LORD their God. (Application) Set this verse beside the words of the curse: there, the people "despised my judgments" and their soul "abhorred my statutes" (v. 43, Summary); God's answer by verse 44 is — I will not abhor you. Man abhors the covenant; God does not abhor man. That single asymmetrical step is where the chapter's grace lands. Verse 11, inside the blessing, said "my soul shall not abhor you"; verse 44, at the end of the curse, says it again: the head of the blessing and the tail of the curse are clasped by the same sentence. The punishment is real, but utter destruction is not in the terms; the scattering is real, but rejection is expressly excluded. The end of the discipline was fixed at the moment the text was written: return, not annihilation.

(Fact) Finally, the conditional structure of this section must be seen plainly, lest grace be preached cheap. Verse 40 opens with "if they shall confess," and verse 42 answers with "then will I remember" — man's confession first, God's remembering after, the order unmistakable. But the other side is equally plain: the covenant God remembers is the covenant He Himself made with the patriarchs; the people's confession adds nothing to the covenant, whose foundation lies in God's initiative — as the exodus of deliverance preceded the covenant of Sinai. (Application) This section is therefore neither "man repents enough and God reluctantly relents," nor "God will remember anyway, so confession is optional." The picture the text draws is of a God who made the covenant long ago and keeps it even amid the calamity, waiting for a people who, in the enemies' land, open their mouths to confess. The moment man opens his mouth, the moment of God's remembering has come. The discipline of the covenant aims at restoration: discipline that cannot bring a man back into the covenant would be mere revenge, and the discipline of Leviticus 26 leaves the door of verse 40 standing open at every step of its escalation — and on the door are written two words: confess it.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: the last stroke falls on remembrance

(Fact) The chapter's colophon is a single verse, verse 46: "These are the statutes and judgments and laws, which the Lord made between him and the children of Israel in mount Sinai by the hand of Moses." (Lev 26:46, KJV) A plain, unadorned sentence of record, yet it nails the whole chapter back to the place it set out from: Mount Sinai. The Holiness Code (Lev 17-26) began with sacrifice and the rules of holiness, passed through the feasts, the Sabbatical year, and the Jubilee, and closes with the sanctions of this twenty-sixth chapter, sealed by this verse — these statutes, judgments, and laws were delivered at the making of the covenant. (Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, the sanctions are terms of the covenant, not God's temper: blessing and curse were spoken once, at Sinai, both roads set out at the starting point, and God never appends rules afterwards. Second, the climax of the blessing is not the barn but the companionship: "And I will walk among you, and will be your God, and ye shall be my people." Every blessing has God-in-the-midst as its summit, and every curse is that picture withdrawn piece by piece. Third, the last stroke falls on remembrance: of the chapter's forty-six verses the calamities take more than twenty, far more space than the blessing — yet the chapter's final action is not punishment but "then will I remember," and the covenant's last word is not rejection but "I am the Lord their God" (v. 44, Summary). The longest portion is the warning; the weightiest is the remembrance — and whoever preaches this chapter must get that proportion right: the warning preached in full, until hearts tremble; the remembrance preached through, until men dare to turn back.

(New Testament reading) Two New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Leviticus. The first is in 2 Corinthians 6, where Paul, urging the church to be separate, quotes this very chapter: God has said, "I will dwell in them, and walk in them; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people." (2 Cor 6:16, KJV; the words quoted are God's speech as Paul cites it, whose source is Lev 26:11-12.) In the New Testament reading, the promise of walking in the midst has not expired; it has been received by a new people. The second is in Galatians 3, where Paul argues that as many as are of the works of the law are under the curse, for it is written that cursed is every one who does not continue in all things written in the book of the law to do them; and that "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree:" (Gal 3:13, KJV; v. 10 in summary). (Application) In the New Testament reading, the fivefold-escalating curse of Leviticus 26 is no longer merely a historical warning; it becomes the measure of the depth of Christ's bearing: the curse of the covenant is real, and Christ's bearing of it is real. But this sermon keeps the layers straight: Leviticus 26 is first of all itself — the covenant sanctions given to Israel at Sinai: blessing, curse, confession, remembrance. The New Testament reading is the depth the church sees when it looks back at this chapter in Christ; it must not be used to dilute the seriousness of this chapter's own text. A man who has never been examined by this chapter's "if" cannot know how heavy the words "hath redeemed us" in Galatians are.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's own hands. Leviticus 26 does not let us watch Israel's history from outside as spectators; with its chain of "if ye" it draws every hearer in: if ye walk, if ye will not hearken, if they shall confess — the door of the conditional stands open toward every age. Our generation's problem may not be statues of stone or a forgotten Sabbatical year; but the posture of "walking contrary" has never gone out of date: knowing His road, and walking to meet Him head-on; under discipline, hearing the sound of a leaf as the sound of a sword, and running ever further away. For such a person this chapter leaves one road, and it is enough: confess — confess one's own iniquity, confess one's contrary gait, and let the uncircumcised heart be humbled. Then hear the word that comes from the depths of the covenant in verse 42: God remembers His covenant, and He remembers the land. May we be people who learn confession today rather than in desolation — people who walk upright, walking with God, as the blessing section wrote it: the bands of the yoke are already broken, the road is already open, God walks in the midst, and we have only to go upright.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Leviticus 26 sets me between two night scenes. In one, I lie down and none makes me afraid — the sleep of a person among whom God walks. In the other, the sound of a shaken leaf chases me — the terror of one who has walked contrary to Him. My peace, I see now, does not depend on how loud the world is, but on how near He is. This chapter also shows me that when the discipline deepened step by step, the door was never shut: the words of confession stand written after the long list of judgments, as if left there for me. I confess that often I have not lost the way; I have walked contrary, meeting Him head-on. Now the text speaks plainly: if they shall confess, then will He remember. So I begin with confession — my own iniquity, not blamed on my circumstances or on those before me — and I ask Him to humble my uncircumcised heart. Then I dare to lift my head, for the last stroke of the chapter is not my failure but His remembering: He remembers His covenant, and He remembers the land. The bands of the yoke are broken; may I go upright, lie down unafraid, and confess without hiding.

2. Title and Lev 26:3: the KJV reads the verse in full as quoted in section II; the Chinese base text ends the verse at "my commandments" with a comma, the consequence beginning at 26:4. Each title is taken from its own base text's actual wording at 26:3.
3. The count of "seven": the intensification is marked at Lev 26:18, 21, 24, and 28 — four occurrences in all, counted verse by verse. The KJV renders all four with "seven times"; the Chinese base text reads "sevenfold" at vv. 18 and 21 and "seven times" at vv. 24 and 28. The count in the sermon is of the four passages together, not of any single rendering.
4. qerî (H7147) in this chapter: Lev 26:21, 23, 24, 27, 28, 40, 41 — seven occurrences, checked one by one against the Hebrew text (BibleHub interlinear and the BHS text). At vv. 21, 23, 27 the subject is the people; at vv. 24, 28, 41 the subject is God; at v. 40 the word stands inside the people's own confession.
5. hithallēkh (26:12): the Hithpael form of hālak (H1980), "to walk to and fro, to move about." The "walk" of 26:3 and the "walk" of the contrary-walking passages are likewise forms of hālak; "walking" is the chapter's verbal thread, on which sections II and III both rest.
6. šeḇaʿ (Strong's H7651), "seven": the intensifying expressions at Lev 26:18, 21, 24, 28 all use this word. That seven signifies completeness in Scripture is common scholarly ground; the sermon states the counts as fact and develops the point only at the (Application) level.
7. mōṭōṯ ʿōl (26:13): mōṭōṯ, "bars, cross-pieces"; ʿōl (Strong's H5923), "yoke"; KJV, "the bands of your yoke." qôməmiyyûṯ (Strong's H6968) occurs only at 26:13 in the Old Testament, denoting the erect, head-raised posture.
8. hithwaddû (26:40): the Hithpael form of yādāh (Strong's H3034); the root's senses include confess, give thanks, declare. Here it takes the sense "confess (iniquity)," as the KJV renders it.
9. lēḇ ʿārēl (26:41): ʿārēl (Strong's H6189), "uncircumcised"; the "uncircumcised heart" is the heart not yet reached by the covenant's mark, still obstinate. The sermon states the lexical fact and the text's usage and builds nothing further on the word.
10. zāḵar (Strong's H2142), "to remember": in the Hebrew, three times at 26:42 (wəzāḵartî / ʾezkōr / ʾezkōr), once at 26:45 — four occurrences in vv. 40-45, counted verse by verse against the BHS interlinear. (The Chinese base text reads twice at 26:42, three in all; an earlier form of this note followed that surface count — corrected here.) Exod 2:24, where God hears the groaning and remembers His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, is the parallel usage, cited in summary.
11. The land's Sabbaths (26:34-35, 43): the verb at 26:34 (tirṣeh) and the related form at 26:43 both come from rāṣāh (Strong's H7521), whose basic senses are "to accept, to make payment"; the versions render the land's "enjoying" her Sabbaths. This sermon states the root fact only: commentators often connect the land's "enjoyment" with this root's payment sense, and no theological conclusion is rested on the root alone. šāmēm (Strong's H8074) is the root of "desolate/desolation," in forms at 26:31, 32, 34, 35.
12. The fivefold division (vv. 14-17, 18-20, 21-22, 23-26, 27-39) is a reading common in the commentary tradition; Milgrom and Wenham both take the repeated conditional openings as the section markers. Commentators differ slightly at the seams; the sermon notes this in the text.
13. The relation of 2 Cor 6:16 to Lev 26:11-12, and of Gal 3:10-13 to the curse of the covenant, are intertextual relations checkable in the New Testament text itself; this sermon places them at the (New Testament reading) level and does not read them back as the original sense of Leviticus.

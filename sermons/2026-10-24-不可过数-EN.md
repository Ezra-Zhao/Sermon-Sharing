Deuteronomy 25 is a miscellany in the later part of the legal detail (chs. 12–26): the whip at the judgment seat, the ox on the threshing floor, the shoe before the gate, the hand in a brawl, the weights in the bag, the remembrance on the road — nineteen verses, six scenes. Beneath them run three threads: "number" — the beating is by number, forty is the limit, the weights must be exact, the days are lengthened; "name" — the dead brother's name is to survive, the refuser's name is the house of him that hath his shoe loosed, the remembrance of Amalek is to be blotted out; "brother" — the man beaten is a brother, the dead man is a brother, and in the original the two parties to the strife are also a man and his brother. The divine name occurs only four times in the chapter (vv. 15, 16, 19), all in the weights group and the remembrance group. Verse 19 closes the chapter with "thou shalt not forget it": the chapter's last command governs not the hand but the memory.

## Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Fact Layer] The chapter opens before the judgment seat: "If there be a controversy between men, and they come unto judgment, that the judges may judge them; then they shall justify the righteous, and condemn the wicked." (25:1) Then the camera moves steadily on: the wicked man lies down before the judge's face to be beaten, and only forty stripes may be given; the ox treading out the corn is not to be muzzled; a brother dies without a child, and his wife shall not marry without unto a stranger; the man who will not take her has his shoe loosed at the gate; two men strive together, and the wife who takes hold of the secrets has her hand cut off; divers weights shall not be in the bag; and at the end the view returns to the road out of Egypt: what Amalek did is to be remembered — thou shalt not forget it. Six groups of regulations in the whole chapter; the prohibitions — counted in the Chinese base text by the prohibitive word — number seven (Note 2), and the divine name occurs four times, all in the last two groups.

[Theological Development Layer] One general inference is set down first (this is an inference): the common object of the chapter's six groups of regulations is "the stronger hand" — the hand that holds the whip, the hand that leads the ox, the hand that holds the standing of the elder brother's name, the hand that holds the scale, the hand that holds the sword after rest has been given. What the law does repeatedly is set a number upon that hand: the beating has a number, the scale has an exact standard, the name has survival or blotting out, memory has a command. The grounds of the boundary differ from group to group — the brother's dignity (v. 3), the dead man's name (v. 6), God's abomination (v. 16), the old event on the road (v. 17) — but the act of drawing the line is one and the same.

[Summary Layer] Deuteronomy 25: six groups of regulations, nineteen verses; number as the boundary, name as the line, brother as the ground; and the last sentence lands on the memory: thou shalt not forget it.

## 1. The Beating by Number, and the Ox Treading Out the Corn (Deut 25:1–4; 05:00–16:00)

[Fact Layer] These four verses form two Masoretic units: vv. 1–3 one unit (samekh at the end of v. 3), v. 4 its own unit (samekh at the end of v. 4). First the procedure of judgment (vv. 1–2): "If there be a controversy between men, and they come unto judgment, that the judges may judge them; then they shall justify the righteous, and condemn the wicked." — A controversy (rîḇ, H7379) comes before the judges (šāpaṭ, H8199), and the verdict runs in two directions: justify the righteous (hiṣdîq, H6663) and condemn the wicked (hiršîaʿ, H7561). If the wicked man (rāšāʿ, H7563) is worthy to be beaten, the procedure has three steps: "And it shall be, if the wicked man be worthy to be beaten, that the judge shall cause him to lie down, and to be beaten before his face, according to his fault, by a certain number." (25:2) — Cause him to lie down (the hiphil form of nāpal, H5307), to be beaten (nāḵâ, H5221) according to his fault (rišʿâ, H7564), by a certain number (mispār, H4557). Then the ceiling (v. 3): "Forty stripes he may give him, and not exceed: lest, if he should exceed, and beat him above these with many stripes, then thy brother should seem vile unto thee." — Forty (ʾarbāʿîm, H705) is the expressly written number-limit; "and not exceed" in the original is lōʾ yōsîp (yāsap, H3254), literally "he shall not add"; the consequence of exceeding is not that the procedure is void, but that "thy brother should seem vile unto thee" — the designation of the man punished changes from "the wicked" in vv. 1–2 to "thy brother" in v. 3 (ʾāḥ, H251); in the original, "thy brother is made vile before thine eyes" (qālâ, H7034, niphal form). Verse 4 turns to the threshing floor: "Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn." — The ox (šôr, H7794) treading out (dûš, H1758) the corn is not to be muzzled (ḥāsam, H2629); the verse carries no reason clause, and in the Chinese base text it opens and closes its own quotation.

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the punishment in this section has three boundaries, each deeper than the last (this is an inference): a procedural boundary — judgment first, then beating; no beating without judgment; a numerical boundary — the beating is by a certain number, and the total is capped at forty; a dignity boundary — when the forty are full, the man punished is still "thy brother," and the sin of exceeding lies not in beating wrongly but in making a brother seem vile. Second, the change of designation in v. 3 is the key to the chapter (this is an inference): in the verdict he is "the wicked"; in the execution he is "thy brother" — the verdict falls on the deed, brotherhood is the standing; standing is not cancelled by a verdict, and so the execution has a ceiling. Third, that v. 4 follows immediately upon the beating is no idle stroke (this is an inference): a boundary has just been set for the punished man, and a boundary is at once set for the labouring animal — the labourer has a share in the strength it puts forth; this is the hidden thread between the two small units; the correspondence with the hired servant's wages in Deut 24:14–15 is registered only and not established here.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, a register of the ancient Near Eastern background of flogging: corporal punishment as a judicial penalty genuinely existed in ancient Near Eastern legal life (its existence is registered only; no clauses are quoted); what distinguishes this chapter is not that it has flogging, but that its flogging has procedure, number, ceiling, and a reason clause — the comparison is registered only. Second, a register of the discussion of the number forty: one reading takes forty as a round-number ceiling, another connects it with the uses of forty elsewhere in this book (verse references registered only, the passages not used to gloss each other); the counting caution of "rather too few than too many" in the tradition of execution is registered as a phenomenon only, for comparison with 2 Cor 11:24 in the reading layer. Third, a register of the comparison between the verdict of v. 1 and Prov 17:15: "He that justifieth the wicked, and he that condemneth the just" are set side by side in the wisdom literature as an abomination to the LORD (Prov 17:15; the excerpted sentence was verified directly, see Note 6) — the two-directional verdict of v. 1 of this chapter appears there in reverse form; set side by side only.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Three registrations, strictly under Discipline 1: the echo between 25:1–3 and 2 Cor 11:24 is registered in this layer only and is not read backwards. First, a direct-verification register of 2 Cor 11:24: "Of the Jews five times received I forty stripes save one." (2 Cor 11:24, KJV verbatim) — Paul states that he received stripes from the Jews five times, each time "forty save one"; the correspondence with this chapter's "Forty stripes he may give him, and not exceed" is registered only: the number (thirty-nine) falls one short of the ceiling (forty), and the traditional explanation — that those who administered the beating subtracted one of their own accord for fear of exceeding — is registered as a reading only and is not used to revise this chapter's text; 11:25, "thrice was I beaten with rods" (registered by verse reference only, no quotation), is a different instrument, registered by verse reference only. Second, a register of the New Testament echo of v. 4: 1 Cor 9:9, "For it is written in the law of Moses, Thou shalt not muzzle the mouth of the ox that treadeth out the corn. Doth God take care for oxen?" (KJV verbatim) and 1 Cor 9:10, "Or saith he it altogether for our sakes? For our sakes, no doubt, this is written: that he that ploweth should plow in hope; and that he that thresheth in hope should be partaker of his hope." (KJV verbatim) — Paul directs this verse toward the support of those who preach; the fact and direction of the quotation are registered only, and the New Testament use is not read back into the verse's original place. [Summary Layer] Section 1: a controversy passes judgment first — the righteous are justified, the wicked condemned; the beating is before the judge's face, by a certain number, with forty as the limit, and to exceed is to make a brother seem vile; the ox on the threshing floor is not to be muzzled.

## 2. Keeping a Name for the Dead Brother, and the House of Him That Hath His Shoe Loosed (Deut 25:5–10; 16:00–28:00)

[Fact Layer] These six verses form one whole Masoretic unit (samekh closing at the end of v. 10). First the positive provision of the regulation (vv. 5–6): the condition is "If brethren dwell together, and one of them die, and have no child," (25:5, excerpt); the duty stands in the same verse: "the wife of the dead shall not marry without unto a stranger: her husband's brother shall go in unto her, and take her to him to wife, and perform the duty of an husband's brother unto her." — Her husband's brother (yāḇām, H2993) is to perform the duty of an husband's brother (yāḇam, H2992, verb form). The effect stands in v. 6: "And it shall be, that the firstborn which she beareth shall succeed in the name of his brother which is dead, that his name be not put out of Israel." — The firstborn (bəkôr, H1060) shall succeed in the name of his brother which is dead (qûm ʿal-šēm, literally "shall stand upon the name"; name, šēm, H8034), and the purpose clause is that his name be not put out (māḥâ, H4229). Then the procedure for refusal (vv. 7–10), in four steps. First, the woman makes her complaint: "And if the man like not to take his brother's wife, then let his brother's wife go up to the gate unto the elders, and say, My husband's brother refuseth to raise up unto his brother a name in Israel, he will not perform the duty of my husband's brother." (25:7) — "Like not" (ḥāpaṣ, H2654), "refuseth" (mēʾēn, H3985), and "he will not" (ʾāḇâ, H14) are layered together; the place is the gate (šāʿar, H8179), before the elders (zāqēn, H2205), and the complainant is the woman herself. Second, the elders call and question him: "Then the elders of his city shall call him, and speak unto him: and if he stand to it, and say, I like not to take her;" (25:8) — "Stand to it" in the original is wəʿāmad (ʿāmad, H5975), literally "and he stands." Third, the woman performs the rite: "Then shall his brother's wife come unto him in the presence of the elders, and loose his shoe from off his foot, and spit in his face, and shall answer and say, So shall it be done unto that man that will not build up his brother's house." (25:9) — Loose his shoe (ḥālaṣ, H2502; shoe, naʿal, H5275), spit (yāraq, H3417) in his face (pānîm, H6440), and one sentence of declaration, the charge fixed as "will not build up his brother's house" (bānâ, H1129). Fourth, the naming close: "And his name shall be called in Israel, The house of him that hath his shoe loosed." (25:10) — The consequence of refusal is not a penalty but a name: the house of him that hath his shoe loosed. What must be kept distinct in the whole section: the text leaves refusal a public way out — once the procedure is completed, the text says nothing about what becomes of the woman thereafter or whether the man receives any corporal punishment.

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the institution's purpose in this section is "keeping the name," not "continuing the seed" as such (this is an inference): v. 5 commands the taking, but v. 6 speaks of the name — the firstborn succeeds in the name of his brother which is dead, that his name be not put out. In this chapter's world, a man's name is bound up with his inheritance and his place in Israel; the duty of a brother is to guard that place for the dead. Second, the executor of this section's procedure is the woman (this is an inference): she makes the complaint, she looses the shoe, she speaks the declaration — the law places both the words and the actions of this public procedure in the widow's hands; the elders only call the man and question him. She is not the object of this section; she is the subject of its procedure. Third, the price of refusal is a name, not a penalty (this is an inference): this section stands in exact contrast with Section 1 — the wicked man's beating has a ceiling, and the refuser receives only a name of shame; here the law distinguishes "sin" from "will not": failure to perform a brother's duty is publicly named, but it is not corporally punished.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Four items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, a terminological register: this institution is commonly called levirate in scholarship (from the Latin levir, a husband's brother), denoting the arrangement by which a dead brother's brother takes the widow to raise up seed and a name for the dead; the Chinese designation used in the source is a descriptive rendering of the practice — the origin of the term is registered only. Second, two readings of the scope of "brethren dwell together" are set side by side: (a) brothers who live in one place and share one livelihood — the duty is conditioned on dwelling together; (b) brothers in general — "dwell together" is the formula that sets the scene. The two readings stand side by side; the sermon text states the base text's literal wording only. Third, a register of the nature of the shoe-loosing rite: in this section the loosing of the shoe is a ritual act of public shame — performed by the woman, with the elders present, and accompanied by a declaration; the loosing of a shoe has other uses in ancient Israelite legal life, registered as a phenomenon only, for the comparison in the reading layer. ## 3. The Hand in a Strife (Deut 25:11–12; 28:00–36:00)

[Fact Layer] These two verses form one whole Masoretic unit (samekh closing at the end of v. 12); they are the shortest group in the chapter, and they carry the chapter's heaviest penalty. The case stands in v. 11: "When men strive together one with another, and the wife of the one draweth near for to deliver her husband out of the hand of him that smiteth him, and putteth forth her hand, and taketh him by the secrets:" — Men strive together (nāṣâ, H5327, niphal form; in the original, "a man and his brother," base-text entry 7); the wife of the one draws near, and her motive is expressly recorded: to deliver her husband out of the hand of him that smiteth him; her act is to take hold (ḥāzaq, H2388, hiphil form) of the man's secrets (məḇušîm, H4016). The verdict stands in v. 12: "Then thou shalt cut off her hand, thine eye shall not pity her." — Cut off her hand (kap, H3709, literally the palm); the closing sentence is "thine eye shall not pity her" (ḥûs, H2347) — this book's formula sentence, occurring in the same form at Deut 7:16; 13:8; 19:13; 19:21 (verse references registered only). What must be kept distinct in this section: the text records no sin of intent for the woman; the charge falls on the act alone; the two men striving receive no penalty, and the penalty falls only on the intervening hand; the occasion and witnesses of the execution are not written in the provision — stated only, nothing supplied.

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, this section's penalty stands in exact counterpart to the "name" of Section 2 (this is an inference): what the taking hold of the secrets endangers is the other man's capacity to raise up seed and a name — the name and posterity that Section 2 spends its whole length preserving are, in this section, gripped by one hand; the law's answer to that hand is the chapter's only penalty of severing a limb. Second, rescuing a husband is not the charge; the method is (this is an inference): the text first writes her motive in full — "for to deliver her husband" — and only then passes sentence on her hand; here the law distinguishes heart from hand: the heart may be commendable, the hand has crossed the boundary. The boundary lies not in intervening as such, but in the part taken and the harm made possible. Third, that "thine eye shall not pity her" closes the heaviest penalty shows precisely the consistency of this book's formula (this is an inference): the same formula is used in this book for the false witness and for the shedding of innocent blood (Deut 19; verse references registered only), and in this section it is used for a woman rescuing her husband — the formula's severity is not discounted by the pitiableness of its object; but the formula falls within the chapter's shortest section, and that too moves the reader's attention from the display of the penalty to the immovability of the boundary.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Four items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, a uniqueness register: the penalty of cutting off a hand occurs only here in this book's law (stated for the scope of this book only); bodily penalties have their own examples in ancient Near Eastern codes (their existence is registered only; no clauses are quoted and no sentencing comparison is made). Second, two readings of the letter and substitution are set side by side: (a) the literal-execution reading — the provision, read literally, is a penalty of cutting off the hand; (b) the substitution reading — in later Jewish legal tradition a fine was in most cases substituted for this penalty (the traditional phenomenon registered only). The two readings stand side by side; the sermon text states the base text's literal wording only and establishes nothing further. Third, a side-by-side register with the brawling provision of Exod 21:22–25: there two men strive and hurt a woman with child, and the distribution of penalty and fine has another structure (verse references and theme registered only); the "strife — woman — penalty" triangle of the two cases is set side by side only, neither used to gloss the other. Fourth, a register of the comparison with the "hand for hand" formula: Deut 19:21, "And thine eye shall not pity; but life shall go for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot." (KJV verbatim) — there "hand for hand" is one item in the enumeration of the principle of recompense, while the cutting off of the hand in this section is a concrete penalty; both places use "thine eye shall not pity"; set side by side only.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Two registrations. First, a register of the echo of the "cut off the hand" vocabulary: Matt 5:29–30 and 18:8 speak of "if thy right hand offend thee, cut it off, and cast it from thee" (the quoted sentence of Matt 5:30 was corrected by direct verification in the leg-supply round: the earlier draft's wording had admitted a form from another Chinese edition and dropped the closing words; Matt 5:29 and 18:8 are registered by verse reference and theme only) — the hand-cutting vocabulary of the Sermon on the Mount and the penalty of severing a hand in this section are set side by side in the reading layer: there the language is figurative self-judgment, here it is a judicial provision; the vocabulary echo is registered only and no equivalence is established. Second, this section has no direct citation in the New Testament (absence registered only): neither the Gospels nor the Epistles quote 25:11–12; registered only, with no inference drawn.

[Summary Layer] Section 3: two men strive together, the wife comes to deliver her husband and puts forth her hand to take hold of the man's secrets — then thou shalt cut off her hand; thine eye shall not pity her. The shortest section in the chapter, and its heaviest penalty.

## 4. Divers Weights (Deut 25:13–16; 36:00–47:00)

[Fact Layer] These four verses form two Masoretic units: v. 13 its own unit (samekh at the end of v. 13), vv. 14–16 one unit (petuhah closing at the end of v. 16). The syntax is two prohibitions, one positive provision, one verdict. The first prohibition: "Thou shalt not have in thy bag divers weights, a great and a small." (25:13) — In the bag (kîs, H3599) there shall not be divers weights (ʾeḇen, H68, literally "stone"), a great and a small. The second prohibition: "Thou shalt not have in thine house divers measures, a great and a small." (25:14) — In the house there shall not be divers measures (ʾêpâ, H374), a great and a small. The object of both prohibitions is "have," not "use": the provision does not wait for a transaction to occur; it bans the instruments themselves first. The positive provision stands in v. 15: "But thou shalt have a perfect and just weight, a perfect and just measure shalt thou have: that thy days may be lengthened in the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee." — Perfect (šālēm, H8003, literally "complete") and just (ṣeḏeq, H6664) are paired twice over (once for the weight, once for the measure); the effect is "that thy days may be lengthened" (ʾārak, H748) — in the same form as the promise of the fifth commandment in Deut 5:16 (Note 6). The verdict stands in v. 16: "For all that do such things, and all that do unrighteously, are an abomination unto the LORD thy God." — Unrighteously (ʿāwel, H5766) and abomination (tôʿēḇâ, H8441) meet in this verse; the divine name occurs twice in this section, both times as "the LORD thy God." The person throughout the section is singular: the bag is thine, the house is thine, the days are thine.

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, this section fixes the moment of conviction before the transaction (this is an inference): divers weights lie in the bag and not one load of goods has yet been weighed, and the provision already says "thou shalt not have" — the instruments prepared for fraud are the beginning of fraud; the scale has not moved, and the heart is judged first. Second, that the promise of "days lengthened" falls upon the scale is this section's weightiest wording (this is an inference): in this book that form of words is given first to honouring father and mother (Deut 5:16; verse reference registered only), and now it is given to the man who keeps a perfect and just weight — the law places honesty in the market in the same account as the promise attached to a commandment. Third, this section joins head-to-tail with Section 1 (this is an inference): the judge of Section 1 renders verdicts impartially, and the merchant of this section prepares two kinds in his bag — righteousness in this chapter has two faces, one at the judgment seat and one behind the counter; and the "abomination" of v. 16 answers the "justify the righteous, and condemn the wicked" of v. 1: God's determination of unrighteousness precedes any man's scale.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items registered side by side, with no ruling. First, Lev 19:35–36 set side by side: "Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in meteyard, in weight, or in measure." (Lev 19:35, KJV verbatim) "Just balances, just weights, a just ephah, and a just hin, shall ye have: I am the LORD your God, which brought you out of the land of Egypt." (Lev 19:36, KJV verbatim) — there judgment and weights and measures are set in parallel under a single "ye shall do no unrighteousness," and the backing is the Exodus; the backing here is days lengthened and God's abomination. The two places are set side by side only. Second, the wisdom literature's echoes set side by side: Prov 11:1, "A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight." (KJV verbatim); Prov 20:10, "Divers weights, and divers measures, both of them are alike abomination to the LORD." (KJV verbatim); Prov 16:11, "A just weight and balance are the LORD'S: all the weights of the bag are his work." (KJV verbatim) — all three correspond closely to this section's wording (divers, weights, the bag, abomination); registered only, with no discussion of source or derivation. Third, the prophetic echoes set side by side: Mic 6:11, "Shall I count them pure with the wicked balances, and with the bag of deceitful weights?" (KJV verbatim); Amos 8:5 speaks of "making the ephah small, and the shekel great, and falsifying the balances by deceit" (Amos 8:5, KJV verbatim excerpt) — the prophets read this section's list of instruments as the indictment of a contemporary market.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Two registrations. First, a register of the echo in 1 Thess 4:6: "That no man go beyond and defraud his brother in any matter: because that the Lord is the avenger of all such, as we also have forewarned you and testified." (KJV verbatim) — "defraud his brother" and "the Lord is the avenger" are set side by side in the reading layer with this section's "all that do unrighteously … are an abomination"; registered only, not read backwards. Second, a register of 1 Cor 6:9–11, "the unrighteous shall not inherit the kingdom of God" (verse references and theme registered only): the echo of "unrighteous" in the New Testament ethical lists; registered only, and not read back into the context of ʿāwel in v. 16 of this chapter.

[Summary Layer] Section 4: there shall not be divers weights in the bag, nor divers measures in the house; a perfect and just weight and a perfect and just measure shall be kept, and the days may be lengthened — for all that do unrighteously are an abomination unto the LORD.

## 5. Remember Amalek (Deut 25:17–19; 47:00–57:00)

[Fact Layer] These three verses form one whole Masoretic unit, and the chapter closes with petuhah (at the end of v. 19). The syntax has three layers: a command to remember, the old event, and a command. The command to remember stands in v. 17: "Remember what Amalek did unto thee by the way, when ye were come forth out of Egypt;" — Remember (zākar, H2142) opens with the infinitive absolute, the intensified form of a command. The old event stands in v. 18: "How he met thee by the way, and smote the hindmost of thee, even all that were feeble behind thee, when thou wast faint and weary; and he feared not God." — He met thee (qārâ, number pending verification, Note 8) by the way; the moment was "when thou wast faint and weary" (faint, ʿāyēp, H5889; weary, yāgēaʿ, H3023); the method was to smite the hindmost, even all that were feeble behind (zānaḇ, H2179, literally "to strike the tail"; "all that were feeble," number pending verification, Note 8); the determining sentence is "and he feared not God" (yārēʾ, H3372). The command stands in v. 19: "Therefore it shall be, when the LORD thy God hath given thee rest from all thine enemies round about, in the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee for an inheritance to possess it, that thou shalt blot out the remembrance of Amalek from under heaven; thou shalt not forget it." — The condition comes first: after the LORD has given rest from all enemies (ʾôyēḇ, H341) round about and the land given for an inheritance (nāḥălâ, H5159) is possessed (rest, the hiphil form of nûaḥ, H5117); the command is to blot out (māḥâ, the same word as in v. 6) the remembrance (zēker, H2143) of Amalek from under heaven; the close is "thou shalt not forget it" (šākaḥ, H7911) — forming a ring, head to tail, with the command to remember in v. 17. The divine name occurs twice in this section, both times "the LORD thy God" (v. 19).

[Theological Development Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, this chapter has two kinds of remembrance, in opposite directions (this is an inference): the remembrance of Deut 24 is remembrance of grace — remember that thou wast a bondman in Egypt (Deut 24:18, 22; verse references registered only); the remembrance of this section is remembrance of evil — remember what Amalek did by the way. Both remembrances are commands: remembering grace keeps a man from oppressing the weak; remembering evil keeps him from forgetting that there is evil which falls precisely upon the weak. Second, the execution is expressly postponed (this is an inference): the moment for blotting out the remembrance is not on the road and not in the wilderness, but after "rest" has been given — remembrance is the present duty, action has its own hour; the law schedules memory and action separately: memory may not lapse, action may not jump the start. Third, the word "blot out / put out" occurs twice in this chapter, in opposite directions (this is an inference): v. 6 guards a dead brother's name from being put out; v. 19 blots out the remembrance of Amalek — the same verb, in one place a rescue and in the other a judgment; whose name is kept and whose name is blotted out is one of the chapter's final verdicts.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items registered side by side, strictly under Discipline 3: the correspondence with Exod 17:8–16 and 1 Sam 15 is set side by side only, with no ruling. First, Exod 17:8–16 set side by side: Exod 17:8, "Then came Amalek, and fought with Israel in Rephidim." (KJV verbatim) — the narrative records only the frontal battle, and the detail of v. 18 of this section, the smiting of the hindmost and the feeble, is not expressly recorded in that narrative; the fullness and brevity of the two places are set side by side only. Exod 17:14, "And the LORD said unto Moses, Write this for a memorial in a book, and rehearse it in the ears of Joshua: for I will utterly put out the remembrance of Amalek from under heaven." (KJV verbatim) — there the putting out is God's declaration and the remembrance rests on the writing of a book; here the blotting out has become the people's command; the two places are set side by side only. Exod 17:16, "For he said, Because the LORD hath sworn that the LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation." (KJV verbatim) — the "from generation to generation" framework and this section's "when … hath given thee rest" moment are set side by side only. Second, 1 Sam 15 set side by side: 1 Sam 15:2, "Thus saith the LORD of hosts, I remember that which Amalek did to Israel, how he laid wait for him in the way, when he came up from Egypt." (KJV verbatim) — "by the way … what Amalek did" corresponds in wording to vv. 17–18 of this section, and "I remember" answers "thou shalt not forget it": in one place God's remembrance, in the other a command of remembrance laid on man; set side by side only. The command of 1 Sam 15:3 ("Now go and smite Amalek, and utterly destroy all that they have," KJV verbatim excerpt) and Saul's incomplete execution in 15:8–9 and the "to obey is better than sacrifice" of 15:22 (registered only) — the narrative's success, failure, and verdict are registered under that narrative's own name only and are not used to adjudicate the nature or the moment of this section's provision. Third, a register of Num 24:20: "And when he looked on Amalek, he took up his parable, and said, Amalek was the first of the nations; but his latter end shall be that he perish for ever." (KJV verbatim) — a prophetic oracle in poetic form and this section's command to remember in legal form; set side by side only.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Two registrations. First, this section has no direct citation in the New Testament (absence registered only): the New Testament does not quote 25:17–19 and names Amalek no more; registered only, with no inference drawn. Second, a register of a vocabulary echo: Rom 3:18, "There is no fear of God before their eyes." (KJV verbatim excerpt) and v. 18's "and he feared not God" — the echo in the New Testament of the determining sentence "feared not God"; registered only, and no direct relation to this section is established.

[Summary Layer] Section 5: remember what Amalek did when ye were come forth out of Egypt — by the way, when thou wast faint and weary, he smote the hindmost, even all that were feeble behind thee, and he feared not God; when the LORD hath given thee rest, thou shalt blot out the remembrance of Amalek from under heaven — thou shalt not forget it.

## Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Fact Layer] A recount of the whole chapter: the beating by number and the ox treading out the corn, four verses (vv. 1–4); keeping a name for the dead brother and the house of him that hath his shoe loosed, six verses (vv. 5–10); the hand in a strife, two verses (vv. 11–12); divers weights, four verses (vv. 13–16); remember Amalek, three verses (vv. 17–19). The prohibitions, seven in the Chinese base-text count, are distributed across the five groups, and the divine name occurs four times, all in the last two groups; "blot out / put out" occurs twice — once keeping a name, once blotting out a remembrance — in exact opposition.

[Theological Development Layer] One closing general inference (this is an inference): this chapter's righteousness is righteousness with a number. The whip has a number — forty, and no more; the name has a number — survival and blotting out are both entered in the account; the hand has a boundary — cross it, and it is cut off; the scale has an exact standard — two kinds are unrighteousness; memory has a command — thou shalt not forget it. And beneath every number stands a person: the brother beaten, the brother dead, the buyer defrauded, the feeble at the rear. The number is not cold; the number is the law's way of protecting persons.

[Summary Layer] Conclusion: Deuteronomy 25 — the beating is by number and shall not exceed; the ox treading out the corn is not muzzled; the dead brother's name is to be kept, and the refuser is recorded as the house of him that hath his shoe loosed; the hand that rescues a husband shall not cross its boundary, and the weights in the bag shall not be divers; what Amalek did is to be remembered — thou shalt not forget it.

## Devotional Extract

**Devotional Extract (KJV)**

The number that stops you in Deuteronomy 25 is forty. A guilty man deserves to be beaten, yet the law first sets a number on the whip: forty stripes, and not one more. Why? Because, midway through the sentence, the text changes his name. He is no longer only "the wicked"; he is "thy brother." The verdict falls on his deed, but brotherhood is his standing — and standing is not cancelled by a verdict, so the beating has a ceiling. Past forty, the sin is no longer his; it is yours, for degrading your brother. This chapter draws a line beside every stronger hand: the ox treading the grain must not be muzzled, for the laborer has a share in what it threshes; a dead brother's name must be carried on, and the man who refuses bears a name of shame, not a flogging; the weights in the bag must not be two kinds, for God sees the heart behind the scale before the market does. And at the end, the line is drawn around memory itself: Amalek struck the weary stragglers at the rear, and that must be remembered, not forgotten. We may hold no whip and keep no shop, yet every day we stand where the stronger hand is — one word too many for the one who failed, one moment too little for the one who lags, one inch too far toward the easy gain. Remember the word behind the forty: the person facing you is a brother. "Forty stripes he may give him, and not exceed: lest, if he should exceed, and beat him above these with many stripes, then thy brother should seem vile unto thee." (Deut. 25:3, KJV)

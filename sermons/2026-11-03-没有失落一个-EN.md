> Reading cautions — five things to fix firmly when teaching this chapter. First, the tears are real and the restoration is real, and the order cannot be reversed: the text lets David and the six hundred weep until they have no strength left before it speaks of inquiry and pursuit; the preacher must not skip the tears to reach the victory, nor use this chapter to rebuke those who are grieving for crying too long or believing too little. Second, David's strength here is not a stronger nature: his two wives were taken too; he is a victim like the rest. His only difference is the next step — he inquired. To preach David as a hero who did not cry is to alter the text; the text describes a man who cried until he had no strength, turned to God, and was strengthened again. Third, the people's talk of stoning David came from bitterness, not from righteous judgment; do not preach the six hundred as ungrateful villains — they had lost their families, and the words of grief are a human constant; the lesson lies not in judging them but in how David did not repay resentment with resentment. Fourth, the two hundred stayed at the Besor because they were exhausted, not cowardly: the text says plainly they were too exhausted to cross, and David himself later legislated equal shares for them; "staying behind" must not be preached as second-class, nor "going to battle" as the only honored place; those who watch the baggage in church and family have a formal standing in this chapter. Fifth, the victory here must not be preached as a formula for suffering: not everything lost in this life will be recovered in kind — this chapter records a promise God gave David at a specific moment ("you shall surely overtake and surely rescue"), received after inquiry; it is not a guarantee human beings may issue in God's name to every sufferer. Preach the source of the promise clearly, and leave those who have not seen restoration in kind to God's mercy; do not press this chapter upon them.

## Abstract

First Samuel 30 is the darkest day of David's life and the most important lesson before he becomes king. Sent back from Aphek by the Philistine lords (1 Samuel 29), he returns on the third day to Ziklag to find the city burned by the Amalekites, wives, children and everyone carried off — no one killed, yet a whole household taken root and branch. David and the six hundred weep until they have no strength; before the weeping ends, the people in their bitterness speak of stoning David. David does not defend himself, suppress them, or flee; he strengthens himself in the LORD his God, and the first thing he does is inquire: if I pursue, shall I overtake? God says: pursue, for you shall surely overtake and surely rescue. On the way, two hundred exhausted men stay at the Besor and four hundred go on; an Egyptian youth, abandoned in the field by his Amalekite master, eats bread and water, revives, and becomes the guide. David fights from dawn until the evening of the next day and recovers everything: the two wives, everyone's children, flocks and herds — not one was missing. On the return he establishes a statute for those who stayed: those who go to battle and those who watch the baggage share alike, "to this day"; and he sends gifts from the spoil to the elders of Judah. This sermon makes three points: true strength is not never crying, but still being willing to inquire after crying until you have no strength; God's restoration often comes led by a person others discarded; and the mark of a victor is not only recovering what was lost, but being willing to share what was gained with those who did not go to battle.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, some among us today read this chapter and recognize Ziklag: the place we came back to is no longer what it was, the people who should be there are gone, we have cried until we have no strength, and we have heard more than a few words of blame. Do not let us see only David's victory in this chapter; first let us sit a while in the tears and recognize that You are there too. Then teach us David's next step: not to rely on our own strength, nor to brace ourselves with an empty slogan, but simply to trust in You, to be strengthened in our hearts again, and to be willing to open our mouths and inquire of You. And remind those who today hold "spoil" in their hands: grace received is to be shared; those who watch the baggage, those who stayed behind exhausted, those who were discarded — all have a standing in Your account. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: From the Steps at Aphek to the Fire at Ziklag

Read chapter 29 together with this chapter. In chapter 29 God uses the suspicion of the Philistine lords to close a door and send David safely out of a wrong battlefield; at the opening of this chapter David walks back down those steps, and on the third day reaches Ziklag — what greets him is not comfort but a burned city. Set side by side, the two chapters break an illusion first: turning back from a wrong road does not mean the way back has no tears. That God kept David from shedding his own people's blood does not mean God promised him that all would be as before when he got home. The steps are grace, and the fire is also real; what David must learn, and we with him, is where a person stands when, on a return journey given by grace, he meets disaster.

The chapter has three movements. First, the burning and the great weeping (vv. 1–6): the Amalekites take the city in its emptiness, burn it and carry off the people; David and his men weep until they have no strength; in bitterness the people speak of stoning him; but David strengthens himself in the LORD. Second, inquiry and pursuit (vv. 7–15): David has Abiathar bring the ephod and inquires, receiving the promise; the six hundred pursue to the Besor, two hundred stay exhausted, four hundred go on; in the field they find a discarded Egyptian youth, feed him, question him, and are led by him down to the enemy camp. Third, recovery and sharing (vv. 16–31): David strikes the Amalekites and recovers everything — not one was missing; on the return he wins equal shares for the two hundred who stayed and makes it a statute; and he sends gifts from the spoil to the elders of Judah and to people in many places.

Three inner threads run through the chapter. The thread of weeping: the first sound in the chapter is loud weeping, weeping until there is no strength; yet weeping is neither the end nor a sin — it is this company's most honest response to those they love; God does not rebuke this weeping, and the restoration rises precisely from among people who have wept. The thread of asking: in chapter 27 David decided on his own to go down to Philistine territory, in chapter 29 he was sent back by others; in this chapter he learns the most costly lesson of the whole fugitive record — inquire before you act, and ask specifically: shall I overtake, or not? God's answer is equally specific: you shall surely overtake and surely rescue. The thread of sharing: more than half of the last movement is not about fighting but about dividing — dividing with those who stayed, dividing with the elders of Judah; how a battle is wrapped up shows who the fighters really are, and David's kingliness in this chapter lies not in his sword but in his sharing.

Before reading, set three boundaries. First, do not pass lightly over the Amalekites' evil: striking while the men are away, burning the city and carrying off women and children is Amalek's habitual way throughout the Old Testament (cf. Deut 25:17–18, where they attacked the exhausted stragglers on the road — condensed rendering); the evil in this chapter has a concrete shape, and the preacher should name it, lest hearers reduce disaster to an abstract "low point." Second, do not preach the two hundred and the four hundred as grades of faith: staying behind from exhaustion is a bodily fact, and David himself later legislated for them, which is an official stamp — staying is not shame; if today's church honors only those at the front, this chapter is the first to object. Third, do not miss the Egyptian youth: a slave, a sick man, a person discarded by his master, worthless in everyone's eyes — yet God's restoration is led by him; God did not hand this chapter's map to any warrior, but to a man who had just eaten a cake of figs and barely revived.

## Part One: Returning to a Burned City (1 Samuel 30:1–6)

### 1. On the third day, the city had already been burned (30:1–3)

On the third day David and his men came to Ziklag; the Amalekites had raided the Negev, struck Ziklag, burned it with fire, and carried off the women and all who were in it, small and great, killing no one, but carrying them away (condensed rendering of 30:1–2).

Ziklag, Hebrew צִקְלַג (Ṣiqlaḡ, H6860), a place name; Achish had given this city to David as a dwelling (cf. 1 Sam 27:6, condensed rendering). See Brown, Driver and Briggs, A Hebrew and English Lexicon of the Old Testament (hereafter BDB), s.v. צקלג.[1] "Burned," Hebrew שָׂרַף (śārap̄, H8313), to burn. See BDB, s.v. שרף.[2] "Carried off," Hebrew שָׁבָה (šāḇâ, H7617), to take captive. See BDB, s.v. שבה.[3] The text has one sentence calm enough to hurt: yet they killed no one. No news of death should be the best news, but captivity wears a person down more than a death report — the people are alive, yet gone; the home is still on the map, and the city is only ash. Notice also the timing the Amalekites chose: David's whole company had gone north to Aphek with the Philistine army, leaving the rear empty, and the raiders came from the Negev — this is the other half of the bill for David's sojourn in an enemy land: in chapter 27 he calculated that Saul would no longer seek him, but he did not calculate that Ziklag would pay for his absence. The preacher should say one thing plainly for the hearers here: some disasters come not because of an evil done on the spot, but because a long-standing displacement has come to its day of reckoning. David's absence was not one morning's carelessness; it was the sum of one year and four months living where he should not have lived (cf. 1 Sam 27:7). To recognize this layer is not to add guilt to a wound, but to show people clearly: the road of restoration also begins with recognizing the displacement. In this chapter David does not first investigate whose fault it was; he weeps first, asks first, pursues first — the order comes later; in this section first acknowledge the fire for what it is: the city truly burned, the home truly emptied, and no sentence of faith may make this fire smaller than it was.

### 2. They wept aloud until they had no more strength to weep (30:4–5)

David and his men wept aloud until they had no more strength to weep; David's two wives, Ahinoam and Abigail, had also been taken captive (condensed rendering of 30:4–5).

"Weep," Hebrew בָּכָה (bākhâ, H1058), to weep. See BDB, s.v. בכה.[4] The text gives this weeping unusual weight: aloud, and then, until they had no more strength — in the original sense, weeping until there was no strength left to weep. Six hundred men who had fought battles wept themselves empty in the ashes, and the narrator neither covers their shame nor urges them to pull themselves together. This verse leaves a formal seat in the chapter for everyone who grieves: the Bible does not treat tears as the opposite of faith. Abraham mourned and wept for Sarah (cf. Gen 23:2, condensed rendering); Joseph repeatedly turned aside to weep before his brothers (cf. Gen 42–45); the Lord Jesus also wept at Lazarus's tomb (cf. John 11:35, condensed rendering). Weeping until there is no strength is not evidence of weakness but of love: without love, no one weeps to exhaustion. Today's church has an impatient habit — seeing someone cry, it rushes to hand over doctrine, a golden verse, a "be strong"; but this chapter's narrative first sits in the ashes with these men until they finish weeping. The preacher should teach this lesson first to the comforters: when a person has wept until he has no strength, what he needs first is not an explanation, but someone to acknowledge that his city really burned and his people really are gone. Explanations can wait for the time of inquiry; the moment of tears calls only for company, not for hurry.

### 3. The people spoke of stoning him, but David strengthened himself (30:6)

David was greatly distressed, for the people spoke of stoning him, because they were bitter over their sons and daughters; but David strengthened himself in the LORD his God (condensed rendering of 30:6).

"Distressed," Hebrew צרר (ṣārar, H6887), to be hemmed in, narrow, distressed. See BDB, s.v. צרר.[5] "Strengthened himself," Hebrew חָזַק (ḥāzaq, H2388), to be strong, firm; here in its reflexive use — David caused himself to be strengthened in the LORD (for the form see the endnote). See BDB, s.v. חזק.[6] Both sides of this verse must be preached in full. The people's side: they spoke of stoning David — heavy words, whose root the text states: bitterness over their own children. When people reach the extremity of bitterness, the nearest explanation at hand is the man who led them here. This is not justice; it is grief looking for an exit; had David argued, recited his merits, or settled old accounts at that moment, the stones would have fallen on the spot. David's side: he did not defend himself, did not plead that he too was a victim, and did not crush them with a leader's authority; he did one thing only — he strengthened himself in the LORD his God. Note where this strength is located: not after the situation improved, not after the people changed their minds, but while the stones were still in their hands and everything around was weeping and resentment. Strength is not a mood that comes once the environment quiets down; strength is the act, in the loudest, most unjust, loneliest moment, of handing yourself back into God's hands. David had been hunted for years, and this was his loneliest day: Saul wanted to kill him, the Philistines did not want him, his own men wanted to stone him, and his family was gone. Surrounded on four sides, what he grasped was not a company, not a city, but the "his" in the name "the LORD his God" — God was still his God. The preacher should give this sentence to those besieged on every side: you cannot prove your innocence, cannot persuade those who resent you, cannot recover today what was lost today; one thing remains that you can do — while the people are picking up stones, first return to your God and stand firm in Him again. Once you are standing, then comes the next step.

### Ignition Point One: When you have wept until you have no strength, you can still inquire

Let me stop here and speak to those who have wept until they have no strength. Your weeping is no disgrace. When a city has burned, weep; when people have been carried off, weep; weep until you have no strength — the Bible not only does not laugh at you, it writes this sentence into David's biography as the page before the victory. But listen: tears are a seat, not a destination. When David finished weeping, he did not lie in the ashes waiting for dark; he stood up, had the ephod brought, and asked God. It is today — not after your feelings have fully healed. Having no strength is exactly the time to inquire, because inquiry requires not your strength but your turning. Lift your face out of the ashes and set the question before God exactly as it is: Shall I overtake? Can they be rescued? Which step do You still want me to take? One step is enough. God did not ask David to explain the year and four months first, and He does not ask you to tidy yourself into a respectable believer before He will answer you. However far you have wept, He can hear you; you ask, and He answers. When the weeping is done, ask. Today.


## Part Two: Pursuit Only After Inquiry (1 Samuel 30:7–15)

### 1. Bring the ephod here (30:7–8)

David said to Abiathar the priest, "Bring the ephod here"; and David inquired of the LORD, "Shall I pursue this band? Shall I overtake them?" And He answered, "Pursue, for you shall surely overtake and shall surely rescue" (condensed rendering of 30:7–8).

"Ephod," Hebrew אֵפֹד (ʾēp̄ōd, H646), the ephod, used by the priest in inquiry (its relation to the Urim is registered in the endnote). See BDB, s.v. אפד.[7] "Inquire," Hebrew שָׁאָל (šāʾal, H7592), to ask, inquire. See BDB, s.v. שאל.[8] "Pursue," Hebrew רָדַף (rādap̄, H7291), to pursue; "overtake," Hebrew נשׂג (nāśaḡ, H5381), to reach, overtake. See BDB, s.v. רדף and s.v. נשׂג.[9] This scene must be read against David's earlier life. In chapter 27, when he fled to Philistine territory, the text's comment is "David said in his heart" (cf. 1 Sam 27:1, condensed rendering) — he consulted himself and then left; in this chapter he no longer speaks in his heart; he opens his mouth and asks God. The question is also without decoration: shall I overtake, or not? It is the question of a man drained of strength, with no grand ambition, only one concrete fear: fear of running in vain, fear of catching up only to worse news. God's answer is equally concrete, and doubly so: you shall surely overtake — a guarantee for the road; you shall surely rescue — a guarantee for the outcome. Note that God does not first settle chapter 27's old account with David, nor attach a string of conditions; He first lifts the man up and points out the road, leaving the old account to be settled slowly once the man is standing — this is exactly God's order with those who turn back. The preacher should remind hearers: inquiry is not a sense of ceremony; it is handing the decision back. David moved only after asking, so every later step — crossing the wadi, leaving men behind, feeding the Egyptian, the dawn attack — is walked inside the promise. Busyness without inquiry often runs anxiety into a deeper displacement; pursuit that has inquired may move fast, but its heart is settled.

### 2. Two hundred stayed at the Besor (30:9–10)

David and the six hundred men with him came to the Wadi Besor, and two hundred stayed there, too exhausted to cross the wadi; David pressed on with four hundred men (condensed rendering of 30:9–10).

"Besor," Hebrew בְּשׂוֹר (Bəśôr, H1308), the name of a wadi. See BDB, s.v. בשׂור.[10] "Exhausted," Hebrew פָּגַר (pāḡar, H6296), to be exhausted, strengthless. See BDB, s.v. פגר.[11] The chapter sets this verse down lightly here and takes it up solemnly in Part Three; record three things now. First, the two hundred were not retreating at the battle line: they had already wept until they had no strength and then force-marched to the Besor; their bodies had reached the limit — a fact, not an excuse; the text uses "exhausted," not "afraid," and the word choice is itself the verdict. Second, David neither dragged them across nor lectured them on the spot: he left them by the wadi and pursued with four hundred — half of a leader's maturity is knowing who can still walk, and half is letting those who cannot walk stay with dignity, without writing their staying as shame. Third, the place where these two hundred stayed later became the birthplace of a statute (30:21–25); many of the church's most important institutions are made precisely for the weak: not waiting until everyone is strong to speak of fairness, but at the very moment someone is exhausted, putting "equal shares" up front. The preacher may leave a sentence here for those who stay, to be unfolded in Part Three: that you stopped at the Besor does not mean you fell out of God's company; this side of the wadi and the far side are a difference of post, not of worth.

### 3. In the field they found a discarded man (30:11–15)

In the field they found an Egyptian and brought him to David; they gave him bread to eat and water to drink, and a cake of figs and two clusters of raisins; and when he had eaten, his spirit revived, for he had not eaten bread or drunk water for three days and three nights. David asked him, "To whom do you belong, and where are you from?" He said, "I am a young man of Egypt, servant to an Amalekite. My master left me behind because I fell sick three days ago. We had made a raid on the Negev of the Cherethites, on the Negev of Judah and the Negev of Caleb, and we burned Ziklag with fire." David asked, "Will you take me down to this band?" He said, "Swear to me by God that you will not kill me or hand me over to my master, and I will take you down" (condensed rendering of 30:11–15).

The turning point of this chapter is not on the battlefield but at this meal. On a pursuit where every second counted, David stopped for an enemy camp's slave: bread, water, a fig cake and raisins; he waited until the young man's spirit revived, and only then questioned him slowly. This unhurriedness carries spiritual weight: an anxious man wants only to pry open his mouth; David first fed the man. The youth's story tells the whole logic of the Amalekite camp in three sentences — he fell sick, he was useless, he was discarded in the open field to die; he owed that camp no loyalty at all, and all he asked of David was one oath: do not kill me, and do not hand me back to my master. David agreed, and the youth led the way. Two threads must be preached side by side. One is God's preparation: who the enemy of Ziklag was, where their camp lay, by what road to go down — none of this intelligence was hidden in a Philistine war tent or in Saul's palace, but in a discarded sick man; God's restoration is often led by people already written off in society's ledger, and whoever despises the discarded may pass by his own guide. The other thread is David's change: the David of old nearly drew his sword over a meal withheld at Nabal's house (cf. 1 Samuel 25, condensed rendering); this David, on the road to rescue his own wives and children, first shares out food to feed a stranger. In chapter 25 Abigail stopped his sword with a meal; in this chapter he gains his road with a meal — the grace a person has once learned grows out at the most urgent pass. The preacher should give this section to everyone "in a hurry": on the road of your most urgent matter, if a discarded person reaches out a hand to you, do not walk around him first; when God places the guide at the roadside, the man who walks around him is walking around his own restoration.

## Part Three: Everything Recovered — and Shared (1 Samuel 30:16–31)

### 1. From dawn until evening, not one was missing (30:16–20)

The youth took them down, and there the Amalekites were spread over all the ground, eating, drinking and dancing. At dawn David attacked them and fought until the evening of the next day; none escaped except four hundred young men who fled on camels. David recovered all that the Amalekites had taken, and rescued his two wives; nothing was missing, small or great, sons or daughters, spoil or anything they had taken — David brought back all (condensed rendering of 30:16–19; v. 20, David's recovery of the flocks and herds, is also registered).

"Spoil," Hebrew שָׁלָל (šālāl, H7998), spoil, plunder. See BDB, s.v. שלל.[12] Bring out the contrast in this paragraph: on the Amalekites' side, eating, drinking and dancing — they thought the plundering was already accomplished and the victory feast was at its height; on David's side, action at dawn and fighting until the evening of the next day — a hard battle lasting from dawn straight through to the next evening, more than a full day and night (on the chronology see the endnote). Victory is not something that lands automatically the moment the promise is spoken: the promise gives direction and outcome; the battle still has to be fought blow by blow and the night endured hour by hour. God's "you shall surely overtake" does not mean David may lie down and wait for the Amalekites to deliver the people back; promise and sweat stand side by side in this chapter, as James says, faith without works is dead (cf. Jas 2:26, condensed rendering). Then the text reports the outcome very slowly and carefully: the two wives rescued; all who were taken, small and great, children and goods, recovered; and finally it nails down the sentence — not one was missing. This sentence is the title of this sermon and the heart of the chapter. God's restoration in this chapter is not approximate, not nine-tenths; it is a completeness that can be counted one by one. Let the preacher steady his voice here and give one measured word to those who have lost and have not seen restoration in kind: this chapter's "not one was missing" is the record of David's day, not a general cheque issued to everyone; yet it proves at least this — when God Himself steps in to restore, He counts by number and recognizes by name, and does not settle accounts with "roughly fine." Your loss, too, He can count and recognize; not everyone in this life waits for a Ziklag-style reunion (see Reading Caution Five), but no tear is recorded in His ledger as "roughly."

### 2. Those who go to battle and those who watch the baggage share alike (30:21–25)

David came to the two hundred men who had been too exhausted to follow him and had stayed at the Besor; they went out to meet David and his men, and David came up and greeted them. Then the wicked and worthless fellows among those who had gone with David said, "Because they did not go with us, we will give them nothing of the spoil we recovered, except that each may take his wife and children and go." David said, "My brothers, you shall not do so with what the LORD has given us; He has preserved us and given this band into our hand. The share of the one who goes down to the battle shall be the same as the share of the one who stays by the baggage; they shall share alike." And from that day forward David made it a statute and an ordinance for Israel, to this day (condensed rendering of 30:21–25).

"Worthless fellows," Hebrew בְּלִיַּעַל (bəliyyʿal, H1100), worthless, base fellows. See BDB, s.v. בליעל.[13] "Baggage," Hebrew כֵּלִים (kēlîm, H3627), gear, baggage. See BDB, s.v. כלי.[14] "Statute and ordinance," Hebrew חֹק (ḥōq, H2706), statute; מִשְׁפָּט (mišpāṭ, H4941), ordinance, precedent. See BDB, s.v. חק and s.v. שפט.[15] This paragraph is a rehearsal of David's kingship, showing his weight more than any battle. First look at the words refusing to share: they are not wholly without logic — the four hundred fought desperately for more than a day and night, the two hundred stayed by the wadi; why share alike? This is the world's oldest arithmetic, computed daily in church, company and family. David refutes it by changing the root of the arithmetic: he says, "what the LORD has given us" — victory is first credited to God, not to our merit; since it was given, the dividing cannot follow the merit ledger but the family ledger. Next look at his order of speech: he first goes in person to greet those who stayed (v. 21), and only then handles the distribution — greet first, settle accounts after; this order is a shepherd's order. Finally look at the outcome: this is not one-time generosity; the Bible says he made it a statute and an ordinance, to this day. A leader's passing kindness is nothing much; turning kindness into an institution, so that later weak people need not beg each time — that is governance. This chapter speaks directly to today's church: the worship team and those who watch the car park, those on the platform and those in logistics, those who charge ahead and those who stay behind caring for old and young, share alike in God's account; a church that counts only at the front and never greets at the wadi still has one lesson to learn from the day David made the statute. It is even more so in the family: the one who earns and the one who keeps the home, the one who fights outside and the one who cannot walk far because of children and the elderly — they share alike; not as a favor, but as an ordinance.

### 3. Sent throughout Judah (30:26–31)

When David came to Ziklag, he sent part of the spoil to the elders of Judah, his friends, saying, "Here is a present for you from the spoil of the LORD's enemies." He sent to those in Bethel, Ramoth of the Negev, Jattir, Aroer, Siphmoth, Eshtemoa, Racal, Hormah, Bor-ashan, Athach and Hebron, and to all the places where David and his men had habitually roamed (condensed rendering of 30:26–31; for the list of place names see the endnote).

The last stroke of the victory is sending outward. David is still a fugitive, the ash of Ziklag not yet cold, yet he divides the spoil and sends it throughout Judah — places that, on his fugitive road, had sheltered him and given him and his men a place to stay (the text says, "the places where they had habitually roamed"). This sending carries gratitude, foresight, and also an innocence: he is telling Judah, I, David, have come back not to exact but to repay. When later he is anointed king by the men of Judah at Hebron (cf. 2 Sam 2:4, condensed rendering), part of the root lies in the gifts divided on this day — people's hearts are not won on the day of the vote, but gift by gift, sent out while one is still a fugitive. The preacher should give this section to those waiting until "someday, when I am able, then I will be grateful, then I will give": when David sent his gifts, his own home had just been burned and the throne was far away; gratitude and giving have never been the privilege of the surplus-rich — they are the mark of the restored. When a person, in his tightest days, still divides outward, his heart has already been recovered from the plundering — and this may be the deepest layer of restoration in this chapter: a person recovers not only wives, children and goods, but also the heart that divides, sends, and remembers grace. Goods can be plundered and recovered; but if a heart, in suffering, grows hard, calculating, only taking in and never letting out — that is what the Amalekites truly carried away.

### Ignition Point Two: Not one was missing — do not count anyone short

Now I speak to those after the victory, and also to those still counting their losses. The restoration God gave in this chapter is counted one by one: two wives, everyone's children, goods small and great — not one was missing. If you are one of the four hundred, listen: the battle is over, and the real test is only beginning — not in the dawn charge, but in the division at the wadi. Will you say to the exhausted, "Brother, we share alike"? Will you first credit what you gained to God, and then divide it by the family ledger? It is today — not waiting for the church to meet and set a system: begin with this portion in your own hands. Divide with those who stayed, with those who guarded your rear, with the one who, in your greatest hurry, met you and led your road. Those who win most easily lose not the spoil but the heart: having beaten Amalek, the heart learns Amalek's arithmetic — the useless are discarded, the weak get less, all the credit goes to oneself. David did not. He counted people, none missing; he divided goods, none left out. Your God counts you the same way, none left out: if you think you are one of the exhausted two hundred, or the one discarded in the field, I tell you, in His register you have a name, a weight, and a portion of your own. Rise today in that counted dignity: you who have wept, rise and pursue; you who are exhausted, keep your post in peace; you who have gained, divide with open hands. Not one was missing — God speaks this sentence to the whole company, and to you.

## Cross-Reading: Four Passages

This chapter's events have echoes elsewhere in Scripture; four cross-readings nail it down. Old Testament, first: Remembrance — Moses commands Israel to remember what Amalek did to them on the road when they came out of Egypt, attacking the exhausted stragglers at the rear, not fearing God (cf. Deut 25:17–18, condensed rendering, checked against the key sense of the Union Version); and cf. Exodus: the LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation (cf. Ex 17:16, condensed rendering). The raid on Ziklag is no accidental banditry; it is the old method of this enemy who specializes in striking the exhausted and exploiting emptiness; the two hundred exhausted men and the discarded youth in this chapter are both his targets, and God's restoration falls first precisely on the exhausted and the discarded. Old Testament, second, a contrast: Saul, in the Amalek affair, could not bear to part with the best sheep and cattle and lost the kingship (cf. 1 Samuel 15, condensed rendering); David in this chapter wars thoroughly against Amalek and divides the spoil among the people — two anointed men facing the same enemy, one leaving a back door out of self-interest, the other winning hearts by sharing alike; the book's earlier and later accounts are registered on facing pages here. Psalms, one passage: David has another prayer from distress — "When I cried aloud to the LORD, He answered me from His holy hill" (cf. Ps 3:4, condensed rendering, checked against the Union Version's key sentence); the title of Psalm 3 marks it as written when David fled from his son Absalom — the same kind of moment as this chapter, driven to the wall by his own people yet answered in crying out; the two passages witness to each other: David's strength was never a temperament; it was trained by crying out. Proverbs, one passage: "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity" (Prov 17:17, Union Version) — the six hundred in this chapter first show resentment in adversity; that is human nature; that David after the victory still calls them "my brothers" (30:23, condensed rendering) is grace making brothers again; and, "the one who scatters freely will gain even more; the one who refreshes others will himself be refreshed" (cf. Prov 11:25, condensed rendering, checked against the Union Version) is exactly the footnote to the sending-out of 30:26–31. New Testament, one passage: Paul says we were so utterly burdened in Asia beyond our strength that we despaired of life itself and felt we had received the sentence of death, so that we would rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead — He delivered us from so great a death, and is delivering us (cf. 2 Cor 1:8–10, condensed rendering, checked against the key sense of the Union Version) — weeping until there is no strength, sentencing oneself to death, yet saved at the point of not relying on oneself: this line runs straight from Ziklag to Corinth; David's "he strengthened himself in the LORD his God" finds its clearest echo in the New Testament here.


## Application: Six Groups of People

First, to those who have just suffered a great loss and are still in the ashes. Your "Ziklag" may be an illness, a lost job, a broken family, savings that cannot be recovered. The first word this chapter says to you is not "cheer up" but permission to weep: David and six hundred warriors wept until they had no strength, and the Bible does not hurry them. First finish your tears; acknowledge that the city has burned; do not rush to gild the disaster. The second word is: when the weeping is done, ask. David in the ashes did not design a whole life-plan for himself; he asked one specific question and received one specific promise. Today you need not think your whole life through either; bring only the nearest step before God and ask — the more specific the question, the more clearly you can hear specific guidance. Keep the third word to verify slowly: if God's promise comes, the battle still has to be fought by you and the night endured by you; the promise is not exemption from sweat; but fighting a promised battle is a different thing from crashing about on your own.

Second, to leaders blamed by their own people. When the team has an accident, the church loses people, the project falls through, the first person the stones are aimed at is often the David who leads. This chapter gives you a lesson in three steps, whose order cannot be scrambled: first, do not defend yourself — hear the people's bitter words first as weeping, not as a verdict; second, be strengthened before God — if your grievance is not first laid down before God, sooner or later it will be reclaimed through authority or coldness; third, let action and distribution speak — David's vindication was not a statement of defense but the "my brothers, we share alike" spoken after he had recovered everything. Your credibility today likewise lies not in the explanation meeting but at the next distribution: if you will greet the people at the wadi first and give the portion to the weak first, the account of the stones will be rewritten in people's hearts by itself.

Third, to the exhausted who stay behind. Because of health, because of caring for the elderly and children, because in this season you truly cannot walk further, you watch others charge ahead while you stop at the Besor, feeling both guilty and afraid of being forgotten. This chapter is a statute the Bible itself makes for you: exhaustion is not desertion; David marched off with the four hundred but did not strike you from the company; when he returned, he greeted you first and then legislated before everyone — those who watch the baggage share the same portion as those who go to battle, to this day. What you are to do is keep this side of the wadi well: the baggage watched, the rear secured, the home kept — this is your post; do not condemn yourself by others' dawn, and do not treat yourself as an outsider for having stopped. In God's company you have a formal portion.

Fourth, to those whose hands hold resources after the victory. A promotion, money recovered, health restored, a case overturned — when the battle is finished, this chapter turns the mirror toward you: how do you divide? The four hundred's arithmetic can come out of your mouth at any time: where was he when I was fighting desperately? David's arithmetic first changes the ownership: "what the LORD has given us." Once this sentence is acknowledged, the portions naturally tilt toward the weak, because if it was given, no one deserves or fails to deserve; there is only who belongs to this family. Today you can check your own distribution list once: are those who stayed, those in logistics, those who once led your road, those who fed you a meal in your downfall — are they on the list? A victor's dignity lies not in the banquet's spectacle but in the list's completeness.

Fifth, to those who have been discarded and feel useless. That Egyptian youth, thrown by his master into the open field when he fell sick, three days without food or drink, was already written off in the Amalekites' ledger; but in God's ledger he is the guide of the whole chapter — without him David could not have found the enemy camp. This chapter says to you: being discarded proves only that those who discarded you saw nothing but usefulness; it does not prove that you yourself have no weight. When God looks for a guide, He often walks past the strong men in the tents and comes out into the open field to find you. What you are to do first is eat the cake God hands you — it may be a rest, a word of comfort, a person willing to listen to you; when your spirit revives, the roads you have walked, the wounds you have suffered, the enemy camps you recognize will all become the map of someone else's restoration. Your experience is waste in the enemy's hands; in God's hands it is intelligence and the whole map for leading the way.

Sixth, to parents and those who run a household. The fire at Ziklag reminds every head of a family: the rear is not naturally safe; the home's security needs someone to watch it and needs to be counted when schedules are arranged — David's whole company went on campaign and the families were left unguarded; this is the chapter's unspoken lesson, whose shape today is parents both fighting outside while children and the elderly face the internet, emotions and accidents alone. Learn two things: first, make your whereabouts known and arrange the rear; do not treat "everyone does it this way" as protection; second, when someone in the home is exhausted and cannot walk, learn David's heart in legislating: the family's merit book should not record only earning and charging; those who keep the home, accompany medical visits, get up at night to check on the children share alike — beginning with a greeting and a portion of respect. A home is not a place where love is divided by output; a home is a place where the wadi side and the battle side share alike. The way of distribution children learn at home today is the statute they will carry into the world tomorrow.

## Closing Prayer of Response

Lord, we gather up 1 Samuel 30 in response. For everyone who has returned to a Ziklag we pray: for those whose city has burned — permit them to weep; for those pointed at with stones — teach them not to defend themselves but first to return into You and be strengthened; for those who stayed exhausted at the wadi — give them a formal standing, and let them know they have not fallen behind; for those discarded in the open field — put the bread into their hands and make them again guides for others; for those whose hands hold spoil — remind them to greet first and share afterward, to divide what they have gained by the family ledger, and to send outward to those who once treated them kindly. May our generation of the church glory not only in the dawn charge but also in the statute at the wadi: not one was missing, and not one was short in the sharing. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotions, 300–500 words)

In 1 Samuel 30, David returns on the third day to Ziklag: the city has been burned by the Amalekites, and the wives and children carried off. David and his men weep aloud until they have no more strength; in bitterness the people speak of stoning David. But David strengthens himself in the LORD his God, and the first thing he does is inquire: "Shall I pursue this band? Shall I overtake them?" God says, "Pursue, for you shall surely overtake and shall surely rescue." On the way, two hundred exhausted men stay at the Besor and four hundred press on; an Egyptian youth, discarded by his master, leads the way. David recovers everything — not one was missing; and on the return he makes a statute: those who go to battle and those who watch the baggage share alike (cf. 1 Sam 30:4–25, condensed rendering). Three questions for meditation today: Have my recent tears been wept out, and am I willing to take the next step and ask God? Am I among the four hundred, among the two hundred at the wadi, or the one discarded in the field? If I hold any "spoil," who is missing from my distribution list? Prayer: Lord, teach me to trust in You and inquire of You when I have no strength, and teach me, when I have gained, to share alike by the family ledger.

[7] Ephod: אֵפֹד (ʾēp̄ōd, Strong H646), ephod. See BDB, s.v. אפד. 1 Sam 30:7 — the priest Abiathar accompanied David with the ephod (cf. 1 Sam 23:6); the inquiry mechanism involving the Urim is registered for the record.
[8] Inquire: שָׁאָל (šāʾal, Strong H7592), to ask, inquire. See BDB, s.v. שאל. 1 Sam 30:8 — David inquired of the LORD, forming a whole-book contrast with Saul's unanswered inquiry (1 Samuel 28).
[9] Pursue: רָדַף (rādap̄, Strong H7291), to pursue; overtake: נשׂג (nāśaḡ, Strong H5381), to reach. See BDB, s.v. רדף and s.v. נשׂג. 1 Sam 30:8 — David asks whether he will overtake, and God promises he surely will; the two words read together.
[10] Besor: בְּשׂוֹר (Bəśôr, Strong H1308), name of a wadi. See BDB, s.v. בשׂור. 1 Sam 30:9–10, 21 — the place where the two hundred stayed; the exact location (often placed south of Gaza) is registered for the record.
[11] Exhausted: פָּגַר (pāḡar, Strong H6296), exhausted, strengthless. See BDB, s.v. פגר. 1 Sam 30:10, 21 — clearly bodily exhaustion, not fear of battle; the main text's division follows this.
[12] Spoil: שָׁלָל (šālāl, Strong H7998), spoil, plunder. See BDB, s.v. שלל. 1 Sam 30:16–27 — the division and sending throughout this chapter are registered with this word as the account's center.

[14] Baggage: כֵּלִים (kēlîm, Strong H3627), gear, baggage. See BDB, s.v. כלי. 1 Sam 30:24 — those who watch the baggage share the same portion as those who go to battle; the word belongs to the baggage-and-gear register.

[17] Textual note: the list of places in 1 Sam 30:26–31 numbers more than ten; this manuscript excerpts the essentials; Hebron stands at the end of the list, registered in intertext with David's anointing at Hebron in 2 Samuel 2.

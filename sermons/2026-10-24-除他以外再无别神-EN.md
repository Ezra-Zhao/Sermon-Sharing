Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: First English translation, Deuteronomy Batch 2, 25 October 2026; draft translation pending proofreading (初译待校), unpublished (translated in full, paragraph by paragraph, from the finalized Chinese manuscript "There Is None Else Beside Him" on Deuteronomy 4, which has passed strong re-verification in Chinese on 25 October 2026; this English version has not yet had native-speaker proofreading)

Title verification: Five candidates were considered in the Chinese manuscript — (i) "There Is None Else Beside Him," taken from the declaration that stands twice in the chapter, at 4:35 and 4:39; in the KJV at 4:35 the words stand verbatim as "there is none else beside him" (the full sentence: "Unto thee it was shewed, that thou mightest know that the Lord he is God; there is none else beside him."), and at 4:39 as "there is none else"; (ii) "Ye Shall Not Add, Neither Shall Ye Diminish," taken from 4:2, which covers only the textual form laid down in the first part; (iii) "Ye Heard a Voice, but Saw No Similitude," taken from 4:12, which covers only the formless theme of Parts Two and Three; (iv) "If Thou Seek Him with All Thy Heart, Thou Shalt Find Him," taken from 4:29, which covers only the way back in Part Four and not the skeleton of the whole chapter; (v) "Teach Them Thy Sons, and Thy Sons' Sons," taken from 4:9, which covers only the line of transmission and not the main trunk of the argument for the one God. The declaration stands twice in the chapter: once at the landing point of the review of the miracles (v. 35), and once at the head of the chapter's closing charge (v. 39). The English title is fixed as: There Is None Else Beside Him (from KJV 4:35, "there is none else beside him").

## Main Text

Introduction (00:00–05:00). (Fact) Deuteronomy chapter 4 is the closing section of Moses' first address (1:1–4:43). The first three chapters are retrospect: chapter 1 records why the forty years were delayed, chapter 2 the circuit and the first victory, chapter 3 the second victory, the division of the land, and the handing over of the staff; in this chapter Moses lays narrative down and turns to direct exhortation and argument — first laying down the form of the law, then looking back to Horeb, then laying down the command against any form, then foretelling corruption and return, and finally closing, by an examination of history, on the declaration of the one God, with the appointment of the cities of refuge appended (vv. 32–49). The chapter has forty-nine verses in all, and its structure is registered first: the body of the address, forty verses (1–40), and a historical appendix, nine verses (41–49); this chapter is also the chapter of Moses' addresses in which the theological argument is most concentrated — the question it asks is only one: on what ground is Israel different from all the nations? The answer it gives has three layers: a law to which nothing may be added and from which nothing may be taken away, a God who is only heard in His voice and never seen in a form, and a stretch of history that no other nation has. (Argument) Notice the order of Moses' argument: he does not first speak theology and then history; he first sets the history out, and then reads the theology out of the history. The declaration of verse 35, "there is none else beside him," is not the conclusion of a speculation; it is the result of what was "shewed" unto thee (v. 35) — in this chapter the one God is not proved; He is summed up from what He has been seen to do. Before the law is repeated from chapter 5 onward, Moses first tells the second generation who the God behind the law is. (Summary) The governing sentence of the chapter can be put this way: hearken, because He is one alone; take heed, because He has no form; return, because He is merciful. The whole chapter lands at last on two words — "this day" (4:39): knowing is not a matter for another day; it is a matter for this day.

### Part One: Hearken and Do — Ye Shall Not Add, Neither Shall Ye Diminish (4:1–8; 05:00–15:00)

(Fact) Verse 1 is the opening sentence of the whole chapter, quoted in full from the base text of this translation: "Now therefore hearken, O Israel, unto the statutes and unto the judgments, which I teach you, for to do them, that ye may live, and go in and possess the land which the Lord God of your fathers giveth you." Register the items one by one: first, "now therefore" in the original is ʿattâ (now), not "this day" (hayyôm) — the chapter's account of "this day" is counted from verse 4 (Endnote 3); second, "teach" (lāmad, H3925) and "hearken" (šāmaʿ, H8085) are a pair: Moses' address is the teaching, and the people's response is to hearken and do; third, the purpose clause has two layers — "that ye may live" first, and "go in and possess the land" (yāraš, H3423) after: in the chapter's first sentence the law is first the way of life, and only then the way of taking the land. (Fact) Verse 2 lays down the textual form of the whole chapter, quoted in full: "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish ought from it, that ye may keep the commandments of the Lord your God which I command you." Register the items: first, "add" (yāsap, H3254) and "diminish" (gāraʿ, H1639) are a pair of opposite verbs — a fraction more and a fraction less both fall under the prohibition; second, the place of this sentence is before the body of the law (chapter 5): Moses first locks the words, and only then opens his mouth to speak — every sentence of this chapter from here on stands under the jurisdiction of this sentence; third, "keep" (šāmar, H8104) is the chapter's pivot word, occurring six times in the chapter (Endnote 5); this is its first occurrence. (Fact) Verse 3 gives a near, negative example (registered paraphrase): what the Lord did because of Baalpeor, your eyes have seen; all the men that followed Baalpeor, the Lord destroyed from among you — the details are in Numbers 25 (the plague, twenty-four thousand men; registered paraphrase), and this chapter only names the matter. Verse 4 gives the positive remainder, quoted in full: "But ye that did cleave unto the Lord your God are alive every one of you this day." Register the items: first, "did cleave" (dāḇaq, H1692) means to stick fast, to cling close; second, verses 3 and 4 are a paired account: those who followed Baalpeor were destroyed, and those who cleaved unto the Lord are all alive — the same generation, the same plague, and the dividing line lies only in the word "cleave"; third, "this day" is the first of the chapter's seven occurrences (Endnote 3): forty years have passed, the plague has passed, and the survivors are standing at this moment on the scene of the address. (Fact) Verse 5 (registered paraphrase): Moses says, Behold, I have taught you statutes and judgments, even as the Lord my God commanded me, that ye should do so in the land whither ye go to possess it. Verse 6 is the law's public fruit, quoted in full: "Keep therefore and do them; for this is your wisdom and your understanding in the sight of the nations, which shall hear all these statutes, and say, Surely this great nation is a wise and understanding people." Register the items: first, the two words "wisdom" (ḥokmâ, H2451) and "understanding" (bînâ, H998) occur twice in this verse — first in Moses' verdict, then in the nations' comment; the words are the same and the mouths are different; second, the witnesses of the wisdom are the nations: the law's fruit is not to be self-assessed behind closed doors, but spoken by outsiders "in the sight of the nations"; third, the cause the text gives for the nations' comment is a single item: "which shall hear all these statutes." Verse 7, quoted in full: "For what nation is there so great, who hath God so nigh unto them, as the Lord our God is in all things that we call upon him for?" — "nigh" (qārôb, H7138) occurs twice in this verse, pointing first to God's being nigh to men, and then to His being nigh when they call upon Him; "call upon" (qārāʾ, H7121) and "nigh" stand side by side with a near sound — a rhetorical juxtaposition of similar sounds, with no assertion that they share a root. Verse 8, quoted in full: "And what nation is there so great, that hath statutes and judgments so righteous as all this law, which I set before you this day?" — "righteous" (ṣaddîq, H6662) is the register of the quality of the statutes and judgments; "law" (tôrâ, H8451) in this chapter points to all of this that Moses sets before them, mutually attested by verses 44 and 45. (Argument) The theological centre of gravity of this part is the law's threefold identity: toward Israel it is the way of life (vv. 1, 4); toward the nations it is the witness of wisdom (v. 6); toward itself its quality is righteousness (v. 8). None of the three layers is Moses' boasting of himself: life has the paired account of Baalpeor, and the wisdom is spoken out of the nations' mouths. One further order deserves notice: first there is "God so nigh" (v. 7), and only then "statutes and judgments so righteous" (v. 8) — the law is not a wall between God and men; it is one of the ways of His nearness; Israel's true difference from the nations lies not in their having a law, but in their God's willingness to be nigh when they call upon Him. (New Testament Reading) The motif of verse 2, "Ye shall not add … neither shall ye diminish," and its echo at the end of the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only: Revelation 22 lays the same warning, neither to add nor to take away, over the words of that book (registered paraphrase); Matthew 5 records the Lord saying that one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law (registered paraphrase). Neither place is a verse-by-verse quotation; only the correspondence of the motif is registered. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) On the scope of the object of "shall not add, neither shall ye diminish," the readings are set out side by side: one reading takes it as the form of the words delivered in this chapter (limited to this address of Moses); one takes it as the seal of the whole law (set before the body of the law, which is exactly a seal's position); one takes it as the customary closing formula of ancient Near Eastern treaty and law-code style (akin to the words in code prologues and epilogues cursing whoever adds or takes away — registered only, with no assertion of derivation). The three readings coexist; this manuscript registers only this verse's position and wording and does not adjudicate. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: this part both locks the law and hangs a sign on it — the lock is "shall not add, neither shall ye diminish," and the sign is "wisdom, righteous, God so nigh." Moses wants the second generation to understand first: what you are about to receive is not a set of rules; it is the word of a God who is nigh; those who keep it are alive this day, and the nations who hear it will call it wisdom.

### Part Two: That Day at Horeb — Ye Only Heard a Voice (4:9–14; 15:00–24:00)

(Fact) Verse 9 tightens Part One's "keep" into "take heed," quoted in full: "Only take heed to thyself, and keep thy soul diligently, lest thou forget the things which thine eyes have seen, and lest they depart from thy heart all the days of thy life: but teach them thy sons, and thy sons' sons;" Register the items: first, "take heed" is the niphal form of šāmar (to keep oneself), occurring three times in the chapter (vv. 9, 15, 23; Endnote 5); second, the danger in this verse is not betrayal but "forgetting" (šākaḥ, H7911) — and the path of forgetting is also written out: first they depart from the heart, and afterwards they are forgotten; the path of keeping runs the other way: first keep the soul, and the things will not depart from the heart; third, the destination of the keeping is the next generation — "teach them" (the hiphil form of yādaʿ, to make known, H3045) thy sons and thy sons' sons: what one has oneself seen with one's eyes is to become what one's descendants hear with their ears. (Fact) Verse 10 looks back to Horeb, quoted in full: "Specially the day that thou stoodest before the Lord thy God in Horeb, when the Lord said unto me, Gather me the people together, and I will make them hear my words, that they may learn to fear me all the days that they shall live upon the earth, and that they may teach their children." Register the items: first, "Horeb" occurs twice in this chapter (vv. 10, 15; Endnote 8); second, this verse embeds a speech of God, the chapter's only directly quoted words of God — its content is the purpose of the covenant: gather (qāhal, H6950), make them hear, learn to fear (yārēʾ, H3372), teach their children — from the very beginning the covenant at Horeb carried the clause "teach the next generation," dovetailing with verse 9 from head to tail; third, God's words are carried in this chapter through Moses' report, in the form "when the Lord said unto me." (Fact) Verse 11 (registered paraphrase): ye came near and stood under the mountain; and the mountain burned with fire unto the midst of heaven, with darkness, clouds, and thick darkness — the sensory account is registered first: what could be seen was fire and darkness, and what could be heard was a voice (v. 12). Verse 12 is the chapter's sensory theorem, quoted in full: "And the Lord spake unto you out of the midst of the fire: ye heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude; only ye heard a voice." Register the items: first, "the voice of the words" (qôl dəbārîm) is the whole positive content of the Horeb experience; second, "similitude" (təmûnâ, H8544) occurs five times in this chapter, and this is its first occurrence (Endnote 4); third, "only … but … no" is an exclusionary construction: it is not that they saw little; the item "similitude" is entirely empty. Verse 13, quoted in full: "And he declared unto you his covenant, which he commanded you to perform, even ten commandments; and he wrote them upon two tables of stone." — "covenant" (bərît, H1285) occurs three times in this chapter, and this is its first occurrence (Endnote 6); "ten commandments" in the original is ʿăśeret haddəbārîm (the ten words, dābār, H1697) — the content of the covenant is counted in "words," which exactly matches the Horeb experience of "only ye heard a voice": the formless God leaves behind words; and words written on tables of stone can be handed down, kept, and taught. (On the difference between the KJV's "even ten commandments" and the Chinese base text's shorter form at this verse, see Base-text note (4) at the head of this manuscript.) Verse 14 (registered paraphrase): at that time the Lord commanded Moses to teach the people statutes and judgments, that they might do them in the land whither they go over to possess it — the covenant at Horeb here joins Part One's statutes: what was heard at the foot of the mountain is to be done in the promised land. (Argument) This part sets up the mechanism by which the faith is handed down: Israel's tradition is not an image tradition but a telling tradition. The gods of other nations have images, which can be copied, carried, and enshrined; Israel's God has only a voice, and a voice cannot be copied, only retold — so "teach them thy sons, and thy sons' sons" is not an added filial duty; it is this faith's only mode of survival: if the older generation does not tell, the next generation has nothing to hear. In verse 9 Moses sets the order out clearly: first take heed to and keep one's own soul, and only afterwards teach one's sons and sons' sons — transmission is not a question of technique but of keeping: what has first departed from the heart cannot be spoken out; what is kept in the heart can be handed down. (New Testament Reading) The echoes of the Horeb (Sinai) experience in the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only: Hebrews 12 recalls the fire and darkness of Sinai in the words "ye are not come unto the mount that might be touched," and closes with "see that ye refuse not him that speaketh" (registered paraphrase) — the exhortation not to refuse the One who speaks is of the same pattern as this chapter's structure of "only ye heard a voice"; John 20's "blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed" (registered paraphrase) stands at a distance answering this chapter's formless theme; neither is read back as this chapter's original meaning. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters are set out side by side. First, the mountain's names, Horeb and Sinai: the place of the covenant in Exodus 19–20 is Sinai, while this chapter uses Horeb at both its occurrences; the whole book of Deuteronomy regularly calls it Horeb, while Exodus and Numbers mostly call it Sinai — one reading takes them as two names for the same mountain (Horeb being the Deuteronomic tradition's name), and another takes them as pointing to different peaks or districts of the same mountain system; this manuscript registers only the two names' distribution in their own texts and makes no determination. Second, the surface tension between verse 9's "the things which thine eyes have seen" and verse 12's "saw no similitude": verse 9 says there are things that can be seen, and verse 12 says there is no similitude to be seen — the text's own division of labour is: what can be seen is the works (the fire, the cloud, the miracles of the exodus), and what cannot be seen is the similitude; two readings are set out side by side: one takes it as a deliberate contrasting rhetoric (seeing the works and not seeing a form is precisely this chapter's way of knowing God), and another takes "the things which thine eyes have seen" as referring generally to experience and not as set against the similitude. At the (Fact) layer this manuscript registers only the two verses' wording, and leaves the contrasting reading to this layer. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: what Horeb left Israel as its inheritance is a pair of ears, not a pair of eyes — ye only heard a voice, and saw no similitude. So this faith lives by telling: those who saw with their own eyes keep the things in their hearts, and then teach them to their sons and their sons' sons.

### Part Three: Ye Saw No Similitude, Therefore Make No Graven Image (4:15–24; 24:00–36:00)

(Fact) Verse 15 opens a new stretch of the address, quoted in full: "Take ye therefore good heed unto yourselves; for ye saw no manner of similitude on the day that the Lord spake unto you in Horeb out of the midst of the fire:" — "good heed" is the second occurrence of the niphal of šāmar (Endnote 5); and the order of the argument's words is registered first: first there is "for … ye saw no," and only then "therefore … take ye good heed" — the ground of the prohibition is experience: men first had the experience of "seeing none," and the prohibition simply writes the experience into a rule. (Fact) Verse 16, quoted in full: "Lest ye corrupt yourselves, and make you a graven image, the similitude of any figure, the likeness of male or female," Register the items: first, "corrupt" (šāḥat, H7843) occurs three times in this chapter, and this is its first occurrence (Endnote 10); second, the image-class words occur four times in this one verse — "graven image" (pesel, H6459, a carved image), "similitude" (the construct form of təmûnâ), "likeness" (semel, H5566), "likeness" (tabnît, H8403, the likeness of male or female): four words, from the finished product, the shape, the model, and the pattern, say "image" out in full; third, the list begins with man: male, female — the first thing man wants to remake God after is himself. Verses 17–18 complete the list (registered paraphrase): the likeness of any beast that is on the earth, of any winged fowl that flieth in the air, of any thing that creepeth on the ground, of any fish that is in the waters beneath the earth — the list's range is the whole catalogue of the created world in Genesis 1; the two lists have the same bounds and opposite directions: Genesis is God making man in His own image, and this chapter guards against man making God in the image of created things. (Fact) Verse 19 guards against another road outside the list, quoted in full: "And lest thou lift up thine eyes unto heaven, and when thou seest the sun, and the moon, and the stars, even all the host of heaven, shouldest be driven to worship them, and serve them, which the Lord thy God hath divided unto all nations under the whole heaven." Register the items: first, "all the host of heaven" (ṣəḇāʾ haššāmayim, H6635); second, "divided" (ḥālaq, H2505) has the sense of allotting — the sun, moon, and stars are what God has allotted to all the nations under heaven, not allotted to Israel to worship; to worship them is to mistake the heavens allotted to the nations for the object of worship; third, "shouldest be driven" (nādaḥ, H5080, in its niphal form) is passive: worshipping the heavens is not an active seeking but a being led away, a loss of one's footing. Verse 20 gives Israel another way out, quoted in full: "But the Lord hath taken you, and brought you forth out of the iron furnace, even out of Egypt, to be unto him a people of inheritance, as ye are this day." — "the iron furnace" (kûr habbarzel, H3564 + H1270) is a figure for Egypt, the place of slavery, also found at 1 Kings 8 and Jeremiah 11 (registered paraphrase); "inheritance" (naḥălâ, H5159) and "this day" each add a further entry: the nations receive the heavens as their portion, and Israel's portion is to be God's own people of inheritance. (Fact) Verses 21–22 are Moses' personal parenthesis (registered paraphrase): the Lord was angry with Moses for Israel's sakes and sware that he should not go over Jordan nor go in unto that good land; Moses says he must die in this land and not go over, but the people shall go over and possess that good land — the accounts of the attribution, on the same theme as 3:26, have been set out side by side in that manuscript; this chapter registers only this: the man who speaks all these prohibitions is a man who himself may not enter. Verse 23, quoted in full: "Take heed unto yourselves, lest ye forget the covenant of the Lord your God, which he made with you, and make you a graven image, or the likeness of any thing, which the Lord thy God hath forbidden thee." — "Take heed" is the third occurrence of the niphal of šāmar; "covenant" its second occurrence; the original of "forbidden" is ṣāwâ (H6680), registered as the same word as the "charge" of chapter 3. Verse 24 is this part's declaration, quoted in full: "For the Lord thy God is a consuming fire, even a jealous God." — "consuming fire" in the original is ʾēš ʾōkəlâ (a fire that eats, ʾākal, H398); "jealous" (qannāʾ, H7067) is the same word as in the second of the ten commandments (Exod 20:5, registered paraphrase): the formless God is not an unresponsive God — He forbids the making of images precisely because He takes this relationship seriously. (Argument) The theological centre of gravity of this part is the two faces of formlessness: toward God, it is His self-definition — He refuses to be made into the shape of any created thing, because any image shrinks Him into one compartment of the created world; toward men, it is the faith's guardrail — man's motive in making an image is on the surface worship and underneath control: once an image is made, God has become a thing that can be moved about and used to get things done. In the Horeb experience Moses blocks this road: on that day ye saw no similitude, not because God was hiding, but because He has never dealt with men by similitude, only by word. A word cannot be possessed; it can only be hearkened to; and so the "consuming fire" and "jealous God" of verse 24 stand: the formless God is less to be trifled with than any formed image. (New Testament Reading) The echoes of this part's motif in the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only: John 4, "God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth" (registered paraphrase); 1 Timothy 1 calls God "the King eternal, immortal, invisible" (registered paraphrase); Romans 1, "and changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man, and to birds, and fourfooted beasts, and creeping things" (registered paraphrase) — its catalogue of created things is broadly the same catalogue as this chapter's verses 16–18, and agreements and differences of order are registered only, with no assertion of quotation. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters are set out side by side. First, the golden calf's blank: Exodus 32 records the making of the golden calf at the foot of Horeb at the same season as the covenant (registered paraphrase), and Psalm 106 likewise recalls it in the words "they made a calf in Horeb" (registered paraphrase); this chapter retells Horeb and does not say a word about the calf — the readings are set out side by side: one takes it as a choice of the address genre (the hearers are the second generation, and Moses holds up their own near example, Baalpeor, at v. 3, rather than reopening the older generation's account); another takes it as rhetorical restraint (building the argument on a positive experience rather than on a shameful incident). This manuscript registers only the blank itself and does not adjudicate. Second, the object of the "dividing": at verse 19 the sun, moon, and stars are "divided unto all nations under the whole heaven" — the readings are set out side by side: one takes the dividing to mean that the nations each receive the heavens for observing the heavens and fixing the seasons (a functional allotment); another takes it as implying that the nations' worship of the heavens also stands under the allotment of God's sovereignty (compare the register of "divided" at 29:26, registered paraphrase). Neither reading changes the prohibition on Israel's side. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: on that day at Horeb, ye saw no similitude — Moses leaves the sentence no way of retreat: ye saw none (vv. 12, 15), therefore ye shall not make; ye shall not make, because He is a consuming fire, even a jealous God. Formlessness is not this faith's defect; it is this faith's birth certificate.

### Part Four: If Ye Corrupt Yourselves after Dwelling Long — the Foretelling of Destruction and the Way Back (4:25–31; 36:00–45:00)

(Fact) Verse 25 opens a new stretch of the address, quoted in full: "When thou shalt beget children, and children's children, and ye shall have remained long in the land, and shall corrupt yourselves, and make a graven image, or the likeness of any thing, and shall do evil in the sight of the Lord thy God, to provoke him to anger:" Register the items: first, the time condition is written at the head of the sentence — "ye shall have remained long in the land," and shall beget children and children's children: the corruption is not in the year of entering Canaan; the foretelling's object is a generation long settled and grown numerous; second, "corrupt yourselves" is šāḥat, the second occurrence of the same word as at verse 16 (Endnote 10); third, "the likeness of any thing" is the fifth occurrence of təmûnâ — Part Three's list of prohibitions here becomes a future list of charges, the same words and a different matter. (Fact) Verse 26 is a witness-form declaration, quoted in full: "I call heaven and earth to witness against you this day, that ye shall soon utterly perish from off the land whereunto ye go over Jordan to possess it; ye shall not prolong your days upon it, but shall utterly be destroyed." Register the items: first, "call … to witness" in the original is the hiphil form of ʿûd (H5749) — the formula of calling heaven and earth to witness is found again at 30:19, 31:28, and 32:1 (registered paraphrase); second, "this day" adds a further entry: the witness's moment is the today of the address; third, the punishment's wording is a doubled soon-and-utterly — "shall soon utterly perish" and "shall utterly be destroyed" — and the causal chain of verses 16 and 25, "corrupt," closes here: men corrupt themselves, and the land no longer keeps them. Verses 27–28 (registered paraphrase): the Lord shall scatter you among the nations, and ye shall be left few in number; and there ye shall serve gods, the work of men's hands, wood and stone, which neither see, nor hear, nor eat, nor smell — the sensory account here turns over: the One of Horeb can speak and can hear when men call upon Him (v. 7); this one made by men's hands lacks all four senses. The punishment of exile carries an exact irony: they would not have the God who speaks, so they shall serve the god who cannot speak. (Fact) Verse 29 is the hinge on which the whole chapter turns, quoted in full: "But if from thence thou shalt seek the Lord thy God, thou shalt find him, if thou seek him with all thy heart and with all thy soul." Register the items: first, the turning word is "But" — the words of the sentence are not finished, and the words of return are already joined on; second, "from thence": the place of seeking is not Canaan but the place of scattering, the place of serving wood and stone; third, "seek" here uses two words side by side — bāqaš (H1245) and dāraš (H1875); fourth, "with all thy heart and with all thy soul" (ləḇāḇ, H3824; nepeš, H5315) — the condition of return is not sacrifice and not distance, but the heart's whole measure; fifth, "thou shalt find him" (māṣāʾ, H4672) is an unconditional form of promise: between seeking with all the heart and finding, the text leaves no gap. Verse 30, quoted in full: "When thou art in tribulation, and all these things are come upon thee, even in the latter days, if thou turn to the Lord thy God, and shalt be obedient unto his voice;" — the separate register of the original wording of this verse and the KJV's reading has been given at the head of this manuscript (and see Endnote 9); "turn" (šûb, H7725) and "be obedient unto his voice" (šāmaʿ bəqôl) are the actions that follow verse 29's seeking: first find, then turn, then obey — the order is itself the order of repentance. Verse 31 gives the ground on which return is possible, quoted in full: "(For the Lord thy God is a merciful God;) he will not forsake thee, neither destroy thee, nor forget the covenant of thy fathers which he sware unto them." Register the items: first, "is a merciful God" (raḥûm, H7349) is God's self-introducing title, the same word as the proclamation of Exodus 34:6 (registered paraphrase); second, "will not forsake" (rāpâ, H7503) — God does not let go His grip; third, "neither destroy thee" is šāḥat, the chapter's third occurrence of the word (Endnote 10): men and God stand opposite each other on the same root — men šāḥat themselves (vv. 16, 25), and God does not šāḥat men; fourth, "nor forget" stands directly opposite men's "forget": šākaḥ occurs three times (vv. 9, 23, 31; Endnote 6), the first two on men's side and the third on God's side — men forget, God does not forget; this is the foundation of the way back. (Argument) This part's foretelling is in its structure "grace first, before the punishment is finished": the more concretely the judgment of verses 25–28 is written, the heavier the word "But" of verse 29 becomes. Moses does not write the return as men's merit: the seeking is with all the heart and all the soul, yet it is still "from thence" — the scene of the punishment, not a sanctuary; the finding is certain only because of the One of verse 31, who does not forsake, does not destroy, and does not forget. On this chapter's account of repentance, men contribute only one thing: to seek Him with all the heart and all the soul; everything else was laid down by Him beforehand. There is one further contrast: the wood-and-stone god of verse 28 "nor smell," while the Lord of verse 7 is nigh "in all things that we call upon him for" — the prayer of return is effective not because men cry loudly enough, but because the One who hears could hear all along. (New Testament Reading) The echoes of this part's motif in the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only: in Luke 15 the prodigal, "from thence," comes to himself and thinks of his father and rises and returns (registered paraphrase), its structure of the same pattern as this part's (scattering, remembering, returning, a father waiting); James 4, "Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you" (registered paraphrase), answers from afar verse 7's "nigh" and verse 29's "seek — find." (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters are set out side by side. First, the character of verses 25–31: the readings are set out side by side — one takes them as Moses' true foretelling of the future, its form a conditional warning (the condition first, the punishment after), the later exile being the warning's fulfilment; one takes them as an exilic or later editorial looking-back, restating Moses' address out of historical experience (the so-called prophecy-after-the-event reading); one takes them as a mixed genre of warning and looking-back, the warning first and a later editor reinforcing it according to its fulfilment. The three readings differ in nothing on the registration of the wording; the divergence lies in the judgment of the book's composition, and they are set out side by side without adjudication. Second, the relation of this part to Deuteronomy 30:1–10: there this part is restated in almost the same order — the curse befalling, the heart turning, the return, the obedience, God's gathering (registered paraphrase); the readings are set out side by side: one takes chapter 30 as this part's expansion and promise-form (this part's weight on warning, that place's weight on restoration), and another takes the two passages as two versions of one tradition. The correspondence of the two passages is registered only as above. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: these are the chapter's heaviest and also its warmest seven verses — heavy at "ye shall soon utterly perish," warm at "But if from thence thou shalt seek." Moses speaks the worst words first and paves the way back in the middle of the judgment — because the God he speaks of is One whom men may corrupt themselves to the point of scattering, and who yet will not forsake, will not destroy, and will not forget.

### Part Five: There Is None Else Beside Him (4:32–49; 45:00–57:00)

(Fact) Verse 32 opens the whole chapter's summary conclusion (registered paraphrase): ask now of the days that are past, from the one side of heaven unto the other, since the day that God created man upon the earth, whether there hath been any such thing as this great thing is, or hath been heard like it — the argument's method is stated plainly here: not speculation, but inquiry. Verse 33 continues the question (registered paraphrase): Did ever people hear the voice of God speaking out of the midst of the fire, as thou hast heard, and live — the Horeb experience (vv. 12, 36) is here set up as the sole case: hearing God speak and living — this one nation only. Verse 34 is the chapter's longest single question, quoted in full: "Or hath God assayed to go and take him a nation from the midst of another nation, by temptations, by signs, and by wonders, and by war, and by a mighty hand, and by a stretched out arm, and by great terrors, according to all that the Lord your God did for you in Egypt before your eyes?" Register the items: first, "hath … assayed" in the original is nāsâ (H5254, to attempt) — the question asked is: has any god ever assayed such a thing; second, the list of means has seven items: temptations (massâ, H4531), signs (ʾôt, H226), wonders (môpēt, H4159), war (milḥāmâ, H4421), a mighty hand (ḥāzāq, H2389), a stretched out arm (nāṭâ, H5186), and great terrors (môrāʾ, H4172); third, the seven items' anchor is "in Egypt before your eyes" — the argument's evidence is not in ancient legend but before the hearers' eyes and in their memory. (Fact) Verse 35 is the chapter's first declaration, quoted in full: "Unto thee it was shewed, that thou mightest know that the Lord he is God; there is none else beside him." Register the items: first, "it was shewed unto thee" — the theme of "seeing" here turns over: the people saw no similitude of God (vv. 12, 15), yet God shewed them His works to see: they did not see His form; they saw His deeds; second, "that thou mightest know" — verses 32–34's examination and questioning have as their destination "to know"; third, the declaration's formula: the Lord he is God, plus, there is none else beside him — the chapter's title comes from here. Verse 36, quoted in full: "Out of heaven he made thee to hear his voice, that he might instruct thee: and upon earth he shewed thee his great fire; and thou heardest his words out of the midst of the fire." — "instruct" is yāsar (H3256); the two lines, out of heaven and upon earth, are set side by side: the voice from heaven, the great fire upon earth — the Horeb experience is gathered up here, interwoven with verse 12. Verse 37 (registered paraphrase): because he loved thy fathers, therefore he chose their seed after them, and brought thee out in his sight with his mighty power out of Egypt — the numbers of the two words "loved" and "chose" were originally not checked directly and were registered as pending (Endnote 11; for their clearance in the strong re-verification of the Chinese manuscript, see the review record at the end of this manuscript); the order is registered as it stands: first He loved the fathers, then He chose their seed after them — the root of the choosing is not in those chosen, but in God's going before. Verse 38, quoted in full: "To drive out nations from before thee greater and mightier than thou art, to bring thee in, to give thee their land for an inheritance, as it is this day." — the causative form of yāraš in "drive out / give for an inheritance" shares its root with verse 1's possessing by inheritance; "mightier" (ʿāṣûm, H6099, see BDB; the word-form seen directly in this verse's BHS line); "as it is this day" is the sixth entry in the account of "this day" (Endnote 3) — the accomplished fact east of the river is the warrant for the future west of it. Verse 39 is the chapter's second declaration, quoted in full: "Know therefore this day, and consider it in thine heart, that the Lord he is God in heaven above, and upon the earth beneath: there is none else." Register the items: first, "Know therefore this day" — the seventh occurrence of "this day," and also the chapter's landing point of tense: examination (v. 32) and knowing (v. 35) here become today's lesson; second, "consider it in thine heart" in the original is the hiphil form of šûb with "unto the heart" (H7725 + lēḇāḇ) — to send what one knows back into the heart; the same šûb is "turn" at verse 30 and "consider it in thine heart" here: the action of return is first completed in the heart; third, "in heaven above, and upon the earth beneath" answers verse 36's out of heaven and upon earth: the declaration's range and the experience's range have the same bounds. Verse 40 is the address's close, quoted in full: "Thou shalt keep therefore his statutes, and his commandments, which I command thee this day, that it may go well with thee, and with thy children after thee, and that thou mayest prolong thy days upon the earth, which the Lord thy God giveth thee, for ever." — the Chinese base text's quotation marks for the address close here (punctuation of the whole-chapter page registered); "his statutes, and his commandments" answers the head's "the statutes and unto the judgments" of verse 1 from tail to head (Endnote 7); "prolong thy days" (ʾārak, H748) forms a pair with Part One's "that ye may live" (v. 1): the address begins with life and ends with length of days. (Fact) Verses 41–49 are the historical appendix. Verses 41–42 (registered paraphrase): Then Moses severed three cities on this side Jordan toward the sunrising, that the slayer might flee thither, which should kill his neighbour unawares, and hated him not in times past, and live — answering the command of Numbers 35: there three cities east of Jordan are commanded to be severed, and this chapter records that Moses has already done so; the detailed rules are there, and this chapter records only the fact of the severing and the three cities' names. Verse 43, quoted in full: "Namely, Bezer in the wilderness, in the plain country, of the Reubenites; and Ramoth in Gilead, of the Gadites; and Golan in Bashan, of the Manassites." — three cities and three tribes, one answering to one; Joshua 20:8 restates these same three cities by the same names (registered paraphrase). (The KJV's spellings — Bezer, Ramoth, Golan — are registered here as the anchor gives them.) Verses 44–45 are a title-form register of the address (registered paraphrase): And this is the law which Moses set before the children of Israel: these are the testimonies, and the statutes, and the judgments, which Moses spake unto the children of Israel, after they came forth out of Egypt — the threefold form is seen again here (Endnote 7), and it is expressly said that the law's promulgation came after the exodus: salvation first, the law after. Verses 46–47 (registered paraphrase): the place is on this side Jordan, in the valley over against Bethpeor, the land of Sihon and of Og whom Moses and the children of Israel smote after they came forth out of Egypt — pointing to the same place as 3:29 (mutual register). Verse 48, quoted in full: "From Aroer, which is by the bank of the river Arnon, even unto mount Sion, which is Hermon," — the appendix's geography closes on a single north–south axis: south from Aroer, north to Hermon; "mount Sion" (śîʾōn, H7865) is another name of Hermon, to be read alongside the register of the three names at 3:9 (Endnote 15). Verse 49 (registered paraphrase): and all the plain on this side Jordan eastward, even unto the sea of the plain, under the springs of Pisgah — with the same wording pointing back to the boundary account of 3:17, the geography of the first address here closes its loop. (Argument) This part's mode of argument is the whole chapter's signature: Moses does not prove God's oneness; he lays the history out and tells men to examine it for themselves (v. 32) and to know for themselves (vv. 35, 39). Between the two declarations there lie not more doctrines but more facts — a voice, a great fire, the fathers, the exodus, the driving out of mightier nations. In Moses' hands the faith in the one God is never a philosophical proposition; it is a way of reading history. One further order must not be let pass: after the declaration (v. 39) what follows immediately is not a song of triumph but commandments (v. 40) — knowing that He is one alone lands on keeping His statutes and His commandments; in this chapter cognition never stays alone; it always walks into obedience, just as the address begins at verse 1 with "hearken … for to do them" and ends at verse 40 with "Thou shalt keep therefore." (New Testament Reading) The echoes of the one-God declaration of verses 35 and 39 in the New Testament are registered at the reading layer only: 1 Corinthians 8's "an idol is nothing in the world" and its knowing that "there is none other God but one," and its sentence-form "in heaven" and "in earth" (registered paraphrase), answer from afar this chapter's range-form, "the Lord he is God in heaven above, and upon the earth beneath"; 1 Timothy 2's "there is one God" (registered paraphrase) belongs to the same confessional tradition. Both places are registered at the reading layer only. (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters are set out side by side. First, the genre boundary between 4:1–40 and 41–49: the readings are set out side by side — one takes what begins at verse 41 as an editor's historical appendix (the severing of the cities of refuge and the restatement of the address's provenance, the tone turning to third-person narrative), belonging to a different layer from the body of the address (first and second person); another takes the whole chapter as one whole (the appendix is the address's signature and geographical seal, of the same form as the book's opening superscription at 1:1–5). The turning point is at verse 41; it is registered only, without adjudication. Second, the chain of records of the three cities of refuge has been registered in Endnote 14; scholarship holds several proposals side by side for the three cities' present-day locations, and this manuscript makes no determination. (Summary) The governing sentence of this part: the chapter's ending gathers everything into one sentence — in heaven above and upon the earth beneath, the Lord he is God; there is none else beside him. This sentence has history as its witness (Egypt, Horeb, east of the river), today as its date (know therefore this day), and the commandments as its landing point (Thou shalt keep therefore). The appendix closes with three cities of refuge: even for the man who has killed unawares, in the kingdom of this one God there is a way of life.

Conclusion (57:00–60:00). (Argument) Look back over this chapter: it begins with "now" (v. 1) and ends on the account of "this day" — alive this day (v. 4), set before you this day (v. 8), as ye are this day and as it is this day (vv. 20, 38), I call heaven and earth to witness against you this day (v. 26), know therefore this day (v. 39), which I command thee this day (v. 40): seven "this day"s compress Horeb of forty years ago, the wilderness of the forty years, and the Canaan not yet entered, into this one day of Moses' speaking. In this chapter the faith's tense is never yesterday and never tomorrow; it is today. Now it is today's hearers' turn: our road of knowing God is the same road as the second generation of Israel's — we see no similitude, so we give our hearts to hearing the voice; we did not ourselves experience Horeb, so we hear those who did experience it tell us the things, and then teach them to our sons and sons' sons. We too shall have our own "from thence" — scattered and weak circumstances; this chapter's words are effective there in the same way: seek Him with all the heart and all the soul, and thou shalt find Him. May we know today, and consider it in our hearts: in heaven above and upon the earth beneath, the Lord he is God; there is none else beside him; and may we keep what we know, neither adding nor diminishing, and teach it to our sons and our sons' sons.

## Devotional Extract (for daily devotional use)

"But if from thence thou shalt seek the Lord thy God, thou shalt find him, if thou seek him with all thy heart and with all thy soul." (Deut 4:29) These words of Moses were spoken beforehand to a people who had not yet sinned. If in days to come ye dwell long in that land, and corrupt yourselves, and are scattered among the nations, and there serve gods of wood and stone, which neither see nor hear — right there, ye shall seek the Lord. It is not first to return to Canaan and then to seek; it is to seek in the place of scattering, in the least seemly of circumstances; and it is not that after seeking long one might perhaps find; it is that, seeking with all the heart and all the soul, thou shalt find him. In our lives too we each have our own "from thence": we have gone very far away, our circumstances are very unseemly, and even prayer feels undeserved. Moses' words wait precisely there: He is a merciful God; he will not forsake thee, neither destroy thee, nor forget the covenant of thy fathers. Men forget God; God does not forget the covenant. Today there is no need to wait until circumstances improve, no need to wait until we are qualified enough — right where you are at this moment, seek Him with all your heart and all your soul. Those who seek never come away empty, because the One who hears is, in all things that we call upon him for, already nigh unto us. Prayer: Lord, I seek You in my own "there." Thank You that You do not forsake, do not destroy, and do not forget; let me know today, and consider it in my heart: in heaven above and upon the earth beneath, You alone are God; there is none else beside You. Amen.

### Devotional Extract (English, KJV-based)

Read Deuteronomy 4:39 (KJV): "Know therefore this day, and consider it in thine heart, that the Lord he is God in heaven above, and upon the earth beneath: there is none else." Moses spoke these words to a generation that had heard God's voice at Horeb but never seen His form — "ye heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude; only ye heard a voice" (4:12, KJV). Faith here is not built on an image but on a history: what God did in Egypt, what He spoke from the fire. ## Endnotes (accounts recounted by hand)

Endnote 1 (this chapter's place and the address's divisions): This chapter belongs to the closing section of Deuteronomy's first address (1:1–4:43): chapter 1 recalls the cause of the delay, chapters 2 and 3 record the two victories, the division of the land, and the handing over of the staff, and this chapter turns to exhortation and argument, its verses 44–45 titling themselves the bridge to the body of the law in the following book (from chapter 5 onward) (registered paraphrase). The address's geographical point is only named in this chapter's appendix (v. 46, "in the valley over against Bethpeor"), mutually attested by 1:5 ("on this side Jordan, in the land of Moab") and 3:29 (registered paraphrase).

Endnote 2 (the structural account): The whole chapter has 49 verses: Hearken to the Statutes 1–8 (8 verses) + the Horeb Retrospect 9–14 (6 verses) + the Command against Any Form 15–24 (10 verses) + Corruption and Return 25–31 (7 verses) + the Examination, the Declaration, and the Appendix 32–49 (18 verses) = 8 + 6 + 10 + 7 + 18 = 49. By another division: the Body of the Address 1–40 (40 verses) + the Historical Appendix 41–49 (9 verses) = 49; the two divisions agree. The paragraph division points of the Chinese base text's quotation marks for the address are registered at verses 9, 15, and 25, closing at the end of verse 40 (punctuation of the whole-chapter page registered).

Endnote 3 (the account of "this day"): hayyôm occurs seven times in the whole chapter — v. 4, "are alive every one of you this day"; v. 8, "which I set before you this day"; v. 20, "as ye are this day"; v. 26, "I call heaven and earth to witness against you this day"; v. 38, "as it is this day"; v. 39, "Know therefore this day"; v. 40, "which I command thee this day" — all seven verses confirmed by direct checking on the verse-by-verse pages.

## Summary (approx. 100 words)

The two angels arrived at Sodom at evening. Lot, sitting at the city gate, met them and strongly urged them to come into his house. The men of the city surrounded the house to commit evil, and the angels struck them with blindness. The angels urged Lot to take his family and escape for their lives. Lot lingered, but because the Lord was merciful to him, the angels took him, his wife, and his daughters by the hand and led them out of the city, commanding: Escape for your life! Do not look behind you. Brimstone and fire fell, overthrowing the city; Lot's wife looked back and became a pillar of salt. Early in the morning, Abraham went to the place where he had stood and saw smoke ascending. God remembered Abraham and delivered Lot out of the overthrow. This sermon examines three things: the person sitting at the city gate is too close to the world; the lingering person is pulled out by mercy; and the cost of looking back is leaving one's life halfway along the road.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace be with you all!

At the end of the previous sermon, Abraham stood on the high ground, looking toward Sodom, interceding for the city down to ten righteous people. Today, we go down into the city. At evening, a man is sitting at the city gate—Lot. In chapter 13, he chose the plain of Jordan; in chapter 14, he moved into the city, was taken captive once, and was rescued by Abraham. Now, he is in the city again, and sitting at the city gate. In those days, the city gate was the place for deliberation, judgment, and serving as an elder. Lot did not merely live in Sodom; he already held a position in Sodom.

Please examine chapter 14 and today's chapter side by side: During that captivity, God rescued him through Abraham—that was a warning. After the warning, he still returned to Sodom. Today is the second time God rescues him, but this time, the city is gone.

Let us first read several key passages of Scripture:

"Now the two angels came to Sodom in the evening, and Lot was sitting in the gate of Sodom. When Lot saw them, he rose to meet them, and he bowed himself with his face toward the ground;" (Gen. 19:1, excerpt)

"For we will destroy this place, because the outcry against them has grown great before the face of the Lord, and the Lord has sent us to destroy it." (Gen. 19:13, excerpt)

"And while he lingered, the men took hold of his hand, his wife’s hand, and the hands of his two daughters, the Lord being merciful to him, and they brought him out and set him outside the city." (Gen. 19:16, excerpt)

"Escape for your life! Do not look behind you nor stay anywhere in the plain. Escape to the mountains, lest you be destroyed." (Gen. 19:17, excerpt)

"But his wife looked back behind him, and she became a pillar of salt." (Gen. 19:26)

Let us pray: Lord, today’s passage is heavy. May You grant us to see our own position clearly: where we are sitting, what we are lingering over, and what we are looking back toward. We ask You in Your mercy to take hold of our hands and lead us out of dangerous places. In the Lord Jesus' name, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: The Man at the City Gate—Lot Was Too Close to Sodom (19:1–11)

First, look at Lot's good qualities. When the angels arrived at the city gate, Lot rose to meet them, bowed himself with his face toward the ground, strongly urged them to come into his house, prepared a feast, and baked unleavened bread—this is remarkably similar to Abraham's hospitality at Mamre; after all, he had learned at Abraham's side. Second Peter calls Lot "righteous Lot," saying he was "oppressed by the filthy conduct of the wicked" and that "dwelling among them, he tormented his righteous soul from day to day by seeing and hearing their lawless deeds" (2 Pet. 2:7–8, excerpt). So Lot was not an unbeliever; he was a believer. His problem was not a lack of faith, but that his faith had become disconnected from his position: his soul was tormented, yet he was sitting at the city gate.

What happened that night exposed Sodom to the core: the men of the city, from every quarter, both old and young, surrounded Lot's house, demanding that he bring out the guests so they could abuse them. Lot went out, shut the door behind him, and pleaded with them not to do so wickedly, even offering to hand over his two daughters—hearing these words today is shocking, and it shows precisely how deeply Lot's values had been corrupted by this city: he tried to counter one evil with another evil. Brothers and sisters, the environment shapes people far faster than we think. You think you live in the city to influence it, but after living there long enough, you discover that the city is redefining your bottom line.

The crowd mocked him in turn: "This one came in to stay here, and he keeps acting as a judge!" Their insult was biting, yet it laid bare the truth: Lot had lived in Sodom for so many years, but when push came to shove, the city still considered him an outsider. The position he had desperately tried to assimilate into had never truly accepted him. He wanted both sides: he wanted Abraham's God, and he also wanted the city of Sodom—and in the end, neither side regarded him as one of their own.

The angels pulled him into the house, shut the door, and struck the men at the door with blindness so that they became weary of trying to find the door. Notice this: the very first action of Lot's salvation was not something he did; it was that the angels pulled him inside. Our salvation is always, first and foremost, being pulled in.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part 2: The Lingering Man—Mercy Takes Hold of His Hand (19:12–22)

The angels laid it out plainly: The outcry against the city has grown great before the Lord, and we have come to destroy this place. Whoever else you have here, bring them all out. Lot went out in the night to speak to his sons-in-law, but to them, he seemed to be joking. This statement pierces the heart: in the eyes of his sons-in-law, this father-in-law normally lived no differently from them, so that when he spoke the truth, it was taken as a joke. Witnessing is something shaped by your daily life years before the critical moment arrives.

When the morning dawned, the angels urged Lot: "Arise, take your wife and your two daughters who are here, lest you be consumed in the punishment of the city." And then comes the most moving sentence in the entire chapter: "And while he lingered..."

To linger, in the original Hebrew *mahah* (H4102), means to dawdle, delay, or hesitate. Did Lot believe the angels' words? Yes, he believed; he even went to warn his sons-in-law. Then why did he linger? Because everything he had was in that city: his house, his property, his position, his sons-in-law, and a network of relationships built over half a lifetime. Knowing that one ought to leave and actually pulling one's feet out are two entirely different things.

What the Bible says next shows the face of grace: "the men took hold of his hand, his wife’s hand, and the hands of his two daughters, the Lord being merciful to him, and they brought him out and set him outside the city." Mercy, in the original Hebrew *chemlah* (H2553, from root *chamal*, H2550), means compassion, pity, tender affection, or having one's heart moved. What the angels took hold of was not their wrists, but their hands; they did not push them, but led them by the hand. Lot’s family of four was led out of the city by the hand. Brothers and sisters, this is the reality of salvation for many: it is not that we have such strong willpower that we run out of the city of sin on our own; it is that God cannot bear to let us go, and while we are still dawdling, He takes hold of our hands.

Once out of the city, the angel’s words took on the tone of a military command: “Escape for your life! Do not look behind you nor stay anywhere in the plain. Escape to the mountains.” Even at this moment, Lot was still bargaining: the mountains were too far, he feared disaster would overtake him, and he begged to flee to that small town, Zoar. God granted it, showing compassion even to this weakness: “See, I have favored you concerning this thing also, in that I will not overthrow this city for which you have spoken.” God’s mercy not only drags people out, but also leaves them a small town within reach. We often misunderstand God’s severity; in reality, throughout this chapter, every single one of God’s commands presses upon life, and every single one also preserves life.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The Pillar of Salt and the Watcher—Looking Back and Remembering (19:23–38)

By the time Lot reached Zoar, the sun had already risen. At that very moment, “the LORD rained brimstone and fire on Sodom and Gomorrah, from the LORD out of the heavens. So He overthrew those cities, all the plain, all the inhabitants of the cities, and what grew on the ground” (19:24–25, excerpt). The judgment was real, thorough, and left no room for bargaining—the “ten righteous people” Abraham had inquired about were nowhere to be found in the city; God’s mercy in dragging Lot’s family out was already the final concession of grace.

Then comes one of the shortest, coldest sentences in the entire Bible: “But his wife looked back behind him, and she became a pillar of salt.” A pillar of salt—in the original Hebrew, *netziv melach*; *netziv* (H5333) means a pillar or monument, and *melach* (H4417) means salt. Hers was not a mere curious glance—the Lord Jesus later quoted her with His own lips, saying: “Remember Lot’s wife. Whoever seeks to save his life will lose it” (Luke 17:32–33, excerpt). She looked back because her heart remained in the city. Her feet were fleeing for her life, but her heart was heading back to the city. The person had left Sodom, but Sodom had not left her.

Brothers and sisters, this is a warning to all who have “gone halfway.” Going halfway on the road of salvation is the most dangerous: having passed through the city gates, yet not having arrived at Zoar; having departed from the old life, yet being unable to let it go. Looking back takes only a second, but the cost is an entire lifetime. She became a pillar of salt, standing beside the road, becoming a signpost for every fugitive who followed: when God drags you out, do not betray that hand by looking back.

The camera turns to Abraham in the early morning. He “went early in the morning to the place where he had stood before the LORD. Then he looked toward Sodom and Gomorrah, and toward all the land of the plain; and he saw, and behold, the smoke of the land which went up like the smoke of a furnace” (19:27–28, excerpt). He had asked about fifty, and he had asked about ten; the plume of smoke he was watching told him there were not even ten in the city. He said nothing, nor did Scripture record his reaction. Yet the following verse is the core theme of the entire chapter: “And it came to pass, when God destroyed the cities of the plain, that God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out of the midst of the overthrow, when He overthrew the cities in which Lot had dwelt” (19:29, excerpt). Remembered—in the original Hebrew, *zakar* (H2142)—means to remember, be mindful of, and act in accordance with what is remembered. That Lot was saved was not because Lot was so deserving; Scripture’s stated reason is that God remembered Abraham—the one interceding from afar. Brothers and sisters, the tears you weep and the prayers you intercede from afar for your loved ones are recorded on God’s ledger. Lot himself did not know that his life was spared because someone on the mountain was standing before God on his behalf.

The chapter ends with Lot’s fate in the cave: his two daughters got their father drunk with wine, committed incest with him, and gave birth to Moab and Ben-Ammi, who became the ancestors of the Moabites and Ammonites. Scripture does not whitewash: if a person rescued from the city does not genuinely repent, Sodom will be reenacted even in a mountain cave. Leaving the city is only the beginning; only when the heart is saved is salvation truly attained.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

First, believers sitting at the city gate. You have faith, but your position is thoroughly soaked in the world, with your baseline dropping lower year by year. Today the question is not whether you believe, but where you are sitting.

Second, those who linger. You know it all; you understand every principle, yet you cannot lift your feet. Rather than waiting for God to seize your hand, stand up and walk today—mercy is grace, not a license to procrastinate.

Third, those whose witness has lost its effect. When you speak the truth, your family takes it as a joke. Do not lose heart; first examine the impression your daily life gives, and only then open your mouth; words spoken at crucial moments are built on the foundation of ordinary days.

Fourth, those who have gone halfway. Having left the city, do not look back. The old circle, old habits, and old relationships are calling you from behind; remember who that pillar of salt standing by the road is meant for.

Fifth, those who have interceded for loved ones for many years. Abraham looked upon smoke; you may be looking upon loved ones who haven't changed for years. Scripture says God remembers. Do not stop; your intercession is recorded on God’s ledger.

Sixth, those who have been warned once before. Being captured in Chapter 14 was Lot’s first warning. Has God given you a signal? A red mark on a medical exam, a phrase from a child, a sudden twinge of unease in your heart. Whoever has heard the warning sound once must take it seriously.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion and Response Prayer

In today’s chapter, a hand pulls people out of the burning city, and a pillar of salt sets a warning beside the road. God remembers those who intercede, and He shows mercy to those who linger, but God’s word is also clear: when escaping for your life, do not look back.

Let us pray together: Lord, we confess that among us, some are sitting at the city gate, some are lingering, and some keep looking back. We pray that You would seize our hands just as You seized Lot’s, and not let us perish through our own hesitation. We also pray that You would remember those who intercede for us, and enable us to become those who stand before You on behalf of others. Bring our hearts completely out of the old city, and establish them securely upon Your mountain. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## End-of-Chapter Notes

[1] *mahah*: Hebrew מָהַהּ (H4102), to linger, delay, tarry; Gen. 19:16 “And while he lingered”; lexical meanings according to BDB and HALOT.

[2] *chemlah*: Hebrew חֶמְלָה (H2553), mercy, compassion, pity, derived from the root *chamal* (חָמַל, H2550); used as a noun in the original text of Gen. 19:16 “the LORD being merciful to him”; lexical meanings according to BDB and HALOT.

[3] *netziv melach*: Hebrew נְצִיב (H5333, a pillar, monument) used in construct with מֶלַח (H4417, salt); Gen. 19:26 “became a pillar of salt”; lexical meanings according to BDB and HALOT.

[4] *zakar*: Hebrew זָכַר (H2142), to remember, be mindful of, and act accordingly; Gen. 19:29 “God remembered Abraham”; lexical meanings according to BDB and HALOT. Same word as in Gen. 8:1 “Then God remembered Noah,” previously used in the Gen. 8 sermon of this series; here it serves as the corresponding point of Abraham’s intercession being remembered.

[5] Regarding Lot as a righteous man: 2 Pet. 2:7–8 refers to “righteous Lot,” describing how his righteous soul was tormented daily by lawless deeds; the first part of this manuscript draws upon this to portray Lot as a believer whose “faith is disconnected from his position,” citing only in excerpt rather than quoting the full verse directly.

[6] Regarding Lot’s proposal to offer his daughters (19:8): This manuscript reads it as a narrative portrayal of “an environment reshaping one’s moral baseline,” not as a moral endorsement; commentators (such as Wenham) widely point out that this proposal reflects how Lot’s values had been eroded by Sodom, and the narrative nowhere commends this action. Noted here specifically to prevent misreading.

[7] Regarding Zoar: Gen. 19:20–22, Lot pleaded to flee to a small city, and God granted that He would not overthrow it; “Zoar” in the original Hebrew is *Tzo'ar* (H6820), meaning “smallness,” corresponding to Lot’s words, “Is it not a little one?”; geographical etymology according to BDB.

[8] Regarding the Lord Jesus quoting the pillar of salt: Luke 17:32–33 “Remember Lot’s wife”; this manuscript interprets “looking back” on this basis as the heart still clinging to the old state rather than a mere physical glance; noted here specifically as an exegetical reading based on the New Testament quotation.

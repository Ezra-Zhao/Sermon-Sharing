## Summary (about 100 words)

Esau was not the one who bore the covenant of promise. With his wives, his five sons, and all his livestock, he moved to Mount Seir and established his household there. Genesis uses a whole chapter to record his genealogy, the list of the Horites, the kings of Edom, and its chiefs. This is not a filler page; it is a ledger of grace: God did not choose him to carry the covenant, yet He preserved him, gave him a mountain and descendants, and commanded Israel not to seize a brother's possession.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

When we reach Genesis chapter 36, many people's eyes naturally slow down, and some even want to skip past it. Just before, Jacob returned home, Rachel died, and Isaac was buried; immediately after, Joseph will put on his coat of many colors, dream his dreams, and be sold by his brothers into Egypt. Right in the middle sits a whole chapter of names: who married whom, who fathered whom, who became a chief, which king succeeded which king. There is no miracle, no dialogue, not even one sentence spoken directly by God.

But please do not look down on this chapter. If the biblical author had only wanted to write about Jacob's branch, he could have dismissed it in one sentence: Esau lived in Mount Seir, that is, Edom. In fact, verse 8 says exactly that. But he did not stop there; he used 43 verses to write down the names of Esau's household one by one. The more detailed the list, the heavier its meaning: this man was not deleted from God's remembrance.

Let us read several passages of Scripture first:

"Now these are the generations of Esau, who is Edom. Esau took his wives of the daughters of Canaan; Adah the daughter of Elon the Hittite, and Aholibamah the daughter of Anah the daughter of Zibeon the Hivite; And Bashemath Ishmael's daughter, sister of Nebajoth." (Genesis 36:1-3, excerpt, KJV)

"And Esau took his wives, and his sons, and his daughters, and all the persons of his house, and his cattle, and all his beasts, and all his substance, which he had got in the land of Canaan; and went into the country from the face of his brother Jacob." (Genesis 36:6, excerpt, KJV)

"Thus dwelt Esau in mount Seir: Esau is Edom." (Genesis 36:8, KJV)

"And these are the kings that reigned in the land of Edom, before there reigned any king over the children of Israel." (Genesis 36:31, KJV)

And read one later command, which God spoke to Israel through Moses: "Meddle not with them; for I will not give you of their land, no, not so much as a foot breadth; because I have given mount Seir unto Esau for a possession." (Deuteronomy 2:5, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, we are used to staring only at the story of our own branch and counting only the grace we ourselves have received. Open our eyes today to see how You preserved the brother who was not the chosen one. Teach us not to envy another's possession and not to despise any name on the list, for behind every name stands a person whom You remember. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Three Wives and Five Sons — A Household's Days Are Recorded First (36:1-14)

This chapter does not begin with doctrine; it begins with household matters. "Now these are the generations of Esau, who is Edom." The name Edom takes us back to chapter 25. Esau came back from hunting, exhausted and hungry, and sold his birthright for a bowl of red stew. The text ties the name Edom to that red stew — "therefore was his name called Edom" (Genesis 25:30) — and sums up the same act separately: "thus Esau despised his birthright" (Genesis 25:34). The name came from the red stew; the despising was his attitude in the act. Red became one of the marks of his life. This name carries his weakness and one rash choice. The Bible did not rename him for him, and it did not tear that page out. But please note: God also did not, because of that one failure, write off the rest of his life in a single stroke. The name records his debt; the list that follows records the days God gave him. People easily define a person by his ugliest page alone; God's ledger is wider than man's.

First note how this chapter opens: "Now these are the generations of Esau, who is Edom." The whole Book of Genesis advances section by section under headings of this kind — the generations of this one and of that one all have a place in God's narrative. Note the ordering: the generations of Ishmael are recorded in Genesis chapter 25, recorded in full, and only then does the text return to Isaac's branch; here, the generations of Esau are recorded in chapter 36, recorded in full, and only then does chapter 37 formally enter the story of Joseph and Jacob's sons. That is to say, the Bible has a considerate habit: every time it is about to go on telling of the chosen branch, it first pauses, settles the account of the other branch, and records that branch's names in full, before walking on. This is not done in passing; it is respect. A branch is recorded in full before it is set down — not thrown away, but settled in place. If we read Scripture only chasing the main line, these passages will seem superfluous; if we understand God's heart, we will know: when God tells a story, He never treats any branch of people as a mere transition.

Then come the three wives. Adah, daughter of Elon the Hittite; Aholibamah, daughter of Anah the Hivite; Bashemath, daughter of Ishmael and sister of Nebajoth. None of these three was an idle outsider to the land of promise; each was connected to a family. Esau's early marriages had caused Isaac and Rebekah grief of heart, and his married life had taken detours — Genesis has already told us this earlier, and we need not whitewash it. But by chapter 36 the writing is very calm: no scolding, no praising, only the names of wives and sons arranged in order. The calm itself is an attitude — here the Bible is not a judgment seat but the table of a family register. A household living its days, bearing children, accumulating livestock — such ordinary things are also worth recording.

The five sons are clearly grouped by mother. Adah bore Eliphaz; Bashemath bore Reuel; Aholibamah bore Jeush, Jaalam, and Korah. Eliphaz's descendants in turn bore Teman, Omar, Zepho, Gatam, and Kenaz, and also Amalek — a name that would later become a heavy adversary on Israel's wilderness road. We will not expand on that today, only note one sentence: a genealogy never guarantees that descendants will walk the same road; a blessed ancestor may still have descendants who go astray, so the list must be read with reverence, not romanticized. Reuel's branch bore Nahath, Zerah, Shammah, and Mizzah. Timna, a concubine of Eliphaz, bore Amalek, and her status is also recorded. The Bible does not record only those born of the wives; it does not omit even a concubine's name. This is not an endorsement of the marriage customs of that time; it lets us see that when God records people, He does not omit people according to human rank.

Reading a genealogy requires learning to slow down. Modern readers read a list always looking for a plot, and when they find none they call it boring. Ancient readers read a genealogy for belonging: where do I come from, to whom do I belong, does anyone remember my name? For Esau, this list amounts to a declaration: although you are not on the main line of the covenant, your branch is not a nameless branch. Your wives have names, your sons have names, your grandsons have names. Brothers and sisters, we must learn this lesson in the church as well. Some co-workers are not on the platform, some families do not often appear, some people never do anything spectacular in their whole lives, and their names are rarely mentioned. But in God's register there is no nameless person. The names you cannot remember, God remembers.

There is also a detail in this part that is easily overlooked: the five sons were "all born in the land of Canaan." That is, Esau's next generation was born in the land of promise, and his household's start was also in this land. His later departure was not a story of being driven out; as the next part will tell, it was a separation made because the possessions were too great. Esau's life cannot be judged in one sentence: he despised his birthright, he caused his parents grief in marriage, and he also once opened his arms to welcome his returning brother, fell on his neck, kissed him, and wept. The tears of the brothers embracing in Genesis chapter 33 are real, and this long string of names in chapter 36 is also real. When God records a person, He records the whole person — not only the fall, and not only the one time he did right.

One more thing must be faced honestly: Esau's branch was not always at peace with Jacob's branch afterward. On the road of the exodus, the king of Edom once refused passage; in David's days the two branches went to war; on the day Jerusalem was broken into, Edom stood by and watched the spectacle. Those are later matters, accounts for the Book of Obadiah to settle. But note the order: God gave grace first; people sinned later; judgment follows sin, not birth. If we take the later conflicts and run them backward to claim that Esau's household was never preserved from the start, that is tampering with the ledger. A ledger must be read page by page: this page, chapter 36, is about preservation; on the later pages, what deserves rebuke is rebuked and what deserves judgment is judged — not one page fewer, and not one page early. Readers of Scripture who first learn this order will not easily write a person off wholesale.

We should treat people the same way. When one child in a family has gone far away, one relative has not been close for many years, or one brother's faith does not run on our line, we easily cross him out in our hearts first, too lazy even to mention his name at festivals. Chapter 36 stops us: God did not cross out Esau. If He had wanted to cross him out, this chapter would not exist at all. This chapter exists so that readers of Scripture in later generations would read the names of Esau's descendants aloud again, generation after generation. Reading a name aloud is the minimum of remembrance, and also the minimum of love — if I can still call your name, I have not yet cancelled you from my heart.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Livestock Were Too Many, So They Lived Apart — Mount Seir Is the Possession God Gave a Brother (36:6-30)

The second part begins with a migration. "And Esau took his wives, and his sons, and his daughters, and all the persons of his house, and his cattle, and all his beasts, and all his substance, which he had got in the land of Canaan; and went into the country from the face of his brother Jacob. For their riches were more than that they might dwell together; and the land wherein they were strangers could not bear them because of their cattle." This passage sounds familiar. Long ago Abram and Lot also had livestock too many to dwell together, so they separated in peace: Lot chose the plain, and Abram remained in Canaan. In the generation of Jacob and Esau, the same thing happened once more, except that this time the one who left was the elder brother, and the one who remained to inherit the promised land was the younger.

Please note the tone of the text: no quarrel, no expulsion, no one seizing another's things. Both households were too prosperous; the land could not bear them — a very practical problem for a herding family. Esau made a decision that made room for both households: he moved away. He moved to Mount Seir, that is, the land of Edom. Many people, when they preach Esau, always present him as a failure totally abandoned by God, as if his leaving Canaan were a matter of being driven into darkness. But Deuteronomy states this matter plainly: Mount Seir was given by God to Esau as a possession. When Israel came out of Egypt and walked through the wilderness, passing the border of Edom, God commanded them to buy food and water and not to strive with the Edomites; He would not give Israel even so much as a foot breadth of that land, because that land had already been given to the brother. Brothers and sisters, please weigh the weight of this sentence: the God who gave Canaan to Jacob's descendants personally gave Seir to Esau's descendants, and He guarded the boundary for Esau, not permitting the covenant branch to reach out and seize it.

Election is not monopoly. God chose Jacob in order to bring blessing to all families through his branch — that is the route of the covenant; but outside the route of the covenant, God's care did not stop. Esau had a mountain, water and pasture, livestock, descendants, and a territory of his own. The mistake Israel would later be most prone to make was to hear "God chose me" as "God loves only me, and everyone else is a foil." Deuteronomy blocks this road in advance: you shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother. A brother is a brother, even if the relationship between the two branches would later go up and down, and even if Edom in history also kicked Israel when it was down and was rebuked by the prophets — those accounts will be settled by the Book of Obadiah and the Book of Malachi. This chapter first lays the foundation straight: Edom's possession came from God's gift, and no one may seize it in God's name.

Deuteronomy chapter 2 also records the details: as Israel passed along Edom's border, they were to buy food and water from them with money, and even to ask passage politely. God even reminded Israel that they had lived long enough around Mount Seir with their brother and were not to take advantage of them. In the same chapter, God also mentions that He had given Ar to the Moabites as a possession, and had drawn boundaries for other peoples as well. In other words, Seir is not an isolated case — in God's governance, the distribution of land has never concerned Israel's clan alone. God is the God of all families, and the boundaries He gives to each people and each person are all within His arrangement. If the covenant people imagine that whatever those outside the covenant have received is all luck, all temporary keeping, all destined sooner or later to revert to themselves, they have read the Bible exactly backwards. The covenant is a trust, not a license to plunder. Israel paid tuition for this lesson later in history; reading it today, we ought to learn it first.

Living apart does not mean cutting off affection. Jacob and Esau later together buried their father Isaac, as recorded at the end of Genesis chapter 35. Two sons stood before their father's grave and together fulfilled a son's duty. Our families also have such moments: brothers and sisters have divided the household, live in different cities, and walk different roads in life, yet when a parents' matter comes, they must stand again beside the same table. The reminder of chapter 36 is this: separation is permitted, despising is not; living each one's own days is permitted, cancelling the other in one's heart is not. That the land could not bear both households' livestock is a problem of geography; that the heart cannot bear a brother is a problem of sin. Do not live a problem of geography into a problem of sin.

After the migration, the text lists Esau's descendants as chiefs, and inserts a section on the descendants of Seir the Horite. Seir's people were the original inhabitants of that land; the Horites lived in the mountains. The list includes Lotan, Shobal, Zibeon, Anah, Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan. Anah, son of Zibeon, is the father of Aholibamah mentioned earlier; the text even specially records what Anah found in the wilderness as he fed the asses. (For those reading in the KJV: Genesis 36:24 there reads that Anah "found the mules" in the wilderness; the Hebrew word has also been understood as "hot springs." Either way, the point stands.) A small matter — something found while pasturing donkeys — is also written into the Bible. This is the brushwork of genealogy: the scraps at the edge of great history are major matters in the household of the person concerned. The places where we yawn while reading a list are often precisely the whole of someone else's life. Learning to slow down before another person's list is a courtesy that a reader of Scripture ought to have.

The list of the Horites carries another layer of meaning: when Esau came to Seir, it was not empty land waiting for him; people already lived there, with their families, their chiefs, and their stories. Edom later established itself in this mountain land, intermarrying and succeeding the local people; the Bible does not detail fighting and killing in this history, but places the two groups of people side by side in lists. Deuteronomy says that God caused Esau's descendants to dispossess the Horites, "as Israel did unto the land of his possession, which the LORD gave unto them" (Deuteronomy 2:12) — reading this, we must be honest: the complexity of tribal succession in the ancient world cannot be adjudicated in one sermon today. What we can hold with certainty is the text's own emphasis: that Seir belonged to Esau as a possession was by God's arrangement, and Israel was later commanded to respect that arrangement. The emphasis falls on the restraint of the covenant people, not on the justification of seizure. Those who carry the promise must all the more keep their hands in check.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Kings Came First and the Chiefs Are Listed — The Ledger of Grace Must Be Read to the End (36:15-43)

The third part has three tables: the table of chiefs from Esau's descendants, the table of the kings of Edom, and the closing general table of chiefs. First, the chiefs. The word 'alluph refers to the head of a tribe or family, a leader of the rank of a captain of a thousand. From Eliphaz's branch came chief Teman, chief Omar, chief Zepho, chief Kenaz, chief Korah, chief Gatam, and chief Amalek; from Reuel's branch came chief Nahath, chief Zerah, chief Shammah, and chief Mizzah; from Aholibamah's branch came chief Jeush, chief Jaalam, and chief Korah. Reading through this one table, Esau's grandchildren were already organized: they had land, people, and leaders. The blessing God gives a person is often not for that person to enjoy alone; it lets his household stand firm generation after generation. In his youth Esau sold his birthright for a bowl of stew; who would have thought that his grandsons would become chiefs of a mountain land? Human rashness is real, and God's preservation is also real. Preservation is not indulgence — Esau paid a lifelong price for despising the birthright, and the main line of the promise truly did not run through his branch; but the price was paid, and life still had to go on, and God let his life go on, and go on prosperously. The proportion between these two is grace: it cancels neither the consequence nor the life.

Next, the kings. "And these are the kings that reigned in the land of Edom, before there reigned any king over the children of Israel." This sentence is very quiet, yet very startling. Edom had kings much earlier than Israel. Israel would have to wait until Saul and David to have a king of its own; Edom already lists eight kings in this chapter: Bela, Jobab, Husham, Hadad, Samlah, Saul, Baal-hanan, and Hadar. Read the list closely: only four of the eight are recorded with a father's name (Bela the son of Beor, Jobab the son of Zerah, Hadad the son of Bedad, and Baal-hanan the son of Achbor); the other four have no father named. Only three are given the name of a city (Bela's Dinhabah, Hadad's Avith, and the last king Hadar's Pau); the rest are identified by their homeland or place of origin. Reading closely, one notices a feature: these kings, unlike the house of David, were not succeeded from father to son; not one king was the son of the king before him, and they came from different cities and places. Scholars mostly conclude from this that the kings of Edom were more like leaders elected in turn by the cities than a hereditary dynasty. Whatever the details, what the text wants us to see is this: Edom had an order of governance very early — cities, kings, and the beginnings of a nation. The branch that was not chosen likewise had a history, and its history did not move slowly.

This section is a gentle reminder to Israel's readers, and a reminder to us: do not think that because God's work on your branch is slow, God is not working; and do not think that because another branch had kings first and prospered first, God is partial. Edom had kings first, and Israel had kings later, yet the promised Messiah in the end came from Jacob's branch. The two lines each have their own hour. The most hurtful thing about comparison is measuring one's own hour against another's hour: he established himself first, he married first, he had kings first — why is my branch still walking in the wilderness? Genesis chapter 36 places Edom's table of kings before Israel's table of kings, which amounts to answering this question in advance: another's being first is not your loss; for what God gave him, give thanks for him, and what God has given you, when its hour comes, no one can snatch away.

That sentence in Malachi, "Yet I loved Jacob, and I hated Esau," has always made readers' hearts tighten when they read it, and it must be handled carefully. The context of that sentence is the prophet rebuking Edom's later pride and cruelty toward its brother, declaring God's judgment on Edom's sin, and at the same time showing Israel the firmness of His electing love; it does not ask us to go back and deny this chapter — to deny how God once preserved Esau and gave him a possession. The Book of Obadiah rebukes Edom likewise because Edom stood aloof and gloated on the day Jerusalem suffered calamity. In other words, the later judgment targets concrete sins; it is not a revocation of this chapter's grace. If a family that has received grace later becomes proud, or harms its brother, it will still be disciplined — was Israel itself not the same? Reading the Scripture whole sets our hearts straight: we neither use words of judgment to despise any branch of people, nor use a past of having received grace as a talisman for sinning.

There are also a few small footnotes in the king list that deserve a slow reading. When Bela reigned, the list records his city as Dinhabah; Hadad smote Midian in the field of Moab, and his city was called Avith; Baal-hanan was the son of Achbor; and of the last king Hadar's wife Mehetabel, even her mother's name, Matred, is recorded, and her grandmother Mezahab's besides. Not one of these footnotes is wasted: a capital city means a center of governance; a war means this nation faced real hostility; even a queen's family line is recorded, which shows that in the eyes of God's historian, Edom's royal house was written as real history, not as shadows in a fable. When Israel later read this table, they ought to have understood: your brother, on the other side of the mountain, has already lived more than a hundred years of days with kings — with cities, defenses, and family lines. Do not despise them, and do not pretend they do not exist. The more solidly history is written, the more unavoidable the word "brother" becomes.

Last is the general table of chiefs. Verses 40 to 43 list the chiefs of Edom again by family and by dwelling place, and the closing sentence gathers up the whole chapter: "he is Esau the father of the Edomites." Father — this word carries weight in Genesis. Abraham is the father of Israel, Ishmael has his twelve princes, and here Esau is called the father of the Edomites. A chapter of names, beginning with wives and sons and ending with kings and chiefs, lands at last on the words "the father." The position the Bible gives Esau is not failure, not foil, but the father of a nation. This is what a ledger of grace looks like: stroke by stroke, all of them names; and when the account is settled at the end, it is not a loss, but a nation standing firm.

Putting the three parts together, today's application has only three items, yet each lands in daily life. First, stop comparing. If you keep staring at the mountain of your brother's branch, the kings of that branch, the prosperity of that branch, your own days become impossible to live. Edom has Seir and Jacob has Canaan; the possessions God gives each person differ, and gratitude is the best antidote. Second, respect the possession God gave others. A colleague's clients, a brother's gifts, a relative's circumstances — these are not your examination hall, still less your hunting ground. Deuteronomy would not permit seizing even a foot breadth of land; how much more should we stop trying to plant a foot in another's territory at every turn. Third, family harmony must come early. Jacob and Esau once wept on each other's necks and together buried their father; yet their descendants had times of drifting further and further apart, and Obadiah's rebuke is the later echo. The lesson of reconciliation learned by one generation must be learned again by the next; if our generation first calls the names back, eats the meals together, and fulfills its duty at the grave, that is the most solid inheritance we leave our children.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have read a whole chapter of names to the end. At the head of the list stands a man who sold his birthright for red stew; at the end of the list, he is the father of a nation. In between there is not one sentence spoken directly by God, yet the whole page is full of things God has done: He gave wives, gave sons, gave livestock, gave a mountain land, gave chiefs, gave kings. The one who was not chosen — God did not abandon him.

Brothers and sisters, perhaps today you sit in Esau's seat: the main line of the covenant seems not to run through your branch, the stage lights seem not to fall on you, and the most valued person in the family is not you. Please hear this chapter well: God's election has His sovereignty, and God's preservation has His faithfulness. That you are not on that line does not mean you are not in His heart. The mountain He has prepared for you — go and live in it well; and your name, written on His ledger, will not have a single character blotted out.

Before you walk out of this sanctuary, leave yourself one small assignment: think of a person whose name you have already crossed out in your heart — perhaps a brother you have not spoken to for years, perhaps a child who has gone far away, perhaps another branch of relatives you rarely contact. This week, call his name back first: one phone call, one meal, one greeting; there is no need for big speeches. The first step Genesis uses a whole chapter to teach us is nothing else but this: do not delete the name. While the name is still there, the relationship still has a road back; we take this step first, and leave the rest to God.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You; Your ledger is wider than our hearts. We give thanks for the promise our own branch has received, and we also learn to give thanks for the possession our brother's branch has received. Remove the comparison and jealousy within us, teach us to respect the boundaries You have given each person, to be reconciled first at home, and to give thanks first before people. May we and our descendants all be in Your remembrance, with not one left out. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Esau took his wives, and his sons, and his daughters, and all the persons of his house, and his cattle, and all his beasts, and all his substance, which he had got in the land of Canaan; and went into the country from the face of his brother Jacob. For their riches were more than that they might dwell together; and the land wherein they were strangers could not bear them because of their cattle. Thus dwelt Esau in mount Seir: Esau is Edom." (Genesis 36:6-8, excerpt, KJV)

Genesis is willing to spend a whole chapter on a man who was not the chosen one. Esau did not inherit the covenant, and his descendants were not on the main line of the promise, yet God gave him a mountain, descendants, and kings, and He commanded the covenant line: do not strive with him, for that land is his possession. Our hearts are often narrow: we give thanks for what God gave our branch, and feel sour over what He gave our brother's branch. But grace is not a loaf that runs out when it is divided; what God gave him will not take away what is yours. Learn a new lesson today: give thanks that the other branch is kept by God, and say thank You for another's mountain and another's prosperity. The moment comparison stops, your own life begins to widen. On God's ledger, your name and his name are written in the same book. If today your heart aches over "why him and not me," do not rush to pass a verdict on your own life. Esau's story reminds us that God's arrangements have their order and their different boundaries; that He did not take your line does not mean He has prepared no place for you. Draw your eyes back from your brother's mountain, and quietly move into the portion God has given you; in time, on your own list too, you will see a string of names that have been preserved.

# The Eighth Beatitude: Those Persecuted for Righteousness' Sake

## I. The Scripture

"Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, For theirs is the kingdom of heaven." (Matt. 5:10, NKJV)

As the Beatitudes reach the final beatitude, the tone suddenly hardens. Before, it spoke of poverty in spirit, of mourning, of meekness, of hunger and thirst, of mercy, of purity of heart, of making peace — people listening still felt warmth in their hearts. At this beatitude, Jesus places a word before the disciples: persecution. Not if, but something that will be encountered sooner or later. He did not wait for the disciples to go and experience it first and then come back to comfort them; at the very beginning He spoke the words in advance: those who walk this road will be misunderstood, will be excluded, and will sometimes pay a very real cost. And in this very sentence, He used the weightiest word in the Beatitudes — blessed.

This beatitude is the most easily misread. One reading treats suffering itself as spiritual: whoever suffers more grievance is nearer to God. The opposite reading thinks that having believed in the Lord, everything should go smoothly, and if one truly encounters exclusion and loss, one suspects whether one has believed wrongly, or whether God is no longer there. Jesus takes neither. He speaks very precisely: persecuted for righteousness' sake. Not for one's own temper, not for one's own stubbornness, not for one's own wrongdoing being pointed out, but because one stands on the side of righteousness, because one lives like Him, because one refuses to call what is wrong right — only then is one pushed outside by this world. Of such people, Jesus Himself says, they are blessed.

There is another matter to see clearly first: the promise of this beatitude is word-for-word identical to that of the First Beatitude. The First Beatitude says, theirs is the kingdom of heaven; the Eighth Beatitude also says, theirs is the kingdom of heaven. The Beatitudes are like an arch, its two ends resting on the same place. The poor in spirit enter through the door; those persecuted for righteousness' sake walk to the end, and discover they have obtained the same thing: the kingdom of heaven. In other words, from beginning to end, this road is a road on which the kingdom of God holds a person — entering is grace, and walking to the point of being beaten is still grace.

## II. Exegesis of the Original Text

The Greek text: Μακάριοι οἱ δεδιωγμένοι ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.

**1. "Those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake" (οἱ δεδιωγμένοι ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης, hoi dediōgmenoi heneken dikaiosynēs)**

First look at the word "persecuted." δεδιωγμένοι (dediōgmenoi) is the perfect passive participle of the verb διώκω (diōkō, Strong G1377). The original meaning of this verb is to pursue, to drive away, to chase relentlessly; used of prey, it is chasing; used of people, in the New Testament it mostly turns into persecuting, harassing. What Paul did before he believed in the Lord uses precisely this word: he "persecuted" the church (cf. 1 Cor. 15:9; Phil. 3:6, in substance in the NKJV) — the kind of chasing that went house to house to pursue and arrest.

The tense of the participle deserves attention. The perfect tense speaks of something that has already happened and whose effect still remains: they are people who have already been pursued, and that mark is still on them. Jesus was not speaking of a hypothetical danger; He was speaking to a group of people who would truly pay a cost. The passive voice is likewise important, as with the passives in the preceding beatitudes: persecution is not something disciples go looking for; it falls upon them. The Bible never encourages people to seek suffering for themselves. Martyrdom is not a performance, and persecution is not an office for applying for medals. The disciple's duty is faithfulness, not provoking trouble; if cost arrives, that is the shadow of faithfulness, not the goal of faithfulness.

Then look at those words: "for righteousness' sake." ἕνεκεν (heneken, Strong G1752) means "because of, for the sake of," followed by δικαιοσύνης (dikaiosynēs), the genitive of δικαιοσύνη (dikaiosynē, Strong G1343). In the Fourth Beatitude we have already seen this word: the "righteousness" of hungering and thirsting for righteousness speaks both of God's righteousness, what God counts as right, and of a person being justified before God and living out what that justification ought to look like. By the Eighth Beatitude, this word has changed from something longed for in the heart into something to be held at cost. The Fourth Beatitude is hunger; the Eighth Beatitude is holding — a person who has hungered to a certain point will finally be recognized in places outside the dining table: some meals he does not eat, some words he does not say, some money he does not take, some sides he does not stand on. Persecution often begins from these "refusals."

So the boundary of this beatitude must be drawn clearly. Being opposed and excluded for different reasons makes a world of difference. Peter later stated this line most bluntly: you must not suffer as evildoers; if, suffering for doing good, you endure, this is commendable before God; if you suffer for righteousness' sake, you are blessed (cf. 1 Pet. 2:20; 3:14, in substance). Offending people for one's own pride, being avoided for one's own rudeness, being held accountable for problems with one's own integrity — that is called consequence, not persecution. Attaching every grievance to this beatitude is disrespectful to those who truly suffer for righteousness. Conversely, those who truly lose opportunities, lose positions, lose relationships because they do not lie, do not falsify accounts, do not participate in oppression, and do not retreat in faith — they need not doubt themselves; they are standing precisely inside this verse.

There is a further layer: the One to whom "righteousness" ultimately points. As Matthew writes later, it is clear that disciples are persecuted, at root, "for My sake," "for Christ's sake" (Matt. 5:11; 10:22; 24:9, in substance). Righteousness is not a set of abstract standards; righteousness is the form that became flesh in Jesus. The world persecutes disciples, on the surface because disciples do not fit in; at the bone, because disciples resemble Him. In John's Gospel, Jesus made this explicit: "If the world hates you, you know that it hated Me before it hated you. If you were of the world, the world would love its own" (John 15:18–19, NKJV, in substance). Being persecuted for righteousness' sake is, in the end, the friction that those who acknowledge the Lord inevitably encounter in the world for "not being like the world."

The rendering of this verse in the Lü Zhenzhong Translation is broadly close in meaning to the Chinese Union Version; both rest on "persecuted for righteousness' sake" and "theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

English versions for comparison:
- KJV: "Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."
- NIV: "Blessed are those who are persecuted because of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."
- NRSV: "Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

The three versions differ very little at this verse. Worth mentioning is the "for righteousness' sake" of the KJV and NRSV; the word sake states "for the sake of …" very heavily: not incidentally being swept in, but being targeted precisely because of righteousness itself. The Chinese Union Version's "为义" is concise; when reading, this weight must be read out.

**2. "Theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν, autōn estin hē basileia tōn ouranōn)**

"Kingdom of heaven" is ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν (hē basileia tōn ouranōn). βασιλεία (basileia, Strong G932) is kingdom, kingship, rule; τῶν οὐρανῶν is the plural genitive of οὐρανός (ouranos, Strong G3772, heaven, sky), "of the heavens." Matthew likes to use "kingdom of heaven"; Mark and Luke mostly use "kingdom of God"; they speak of the same matter: the place where God is King, the sphere where God's will passes unhindered.

The verb is ἐστιν (estin), present tense — "is," not "shall be." This tense is used in the Beatitudes only in the First and Eighth Beatitudes; the promises of the other beatitudes are mostly future tense: "they shall be comforted," "they shall inherit the earth," "they shall be filled," "they shall obtain mercy," "they shall see God," "they shall be called sons of God." Only in the first and last beatitudes does Jesus place the kingdom of heaven in the present: it is theirs now. The person who enters poor in spirit is now already in the kingdom; the person beaten for righteousness has also not been driven out of the kingdom — on the contrary, at that moment, his evidence of being in the kingdom is clearest. Persecution can take many things from a person: reputation, opportunity, stability, sometimes even freedom and life; it alone cannot take this one thing, because the kingdom of heaven is not a reward given by people, but a belonging God acknowledges now.

The word "theirs" (αὐτῶν, autōn) corresponds front to back seamlessly: the αὐτῶν of the First Beatitude and the αὐτῶν of the Eighth Beatitude are the same group of people. It is not two kinds of people obtaining two kinds of blessing, but one kind of person at the two ends of one road, held by the same promise. So when reading the Beatitudes, do not read them as eight grades, eight electives. The Beatitudes are one complete portrait: a person who from the beginning acknowledges that he is poor in spirit, walking all the way to the end, will discover that the world does not always welcome such a person; but from the first step to the last step, he has never left the kingdom of heaven.

The text does not stop at verse 10. Matthew immediately records 5:11–12, where Jesus turns His words from "they" to "you," as if turning from discourse to face-to-face: "Blessed are you when they revile and persecute you, and say all kinds of evil against you falsely for My sake. Rejoice and be exceedingly glad, for great is your reward in heaven, for so they persecuted the prophets who were before you." (NKJV) This final sentence is comfort, and also positioning: you are not the first, nor are you alone. From prophets to apostles, there have always been people on this road. Persecuted disciples stand in a long line, and at the very front of the line is Jesus Himself.

## III. The Place and Logic of This Beatitude in the Sermon on the Mount

The Eighth Beatitude is the closing of the Beatitudes, and also the threshold of the whole Sermon on the Mount.

First look at its place within the Beatitudes. The first four beatitudes mostly speak of what a person is like coming before God: poor in spirit, mourning for sin, meek, hungering and thirsting for righteousness. Among the last four beatitudes, showing mercy and making peace speak of how such a person acts before people; purity of heart speaks of the root of his standing before God — not a move or two in treating people, but whether the source is clean. Reaching the Eighth Beatitude, the cost such a person must pay in the world is laid out: being persecuted for righteousness' sake. This order is honest almost to cruelty. Jesus did not depict the disciples' outcome as "you live so well, everyone will like you." On the contrary: mercy will offend vested interests, purity of heart will expose others' double-mindedness, making peace will offend both fighting sides at once. For a person who truly lives out the first seven beatitudes, the Eighth Beatitude is almost an unavoidable conclusion. It is not that the world is especially bad; it is that when light enters darkness, darkness necessarily reacts.

Then look at its correspondence, first to last, with the First Beatitude, already mentioned above: both are "theirs is the kingdom of heaven," both present tense. This is Matthew's deliberate arrangement, making the reader understand that the Beatitudes are not eight scattered sentences, but a closed loop. The starting point is grace, the end point is also grace; all the tears, hunger, mercy, and purity of heart in between are inside this closed loop. If the Eighth Beatitude were removed, the Beatitudes would seem too comfortable, as if piety were only an inward matter; with the Eighth Beatitude, the whole series lands on the ground: the citizens of the kingdom of heaven living on earth have friction costs.

Then look at its unfolding into what follows in the Sermon on the Mount. Immediately following the Beatitudes is salt and light (Matt. 5:13–16). The order is not confused: first, those persecuted are blessed, and only then, you are the salt of the earth, you are the light of the world. Salt put into food changes the flavor, and will also be "surrounded" by the food; light shining into darkness, darkness will not applaud. Jesus then warns that if salt loses its flavor, it is only to be thrown out and trampled underfoot by men — that is precisely the outcome of fearing persecution and retreating to preserve safety. In other words, the Eighth Beatitude and the salt-and-light passage are two sides of one body: persisting in being salt and light means preparing to pay the cost of the Eighth Beatitude; evading the cost of the Eighth Beatitude, the salt becomes bland and the light is hidden. The warnings later in the sermon about prayer and fasting being seen by people (Matt. 6:1–18, in substance), the parables of the narrow gate, false prophets, and the two houses in wind and rain (Matt. 7:13–27), can all be read back under this beatitude: the road of the kingdom of heaven was not a broad road from the beginning; having finished the Beatitudes, Jesus calculated the account of walking the narrow road to the disciples, item by item.

For this reason, the Eighth Beatitude is the beatitude that tests the authenticity of the first seven. In smooth circumstances, poverty of spirit and meekness are not easily tested; when cost arrives, what material a person is made of is shown at once. Is it losing an opportunity and still refusing to tell a falsehood, or "flexibly" selling righteousness once; is it still showing mercy when misunderstood, or immediately repaying tooth for tooth — the Eighth Beatitude is not an extra exam question; it is the final exam of the first seven beatitudes.

## IV. Cross References

- Ps. 44:22 — "Yet for Your sake we are killed all day long; We are accounted as sheep for the slaughter." (NKJV) The psalmist cries out to God in innocent suffering: we have not forsaken You, yet we encounter such things. Paul quotes this sentence in Romans 8:36, placing it in the long question, "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" The righteous of the Old Testament had already walked this road long ago; suffering does not equal being forsaken by God.
- Isa. 53 — Isaiah speaks of that suffering Servant: He was despised and rejected by men, a Man of sorrows and acquainted with grief; He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities (in substance). The One in the prophet's pen was later Jesus' own road, and also the deepest comfort when disciples are persecuted: what we suffer, He suffered first; when He suffered He opened not His mouth, and when we suffer we are not alone.
- Jer. 20:7–13 — Jeremiah, because he proclaimed words that were not pleasing to hear, constantly suffered derision and mockery; even close friends watched for his stumbling. The prophet even complained to God that he had become a laughingstock. But in the same chapter he also said that God was with him as a mighty, awesome One. Being persecuted for righteousness' sake was never a matter that only appeared in the New Testament; the company of the prophets walked in just this way — the sentence in Matt. 5:12, "for so they persecuted the prophets who were before you," refers to these people.
- Matt. 5:11–12 — The direct unfolding of this beatitude, already discussed in the original-text exegesis: persecution, reviling, falsely saying all kinds of evil — Jesus lists them one by one, and tells the disciples to rejoice and be exceedingly glad, because their reward in heaven is great. The "rejoicing" here is not forced smiling; the joy in the original is built on reward and belonging, not built on feelings.
- John 15:18–21; 16:33 — In the farewell discourse, Jesus restates: a servant is not greater than his master; if they persecuted Me, they will also persecute you; in the world there will be tribulation, but in Him there is peace; He has overcome the world. Persecution was spoken of beforehand, and peace was also promised beforehand; both were delivered together into the disciples' hands.
- Acts 5:41 — After the apostles were beaten by the council, they departed, rejoicing "that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for His name" (NKJV, in substance). This is the earliest living annotation of the Eighth Beatitude: shame, in the eyes of the world, is disgrace; in their eyes, it was being counted worthy — suffering shame for this name itself became a mark of being claimed by the kingdom of heaven.
- Rom. 8:17–18, 35–39 — If we are heirs with Christ, we shall also suffer with Him, and be glorified together; Paul also says that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us (in substance). He lists tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, one by one, asking through them, and the final conclusion is: all these cannot separate us from the love of God.
- Phil. 1:29 — Paul says to the Philippian church, it has been granted to you, not only to believe in Christ, but also to suffer for His sake (in substance, NKJV: "For to you it has been granted on behalf of Christ, not only to believe in Him, but also to suffer for His sake."). "Granted" and "suffering" in Paul's pen are two sides of the same grace. This sentence speaks the Eighth Beatitude to the bottom: suffering for righteousness is not the absence of grace; it is grace in another face.
- 2 Tim. 3:12 — "Yes, and all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution." (NKJV) Paul speaks calmly, without exception clauses. Reading this sentence together with Matt. 5:10 can stop much self-pity, and also stop much surprise: godliness walking together with persecution is the Bible's consistent expectation, not an accident that only happens when one believes badly.
- 1 Pet. 3:14; 4:12–16 — Peter writes to churches scattered everywhere and undergoing trial: even if you suffer for righteousness' sake, you are blessed; when a fiery trial comes, do not think it strange; if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed, but let him glorify God in this matter (in substance). First Peter can almost be counted as a footnote to the Eighth Beatitude; those he writes to are precisely people living inside this beatitude.
- Rev. 2:10 — The risen Lord says to the church in Smyrna: do not fear any of those things which you are about to suffer; be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life (in substance, NKJV: "Be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life."). Revelation opens the end point of the Eighth Beatitude: the extreme of persecution is death, the extreme of reward is the crown of life. The crown is not compensation; it is the mark on the head of the overcomer.

## V. Life Application

**Scene One: In the workplace, paying small costs for those "refusals."** The persecution most people encounter today is not at the execution ground, but in conference rooms and at dining tables. Asking you to tamper a little with data, asking you to say a sentence before a client that you know is not true, asking you to laugh along when the team excludes someone, asking you to choose between weekend service and a promotion drinking party — most of the time, no one forces you with a knife; they only open the door a little smaller, give the opportunity to someone else first, and lightly place evaluations like "not fitting in" and "too serious" upon you. The characteristic of this kind of persecution is: the cost is neither large nor small, just large enough to make you want to step back. The lesson of the Eighth Beatitude here is to regard every "refusal" as being claimed, not as bad luck. That one time you refused to falsify, that one time you refused to go with the flow — what you lost is visible; what you held is that present-tense sentence, "theirs is the kingdom of heaven." One must also remind oneself: persistence in the workplace must be clean; do not package ability problems and interpersonal problems all as "being persecuted for righteousness' sake." If accounts were done wrongly and you are criticized, that must be admitted; if work was done badly and you are not used, that must be changed. Only when the loss was truly because you refused to cross the boundary of righteousness does it fall into this beatitude. The more honest the boundary, the more powerful the persistence.

**Scene Two: Among family and friends, being cold-shouldered and misunderstood because of faith.** There is a kind of persecution with no opponent, only the silence of a tableful of people. After believing in the Lord, some dinner gatherings you cannot attend, some jokes you cannot laugh at, some family matters — burning incense, fortune-telling, banquet customs — you cannot participate in. At first family members persuade, later they turn cold, still later comes that half-true, half-false sentence at New Year and festivals: "He alone is lofty and pure." This pain is not intense, but it is long, so long that you will doubt whether, for these "small matters," making relationships stiff is worth it. Jesus, in Matt. 10:34–39, speaking of the division He came to bring, and in Luke 14:26–27, speaking of the cost of bearing the cross and following Him (both in substance), speaks of precisely this tension that first arises at home. In application, two things must be done at the same time. First, the standpoint must not soften: hold the boundaries that should be held, do not be vague at the root of faith; vagueness in exchange for harmony does not last long, and will also make family members mistakenly think your faith is only an interest. Second, the posture must be softer still: in places outside the boundary, be doubly filial, doubly helpful, doubly present. What many family members oppose is in fact not Jesus, but their thought that a person who believes in the Lord will abandon this family. Answering with ten years of filial devotion and responsibility, as consistent as one day, is weightier than a hundred arguments. If persecution comes from those dearest, mercy and purity of heart — the Fifth Beatitude, the Sixth Beatitude — are the way you respond to it. The Eighth Beatitude never calls people to clash head-on; it calls people, having paid the cost, still to be like Christ.

**Scene Three: For churches persecuted far away — pray, remember, do not grow numb.** For those of us who believe in the Lord in this generation, our phones carry every day news of churches around the world being demolished, believers imprisoned, families torn apart. Seeing much of it, people grow numb, swipe past and count it done. The Eighth Beatitude places these people before us, not to make us blame ourselves for living too comfortably, but to make us acknowledge kin: they are the "they" inside "theirs," belonging with us to the same kingdom of heaven. There are in fact not a few concrete things that can be done: remember names in prayer — not generally saying "pray for persecuted churches," but remembering one or two concrete churches, concrete names, and praying for a long time; in giving, set aside a portion for ministries that aid persecuted churches, checking the channel clearly before giving; in one's own church, tell these testimonies to the children, letting the next generation know that in many places of the world, faith costs the price of life. There is another matter for ourselves: do not waste comfortable days. What brothers and sisters in persecution most often ask is not our sympathy, but that we, while it is day, accomplish the things they wish to do but cannot do — preaching the gospel, teaching, worshipping, serving. If we are lazy in freedom, we truly fail this beatitude.

## VI. Response Prayer

Lord, You said that those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake are blessed, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Reading this sentence, my heart is both comforted and dares not treat it lightly. You did not deceive us by saying that following You would be smooth sailing; from the beginning You calculated the cost for us to hear, and from the beginning You placed the kingdom of heaven in our hands.

Please preserve me, to stand firm first before small costs. Let me, in the conference room, at the dining table, in the silence of family members who do not understand, neither sell righteousness nor discard love. When I should say no, give me courage to say it clearly; when I am misunderstood, give me purity of heart, without hurrying to prove myself; when I have paid a cost and my heart turns bitter, let me remember that I am not the first to walk this road — the prophets walked it, the apostles walked it, You Yourself walked it first.

I also pray for those brothers and sisters far away who are now suffering for You. Many of them I do not even know by name, yet You know every one of them. Please be with them in prison, in interrogation, on the road of losing their homes, as You have promised. Let them, in the darkest time, hear that Your sentence is true: theirs is the kingdom of heaven. No one can snatch this sentence from their hands.

On that day, we shall meet in the same kingdom. Please make me faithful to the end, without retreating. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us and rose again, I pray, Amen.

## Summary (approx. 100 words)

The LORD visited Sarah at the appointed time, and she conceived and bore Abraham a son; Abraham named him Isaac and circumcised him on the eighth day. Sarah said: "God has made me laugh, and all who hear will laugh with me." On the day Isaac was weaned, a great feast was made; Sarah saw the son of Hagar scoffing and demanded that the bondwoman and her son be cast out. Abraham was grieved, but God consented and personally comforted him: in Isaac your seed shall be called, and the son of the bondwoman shall also become a nation. Hagar wandered with the boy in the wilderness of Beersheba; when the water was gone, the boy wept. God heard the voice of the lad and opened Hagar's eyes to see a well of water. Later, Abraham and Abimelech made a covenant at Beersheba, where Abraham planted a tamarisk tree and called on the name of the LORD, the Everlasting God. This sermon examines three things: when the promise is fulfilled, first remember the origin of laughter; even a blessed household can face painful separations; what God hears is the voice of the lad, not the arrangements of man.

---

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you all, brothers and sisters!

Twenty-five years ago, God said to Abram: "Get out of your country, from your family and from your father's house." Today, the most difficult part of that promise—the son upon whom "I will make you a great nation" hung—has been born. In the very first verse of Chapter 21, the biblical wording is remarkably steadfast: "And the LORD visited Sarah as He had said, and the LORD did for Sarah as He had spoken." "As He had said," "as He had spoken"—what was waited on for twenty-five years was not an empty word of comfort, but a covenant-keeping God.

But notice the structure of this chapter: the account of the birth takes up only a few short verses; the rest of the chapter, by far the greater part, is about another departure. At Isaac's weaning feast, the tents were filled with laughter; yet at that very same feast, Sarah saw Ishmael and demanded that Hagar and her son be cast out. This household, where the promise had just been fulfilled, had first to go through a painful purging.

Let us first read the key passages of Scripture:

"And the LORD visited Sarah as He had said, and the LORD did for Sarah as He had spoken. For Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age, at the set time of which God had spoken to him." (Gen. 21:1–2, excerpted)

"And Sarah said, 'God has made me laugh, and all who hear will laugh with me.'" (Gen. 21:6)

"But God said to Abraham, 'Do not let it be displeasing in your sight because of the lad or because of your bondwoman. Whatever Sarah has said to you, listen to her voice; for in Isaac your seed shall be called. Yet I will also make a nation of the son of the bondwoman, because he is your seed.'" (Gen. 21:12–13, excerpted)

"And God heard the voice of the lad. Then the angel of God called to Hagar out of heaven, and said to her, 'What ails you, Hagar? Fear not, for God has heard the voice of the lad where he is.'" (Gen. 21:17, excerpted)

Let us pray: Lord, You are the God who fulfills promises at the appointed time. Today, grant that in the midst of laughter we do not forget Your faithfulness, and in the midst of tears we may hear the voice that You hear. Please shepherd us in this service Yourself. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: Laughter at the Appointed Time—The Visitation, the Naming, and That Spoken Word (21:1–8)

First, consider "visited." The original Hebrew word is *paqad* (H6485), meaning to visit, take account of, or act according to one's remembrance and care. In Genesis 18, God said, "I will certainly return to you according to the time of life"—and as Chapter 21 opens, the appointed time has arrived, and He indeed returns. Nor does He return empty-handed: Sarah conceives and bears a son. God's visitation is not a mere polite greeting; it is a visitation accompanied by fulfillment. Brothers and sisters, regarding that matter for which you have prayed for years without a sign of movement, God has not forgotten the date; His appointed time simply has not yet arrived. When it comes, not a single word—even what was spoken a year ago at the tent door—will fall to the ground.

"And Abraham called the name of his son who was born to him—whom Sarah bore to him—Isaac." Isaac, *Yitzchaq* (H3327), means "he laughs." This name was designated beforehand by God in Genesis 17. Laughter is the final reckoning of this family's twenty-five years: Abraham had fallen on his face and laughed, Sarah had laughed secretly behind the tent flap, and now Sarah holds the child and declares openly: "God has made me laugh, and all who hear will laugh with me." That secret laugh before was a laugh of little faith; this laugh now is a laugh of victory. It is the very same root word, *tzachaq* (H6711)—God did not change the word, but God transformed the heart of the one laughing. What is more, this laughter is contagious: "All who hear will laugh with me." Joy that truly comes from God is never hoarded in secret; when one family receives the promised blessing, the whole street shares in the light.

On the eighth day, Abraham circumcised Isaac as God had commanded him. Notice the order: as soon as the child of promise was born, he first received the sign of the covenant. A blessed child is not pampered outside the covenant; he is raised within the covenant. When the child was weaned, Abraham made a great feast—weaning occurred around three years of age at that time, marking that the child had survived the most perilous infant years. Laughter, feast, covenant sign—the scene this household had waited twenty-five years for was now complete.

Yet here lies the honesty of Scripture: before the feast had even ended, trouble arrived. Being blessed is not the end of trouble; sometimes, trouble merely puts on a different face and begins on the very day of blessing.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part 2: Farewell at the Feast—Hagar's Second Departure (21:9–21)

"And Sarah saw the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had borne to Abraham, scoffing. Therefore she said to Abraham, 'Cast out this bondwoman and her son; for the son of this bondwoman shall not be heir with my son, namely with Isaac.'" (21:9–10, excerpted). The word "scoffing" (*tsachaq*) shares the same root as "Isaac"—it refers to laughing in jest, mocking, or playing. Paul, looking back on this event in Galatians, interpreted it with the word "persecuted" (Gal. 4:29). Sarah's words sound heartless, and there is no need for us to gloss over them—sending away a woman who had borne the family's firstborn and had lived there for well over a decade was by no means a warm or gentle act. Yet God's response was unexpected: He did not rebuke Sarah, but instead said to the distressed Abraham, "Whatever Sarah has said to you, listen to her voice; for in Isaac your seed shall be called."

God was not endorsing human callousness; God was drawing the boundary line of promise: the transmission of the covenant was to run exclusively through the line of Isaac, without ambiguity. Yet at the same time, God clearly laid out the other line as well: "Yet I will also make a nation of the son of the bondwoman, because he is your seed." Boundaries remain boundaries, and grace remains grace: Ishmael was not within the covenant line, yet he was within God's blessing.

Abraham rose early in the morning, took bread and a skin of water, put them on Hagar's shoulder, handed the boy to her, and sent her away. This scene differs from Chapter 16 where Hagar fled on her own: that time she fled; this time she was sent away. When the water in the skin was finished, Hagar cast the child under one of the shrubs and went and sat down opposite him a great way off, about an arrowshot away; and she lifted up her voice and wept, saying, "Let me not see the death of the boy."

The very next sentence is the beating heart of the entire chapter: "God heard the voice of the lad." Please take note: the one weeping aloud was Hagar, yet Scripture says that what God heard was the voice of the lad. When the angel called to Hagar, his first sentence was a question to her, and his second sentence was: "What ails you, Hagar? Fear not, for God has heard the voice of the lad where he is." Then he commanded her to do something a mother ought to do: "Arise, lift up the lad and hold him with your hand, for I will make him a great nation." Then—"God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water."

The well had been there all along; it was despair that had first blinded her eyes. Brothers and sisters, often what we lack is not a way out, but eyes that are reopened in the midst of our weeping. God first hears the voice of the child, then opens the eyes of the mother, and only then comes the well. This sequence is for everyone who walks into the wilderness: what God hears comes earlier than your weeping; His provision is nearer than what you can see.

Ishmael grew up in the wilderness, became an archer, and took an Egyptian wife—thus Hagar's line went forth. What God promised her by the spring in chapter 16 was fulfilled here piece by piece: toward those outside the covenant, God likewise keeps the word He has spoken.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The Covenant at Beersheba and the Tree—The Everlasting God (21:22-34)

After the farewell, the lens turns to foreign affairs: Abimelech, along with Phichol the commander of his army, came to Abraham, speaking: "God is with you in all that you do." This king, who had suffered loss in chapter 20, took the initiative this time to make a covenant: that you will not deal falsely with me, with my offspring, or with my posterity; but according to the kindness that I have done to you, you will do to me. Brothers and sisters, if your God is truly upon you, the people of the world can see it, they cannot sit still, and they will come to make a covenant with you. The effect of a witness is often first revealed in the attitude of outsiders toward you.

In the midst of making the covenant, a dispute arose: a well of water had been seized by Abimelech's servants. Abraham brought the matter up, and Abimelech said he did not know about it. Notice Abraham's approach: he laid the matter out in the open first, and only then made the covenant. A covenant is not meant to cover up old scores; it is signed only after old scores have been settled. Then he took sheep and oxen and gave them to Abimelech, and set aside seven ewe lambs by themselves as a witness, naming that well. Thus the place-name emerged: Beersheba, Be'er Sheva (H884)—"Well of the Oath," which also shares the same sound and root with "seven" (sheva, H7650 to swear an oath, H7651 seven). Was that well in the same wilderness where Hagar saw the well of water? Scripture does not say, and we need not force an alignment; but in the very same wilderness of Beersheba, God first opened a well for a wandering bondwoman, and then reserved a well of covenant for His friend. This wilderness is filled with nothing but grace.

The close of this chapter is exceedingly beautiful: "Then Abraham planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba, and there called on the name of the LORD, the Everlasting God." The Everlasting God, El Olam—El is God (H410), and Olam (H5769) is everlasting, ancient times. This is the first time in Scripture that God is addressed by this name. Why here? Because Abraham had just sent away one son and received another; on the human side, gathering and parting are unpredictable, but what he laid hold of was that unchanging God. Planting a tree was leaving a mark for future generations: the tree will grow, the covenant will be kept, and God is the Everlasting God. Then Scripture says that Abraham stayed in the land of the Philistines many days—life still had to move forward, Isaac had to grow up, and Mount Moriah lay ahead. But the master of this household had already dropped his anchor in the midst of upheaval: He is the Everlasting God.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: those waiting for the appointed time. Your promise is not on your calendar; it is on God's calendar. The keywords of Genesis 21 are "as He had said," "as He had spoken," and "at the set time of which God had spoken to him"—the covenant-keeping God never delays; your only task is to live your life as one who is prepared.

The second kind: those who have a difficult separation to make in their household. Some lines must be drawn, even if your heart aches when drawing them. When drawing boundaries, learn from God: let the boundary be clear, yet do not revoke grace—for those who are sent away, do not cease praying, and do not withdraw your words of blessing.

The third kind: those weeping in the wilderness. You think God only listens to the prayers of great figures. God heard the voice of the lad. A child does not know how to pray; crying is his voice. When you run out of words, your tears are also a voice.

The fourth kind: those whose eyes are blinded by despair. The well is near you; ask God to open your eyes first, and then look for the path. Many people do not lack a way out; they are weeping so hard that they cannot see the way out.

The fifth kind: those recognized by outsiders as having God with them. Abimelech came to make a covenant because "God is with you in all that you do." If your witness is present, sooner or later someone will come to talk with you about that God—be prepared with your answer.

The sixth kind: those who have experienced gathering and parting. A son was born, a son was sent away; life is just like this, gaining on one hand and losing on the other. Learn from Abraham in planting the tree: plant your hope upon the Everlasting God; that is a tree no wind can shake.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Responsive Prayer

Today's chapter begins with laughter, passes through tears, and comes to covenant-making and tree-planting. The promise was born, not a single word of God fell to the ground; for the one sent away, God also heard, and God also blessed; for the ones who remained, they held fast to the Everlasting God to walk the rest of the journey.

Let us pray together: Covenant-keeping God, we thank You. You visit us at the set time, and not one of Your words falls to the ground. We pray that You restore Sarah's laughter to our sorrowful families; we pray that You also hear the voices of the children in our homes who do not know how to pray; when we must draw boundaries and when we must bid farewell, we pray that Your grace will not be absent. Finally, we pray that You will be our Everlasting God—people may gather and part, but You never change. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Chapter-End Notes

[1] paqad: Hebrew פָּקַד (H6485), to visit, care for, muster, and act according to what has been recorded; Gen. 21:1 "And the LORD visited Sarah as He had said"; word meaning according to BDB, HALOT. It is the same word as when Joseph, near death in Gen. 50:24-25, said, "God will surely visit you."

[2] Yitzchaq: Hebrew יִצְחָק (H3327), Isaac, meaning "he laughs"; shares the same root with tzachaq: צָחַק (H6711), to laugh; Gen. 21:3 naming, 21:6 Sarah's "God has made me laugh," and 21:9 Ishmael's "scoffing" (metzacheq being its participle form) all share this root; word meaning according to BDB, HALOT.

[3] Regarding the interpretation of 21:9: in Gal. 4:29 Paul recounts this event as "he who was born according to the flesh persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit"; this manuscript mentions them together accordingly, and does not directly take the word "scoffing" as a conclusive condemnation, which is hereby noted.

[4] El Olam: Hebrew אֵל (H410) combined with עוֹלָם ('olam, H5769, everlasting, ancient times); Gen. 21:33 "called on the name of the LORD, the Everlasting God," marking the first time in Scripture God is addressed by this name; word meaning according to BDB, HALOT.

[5] Be'er Sheva: Hebrew בְּאֵר שֶׁבַע (H884), Beersheba, "Well of the Oath"; shares the same root with sheva' to swear an oath (H7650) and sheva' seven (H7651); Gen. 21:31 explicitly states "because there the two of them swore an oath"; place-name meaning according to BDB. The body of this manuscript presents both meanings, which is an intra-textual self-explanation (seven ewe lambs and the swearing of an oath both appear in 21:28-31).

[6] 'eshel: Hebrew אֵשֶׁל (H815), the tree planted by Abraham in Gen. 21:33, translated as "tamarisk tree" in the Chinese Union Version (and similarly in others); word meaning according to BDB, HALOT; this manuscript follows the translation name of the Chinese Union Version.

[7] Regarding "God heard the voice of the lad" (21:17): in the narrative, the one who wept aloud is explicitly stated to be Hagar (21:16), whereas what God heard is recorded as "the voice of the lad"; this manuscript builds its argument upon this basis, which is a homiletical interpretation based on textual contrast, hereby noted.

[8] Regarding Isaac’s age at weaning: 21:8 does not specify the number of years; Jewish tradition and commentaries generally estimate it to be around two to three years old. This manuscript uses "around three years old" as an approximate expression.

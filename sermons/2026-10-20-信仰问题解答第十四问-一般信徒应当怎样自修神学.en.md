## I. To Study Theology on Your Own, Begin with One Orthodox Systematic Theology

The person asking said one sentence first: theology is so important. Pastor Tong affirms that sentence. The method he gives does not beat around the bush either: first keep one orthodox theology book at hand, and read it slowly.

The first book he named was the *Systematic Theology* published by the Baptists, whose author is Strong. Pastor Tong's assessment is very down-to-earth: most of the thinking in this book is quite good and worth consulting. But he immediately adds a caution — a good book does not mean every sentence is to be swallowed whole. In Strong's doctrine of redemption there is one point that is his own invention, speaking of Christ's relation to the sin of mankind and His having to bear the responsibility of saving it, which even other Baptist theologians do not agree with; special attention is needed when you read there. In addition, in the parts of the book involving rites, such as sprinkling versus immersion, Pastor Tong says these need not be insisted on absolutely; do not contend to the end with people over a minor point in one book and lose the major doctrines.

This is Pastor Tong's way of introducing a book: he recommends, but does not idolize; he affirms, but points out the disputed places first. Read books this way yourself as well — it is not finding one famous work and swallowing it whole, but knowing where it is strong and where to keep an eye open.

Pastor Tong then introduced a few more: the Lutheran *Outline of Lutheran Theology*; Berkhof's English *Systematic Theology*, of which only an outline had been printed in Chinese at the time; and a book called *Be Mature in Understanding*. He said these are all very good books of doctrine and can help you.

## II. Why the Book Must Be a "Solid" One

Pastor Tong's final sentence was aimed at young Christians, and it left a particularly deep impression on me. He said:

I hope young Christians will not read only novels and love stories and books of that kind; they should read some comparatively solid, theoretical books of theology that have a framework of thought.

Note, he did not say novels cannot be read; what he said was "not only." Reading only light things leaves the mind forever at the level of a child. Paul's sentence fits exactly: "Do not be children in understanding... but in understanding be mature."

**Original-language analysis:** "Understanding" in Greek is ***Phrēn* (φρήν, Strong G5424)**, denoting the mind, the understanding, the part used for judgment (per BDAG); the "mature" in "be mature" is ***Teleios* (τέλειος, Strong G5046)**, denoting grown up, fully developed — not older in years, but grown up in mind. Paul is not telling people to be experienced in malice — in malice one should be a babe, knowing nothing of it, which is best — he is telling people to grow up into adults in the mind that judges truth, and not drink milk all their lives.

The word "theology" itself also explains why it requires effort. ***Theologia*** is composed of ***Theos* (θεός, Strong G2316, God)** and ***Logos* (λόγος, Strong G3056, word, doctrine)**; its basic meaning is the speech and learning concerning God. Since it concerns God, it cannot be cobbled together by feeling; feelings drift, while doctrine states the faith solidly and clearly, so that when you are blown by the winds of false teaching, you will not be tossed to and fro (Eph. 4:14).

So studying theology on your own is not one more item of spiritual decoration. It is the straight road for the mind to grow up: read books that have a framework, and your thinking slowly gains a framework; with a framework in your mind, when you listen to sermons, read Scripture, and discern what is said from the pulpit, you will not simply believe whatever anyone says.

## III. The Method of Self-Study: Putting Pastor Tong's Cautions into Practice

This section is my own application, as the adapter, of Pastor Tong's principles above into concrete practices for those studying on their own today; it is not Pastor Tong's original words:

First, have one main book. Do not buy a whole shelf of books at the start, reading two pages of this one and flipping through three chapters of that one, and in the end finishing none of them. As Pastor Tong said, first keep one volume of orthodox systematic theology and read it slowly from the beginning; where you do not understand, note it down first, and do not get stuck dead in one place.

When you read, read with questions. Pastor Tong's attitude toward Strong's book is the best model: absorb what is good, mark what is disputed, and go and check what other theologians say. Reading questions out of one book is more growth than muddling through ten books.

Light books may be read, but not only light books. Novels, testimonies, and devotional pieces all have their place, but if there is never one solid book of doctrine in your reading list, your mind will have a hard time growing up. Every week, set aside at least a period of time for that "hard" book; even if you read only a few pages, what you read in becomes yours.

## For Us Today

1. First keep one volume of orthodox systematic theology at hand: Pastor Tong named Strong's *Systematic Theology*, the *Outline of Lutheran Theology*, Berkhof's *Systematic Theology*, and *Be Mature in Understanding* as examples usable at the time
2. Even good books must be read with discernment: views the author invented himself, which even the theologians of his own denomination do not agree with, need special attention; disputes over rites such as sprinkling and immersion need not be insisted on absolutely to the point of harming the order of major and minor
3. Do not read only light books: in malice be babes, but in understanding be mature — set aside fixed time to read solid books of theology that have a framework of thought

## Abstract

Leviticus 27 is the last chapter of the book. It deals not with sacrifices that must be offered, but with vows that people make willingly: when a vow involves persons, animals, a house, or a field, how is it to be valued, how redeemed, what may not be exchanged, and what may not be redeemed at all — ending with the tithe of the land's produce and of the herd and flock. This sermon does not expound every regulation in detail; it follows the chapter's structure and theological thread. Willingness does not stand outside the law: freewill devotion is likewise set within the order of holiness. Inside the valuation table hides mercy toward the poor, and the added fifth puts a price on rash retraction. The firstling already belongs to the LORD; the devoted thing is most holy and cannot be redeemed. The tithe is not man's to pick and choose: the tenth that passes under the rod, good or bad, is holy to the LORD. The book that began with a call from the tabernacle ends with the commandments at Sinai: the last lesson of the holy life is to be set apart to the LORD.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 The place of the last chapter: even willing vows stand within the order of holiness

(Fact) First, the chapter's position must be made clear. The end of Leviticus 26 already carries a book-wide concluding sentence (26:46, Summary): that verse ties the statutes, judgments, and laws to what the LORD made with Israel at Mount Sinai through Moses, and it reads as though the book has already closed. Yet the book does not stop there. Chapter 27 opens with the book's customary launching formula — the LORD speaks to Moses and tells him to speak to the children of Israel (27:1, Summary). In other words, the reader turns past a sentence that sounds like an ending, and a whole further chapter stands behind it. And the subject of this chapter is not a new sacrificial system but a marginal topic: vows. (Application) That position is itself a sermon. After sacrifices, cleanness, feasts, the holy life, sabbatical years and jubilee, blessings and curses, the very last supplement concerns the one thing no commandment ever commanded: no statute orders an Israelite to make a vow. A vow is spontaneous, extra, willing. And yet this wholly voluntary act receives an entire chapter of regulation: how to value, how to redeem, what may be exchanged and what absolutely may not. The meaning could not be plainer: voluntary does not mean outside the order; willing does not mean casual. A person's freest act of devotion must also enter the frame of holiness and be measured there.

(Fact) The chapter's skeleton can be set out at the start. Its thirty-four verses fall into roughly four movements: the valuation of vows (vv. 1-25), from persons to animals, to a house, to a field, each class given its rule; the boundaries of sanctification (vv. 26-29), on the firstling and the devoted thing; the tithe (vv. 30-33), on the produce of the land and the tenth of herd and flock; and a single closing verse (v. 34), the conclusion of the whole book. The sequence is deliberate: first what a man vows willingly, then what is owed as a matter of course; first what can be valued and redeemed, then what cannot be redeemed at all. Between looseness and strictness the chapter draws like a net pulled steadily tighter, closing at last on the tithe and the conclusion.

(Fact) The opening sentence deserves to be read word for word: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, When a man shall make a singular vow, the persons shall be for the LORD by thy estimation." (Lev 27:2, KJV) Two words must be handled according to the original. The first is "vow," Hebrew neder (Strong's H5088), a promise solemnly made to God, a votive pledge; it is not the language of a passing emotion but a commitment that must be paid — the Chinese base text's verb at this verse is precisely "to repay": a man repays a special vow. A vow is something to be repaid; that is the chapter's first foundation stone. The second is "estimation," whose noun form is ʿērekh (Strong's H6187), the assessed value, the set price; this word is the chapter's technical pivot — a person's vow must finally be rendered as a countable value and delivered to the sanctuary. (Application) Mark the combination: the most spiritual of motives — a willing vow; the most practical of procedures — conversion by estimation. At one end the heart, at the other the ledger. Leviticus refuses to let the two come apart: if the heart is real, it can bear to be estimated; and if the ledger is kept clear, it must not forget that the ledger began in a willing heart. Faith here is neither empty talk of feeling nor a cold transaction, but ardour received into order, zeal protected by statute.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) On this chapter's position there is a long-standing scholarly discussion: if chapter 26 already has a book-wide conclusion, why does this chapter follow it? One reading holds that the chapter is an appendix attached after the main body of the book was complete, a compilation on sanctuary economics and votive practice whose manner differs somewhat from the preceding chapters. Another reading stresses that the conclusion of this chapter (v. 34) answers the conclusion of chapter 26, showing that the editor deliberately set "vows" and "the tithe" as the book's rear guard, so that the teaching on holiness lands finally on the people's everyday life of devotion. The two readings rest on the same textual facts — the concluding tone of 26:46, the distinctive subject matter of chapter 27, the existence of the conclusion at verse 34 — and differ on how to explain the order and intent of the editing. This sermon sets both readings side by side, with their grounds, and decides neither; to pronounce dogmatically for one side would go beyond what the text of Leviticus 27 itself can bear. (Fact) On one point, however, both sides agree: whatever this chapter's place in the book's editorial history, its place in the canon today is fixed — it is the last chapter of Leviticus, and the last lesson a reader hears before closing the book is vows, valuations, and the tithe.

(Application) The chapter's relation to the whole book should also be stated at the outset. Leviticus opens with the LORD calling Moses from the tabernacle (Lev 1:1, Summary): the book's first sentence brings the reader to the sanctuary door. In its last chapter, the people bring their persons, animals, houses, fields, and produce, item by item, into the sanctuary's order, to be weighed and set apart. Between head and tail runs one complete line: God calls first, and man answers after; God sets up the sanctuary first, and man then brings his life within the sanctuary's range. To place the chapter on vows last is to tell the reader that all the holiness of the preceding twenty-six chapters must finally land on one ordinary person's voluntary, concrete, countable act of devotion. Holiness is not a notion suspended in the air; in the end it always becomes something you can hold in your hand and enter in a ledger.

### II. 08:00-24:00 The valuation table: how vows are converted, and how mercy enters the table

(Fact) The body of the first movement (vv. 1-25) is a valuation table, handling the conversion when a vow involves persons. A man who vows himself or a member of his household to the LORD does not actually enter sanctuary service; the person vowed is rendered, at the assessed value, into silver for the LORD (v. 2, quoted in section I). The valuation is graded on two axes: age and sex (vv. 3-7, Summary). A male from twenty to sixty years old is valued at fifty shekels of silver, by the shekel of the sanctuary; a female of the same age at thirty shekels. From five to twenty years old, the male is twenty shekels and the female ten — this grade the text states concretely, and this sermon quotes it in full: "And if it be from five years old even unto twenty years old, then thy estimation shall be of the male twenty shekels, and for the female ten shekels." (Lev 27:5, KJV) From a month old to five years old, the male is five shekels and the female three. From sixty years old and above, the male is fifteen shekels and the female ten (all grades except v. 5 are given in summary, with every number stated exactly as the text gives it). The discipline must be stated plainly here: this sermon states these numbers only as the text gives them, with no conversion into modern currency and no elaboration by today's wages or metal prices — the text gives the sanctuary's measure, not a market quotation, and a sermon that converts them into present-day amounts on its own authority may sound vivid, but it is making the text speak where the text has not spoken.

(Fact) The unit of valuation also needs to be fixed. The "shekel," Hebrew šeqel (Strong's H8255), is a unit of weight and the basis of the valuation. The movement closes by nailing down the measures (v. 25, Summary): all valuations are to be made by the shekel of the sanctuary, and twenty gerahs make one shekel; the "gerah" is gērāh (Strong's H1626), the smallest unit of weight. (Application) That verse looks like a technical footnote, but theologically it is not light: the valuation uses not the market's scales but the sanctuary's. Market scales can be short; the sanctuary shekel has a fixed standard. When willing devotion enters the sanctuary's order, even the weights used to weigh the silver fall under the sanctuary's rule — the honesty of devotion begins with the weights.

(Fact) Immediately after the table stands the verse most easily skipped and most revealing of heart: "But if he be poorer than thy estimation, then he shall present himself before the priest, and the priest shall value him; according to his ability that vowed shall the priest value him." (Lev 27:8, KJV) Notice the structure: the table is rigid — age and sex settle the price at a glance, without a fraction's concession; yet a door is built into the table in advance: poverty. The door is not an exceptional favour but a formal clause written into the body of the law. The poor man who has vowed and cannot pay the table price is neither turned away nor fined for breach; he is brought before the priest, and the priest revalues him according to his ability. (Application) This is mercy inside the table. The law shows a double character here: it lays down one rule for everyone alike, and it leaves individual room for the man who cannot pay. Without that door, the poor would be frightened off vowing altogether by the price list — devotion would become the privilege of the wealthy. The text deliberately opens the door inside the table: the poor man may vow, and may fulfil his vow, like anyone else; only his price is set by the priest according to his ability. The priest's role here deserves attention: he is not merely the man who receives the silver; he is the man who discerns ability — who must know the circumstances of the household before him and judge between the fixed standard and compassion. The sanctuary's counting house, it turns out, also does the work of mercy.

(Fact) The objects of valuation then widen from persons to things (vv. 9-13, Summary): an animal of the kind that may be offered, vowed and given to the LORD, becomes wholly holy; once given, it may not be exchanged — not a good for a bad, nor a bad for a good; if a man insists on exchanging, both the original and the exchange become holy. An unclean animal, which may not be offered, is brought before the priest, and the priest values it; as the priest values it, so it stands. If the one who vowed wishes to redeem it, the rule that runs through the whole chapter appears for the first time, and this sermon quotes it in full: "But if he will at all redeem it, then he shall add a fifth part thereof unto thy estimation." (Lev 27:13, KJV) The "fifth part" is a form of the ḥămîšî word-family (in this chapter ḥămîšîṯô / ḥămîšît, Strong's H2549; headword ḥămîšî, חֲמִישִׁי), a fifth of the assessed value, charged on top of the price at redemption. (It is not ḥōmeš, חֹמֶשׁ, which is a different word, Strong's H2569, occurring only at Genesis 47:26; an earlier form of this sermon misnamed the word, following the Chinese original, and is corrected here.) (Application) This rule of the added fifth is one of the chapter's keys; this sermon marks it here and will return to it. What does it guard against? Against treating a vow as child's play: offer in a flush of zeal, then, when the heart cools, buy it back at par — in and out, until the sanctuary becomes a shop with unconditional returns. The added fifth puts a price on retraction: you may redeem, but redemption is not cancellation; redemption costs more. That extra fifth goes to no one's pocket; it belongs to the sanctuary — a rash promise must learn carefulness in real silver.

(Fact) Next comes the house (vv. 14-15, Summary): a man who sanctifies his house to be holy to the LORD has it valued by the priest, whether good or bad, and the priest's valuation stands; if the one who sanctified it wishes to redeem his house, he likewise adds a fifth part of the money of the estimation, and the house is his. Last, and most detailed, the field (vv. 16-24, Summary): a man who sanctifies part of a field of his own possession has it valued not by a floating market price but by its seed requirement — a field needing an homer of barley seed is valued at fifty shekels of silver; if he sanctifies it from the year of jubilee, the valuation stands as assessed; if after the jubilee, the priest reckons the price according to the years remaining until the next jubilee, and abates it from the estimation accordingly. A bought field, not part of his possession, if sanctified, is likewise reckoned by the priest up to the year of jubilee and paid that day, and in the jubilee the field returns to the one from whom it was bought. (Fact) Here the intertext with Leviticus 25 is clear: chapter 25 lays down the land's root principle — the land shall not be sold for ever, for the land is the LORD's, and Israel are strangers and sojourners with Him (Lev 25, Summary); this chapter's field valuations operate precisely on that principle. The price of a field declines as the jubilee approaches, because what a man sanctifies is never the perpetual ownership of the land, only the years of its use up to the jubilee. One chapter sets the principle, the other the arithmetic, and the two mesh. (Application) That intertext draws a humble boundary around devotion: what a man gives God is never more than what he actually holds. You have no perpetual estate to give; you have only years to give. Count the years honestly and give them transparently — that is the whole honesty of dedicating a field. The boaster speaks of what he merely administers for a time as though he owned it for ever; the text's arithmetic calmly reminds him: first count how many years remain until the jubilee.

(Fact) The field passage ends with a contrast of two outcomes (vv. 19-21). If the one who sanctified the field wishes to redeem it, the rule stands as before, and this sermon quotes it in full: "And if he that sanctified the field will in any wise redeem it, then he shall add the fifth part of the money of thy estimation unto it, and it shall be assured to him." (Lev 27:19, KJV) But if he will not redeem it, or has sold it to another man, it shall not be redeemed any more; and in the jubilee its outcome is the exact reverse of chapter 25's principle of return: "But the field, when it goeth out in the jubile, shall be holy unto the LORD, as a field devoted; the possession thereof shall be the priest's." (Lev 27:21, KJV) (Application) Hear the sharpness of the contrast: an ordinary field under sale returns to its owner in the jubilee; a field sanctified and then left unredeemed does not return in the jubilee — it leaves its owner for ever and becomes the priest's possession. Consecration is not an intention that can be left pending: once a field is sanctified it enters a one-way road — either redeem it with the added fifth, or let go and watch it become wholly holy at the jubilee. There is no middle state; to drag on undecided is itself a decision. How many intentions of devotion die not by refusal but by suspension — declared year after year, never carried out, until even the window for redemption has closed. The field regulations of this text wrote that ending down in advance.

### III. 24:00-42:00 The firstling and the devoted thing: two boundaries that may not be crossed

(Fact) The second movement (vv. 26-29) tightens abruptly. The first movement was still speaking of the elasticity of valuation, conversion, and redemption; this one sets two boundaries with no elasticity at all. The first concerns the firstling, and this sermon quotes it in full: "Only the firstling of the beasts, which should be the LORD's firstling, no man shall sanctify it; whether it be ox, or sheep: it is the LORD's." (Lev 27:26, KJV) The logic is minimal: the firstling of the flock already belongs to the LORD — since the night of the exodus, the firstborn being the LORD's is an old account written into the whole nation's memory (Summary, referring to the Exodus legislation on the firstborn). Since it is already His, no man may take it and "sanctify" it again, presenting it as the gift of his own vow. (Application) This punctures the most hidden illusion in devotion: taking what already belongs to God and offering it to God as one's own gift. The firstling ox or sheep is not your achievement; it is God's prior right. When you lead it to the sanctuary, that is not generosity but restitution. By setting this boundary amid the busiest vow regulations, the chapter stamps a line of small print on every vow form: first distinguish what is yours from what is His, and only then speak of devotion. To use His property to purchase a name for one's own piety is the first thing this chapter forbids. The firstling of an unclean beast has its own handling (v. 27, Summary): it may be redeemed at the estimation with a fifth added, or, if not redeemed, sold at the estimation — inside the boundary there is still settled procedure, not indiscriminate confiscation.

(Fact) The second boundary is heavier still: the devoted thing. This sermon quotes it in full: "Notwithstanding no devoted thing, that a man shall devote unto the LORD of all that he hath, both of man and beast, and of the field of his possession, shall be sold or redeemed: every devoted thing is most holy unto the LORD." (Lev 27:28, KJV) The "devoted thing" is ḥērem (Strong's H2764): that which is given over to the LORD wholly and permanently, never to return to human circulation; the Chinese base text elsewhere renders the same word "a thing to be utterly destroyed" (Summary), and the two renderings point to two faces of one reality — to be most holy to the LORD is to leave human use and human trade, completely and irreversibly. The following verse (v. 29, Summary) writes the sternest side of devotion as it touches persons: one devoted from among men shall not be redeemed but shall surely be put to death — the heaviest sentence in the chapter, which this sermon records in summary, as to its existence and its place, without rhetorical elaboration. (Application) Set the devoted thing beside the redemption rules of the first movement: what is given by an ordinary vow can be valued and can be redeemed with a fifth added; the devoted thing has no door of redemption at all — it shall not be sold, it shall not be redeemed. Why must there be an irreversible grade like this? Because if every act of devotion keeps a back door, "given to the LORD" remains forever a retractable sentence. The existence of the devoted thing drives a nail into the whole system of devotion: some offerings are not taken back; once the word is spoken and the thing has changed hands, it is His for ever. A man may use the elasticity of redemption within ordinary vows, but he must know that on the map of holiness there is an irreversible territory — most holy. A weightless doctrine of devotion has no such word in it; the last chapter of Leviticus sets it at the chapter's heart.

(Fact) One verb in this movement also deserves checking against the original: the verb rendered "sanctify" has the root qādāš (Strong's H6942), to make holy, to set apart as holy; the causative form of this very verb is what the chapter uses when a man "sanctifies" his house and his field. (Application) Put the three things side by side — the firstling, the devoted thing, and sanctification — and this movement is in fact surveying the territory of the word "holy": one holiness God has fixed beforehand (the firstling is already His); one a man hands over wholly (the devoted thing, irreversible); one a man sets apart on his own initiative while a door of redemption still stands (the thing vowed). Three kinds of holy, three weights. To preach devotion while confusing the three is either to describe restitution as generosity, or to make what should be wholly handed over a matter for bargaining. The fineness of this chapter is a fineness of measure; its severity is a severity of boundaries. To the very last chapter, holiness is still about boundaries — what is His, to what degree, and whether it can be taken back: all of it must be distinguished clearly.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The tithe: set apart to the LORD, and not man's to pick and choose

(Fact) The third movement (vv. 30-33) has only four verses, yet it is the most widely circulated and most often quoted part of the chapter: the tithe. First, the produce of the land; this sermon quotes in full the verse from which the Chinese original takes its title: "And all the tithe of the land, whether of the seed of the land, or of the fruit of the tree, is the LORD's: it is holy unto the LORD." (Lev 27:30, KJV) Two points stand in the wording. First, the scope is "all the tithe of the land" — the seed of the land and the fruit of the tree, field and orchard together, with no reserved plot left out. Second, the characterisation comes first: the tithe "is the LORD's." Notice the order of the sentence: the text does not say "you shall give a tithe to the LORD," but first that it is the LORD's, that it is holy unto the LORD — ownership precedes action. Giving, in substance here, is surrender: to separate out from the harvest, and hand over, the portion that was already His. The "tithe" is maʿăśēr (Strong's H4643), a tenth part, the tithe. (Application) That sentence order carries the whole theology of the verse. If the tithe were man's generosity, how much to give would be a matter of mood; if the tithe is the LORD's, only one question remains: the portion that is His — have you handed it over? In the calmest of tones Leviticus turns devotion from "how much am I willing to give" into "how much is already His." The whole book runs from offerings and feasts to this final chapter, and lands on a statement as calm as this: the tithe is the LORD's. No shouting, no urging — only a declaration of ownership; and a declaration of ownership carries more weight than any shouting.

(Fact) For the tithe of the land's produce, a door of redemption is retained (v. 31, Summary): if a man wishes to redeem any part of his tithe, he adds, by the chapter's standing rule, a fifth part on top of its value. But for the tithe of herd and flock the rule tightens sharply, and this sermon quotes it in full: "And concerning the tithe of the herd, or of the flock, even of whatsoever passeth under the rod, the tenth shall be holy unto the LORD." (Lev 27:32, KJV) The "rod" is šēḇeṭ (Strong's H7626), the shepherd's rod; the manner of counting is to let the animals pass one by one under the rod, and the tenth one that passes is holy to the LORD. The prohibition that follows nails down the character of this sanctification: "He shall not search whether it be good or bad, neither shall he change it: and if he change it at all, then both it and the change thereof shall be holy; it shall not be redeemed." (Lev 27:33, KJV) (Application) Look closely at that picture — passing under the rod. What decides which animal becomes holy is not the owner's eye but the sequence. The tenth comes along, fat or lean, good or bad, and the owner may not ask and may not exchange. Why? Because the moment good and bad are weighed, the tithe becomes a game of selection at once: send the weakest to the sanctuary and keep the strong — the scheming of devotion hides most easily inside selection. The text blocks the scheming with the simplest of methods: count no quality, only sequence; the tenth that comes along is His. And if a man does tamper and exchange, both become holy and neither can be redeemed — the price of calculation is a double sanctification. This verse is the chapter's most unsparing insight into human nature, and its most realistic kindness: God does not want a piety that has been carefully picked over; He wants us to learn, within the plain order of our days, to hand over without picking and choosing.

(Fact) Set the tithe movement beside the vow movement before it, and the structural answer between them is clear: the vow is willing and spontaneous, and the text leaves it ample elasticity of valuation and redemption; the tithe is a matter of course, a standing rule, and the text draws it tighter — the land's produce may still be redeemed at the added price, but the tenth of the herd may not even be exchanged. (Application) That leaves today's reader a lesson in discernment: there are two kinds of giving, and they must not be confused. One is duty — what is His, to be surrendered by sequence and by rule, with no room for bargaining. The other is willingness — what a man vows freely beyond duty, for which Scripture provides honourable procedure and a door of redemption. To preach duty as willingness is to fall short; to preach willingness as duty is to lay a burden. This chapter writes the two separately and sets them in a single chapter precisely so that we learn both: no skimping in duty, no coercion in willingness.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: holy unto the LORD — the last lesson of the whole book

(Fact) The last verse of the chapter, and of the whole book, this sermon quotes in full: "These are the commandments, which the LORD commanded Moses for the children of Israel in mount Sinai." (Lev 27:34, KJV) So Leviticus closes. Look back to the book's opening: the LORD called Moses from the tabernacle (Lev 1:1, Summary), and the voice came out of the tent; at the book's end, the words are gathered up as "the commandments" given at Mount Sinai. From head to tail between them stand offerings, blood, cleanness, feasts, sabbatical years, jubilee, blessings and curses, and finally vows and the tithe. (Application) The weight of this conclusion can only be seen at the scale of the whole book: twenty-seven chapters of teaching are finally gathered by one verse into the word "commandments" — including the contents of this last chapter, which look the most voluntary of all. The vow is willing, yet the tithe is written in the general ledger of the commandments. Willingness and command flow together at the end of this book: Israel's freest devotion is finally set within the frame of the commandments as well. That is not the abolition of willingness but a home for it — willingness without a frame has nowhere to live after three days of heat; willingness with a frame becomes a way of life that can be kept from generation to generation.

(New Testament reading) The New Testament has two direct echoes on the subject of vows; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Leviticus. First, Matthew 5, where Jesus speaks on oaths (Matt 5:33-37, Summary): He carries the topic from the old rule — not to forswear oneself, but to perform one's vows — forward to the disciple's speech itself being truthful: let yes be yes and no be no, with credibility resting not on the added weight of an oath. Second, the "Corban" of Mark 7 (Mark 7:11, Summary): a man declares the resources that should support his father and mother to be Corban, a gift devoted to God, and so evades the duty of supporting them; Jesus states sharply that such a vow in fact nullifies the word of God — a vow may never be used as an instrument for escaping one's duty to others. (Application) Read alongside Leviticus 27, these two New Testament readings make the boundary clearer still: this chapter teaches that a vow once made is to be performed with care, and willing devotion is to enter the order; the New Testament further shows that if a vow becomes an excuse for escaping duty, or an oath becomes a decoration for speech, both have departed from the intent for which these rules were given. Duty comes first — support of parents is duty, honesty toward others is duty — and the willing vow stands on top of duty, never on top of it as a trampling foot.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, willingness does not live outside order: the vow is the freest of acts, yet Leviticus devotes an entire chapter to giving it valuation, redemption, and boundaries — free devotion must be able to bear measurement. Second, devotion begins by distinguishing ownership: the firstling is His, the tithe is His, the devoted thing is most holy and cannot be taken back; a man's true giving starts with confessing, "This was His already." Third, not picking and choosing is itself a lesson in surrender: the tenth that passes under the rod is not asked after, good or bad — learn to hand the sequence back to God, and lay down the scheming of selection. (Application) The last lesson of Leviticus is not a more elaborate rite but the most everyday of exercises: holy unto the LORD. This book began with a call from the tabernacle, requiring man to bring his whole life into holiness; when it ends, it gives him no higher mountain — only a valuation table, a shepherd's rod, and a concluding sentence. The end point of holiness turns out to land in the daily ledger and the daily sequence: the vow you made, reckon it clearly; the tithe you owe, do not pick and choose; what you have offered, do not take back. May we, having read this book through, learn exactly this clarity — to vow willingly, to reckon honestly, to hand over in sequence, holy unto the LORD.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Leviticus ends not with a grander rite, but with a valuation table and a shepherd's rod. I had always treated giving as a matter of mood: give more when moved, postpone it when busy. This chapter quietly tells me that the tithe is the LORD's: "And all the tithe of the land, whether of the seed of the land, or of the fruit of the tree, is the LORD's: it is holy unto the LORD." Ownership comes before action; what is His, I must learn not generosity but surrender. Sharper still is the tenth animal passing under the rod — no searching whether it be good or bad, no changing it. How I want to keep the best of the sequence for myself and send the leftover to God; the text blocks all picking and choosing, and teaches me to hand over honestly in the plain order of my days. And there is mercy inside the table: the poor man who cannot pay is valued by the priest according to his ability — God does not cancel my vow because of my limits; He asks me to fulfil it truthfully, according to my ability. This morning I will do one small thing first: set apart this month's portion at the head of my accounts, not scrape it together from what remains, and take the first step today toward a promise I have said I would keep. Willingness does not live outside order — may my willingness from now on be countable, and kept in sequence.

The two renderings are of one root: given over wholly to the LORD, never to return to human circulation. Section III's exposition follows this double sense.
10. The function words and pronouns of the Chinese original's quotations at Lev 27:26 and 27:30 were checked word by word against the base text ("归给" not "归于" at v. 30; "谁也不可" at v. 26); this English manuscript makes no corresponding claim for its own prose, its quotations being KJV throughout.
11. maʿăśēr (H4643): tithe, a tenth part; BDB enters Lev 27:30 and 27:32 under this word. šēḇeṭ (H7626) is the rod, the shepherd's staff; BibleHub's entry 7626 lists Lev 27:32 among its rod-sense occurrences. Section IV's exposition follows these two words.
12. qādāš (H6942): the verb root "to sanctify, to set apart as holy"; the Blue Letter Bible word table for Lev 27:26 indexes "shall sanctify" to this number. The chapter's sanctifying of house and field uses the causative form of this verb.
13. 14. The two New Testament readings — Matt 5:33-37 on oaths and the performance of vows, and Mark 7:11, "Corban" — are given in the body in summary only, with no New Testament verse quoted in full, so that the reading layer is not confused with this chapter's base-text quotations; their location and theme in the New Testament are checkable facts, while their construal is explicitly labelled as a reading layer.

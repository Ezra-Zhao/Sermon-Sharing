# Volume One · Chapter 2 — When the Wine Ran Out, the Good Wine Was Served

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «卷一 · 第 2 篇　酒用尽了，好酒才上桌».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

Guangyi Zhao

(John 2:1-22)

---

John chapter 1 says that the Word who became flesh came to dwell among people. In the chapter that immediately follows, chapter 2, John does not take Him into some solemn, grand occasion; he first takes Him to a wedding feast. The place was Cana in Galilee, a small place, and the family holding the celebration was not a famous one either. The first thing Jesus did publicly happened at a wedding that nearly ended in disgrace. But the second thing had a completely different atmosphere: He went up to Jerusalem, and during the great feast when the crowds were at their largest, He overturned tables in public.

One quiet and one noisy, one gentle and one fierce, placed in the same chapter — this is not John arranging things in disorder. After you finish reading, you will discover that these two events speak of the same Jesus: He cares about the embarrassment of people who have run out of wine to drink, and He also cares about the business of trading in God's name; He saves ordinary people's face completely, but for things that corrupt worship He leaves no face at all.

## I. The Scripture Text

First, look at the wedding feast. Halfway through the feast, something happened that is a small matter today but was a matter of life and death at the time:

> "On the third day there was a wedding in Cana of Galilee, and the mother of Jesus was there. Now both Jesus and His disciples were invited to the wedding. And when they ran out of wine, the mother of Jesus said to Him, 'They have no wine.' Jesus said to her, 'Woman, what does your concern have to do with Me? My hour has not yet come.' His mother said to the servants, 'Whatever He says to you, do it.'" (John 2:1-5, NKJV)

Next, John specifically explains the waterpots at the scene:

> "Now there were set there six waterpots of stone, according to the manner of purification of the Jews, containing twenty or thirty gallons apiece. Jesus said to them, 'Fill the waterpots with water.' And they filled them up to the brim. And He said to them, 'Draw some out now, and take it to the master of the feast.' And they took it. When the master of the feast had tasted the water that was made wine, and did not know where it came from (but the servants who had drawn the water knew), the master of the feast called the bridegroom. And he said to him, 'Every man at the beginning sets out the good wine, and when the guests have well drunk, then the inferior. You have kept the good wine until now!' This beginning of signs Jesus did in Cana of Galilee, and manifested His glory; and His disciples believed in Him." (John 2:6-11, NKJV)

Not long after the wedding feast came the Passover. Jesus went up to Jerusalem and entered the temple, and His tone changed completely:

> "And He found in the temple those who sold oxen and sheep and doves, and the money changers doing business. When He had made a whip of cords, He drove them all out of the temple, with the sheep and the oxen, and poured out the changers' money and overturned the tables. And He said to those who sold doves, 'Take these things away! Do not make My Father's house a house of merchandise!' Then His disciples remembered that it was written, 'Zeal for Your house has eaten Me up.'" (John 2:14-17, NKJV)

The people present demanded that He produce credentials. Jesus' answer was one that not a single person understood at the time:

> "Jesus answered and said to them, 'Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.' Then the Jews said, 'It has taken forty-six years to build this temple, and will You raise it up in three days?' But He was speaking of the temple of His body. Therefore, when He had risen from the dead, His disciples remembered that He had said this to them; and they believed the Scripture and the word which Jesus had said." (John 2:19-22, NKJV)

A few words to clarify first, and the rest will read smoothly.

One is "sign." The word John uses has in the original not only the meaning of a "wonder," but is more like a "sign": a board set by the roadside, which is itself not the destination, but tells people to look further along the direction it points, to see whom it points to. So when John records signs, he never lets the reader stop at astonishment; each time he asks one more question: What about Jesus does this event manifest?

Another is "My hour has not yet come." This "hour" is a word that recurs in the Gospel of John, speaking of the moment when Jesus goes to the cross and accomplishes His mission. Jesus' meaning is not a refusal to help; it is that He does things according to His own time — not to be urged by people, nor arranged by people. His mother understood; she said no more, and only instructed the servants to do as He said.

There is also the form of address in verse 4. The Chinese Union Version renders it "Mother," with an original note saying the original text reads "Woman." This is not coldness, still less talking back. In the usage of the time, this was a solemn and respectful form of address; when Jesus on the cross entrusted His mother to His disciple, He used the same word. That John records it here according to the original text is a reminder to the reader: Jesus did this not because He could not refuse a family obligation, but out of His own determination.

Finally, there are the two senses of "temple." The place from which the merchants were driven was the outer court of the temple, counting both the buildings and the courtyards together; but in verse 19, when Jesus said "Destroy this temple," the original uses the word that refers specifically to the sanctuary itself. John immediately makes it plain: the temple He was speaking of was His own body. The word changed, and the meaning changed with it; it is a pity that the people at the time heard only the layer of stone.

## II. Exposition

First, how serious was it that there was no wine at the wedding feast? Wedding feasts at the time often lasted for several days, with relatives and friends coming from far and near, and the measure of the host's hospitality was written in the banquet. Wine was not a decoration; it was face. To run out halfway was tantamount to admitting in public that this family could not afford it and had not prepared enough, and the bridegroom's family would leave behind a lasting laughingstock among the neighbors. The sentence spoken by Jesus' mother, "They have no wine," was not idle talk; it was a scene about to collapse before everyone's eyes. She did not offer Jesus any suggestions; she only laid the difficulty before Him, and then stepped aside. This is a detail well worth noting: she brought a person to Jesus, but she did not arrange the method for Jesus.

Next, look at what Jesus used. The six stone waterpots were prepared for the purification rites. The Jews had to wash their hands and vessels before and after eating according to the rules, and what the stone waterpots held was water for ritual use. Each could hold twenty or thirty gallons, and the six together added up to a very large quantity. Jesus did not go looking for other wine jars; He used these waterpots. The waterpots of the old rules were filled with new wine — John does not explain this picture; he leaves it for the reader to think about for himself: those days of seeking purification by repeated washing were to be taken over by something new and overflowing. The prophet Isaiah had long ago spoken a sentence that sounds like an invitation: "Ho! Everyone who thirsts, Come to the waters; And you who have no money, Come, buy and eat. Yes, come, buy wine and milk Without money and without price." (Isaiah 55:1, NKJV) Wine obtained freely was, in the Old Testament itself, a sign of God's banquet.

Note also how quiet this sign was. There was no public announcement, no spotlight; the bridegroom did not know, the master of the feast did not know, and most of the guests probably did not know either — only the servants who had drawn the water knew. And those who knew the inside story were precisely the few people of the lowest status at the scene. Jesus saved the face of the bridegroom's family, yet He did not even sign His name to the credit. The remark of the master of the feast unintentionally became the theme of the whole passage: the common rule among men is to serve the good wine first, and the more one drinks, the inferior it gets, because good things always run out; at this feast of Jesus, the order was reversed — the good wine was kept until now. John's characterization of this event is only one sentence: "and manifested His glory; and His disciples believed in Him." (2:11) Glory is not pageantry; it is that the disciples, through this small matter, recognized who He was.

For the second event, please first see the background clearly, and do not hurry to read Jesus as losing control in anger. The Passover is the great feast on which the Jews commemorate the exodus from Egypt, and Jews from everywhere poured into Jerusalem to keep the feast. Sacrifices required oxen, sheep, and doves; those who came from far away could not possibly drive livestock for hundreds of miles, so buying on the spot was originally a convenience. Paying the temple tax and making offerings also required designated money, and currency from different places had to be exchanged first. These businesses were originally set up outside the city, and later moved into the outer court of the temple. That courtyard was originally reserved as a place for the Gentiles to pray; now livestock, stalls, and bargaining filled it, and those who wanted to pray quietly had nowhere to set foot; the margins in the exchange and the trading also became a way for some people to make money. Arrangements that were convenient for people had, step by step, become a business that blocked people from drawing near to God. Jesus made a whip of cords and drove out the oxen and sheep and overturned the tables — fierce actions, but not a momentary impulse: the cords were braided on the spot, which shows He acted only after He had seen clearly and thought it through. What He drove out was the trading, not the people who came to worship; toward those holding dove cages, He still used words: "Take these things away." The sense of proportion was there throughout.

The Scripture the disciples remembered on the spot comes from the Psalms: "Because zeal for Your house has eaten me up, And the reproaches of those who reproach You have fallen on me." (Psalm 69:9, NKJV) David's heart was anxious for God's house, anxious as though a fire were burning. In placing these words upon Jesus, John is saying that His fierceness was not temper but caring. A person does not get this angry over something unrelated; He called that place "My Father's house," and He was anxious because His own Father's house had been occupied by others.

The final conversation is the sentence buried deepest in the whole chapter. Those in authority asked Him for a sign as credentials, and the sign He gave was His own death and resurrection: destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up. Those who heard reckoned by stone: this temple took forty-six years to build and is still not finished — you, three days? At the time the disciples also heard it by stone. John specifically records that it was only after Jesus had risen from the dead that they remembered, and only then believed (2:22). This sentence is very important in the Gospel of John: the disciples' understanding is often late in arriving. Some words can only be understood in hindsight, after the thing has happened. There is no shame in this; John himself came through exactly this way, and he honestly wrote this process into his book.

Put the two events side by side, and the beginning and the end explain each other perfectly. At the wedding feast in Cana, Jesus turned the water of ritual into the wine of a wedding feast, saying that He has come to turn dry, tasteless rules into a life with flavor; in the temple, He drove the trading out of His Father's house, saying that He is serious about worship to the point of not allowing people to exchange it for money. And the prophecy in verse 19 gathers the two events into one: in the future, people's drawing near to God will no longer depend on this stone temple, but on His body; He dies and rises again, and the temple is erected anew in Him.

## III. Application

Why does the joy of life always get drunk dry? This chapter's point of entry begins with that sentence at the wedding feast: "They have no wine."

A wedding is probably the day in life when people are most willing to set out the good wine. The days that follow, however, often go in the opposite direction: the burst of energy when one first starts working, the burst of warmth when one first establishes a family, the sense of fullness when one's health is good — slowly they all reach the bottom. It is not that anyone deliberately causes harm; it is that wine runs out by itself. Many people, by middle age, carry a sentence in their hearts they have not spoken aloud: the liveliness is still there, the feast has not yet broken up, but the cup is empty. That John places Jesus' first sign at such a moment is no accident. He wants the reader to remember first: Jesus is not the One who waits until the feast of your life has broken up to come and clean up the mess; He is already standing at the scene at the very moment the wine has just run out and the embarrassment has not yet spread.

Notice again the way He does things. He did not point out the bridegroom's difficulty in public, nor did He seize the opportunity to lecture people on not preparing enough wine. The difficulty was covered over, the joy continued to flow on, and most people did not even know that anything had gone wrong in between. Our fear of coming before God is mostly a fear of this: afraid that our holes will be exposed in public, afraid that old accounts will be dug up. But the Jesus in this chapter first preserves the face of an ordinary person. If you are willing to speak the sentence "I have no wine" to Him, the first thing you hear will probably not be blame.

The remark of the master of the feast, "You have kept the good wine until now," is worth lingering over a while longer. The order in which this world hands things out is mostly good first, inferior afterward: youth, opportunities, applause — the good is at the front, and the further one goes the more it is a matter of making do. Many people dare not look back, and dare not think ahead either, feeling that the best has already passed. Jesus' order happens to be the reverse. He does not bring out leftover inferior goods to make up the number; what He keeps until later is better. This is not a word of comfort; it is His signature in doing things, and He signed it right here at the first sign. To those who feel they have already missed the best years, this sentence is spoken to you: your story, in His hands, does not necessarily go downward.

Having spoken of the wedding feast, we must also speak of the temple, because some readers' aversion to faith comes precisely from here. You have seen people doing business in the name of faith, seen people collecting money in God's name and setting up rules to press on others, and that sentence in your heart — "so this is all it is" — has been held back for a long time. This chapter asks you to see clearly: the one most angry about this matter is not you, but Jesus Himself. It was He who overturned the tables. To reckon Him and those stall-keepers as one gang is to have reckoned the account wrong. He drove out the trading in order to return to people a place where they could quietly draw near to God; even the place where the Gentiles stood mattered to Him. So do not, because there are stall-keepers in the courtyard, conclude that the master of this house is also a merchant. The Master is clearing the place with His own hands.

There is another layer, spoken to those willing to take one step further in. The temple was later really destroyed — the one made of stone — ruined by war in A.D. 70, and never rebuilt. When John wrote this chapter, that event had already happened or was close at hand. In writing "He was speaking of the temple of His body," he is telling the reader: the road to drawing near to God has not been cut off; it has only changed its location — not in a stone building, but in a person who died and rose again. This is why, when Christians speak of drawing near to God, they always speak of coming before Jesus, not of coming before a set of procedures.

## IV. Response

After reading this chapter, take a quiet moment and think about a few questions.

- In your life, is there a "wine" that was once full to the brim and has now reached the bottom? Whom do you most fear seeing this?
- "They have no wine." If you changed this sentence into your own words, how would you state your difficulty? How is saying it to Jesus different from saying it to other people?
- The world's order is to serve the good wine first, and the more one drinks the more inferior it gets. Jesus kept the good wine until now. Can you trust this sentence? Or have you already concluded that all your good days were used up at the front?
- Are the stall-keepers in the temple courtyard one of the reasons you stay away from faith? After reading this chapter, would you reckon Jesus and those stall-keepers separately?

If you are willing, you may follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, my wine has run out, and I cannot refill it myself. If You really are the Lord who kept the good wine until now, please come into the place where I am empty, let me first taste a little of Your joy, and then lead me onward. Amen."

There is also an entrance for continuing onward. In the previous chapter we read chapter 1: that Word became flesh and dwelt among us; in this chapter, He preserved the joy at a wedding feast and was anxious for His Father's house in the temple. In the next chapter we will read John chapter 3: a learned, high-ranking religious leader came to Jesus in the middle of the night, yet what he asked was a question for which even being a good person is not enough. You may also find a Christian friend you trust, point out to him the sentence in this chapter you are least convinced by, and hear how he answers.

## Abstract

Chapter 1 numbers the people by the number of their names; chapter 2 then sets the numbered people in their places: the tabernacle at the centre, the twelve tribes grouped three by three into four camps on the east, south, west, and north, each camp with its own standard and its own captains, and the order of encamping becoming the order of marching — the camp of Judah first, the camp of Reuben second, the tabernacle and the camp of the Levites in the midst of the camps, the camp of Ephraim third, and the camp of Dan hindmost. The chapter's thirty-four verses contain almost no narrative, only a plan of the camp and a list for the march. This sermon reads that plan in the text's own order: first the governing principle — each man to his own standard, encamping over against the tabernacle; then the eastern and southern camps and the first two ranks of the march; then the tabernacle and the Levitical camp in the midst, with the western camp; then the northern camp, the four camp totals, and the grand total, checked against the total of chapter 1, six hundred thousand and three thousand and five hundred and fifty; and finally the close, in which the people encamp and set forth as they were commanded. The thread the sermon traces is this: chapter 1 counts out names, chapter 2 assigns places — numbers without places are still a heap of figures, and places without a centre are only a scattered diagram. The centre of this chapter is no camp and no standard, but the tabernacle: more than six hundred thousand people encamp facing one centre, and set out from that same centre.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 The governing principle: encamping over against the tabernacle, round about it, each under his own standard

(Fact) The chapter opens by naming the speaker and those addressed: "And the LORD spake unto Moses and unto Aaron, saying," (Num 2:1, KJV) Unlike chapter 1, the word is addressed here not to Moses alone but to Aaron as well. The numbering of chapter 1 was commanded to Moses and Aaron together (Num 1:3, Summary); the arrangement of the camp in this chapter is likewise delivered to the two men together. Verse 2 then gives the chapter's governing principle, its outline in advance: "Every man of the children of Israel shall pitch by his own standard, with the ensign of their father's house: far off about the tabernacle of the congregation shall they pitch." (Num 2:2, KJV) The verse works at three levels, and together they draw the whole plan in advance. The first is belonging: every man by his own standard — each person must first know under which standard he belongs. The second is the visible mark: with the ensign of their father's house — belonging is not merely known inwardly; it has a sign that can be seen, by which every house and clan gathers. The third is direction and position: far off about the tabernacle of the congregation — all the camps face the tabernacle and surround it. First belonging, then the mark, then direction; the thirty-odd verses that follow do no more than work these three levels out, camp by camp.

(Fact) The wording of this verse must be set out term by term, because nearly all the chapter's keywords are concentrated here. First, "standard" is degel (Strong's H1714), the great banner of a division, the mark of a camp made of three tribes; each of the four camps has its degel, and each camp's paragraph opens with "the standard of the camp of" that camp. Second, "ensign" is ʾôt (Strong's H226), whose basic sense is a sign or mark; here it denotes each father's house's own smaller ensign, its badge. The two layers of marks stand side by side: the degel is the flag of the great camp, the ʾôt the sign of the father's house — a man gathers first at the ensign of his own house, and is then gathered under the standard of the camp. Third, "pitch/encamp" is ḥānāh (Strong's H2583), to set up tents and dwell in camp; chapter 1 already used it (Num 1:50, 52, 53, Summary), and in this chapter it becomes the running verb — encamping and setting forward are the chapter's pair of basic actions. Fourth, "about / round about" is sāḇîḇ (Strong's H5439), around, on every side: the people do not line up on one side of the tabernacle but surround it on every side. Fifth, "camp" is maḥăneh (Strong's H4264), which denotes both the encampment as a place and the people encamped in it; "the camp of Judah," "the camp of Reuben" in this chapter name both a sector and the whole host within it. Sixth, "armies/hosts" is ṣāḇāʾ (Strong's H6635), the word chapter 1 already used for the people arrayed by the census (Num 1:3, Summary); in this chapter every camp's and every tribe's figure is set out "throughout their armies." With the six words in place, the chapter's plan becomes three-dimensional: this is no crowd spending the night at random in the wilderness, but more than six hundred thousand people, by two layers of signs, in four directions, in the establishment of one host, dwelling round about the tabernacle.

(Application) Mark especially the direction in verse 2: in the Chinese base text the camps encamp "facing the tabernacle." The text does not say the people encamp with their backs to the tabernacle, nor facing the promised land, nor gathered around their own standards; they encamp facing the tabernacle. That is to say, the direction of every tent is decided first by the centre, not by itself: which side a man lives on, and to which camp he belongs, differ from man to man; but where he faces has one answer for the whole camp. The standards are many — each camp has its own; the centre is one — the whole camp shares it. Variety is never the problem in this chapter: twelve tribes, twelve captains, four standards, two layers of ensigns — the text cancels none of them. What it cancels is every man's being his own centre. A community may have many banners; it can have only one centre. The banners may be many; the facing must be one. This verse is therefore not camp administration only; it is a theological declaration: God dwells in the midst of His people, and the people rearrange their dwellings according to their distance from Him and their facing toward Him. First He in the midst, then we round about; first the centre is fixed, then the places — that is this chapter's first lesson, and the root of every order in it.

(Fact) One further matter of distance must not be read past, though this verse states it without figures: the people encamp round about the tabernacle, and between the tabernacle and the tribal camps stands the camp of the Levites (Num 1:53, Summary; and v. 17 of this chapter, the camp of the Levites in the midst of the camps — see section III). In other words, the surrounding is not pressed up close: the camps of the twelve tribes form the outer ring, the Levites the inner ring, and the tabernacle stands at the very centre. (Application) Nearness without confusion, surrounding without trespass — that is precisely the purpose of the Levites' position in chapter 1, worked out on this chapter's plan. God wills His people to dwell around Him, yet not to crowd up to Him each in his own way; order here is not distance for its own sake but the manner that makes nearness possible. Between man and tabernacle stands a ring of appointed keepers; between man and man stand their own standards and ensigns — and every layer of interval is not coldness but protection: protection of the sanctuary from offence, protection of the people from harm through trespass, and protection of every individual from being swallowed up in an enormous crowd. A multitude of more than six hundred thousand, without standards and camp positions, would turn surrounding into crowding; with standards and positions, surrounding becomes the formation of worship. That is what this chapter's plan draws: how a very great people can be both near to God and unconfused with one another.

### II. 08:00-24:00 The eastern and southern camps: the camp of Judah first, the camp of Reuben second

(Fact) After the governing principle, the text sets out the camps one by one, beginning from the east. Why the east first? The text's own phrase is that the east is "toward the rising of the sun": "And on the east side toward the rising of the sun shall they of the standard of the camp of Judah pitch throughout their armies: and Nahshon the son of Amminadab shall be captain of the children of Judah." (Num 2:3, KJV) The eastern camp is formed of Judah, Issachar, and Zebulun, with Judah at its head (vv. 3-8, Summary presentation; captains and figures as in the text): Judah, captain Nahshon the son of Amminadab, 74,600 (v. 4); Issachar, captain Nethaneel the son of Zuar, 54,400 (v. 6); Zebulun, captain Eliab the son of Helon, 57,400 (v. 8). The three tribal figures were added item by item in preparing this sermon: 74,600 plus 54,400 makes 129,000; plus 57,400, 186,400 — in exact agreement with the eastern camp's subtotal at verse 9: "All that were numbered in the camp of Judah were an hundred thousand and fourscore thousand and six thousand and four hundred, throughout their armies. These shall first set forth." (Num 2:9, KJV) The eastern camp is not only the largest of the four; it is also the first rank of the march: its position is the east, its order is first — in this chapter the direction of encamping and the sequence of marching are two faces of one and the same order.

(Fact) The southern camp follows (vv. 10-16, Summary): on the south side, the standard of the camp of Reuben according to their armies. Reuben, captain Elizur the son of Shedeur, 46,500 (v. 11); Simeon, captain Shelumiel the son of Zurishaddai, 59,300 (v. 13); Gad, captain Eliasaph, 45,650 (v. 15) — as to the name of Gad's captain's father: the Chinese base text at verse 14 of this chapter reads the same two characters as at Numbers 1:14, while the Hebrew text and the KJV differ between the two chapters; that is a matter of the underlying text and is dealt with separately in Endnote 4, and the list in this sermon follows each base text in its own place without merging the two levels. The three figures added item by item: 46,500 plus 59,300 makes 105,800; plus 45,650, 151,450 — in exact agreement with the southern camp's subtotal at verse 16: "All that were numbered in the camp of Reuben were an hundred thousand and fifty and one thousand and four hundred and fifty, throughout their armies. And they shall set forth in the second rank." (Num 2:16, KJV) The southern camp is the second rank of the march, following immediately after the eastern camp.

(Fact) Set the lists of the eastern and southern camps beside chapter 1, man by man, and two facts stand out. First, all twelve captains of chapter 1 reappear in this chapter; apart from the one divergent writing of Gad's captain's father's name in the underlying text, the other eleven are identical in name and father's name in both chapters (for the item-by-item check, see Endnote 5). Second, the order of the two lists differs: chapter 1 lists the helpers tribe by tribe (Num 1:5-15, Summary), while this chapter arranges them by camps — three men of the east, then three of the south, within each camp the captain of the leading tribe first and the other two in sequence. The change of order is not an error but a change of diagram: chapter 1's order is the order of a register; this chapter's is the order of a camp plan. The same men stand by tribes in the register and by directions in the plan. (Application) That is a caution for readers of Scripture: when the same names appear twice in different orders, do not rush to call it a contradiction; ask first what diagram has changed. A register answers the question "who has been numbered?"; a camp plan answers "where does each man dwell, and with whom does he march?" God's people need both the register and the plan: a man who knows he has been numbered but not where he stands or with whom he is teamed will still be in confusion when the march begins; and a man who knows a position but is not in the register holds only an empty place. This chapter re-orders the twelve names precisely so that everyone numbered may at the same time find his place and his companions.

(Application) That the eastern camp stands first also deserves a further pause. The camp that leads is not the tribe of Reuben the firstborn, but Judah; the camp of Reuben is in the south and sets forth in the second rank. By order of birth Reuben stands before Judah; by this chapter's camp order Judah stands before Reuben. The text offers no explanation, and states the arrangement exactly as it stands — and a sermon should state it exactly as it stands, without inventing reasons for the text. Yet the statement itself already teaches: camp positions and marching order are not assigned by birth alone; God arranges places in His own sovereignty and purpose, without answering one-to-one to human seniority and standing. Judah's is the largest number among the twelve tribes (the figures of chapter 1, Summary), and the eastern camp's total is the largest of the four; it stands toward the rising of the sun as the first rank of the whole host, the cutting edge of this people. And the firstborn's tribe, in the south as the second rank, is no less numbered, appointed, and indispensable. To stand first is not the only honour, and to stand second is not a demotion: on this chapter's plan, the difference between the first rank and the second is sequence, not worth. What matters is that every rank knows which rank it is, and moves forward when its turn comes — neither seizing the front nor falling behind. The danger is not to be second; the danger is not to know in which rank one stands.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) Commentators have long read the precedence of the eastern camp alongside the thread of Genesis — Jacob's final blessing, and Judah's rising position among his brothers (Genesis 49, Summary); some have also noted the family composition of the four leading tribes — Judah and Reuben, who lead the east and the south, are both sons of Leah, while the composition of the western and northern camps is arranged otherwise (see the sections that follow). This sermon sets out the existence of that inner-biblical comparison only, and builds no account of the arrangement's reasons on family composition, which the text does not state. As for what devices the four standards actually bore, the text itself says not a word; the assignment of images such as a lion to the leading tribes belongs to later tradition and interpretation, not to this chapter, and this sermon keeps it at the level of discussion, never stating it as a fact of the text. Whether the camp plan was a square or a circle, and what the camp's actual dimensions were, are likewise matters on which reconstructions differ; here too the sermon notes the discussion's existence only, and rests its argument on what the text has written: the four sides, the order, and the numbers.

### III. 24:00-42:00 In the midst of the camps: the tabernacle and the camp of the Levites set forward in the centre, and the camp of Ephraim in the west, third

(Fact) The eastern and southern camps set out, the text's pen pauses — it does not jump straight to the western camp, but first inserts the hinge verse of the whole chapter, on the segment at the very centre of the procession when the march begins: "Then the tabernacle of the congregation shall set forward with the camp of the Levites in the midst of the camp: as they encamp, so shall they set forward, every man in his place by their standards." (Num 2:17, KJV) Mark both the verse's position and its content. In position, it stands between the southern camp (vv. 10-16) and the western camp (vv. 18-24), at the chapter's very middle. In content, it speaks not of a new camp but of the position, in the march, of the centre common to all four camps: after the first two ranks have set forth, the tabernacle sets forward, with the camp of the Levites in the midst of the camps. "As they encamp, so shall they set forward" is the general formula of the chapter's order: the plan of encamping is the plan of marching; the formation in which they dwell is the formation in which they travel; no second scheme is drawn up. Encamped, who is east and who is south, with the tabernacle in the midst; marching, still the east first, the south next, the centre following, the west and the north bringing up the rear — the plan does not change; the plan simply passes from rest into motion.

(Fact) This verse also re-states the Levites' special position. In chapter 1 the Levites were not numbered in the army register, yet were appointed to encamp round about the tabernacle of testimony and to minister to it (Num 1:47-53, Summary); in this chapter, their position on the marching plan is equally clear: in none of the four camps, but in the midst of the camps, travelling with the tabernacle. They are absent from the four camps' combined number (see the total in section IV and v. 33), yet the centre of the camp plan cannot do without them. (Application) This is the consistent teaching of the two chapters, and at this chapter it must be said again, and more concretely: not to be in the number is not to be absent from the plan; not to be arrayed in the army is not to fail to march with the host. The segment the Levitical camp travels is the most vital segment of the whole host — they escort the tabernacle at the heart of the procession, with two ranks opening the way before them and two ranks guarding behind. Those set apart appear on no establishment table of the four camps, yet they stand on the centre line of the whole camp. A community's centre is never made by stacking up numbers; it is constituted by the tabernacle where God Himself dwells, and by those who faithfully escort it. However large the four camps, without this middle segment they would be four unrelated squares. The "midst" of the Levitical camp joins the four camps into one camp.

(Fact) After the hinge verse, the text continues with the western camp (vv. 18-24, Summary): on the west side, the standard of the camp of Ephraim according to their armies. Ephraim, captain Elishama the son of Ammihud, 40,500 (v. 19); Manasseh, captain Gamaliel the son of Pedahzur, 32,200 (v. 21); Benjamin, captain Abidan the son of Gideoni, 35,400 (v. 23). The three figures added item by item: 40,500 plus 32,200 makes 72,700; plus 35,400, 108,100 — in exact agreement with the western camp's subtotal at verse 24: "All that were numbered of the camp of Ephraim were an hundred thousand and eight thousand and an hundred, throughout their armies. And they shall go forward in the third rank." (Num 2:24, KJV) The western camp is the smallest of the four, 108,100 — not even six-tenths of the eastern camp — and its marching order is third, after the tabernacle and the camp of the Levites.

(Application) The western camp's composition is worth checking against chapter 1's old account. Its leader Ephraim and its second tribe Manasseh are Joseph's two sons; chapter 1 has already shown that Joseph counts as two tribes while Levi is not numbered in the army — one tribe out, one tribe doubled, and the tribes remain twelve (Numbers 1, Summary). In this chapter the two stand in one camp, together in the west, together in the third rank: shoulder to shoulder on the camp plan, as they stand side by side in Jacob's blessing (Genesis 48, Summary). Look again at the figures: Manasseh's 32,200 is the smallest of the twelve tribes, and it now forms, with Ephraim and Benjamin, the smallest of the four camps, marching third — by human reckoning, the weak division of the tribes. Yet the text gives the western camp exactly the wording it gives the eastern: the same "throughout their armies," the same recording of captains by name, the same "shall go forward" in its rank. Smallness, in this chapter, never means vagueness: the smallest camp has a position just as clear, an order just as fixed, and an absence at the march just as impossible. If the plan lacked its west, it would not be "round about" but only three sides; God's people surround the tabernacle not by the strongest side holding up the whole diagram, but by every side standing in its own direction. The small camp keeping its small side and the great camp keeping its great side are, on this plan, one and the same faithfulness.

(Fact) By now the chapter's whole order of march can be set out in full, and a sermon must set it out by the text's own verse order, not fill it in from impression: first rank, the camp of Judah in the east (v. 9); second rank, the camp of Reuben in the south (v. 16); then the tabernacle sets forward, with the camp of the Levites in the midst of the camps (v. 17); third rank, the camp of Ephraim in the west (v. 24); hindmost, the camp of Dan in the north (v. 31; see the next section). Two ranks ahead, the centre in the middle, two ranks behind — the tabernacle's position in the marching column is exactly its position in the encampment: the very centre. (Application) That means that every time Israel broke camp and marched, it did not leave worship behind at the old site and go off to war by itself; worship itself travelled in the middle of the procession. God does not wait at the campsite for their return; He dwells at the centre of the column and walks with them. Encamped, He is in the midst; marching, He is in the midst. Stopped, He is the centre; moving, He is still the centre. For a people in the wilderness, that may be the most important lesson of all: the ultimate purpose of order is not a handsome diagram, but that the God who dwells in the midst should remain in the midst of His people whether they halt or walk — and all the people have to do, halt or walk, is to keep the same plan, every man in his place, each to his own standard.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The northern camp of Dan hindmost, and the four camps' grand total of 603,550

(Fact) The last camp set out is the northern (vv. 25-31, Summary): on the north side, the standard of the camp of Dan by their armies. Dan, captain Ahiezer the son of Ammishaddai, 62,700 (v. 26); Asher, captain Pagiel the son of Ocran, 41,500 (v. 28); Naphtali, captain Ahira the son of Enan, 53,400 (v. 30). The three figures added item by item: 62,700 plus 41,500 makes 104,200; plus 53,400, 157,600 — in exact agreement with the northern camp's subtotal at verse 31, whose closing words in the KJV run: "All they that were numbered in the camp of Dan were an hundred thousand and fifty and seven thousand and six hundred. They shall go hindmost with their standards." (Num 2:31, KJV) Mark the KJV's own wording here: Dan's camp does not set forth under a rank number at all, but "hindmost with their standards" — last, yet expressly by their standards. In quotations, function words and near-synonyms are exactly where a text is most easily altered by a copyist's or a preacher's hand; this sermon's quotations follow the base text throughout and are never smoothed. The northern camp is the second largest of the four, yet it marches hindmost — the duty of the rear guard falls on the shoulders of the second largest camp.

(Application) The hindmost position deserves a sermon's further words, because men most easily despise standing last. In a march, the last rank is no unimportant tail: the dust raised by the ranks ahead is walked through by the last rank; stragglers and dropped loads are gathered up by the last rank; and danger from behind is met first by the last rank. The text gives this charge not to the smallest camp, the west, but to Dan's, the second largest of the four — a rear guard needs strength, and the arrangement has its own weight. More important still, verse 31 says this last rank goes "with their standards": to stand last is not to fall away from one's own standard and trail along in disorder; the further back a man marches, the more he must keep to his standard, for when the ranks ahead are out of sight the standard is his direction. In this chapter's order there is no position of which it can be said "anywhere will do": the first rank has the first rank's charge, the hindmost the hindmost's charge. If the first rank does not set forth, the whole host does not move; if the hindmost does not follow, the whole host is incomplete. When God arrays more than six hundred thousand people in one column, He writes even the last man's position into the text — the hindmost charge is given in the same form of words, and with the same solemnity, as the first rank's "These shall first set forth." To be solemnly appointed at the very end is worth more than to press to the front unclaimed by anyone.

(Fact) The four camps set out, verse 32 gives the grand total of the whole camp: "These are those which were numbered of the children of Israel by the house of their fathers: all those that were numbered of the camps throughout their hosts were six hundred thousand and three thousand and five hundred and fifty." (Num 2:32, KJV) Now close the chapter's whole account from the beginning; in preparing this sermon every item was added by hand. The four camp subtotals: east 186,400; south 151,450; west 108,100; north 157,600. Added in sequence: 186,400 plus 151,450 makes 337,850; plus 108,100, 445,950; plus 157,600, 603,550 — in exact agreement with the total at verse 32, six hundred thousand and three thousand and five hundred and fifty, and identical with the total of chapter 1 at verse 46 (Num 1:46, Summary). Check one level deeper: each camp subtotal is in turn the sum of its three tribal items (east: 74,600 plus 54,400 plus 57,400; south: 46,500 plus 59,300 plus 45,650; west: 40,500 plus 32,200 plus 35,400; north: 62,700 plus 41,500 plus 53,400), and the twelve tribal items are precisely the figures of chapter 1's tribe-by-tribe census, not one of them altered. The accounts of the two chapters, computed by two different arrangements — chapter 1 by tribes, chapter 2 by camps — agree to the last man. (Application) That agreement deserves to be stated with emphasis. Re-grouped under a new arrangement and re-ordered in a new sequence, the total still fits exactly — proof that these two chapters' presentation is not a heap of lists assembled at random, but two openings of one and the same register: opened by tribes, it is a genealogy; opened by camps, it is a marching plan. The genealogy guarantees that no man is missed in the count; the marching plan guarantees that no man is left without a place. Figures that survive two methods of reckoning, and places that survive re-grouping — in God's hand His people are both exactly counted and clearly arrayed.

(Fact) After the total, verse 33 appends the Levitical exception, answering chapter 1's exception across the two chapters: "But the Levites were not numbered among the children of Israel; as the LORD commanded Moses." (Num 2:33, KJV) The total of 603,550 is therefore the total of the four camps' armies; it does not include the Levites. The Levites are not inside the total, yet they are at the very centre of this chapter's plan (v. 17). (Application) By this point in the two chapters, the Levites' "not among them" has been stated three times over: chapter 1 says they were not numbered among the people (Summary), and that they stood round about the tabernacle (Summary); this chapter says they were not numbered among the children of Israel, and that they are in the midst of the camps. The repetition is not verbosity; it is lest the reader miss the paradox: the greatest figure of the whole camp does not contain them, and the most central position of the whole camp is theirs. Those who hear this sermon today should remember it firmly: if a man's importance is measured only by which table of figures he appears in, the Levites will be missed — and to miss the Levites is to mistake the centre of the camp plan for an empty space. The table of figures answers "how many?"; the camp plan answers "where?" God's people, reading their own worth, must read both tables — and must never read only the first.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: as they pitched by their standards, so they set forward — according to all that the LORD commanded Moses

(Fact) The chapter closes at verse 34: "And the children of Israel did according to all that the LORD commanded Moses: so they pitched by their standards, and so they set forward, every one after their families, according to the house of their fathers." (Num 2:34, KJV) Across thirty-four verses, the chapter runs from "the LORD spake unto Moses and unto Aaron" (v. 1) to "according to all that the LORD commanded Moses" (v. 34): between them stand the governing principle, the four camps, the figures, the order, and the exception; at head and tail stand speaking and obeying. As in chapter 1, the chapter records no speech of response from the people — their response is the completed plan of encampment and the marshalled column of the march. The phrase of each man returning to his own standard returns at the close (compare v. 17), and the chapter's theme is fastened tight only in its final verse: every one after their families — first the belonging of house and clan, then the place under the standard; first a man owns his house, then he takes his stand.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, the centre of the order is the tabernacle, not any standard: however neat the four camps, if the centre is not the tabernacle where God dwells, they are only a well-drilled army; every direction, standard, and figure in this chapter is generated by facing the tabernacle. Second, the unit of the order is belonging, not the isolated individual: the text never places a man alone; a man always returns first to his house and his clan, and then to his camp and his standard; a man finds his place not by pressing to the front, but by knowing to whom he belongs and with whom he is teamed. Third, the test of the order is the march, not the encampment: a plan drawn ever so well at rest is paper if it collapses on the move; the people's obedience is to use one and the same plan for encamping and for setting forth — as they encamp, so they set forward. To worship at rest, and still to walk in the formation of worship when risen — such a people can walk the whole wilderness through.

(New Testament reading) Two New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. The first is Revelation 4-5: John sees a throne set in heaven, and around the throne the twenty-four elders and the four living creatures, and angels round about, their number ten thousand times ten thousand (Revelation 4-5, Summary) — the earthly camp plan centred on the tabernacle and the heavenly worship centred on the throne answer one another in the very sense of surrounding; the people encamped on every side in this chapter rehearse, in the New Testament reading, a formation of worship gathered around a centre. The second is Paul's word on the church's assembly in 1 Corinthians 14: let all things be done decently and in order (1 Cor 14:40, Summary) — what Paul requires is not rigid ceremony, but that the worship of many be made possible by order, just as this chapter's order exists so that more than six hundred thousand people may draw near together and set forth together. (Application) But the layers must be kept straight: Numbers 2 is first of all itself — the arraying of four camps at Sinai, the figures of the twelve tribes, and the order of the march. The New Testament reading is the light in which the church, in Christ, looks back on it; it must not be used to dissolve this chapter's plan. The more solid this chapter's accounting — camp subtotals addable item by item, the grand total agreeing with chapter 1, the order checkable verse by verse — the more weight the reading layer's comparison carries: between a people on earth encamped in order around the tabernacle and a multitude in heaven gathered around the throne lies the long leading of one and the same God of order.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's own hands. Most of us would like to stand in the east, to be in the first rank, to belong to a conspicuous standard; but this chapter tells us that among the four camps not one is placed at random and not one rank is dispensable — the western camp is the smallest, yet it must keep the west; the northern camp is last, yet it must go hindmost with its standards; the Levites are not in the number, yet they stand at the very centre of the camp. The question for us is never "can I move to a better place?" but "under which standard do I belong, on which side do I stand, and when the march begins am I still in my own place?" The centre is already fixed — the God who dwells in the midst of His people has not moved — and what those round about have to do is simply this: each to his own standard, encamping over against the tabernacle, setting forward as they encamp. May we surround Him when we halt, and follow Him when we rise, each faithful in his own direction, until the whole camp arrives together.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 2 first showed me only sides and standards: on the east, an hundred thousand and fourscore thousand and six thousand and four hundred; on the south, an hundred thousand and fifty and one thousand and four hundred and fifty; on the west, an hundred thousand and eight thousand and an hundred; on the north, an hundred thousand and fifty and seven thousand and six hundred. Reading on, I saw that the centre of this plan is no camp at all, but the tabernacle: the hosts of the four camps pitch facing it, round about it, and when they set forward, they set forward as they encamp, with the tabernacle in the midst of the camp. I am often restless about my own place: I think my rank too far back, my camp too small, and I envy those who set forth first. But the text comforts me: the camp of Ephraim is the smallest, yet it keeps its own side; the camp of Dan goes hindmost, yet it goes with their standards; and the Levites, who were not numbered among the children of Israel, stand in the midst of the camp. In God's camp a place is never seized; it is appointed. Faithfulness is not where a man stands, but standing where he is appointed and not moving from it. So I pray today: Lord, let me first know that Thou art the centre of the camp, and take no standard for my centre; and let me rest under my own standard, keeping the side Thou hast appointed me, setting forward as I encamp — neither pressing to the front, nor lagging behind, nor leaving my place when the camp moves on.

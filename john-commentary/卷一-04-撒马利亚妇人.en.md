# Volume One · Article 4: The One Who Came to Draw Water at Noon

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «卷一 · 第 4 篇　中午来打水的人».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

- Author: Guangyi Zhao
- Core Scripture: John 4:1-42

---

The previous article took place at night. A learned man of standing came to see Jesus under cover of darkness. This article moves to midday. The person who comes has no name and no standing, yet appears alone by the well at the hottest hour of the day, when no one else is there. In the previous article, Jesus spoke to a teacher about being born again; in this one, He speaks to a woman who was avoiding people about living water.

John placed these two conversations one after the other; it was not a casual arrangement. One person came at night, the other at noon; one had everything, the other could not speak of anything. Yet what Jesus spoke to them about was, in fact, the same thing: the well inside a person's heart that can never be filled needs a different source of water.

## I. The Scripture

At the beginning of the account, John first explains why Jesus was there:

> He left Judea and departed again to Galilee. But He needed to go through Samaria. So He came to a city of Samaria which is called Sychar, near the plot of ground that Jacob gave to his son Joseph. Now Jacob's well was there. Jesus therefore, being wearied from His journey, sat thus by the well. It was about the sixth hour. (John 4:3-6)

The sixth hour is about twelve o'clock noon, just when the sun is at its fiercest in the day. A woman came alone to draw water, and Jesus opened the conversation by asking her for a drink:

> A woman of Samaria came to draw water. Jesus said to her, "Give Me a drink." For His disciples had gone away into the city to buy food. Then the woman of Samaria said to Him, "How is it that You, being a Jew, ask a drink from me, a Samaritan woman?" For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans. (John 4:7-9)

Jesus did not argue with her about identity; He first turned the topic to water:

> Jesus answered and said to her, "If you knew the gift of God, and who it is who says to you, 'Give Me a drink,' you would have asked Him, and He would have given you living water." (John 4:10)

The woman did not understand what living water meant; she only thought of this well. Jesus said:

> "Whoever drinks of this water will thirst again, but whoever drinks of the water that I shall give him will never thirst. But the water that I shall give him will become in him a fountain of water springing up into everlasting life." (John 4:13-14)

The woman said, Sir, give me this water, that I may not thirst, nor come here to draw. But Jesus suddenly changed the subject and said, Go, call your husband, and come here. The woman said, I have no husband. Jesus said:

> The woman answered and said, "I have no husband." Jesus said to her, "You have well said, 'I have no husband,' for you have had five husbands, and the one whom you now have is not your husband; in that you spoke truly." (John 4:17-18)

When the woman heard this, she first said that He was a prophet, and then asked about whether worship should be on this mountain or that one. Jesus told her that those who truly worship the Father must worship Him in spirit and truth. Toward the end of the conversation, the woman said she knew that the Messiah was coming. Jesus answered with a sentence that carries great weight in the whole of this Gospel:

> Jesus said to her, "I who speak to you am He." (John 4:26)

At this point in the conversation, the disciples returned. John remembered the woman's next action in fine detail:

> The woman then left her waterpot, went her way into the city, and said to the men, "Come, see a Man who told me all things that I ever did. Could this be the Christ?" (John 4:28-29)

First, let us clarify two expressions.

One is "living water." In the usage of that time, living water did not mean water with miraculous effects; it meant flowing water, spring water, river water, as opposed to the still water stored in a well. When the woman heard "living water," this was also the first meaning she thought of: You have nothing to draw with, and the well is deep; where then do You get that living water? But using this everyday word, Jesus was speaking of another layer: the water He gives is not outside a person; it is to become a fountain within the person. The way the Lü Zhenzhong Translation handles wordplays of this kind can serve as a reference; when reading, one must keep the water outside and the fountain inside distinct.

The other is "spirit and truth." The Greek original, translated literally, is "in spirit and in truth." The meaning is not that in worship one's emotions should be a little more engaged and one's attitude a little more earnest; it is that worship is not a contest over location, but concerns whether a person truly comes before God, and whether he knows Him according to the truth. This phrase will be discussed again in the exposition below.

## II. Exposition

First, look at that word "needed." Jesus was going from Judea to Galilee, with Samaria lying between them. The Jews considered the Samaritans impure in bloodline and impure in faith, and would rather take the long way around across the Jordan than spend a night in Samaria. So on the map, passing through Samaria was actually the shortest route, but it was not the route the Jews normally took. John wrote that He "needed to go through" it; on the surface this speaks of the route, but as you read on you realize that there is another meaning in this necessity: there was a person He had to meet.

The origin of the Samaritans goes back several hundred years. After the northern kingdom of Israel was destroyed by Assyria, foreign peoples were moved in and intermarried with those who remained, and their descendants were the Samaritans. They too read the Pentateuch and worshiped God, only their center of worship was not in Jerusalem but on Mount Gerizim. The Jews regarded them as mixed; they regarded the Jews as self-righteous about their orthodoxy. With several hundred years of prejudice, the woman put it in one sentence: Jews have no dealings with Samaritans. What is more, she was a woman and He was a man; she had come to draw water, and He was a stranger sitting there resting His feet. By opening His mouth to ask for water, Jesus crossed three lines at once — ethnic group, faith, and the distinction between man and woman. Moreover, He was the one who spoke first, and He was also the one who first asked something of another. This order is very important in the whole chapter: it was not that the woman first found something; it was that Jesus first sat by the well waiting for her.

Jacob's well was not just any well, either. Sychar is near Shechem, the place where Abraham first built an altar after entering Canaan, and also the place where Jacob bought land and gave it to his son Joseph. When the woman mentioned Jacob, there was pride in her words: Our father Jacob gave us this well — are You greater than he? This is somewhat like Nicodemus's confidence in the previous article, and yet not entirely like it. Nicodemus relied on his own cultivation and learning; she relied on this well left by her ancestor. People always have to rely on something to prove that their branch is not counted as outsiders. Jesus did not belittle this well; He only pointed out a fact that no one could deny: whoever drinks of this water will thirst again. However ancient the well, it cannot solve the matter of coming to draw water every day for a whole lifetime.

Then comes the sentence that turns most sharply. The woman had just said, Please give me this living water, and Jesus said, Go, call your husband. It looks like a digression; in fact it is a step deeper. Living water is not a drink that lets a person never have to face the past from now on. Jesus first touched the part she was least willing to mention: five husbands, and the one she now had was not her husband. Scripture gives no details about these few words — whether they had died, divorced, or cast her off, we do not know, and there is no need to invent a résumé for her. What can be certain is that this was a history of relationships repeatedly broken. Jesus spoke very accurately, yet there was not a single word of condemnation. He stated the facts, and yet did not nail the person to the facts. The woman's words, "I have no husband," were originally evasive words, yet Jesus said, In that you spoke truly. He first affirmed her rare honesty, and then led that honesty one step further.

The woman then immediately turned the topic to the place of worship: on this mountain, or in Jerusalem? Some say she was changing the subject, and that may be partly true. But do not belittle this question either; this was a real question the Samaritans and the Jews had argued over for several hundred years, concerning whose worship counted. Jesus's answer took the side of neither: the hour is coming when you will neither on this mountain, nor in Jerusalem, worship the Father. It is not that place no longer matters; it is that the worshipers the Father seeks have changed — they must worship in spirit and truth. Behind disputes about place, people are often asking, Where am I accepted? Jesus's answer is that being accepted is not tied to a mountain; it is tied to whether you truly come, and whether you know the One you worship.

The most surprising stroke in the whole chapter is that it is here that Jesus stated His own identity most plainly. Before the Jews, He often used parables and counter-questions; to this Samaritan woman, He said directly, I who speak to you am He. In the Gospel of John, this is the first time Jesus stated so clearly that He is the Messiah, and the one who heard it was not a priest, not a teacher, but a woman who came to draw water at noon and was avoiding people. To what kind of person God entrusts the most important words — this chapter gives a surprising answer.

The ending is also surprising. She left her waterpot behind. The waterpot was her whole reason for coming to the well; she set it down and ran into the city, which shows she had encountered something greater than drawing water. The testimony she bore to the people of the city was completely different from Nicodemus's restraint in the previous article; she almost shouted it: Come and see! What deserves even more attention is the response of the people of the city. At first they came because of her words; later, after staying with Jesus for two days and hearing Him with their own ears, they said to the woman, Now we believe, not because of what you said, for we ourselves have heard Him and we know that this is indeed the Christ, the Savior of the world (see John 4:39-42). This is exactly the proper place of testimony: to bring people to Jesus, then step back and let people hear for themselves and believe for themselves. The title "Savior of the world" is spoken in the Gospel of John only here, by a group of people of Gentile background — which is also very thought-provoking.

## III. Application

The entry point of this article is thirst.

The woman understood the word thirst best. She came to draw water every day, and every day she thirsted again. Jesus's words, "Whoever drinks of this water will thirst again," placed in her days, were not a metaphor; they were her daily life. Our days are much the same, except the well has changed its appearance. Some people draw water in relationships, one relationship after another, each time at the beginning feeling that this time it is finally right, and after a while feeling empty again. Some draw water in work and achievement: promoted one grade, given one raise, and after a few months unable to stay excited, they have to chase the next target. Some draw water in their phones, scrolling until midnight, the more they scroll the more tired they become, and the more tired they become the less they can stop. These things in themselves are not necessarily bad; water is not bad either. The problem is that they can only quench thirst for a while, and after quenching it one must come again, and come more and more frequently.

Here Jesus did not tell the woman to first tidy up her life cleanly, and then come to receive living water. The order was exactly the opposite. He first sat by the well, first spoke, first handed over the living water, and only then touched her past. This order is very important for many people who do not dare to draw near to the faith. Many people have a plan in their hearts: Wait until I have cleaned up this mess, wait until I have quit this habit, wait until I look presentable, then I will believe. But the woman's story says: if you had cleaned it all up, you would not be coming alone at noon to draw water. People come to Jesus never because they are already presentable; it is because they are thirsty. Thirst itself is the qualification.

Next, let us talk about hiding. Scripture does not explicitly say why she chose this hour to draw water. Early morning and evening, when it is cool, were the times women came in groups to draw water and chat along the way. For one person to come under the blazing sun, very likely she did not want to meet people. This inference cannot be written as a settled conclusion, but it reads very plausibly, because in her later words there is hiding everywhere: hiding from the topic of a husband, hiding from her own past, hiding inside disputes about ancestors and theology. People who have hidden for a long time all understand: you can hide from people, but you cannot hide from yourself. At night, when you cannot sleep, those matters are all there, not one missing. The way Jesus treated her is worth looking at slowly: He did not expose her in public — by the well there were only the two of them; nor did He pretend not to know — His words were precisely accurate. When being spoken to exactly and not being condemned happen at the same time, the tightly locked door in a person's heart may open a crack. If you are afraid that after believing, your past will be turned out and put on public display, this chapter first tells you: the One who comes looking for you has first left you room to speak alone.

There is also the waterpot. She set down the waterpot and went into the city; this detail is almost a picture for people bound by the past. The waterpot held her daily livelihood, and it also held her labels: the one who comes to draw water, the one who has had five husbands. When she went into the city calling people, she did not first go back and change into a respectable identity; she went with the very past that had just been spoken out. Nor did the people of the city close their ears because of her reputation. The most shameful segment of a person's life, after Jesus had touched it, actually became her reason for bringing others out. This is not to say the past does not matter, nor that broken relationships are worth boasting about; it is to say that in Jesus's hands, the past no longer has to be hush money, taken out for a lifetime to plug one's own mouth. You do not have to wait until everyone in the city has forgotten your affairs before you are qualified to open your mouth and speak of what you have encountered.

Finally, a word about worship, spoken for those who have been disappointed with religion. The woman asked about place; what we often ask today is about form: which church is good, which ceremony is right, which tradition is pure. Jesus put all of these further back. What the Father seeks is those who worship in spirit and truth. The word "truth" — honesty — is a hidden thread through this chapter. The woman took only a small step from evasion to honesty, and Jesus acknowledged that small step. If you are tired of red tape and tired of performative piety, you might remember: what He wants is not a performance; it is a real person.

## IV. Response

Having read this far, you might pause and think about a few questions.

- What is the well you go to every day now to draw water? Is it relationships, achievement, recognition, or that bit of liveliness in your phone you cannot stop scrolling? When was the last time it truly kept you from thirsting?
- Jesus said to the woman, You have had five husbands. If this sentence were changed to speak of your past, which sentence would you most fear being spoken out? What you fear — is it being spoken to exactly itself, or the condemnation after being spoken to?
- The woman set down her waterpot and went into the city. Is there a pot in your hands you have held too long, holding old labels, holding that way of living you repeat every day yet which cannot quench your thirst?
- The people of the city finally said, Now we believe, not because of what you said, for we ourselves have heard Him. Are you willing to give yourself a chance to hear personally as well, rather than only hearing other people's relayed evaluations of Jesus?

If you are willing, you can follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, I admit I have drunk a lot of water, and I am still thirsty. I do not want to hide anymore. If You truly have living water that can become a fountain in me, please first give me a little, and let me taste what it means to no longer depend on that old well to live. Amen."

There are also entrances for continuing onward. In the previous article we read chapter 3, where Jesus received a teacher at night; in this article, He waited at noon for a woman who was avoiding people. In the next article we will read John chapter 5, and go to the pool of Bethesda, where a man who had been ill for thirty-eight years was lying; the first words Jesus asked him were, Do you want to be made well? You can also find a Christian friend you trust, and ask him to read this chapter through with you once more; as you read, notice whether, in those few sentences in which Jesus touched the woman's past, there is a single word of condemnation.

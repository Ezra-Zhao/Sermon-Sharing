## Executive Summary (approx. 100 words)

Genesis 5 is a genealogy in which the recurring refrain "and he died" appears across ten generations. Death became the fixed conclusion for fallen humanity, with Enoch as the sole exception—the Bible does not record that he died, but only that he walked with God for three hundred years and God took him. This sermon examines three realities through this genealogy: death is real, but it is not the final word; in a generation where everyone goes about daily life as usual, walking with God is a viable way forward; and this line of walking with God was passed down generation after generation, ultimately bearing the fruit of saving an entire household through Noah.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Introduction · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

In our previous message, we concluded Genesis 4: Cain's descendants built cities and forged tools, while Seth's descendants began to call upon the name of the Lord. The two lines diverged.

Today we turn to chapter 5. To be honest, chapter 5 is the very chapter many people are most tempted to skip when reading the Bible: a string of names, one age after another—who lived how many years, who begot whom, how many more years he lived, and what happened in the end. It reads like a telephone directory.

Yet I urge everyone to slow down today. If this chapter were truly dry and tedious, the Bible would not have devoted an entire chapter to it, recording each generation and each age with such deliberate care. Within this chapter lies the heaviest recurring phrase in the entire Bible, as well as its brightest exception. The heavy part consists of those three words: "and he died." The bright part is one man: Enoch.

Let us first read the key passages:

"In the day that God created man, He made him in the likeness of God. He created them male and female, and blessed them and called them Mankind in the day they were created." (Gen. 5:1-2)

"And Adam lived one hundred and thirty years, and begot a son in his own likeness, after his image, and named him Seth. ... So all the days that Adam lived were nine hundred and thirty years; and he died." (Gen. 5:3, 5)

"Enoch lived sixty-five years, and begot Methuselah. After he begot Methuselah, Enoch walked with God three hundred years, and had sons and daughters. So all the days of Enoch were three hundred and sixty-five years. And Enoch walked with God; and he was not, for God took him." (Gen. 5:21-24)

"And Noah was five hundred years old, and Noah begot Shem, Ham, and Japheth." (Gen. 5:32)

Ten generations, from Adam to Noah. We will look at this chapter in three sections:

First, 5:1-20, from Adam to Jared—the conclusion of every generation is that phrase, "and he died." Death became the most certain reality for humanity outside of Eden.
Second, 5:21-24, Enoch—right in the middle of the genealogy, Scripture pauses to speak singularly of how one man walked with God, and how God took him.
Third, 5:25-32, Methuselah, Lamech, Noah—the one who walked with God was not alone; the line of walking with God was passed on, all the way to the one who would prepare an ark for his whole household.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we give You thanks. You have preserved a seemingly dry genealogy in the Scriptures to tell us that You remember every name and You number every day. We pray that today, amid the repetition of 'and he died,' You would cause us to hear Your heart, and that in Enoch we might see a viable path—walking with You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: The End of Every Generation Is "And He Died" (5:1-20)

### 1.1 The Two Familiar Phrases at the Opening of the Genealogy: Man Was Made in the Likeness of God

Chapter 5 does not begin with death; it begins with creation.

When Moses wrote chapter 5, he first looked back and reiterated chapter 1: "In the day that God created man, He made him in the likeness of God" (Gen. 5:1). "Likeness" is the Hebrew word *demuth* (דְּמוּת); see the endnotes for its transliteration and definition [1]. It forms a pair often used alongside the word for "image," *tselem* (צֶלֶם), in chapter 1 [2]. Man bears the image of God. Why must this be stated once more in chapter 5? Because in chapter 3 humanity fell, and in chapter 4 human life was taken. A reader might easily assume: perhaps the image was lost. Moses says: No. Even after the fall, God still affirms this first—man was made in His likeness, He still blessed them, and He still called them Mankind.

Yet immediately in verse 3, the tone becomes somber: "Adam ... begot a son in his own likeness, after his image." Seth was like Adam. Adam was like God, and Seth was like Adam—the aspect that bears God's likeness remained, but the corrupted aspect was also passed down. This is the reality of genealogies: what we pass on to our children is never merely our appearance and estate, but also our disposition, our habits, and that straying heart. Romans 5:12 explains this line thoroughly: "Therefore, just as through one man sin entered the world, and death through sin, and thus death spread to all men, because all sinned."

### 1.2 Over Nine Hundred Years Old, Yet Still "And He Died"

Reading further, notice the repetitive sentence pattern. Every generation follows three steps: he lived a certain number of years and begot a son; he lived a certain number of years after that, having sons and daughters; all his days were a certain number of years—and he died.

Adam lived nine hundred and thirty years, and he died. Seth lived nine hundred and twelve years, and he died. Enosh lived nine hundred and five years, Cainan nine hundred and ten years, Mahalalel eight hundred and ninety-five years, Jared nine hundred and sixty-two years. Each lived longer than the last, reaching lifespans unthinkable to us today. Yet no matter how long the years, the final punctuation was identical: "and he died"—in Hebrew, *wayyamot* (וַיָּמֹת), "and he died," tolling like a pendulum one after another through the genealogy [3].

These repeated tolls are in fact the echo of the warning in chapter 2: "for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die" (Gen. 2:17). God did not break His word. The lesson in the garden became the common conclusion for ten generations in chapter 5. Ecclesiastes says: "One generation passes away, and another generation comes; but the earth abides forever" (Eccl. 1:4). Psalm 90 is a prayer written by Moses himself; when writing this genealogy, he likely understood this verse best: "The days of our lives are seventy years; and if by reason of strength they are eighty years, yet their boast is only labor and sorrow; for it is soon cut off, and we fly away" (Ps. 90:10).

Brothers and sisters, longevity is not an exemption from death. Between nine hundred years and ninety years, the only difference in the outcome is the time of arrival. Chapter 5 sets this reality straight first, so that we can comprehend how brilliantly Enoch's exception shines.

### 1.3 Hope in the Names: Generation After Generation Still Bearing Children, Still Naming Them

Yet chapter 5 is not entirely somber. Notice that in this genealogy, every generation engaged in two acts: bearing children and naming them.

Even knowing they would die, they still dared to have children; even having just buried a father, they still gave names to newborn infants. This was not numbness; it was a resilient hope found in Genesis—the blessing of chapter 1 to "be fruitful and multiply" was still in effect, and the promise of chapter 3 that the seed would bruise the serpent's head had not failed. Therefore, this family refused to let death write the final word of the story.

Proverbs 13:22 says: "A good man leaves an inheritance to his children’s children." What the people in this chapter left behind as an inheritance was not merely livestock and land; it was the spark of hope behind those names. Seth means "appointed, granted" [4], marking Eve's recognition that God had appointed another; Enosh means "frail, mortal man" [5], representing the parents' honest acknowledgment of human limitation. Later in this chapter, the hope spoken when Lamech names his son will ring out even louder, which we will explore in the third part.

To summarize the first part in a single sentence: **Chapter 5 first writes man's end with complete honesty: no matter how long one lives, it still ends with "and he died." Only when we face death honestly will we earnestly seek the path that is not closed by death.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part 2: Enoch—The Man Who Walked with God Has No "And He Died" Recorded (5:21-24)

### 2.1 Right in the Middle of the Genealogy, Scripture Pauses to Tell of One Man

When we read to verse 21, the rhythm changes.

Every preceding generation was a routine ledger, but when it comes to Enoch, Moses breaks the mold and writes several extra sentences: "Enoch lived sixty-five years, and begot Methuselah. After he begot Methuselah, Enoch walked with God three hundred years, and had sons and daughters. So all the days of Enoch were three hundred and sixty-five years. And Enoch walked with God; and he was not, for God took him." (Gen. 5:21-24)

Please notice two things. First, his lifespan is the shortest in the entire chapter—only 365 years, while others easily lived eight or nine hundred years. Second, that concluding phrase for everyone else, "and he died," is missing when it comes to him. In its place are two repeated words: "Enoch walked with God"—stated twice.

In the Bible, repetition is an exclamation mark. The most repeated phrase in this chapter is "and he died," which appears eight times; yet in Enoch’s passage, Moses uses another repetition to push back against the repetition of death: walked with God, walked with God.

### 2.2 What It Means to "Walk with God": Not a One-Time Experience, but a 300-Year Daily Life

"Walked with God"—in Hebrew, the Hitpael form of *halak* is used, which conveys the sense of walking back and forth, continually walking[6]. It is not taking a single trip; it is walking to and fro. It is not a fleeting impulse; it is a way of life. Later, the Bible uses the same phrase to describe how Noah "walked with God" (Gen. 6:9), and when Abraham was called to "walk before Me" (cf. Gen. 17:1 in the Lu Chen-chung translation; the Chinese Union Version renders it "walk before Me and be blameless"). The Book of Micah summarizes this path into words that everyone can understand: "He has shown you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Mic. 6:8).

How long did Enoch walk with God? Three hundred years. Counting from when he fathered Methuselah at age sixty-five, three hundred years. There is a detail here worth pausing to consider: it was only after fathering a son that Enoch was recorded as walking with God for three hundred years. Scripture does not explicitly state a cause-and-effect relationship, and we will not add to it; but the sequence is right there before us—his longest stretch of walking with God was completed during his years of being a father and raising children. The text even deliberately adds: "and had sons and daughters." Enoch was not a man who retreated into the mountains to practice asceticism; he was someone who walked with God in the ordinary routines of daily life: there were children to raise at home, daily duties to attend to with his hands, and in the very midst of these most mundane things, day after day, he walked with God.

In his commentary on Genesis, Warren W. Wiersbe reminds readers when explaining Enoch that walking together implies moving in the same direction, keeping the same pace, and maintaining continuous intimacy, rather than making occasional visits. (Source: Paraphrased from the core teaching of Warren W. Wiersbe's *The Bible Exposition Commentary: Genesis*, cited rather than quoted directly)

### 2.3 "God Took Him": Death Was Not His Ending

"And Enoch walked with God; and he was not, for God took him." (Gen. 5:24)

"Took," in Hebrew *laqach*, means to take or receive[7]. It is the same word used in chapter 3 when God placed man in the garden, and it will appear repeatedly from chapter 6 onward; here, its meaning is very simple and plain: God Himself received this man and took him away, without letting him pass through that phrase "and he died."

The New Testament bears witness to Enoch. Hebrews 11:5 says: "By faith Enoch was taken away so that he did not see death, 'and was not found, because God had taken him'; for before he was taken he had this testimony, that he pleased God." Notice the sequence: first he obtained the testimony that he pleased God, and then he was taken. What Enoch had first was not a supernatural encounter, but God's pleasure; and as for the way he pleased God, Scripture uses only four words: he walked with God. Then verse 6 opens the door to everyone: "But without faith it is impossible to please Him, for he who comes to God must believe that He is, and that He is a rewarder of those who diligently seek Him."

The Book of Jude also records another dimension of Enoch: he was a prophet who warned that generation of coming judgment (cf. Jude 14–15). A person who walks with God is not someone who only cares about his own comfort and security; he lived in an increasingly wicked generation, yet he dared to speak the truth. Walking with God and staying silent have never been the same thing.

Let us bring this home with power.

Brothers and sisters, Enoch had no earth-shattering achievements. The Bible does not record him building a city, fighting a war, or reigning as king. He did only one thing, and he did it for three hundred years: he walked with God.

What does three hundred years mean? It means walking today, and still walking tomorrow; walking when the children are small, and still walking when the children are grown; walking when life is smooth, and walking when life is hard. For many who believe in the Lord, what they lack is not an initial stirring of emotion; what they lack is the willingness to get up and keep walking the next morning.

It begins today, not tomorrow. Stop waiting for a special day or a special feeling before you start walking. Enoch’s three hundred years began counting from an ordinary day. That step you are willing to take today is the very first step of your three hundred years.

Closing the second part: **In the genealogy, others were remembered for their lifespan; Enoch was remembered for his walk. Lifespan is brought to an end by death; walking with God is not.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part 3: The Line of Walking with God Passes Down—From Methuselah to Noah (5:25–32)

### 3.1 Methuselah: The Longest-Living Man and God's Timetable

Enoch's son Methuselah was the longest-living man in the Bible, living 969 years. His name, in Hebrew *Methushelach*, has historically had various etymological explanations; common interpretations link it to "man" and "dart/sending," but this manuscript does not adopt unverified popular interpretations and adheres strictly to what Scripture records[8].

What is truly worth noting is the chronology: calculating by the ages in chapter 5, the year Methuselah died was the very year the flood came (Gen. 7:6, the flood waters were on the earth when Noah was six hundred years old). Scripture does not explicitly state the connection between his death and the flood, and we will not exaggerate; yet by placing the longest lifespan right before the judgment, this chapter carries the very shadow of that verse in 2 Peter: "The Lord is not slack concerning His promise, as some count slackness, but is longsuffering toward us, not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance." (2 Pet. 3:9). The time God gave to that generation was as long as 969 years. Longsuffering does not mean there is no judgment; it means that before judgment, He granted the longest possible span of grace.

### 3.2 Lamech's Words: Hope Voices Itself in the Midst of Toil

Moving down another generation, we come to Lamech—note that this Lamech is not the Lamech in chapter 4 who sang the song of vengeance; they share the same name but are different individuals: one is in the line of Cain, the other in the line of Seth.

Lamech in Seth's line fathered a son and spoke the most moving words in the entire chapter: "And he called his name Noah, saying, 'This one will comfort us concerning our work and the toil of our hands, because of the ground which the LORD has cursed.'" (Gen. 5:29).

Noah, in Hebrew *Noach*, shares the same root with the word for "rest," *nuach*[9]; the word for "comfort" in Lamech's statement is the Hebrew word *nacham*, meaning comfort, relent, or have compassion, which is a different word; Scripture uses a wordplay of similar sounds to tie hope to this child's name[10]. A father who had toiled his entire life on the cursed ground held his newborn child and said: May this child bring comfort to us who are in the midst of toil.

This is the only complete naming monologue in chapter 5. Earlier, when Eve named Seth, she recognized that God had appointed another; here, when Lamech names Noah, he looks forward to God's future comfort. The genealogy is not a list of dead people; it is a record of living people passing hope into the hands of the next generation, generation after generation.

### 3.3 Noah at the End of the Line: Walking with God Leads to Salvation

The final verse of chapter 5: "And Noah was five hundred years old, and Noah begot Shem, Ham, and Japheth." (Gen. 5:32).

The genealogy pauses here, and the next chapter will recount the flood. But please first remember which line Noah came from: he was Enoch’s great-grandson, the descendant of that man who walked with God. When we reach Genesis 6:9, Scripture introduces Noah and gives him this commendation: "Noah was a just man, perfect in his generations. Noah walked with God." The exact same phrase was passed down from Enoch to Noah.

Therefore, walking with God is never just an individual matter. The path Enoch walked became the path under his children's and grandchildren's feet. How you walk with God today provides an already paved path for your children to walk tomorrow; if you remove prayer from your home today, your children will have to learn from scratch tomorrow. The words of Psalm 78:4 fit perfectly here: "We will not hide them from their children, telling to the generation to come the praises of the LORD, and His strength and His wonderful works that He has done."

Closing the third part: **Enoch’s walk did not stop with him; it traveled down the genealogy all the way to Noah. When one person walks with God, it lays the groundwork of salvation for several generations of a family.**

## (52:00 - 60:00) Applications for Six Groups of People · Altar Call · Closing Prayer

### Application 1: For Seekers Who Have Not Yet Believed in the Lord

Friend, chapter 5 lays before you the most honest reality of human life: no matter how long someone lives, there is always the phrase "and he died" waiting at the end. This is not meant to frighten you, but to invite you to consider a serious question: If death is certain, where is my life ultimately heading?

Enoch offers a different answer: he walked with God, so death was not his ending. Today, if you are willing to begin knowing this God, you do not need to master all the doctrines first; you only need to be willing to take the first step—just like Enoch, to begin walking with Him from this day forward.

### Application 2: For Husbands, Wives, Parents, and Children in Marriage and Family

Enoch's walk was completed during the years of having sons and daughters. For parents, this is very practical: you do not need to wait until the children are grown and you have more time to start drawing near to God seriously. Your children watch you busy, and they also watch how you pray in your busyness, how you read Scripture, and how you do not abandon God in difficulties—that is their very first theology class.

Establish a small habit for your household: once a week, read a short passage of Scripture together as a family and share a word of thanksgiving. Do not underestimate this. The genealogies prove that what is passed down is never grand philosophies, but small, day-after-day footprints.

### Application 3: For Those in the Workplace and in Business

People in the workplace are most prone to measuring themselves by years and numbers: how many years of experience, how much income, what kind of title. Chapter 5 reminds us that no matter how large the numbers are, they all conclude in a single sentence. What truly remains is the way you walk.

An exercise: on your way to work every day, spend ten minutes in prayer, committing the most difficult matter of that day to God before walking into the office. Walking with God is not leaving the world; it is bringing God into the world. Enoch did not leave his generation; he walked with God in the midst of his generation, and you can also walk this way at your cubicle and on your job site.

### Application 4: For New Believers

Dear friends who are new to the faith, learn the starting point of walking with God from Enoch: three hundred years of walking, when broken down, is simply one "today" after another.

You do not need to make a three-hundred-year plan at the very beginning. You only need to read one chapter of Scripture today, pray for ten minutes, and obey one small matter that is already clear in your heart. Then do it again tomorrow. What Enoch was remembered for was not a single great emotional stirring, but that he did not stop. Those who stop do not go far; the secret to not stopping is making your steps small enough that you can take them today.

### Application 5: For Leaders, Parents, and Those Who Serve

A leader needs to ask themselves a question: Am I leaving behind years, or footprints, for those who follow me?

Projects will end, terms of service will pass, and spreadsheets will be updated. What is truly passed down is how you handle pressure, how you treat people, and whether you are still walking with God in the valleys. Lamech held Noah hoping for comfort; that hope did not appear out of thin air—it was accumulated through the walking of Enoch and generation after generation. The path leaders walk today is the path beneath their team's feet tomorrow.

### Application 6: For the Entire Church

The church is a people in a genealogy. Among us are those who have fallen like Adam, those who have been appointed by God like Seth, and those who, like Enoch, are learning to walk with God.

The church must hold fast to one thing: do not let "and he died" become the only sentence pattern in our storytelling. We remember the brothers and sisters who have departed, and we also recount God's works among the living; we number our days, and even more, we must tell of them to future generations. When our next generation opens the record of this generation, let them see not merely a list of names, but a clear path of walking with God.

### Igniting Moment

(Pause, slow down tone)

Brothers and sisters, as we read to the end of chapter 5, only one question remains in my heart.

Ten generations, eight times "and he died." If your name and mine were written into a genealogy today, after the years of our age, which sentence would the Bible write? Would it be another "and he died," or like Enoch—"walked with God"?

(Pause, with added emphasis)

The number of your years is not for you to decide, but walking with Him is. How long you live is in God's hands; how you live is beneath your feet today.

Right today, not tomorrow. Do not let busyness, do not let weariness, do not let "when I have time" steal another day. Enoch did not wait until retirement to walk with God; he started walking during the years of raising his children. You, too, start today: open your Bible, kneel in prayer, take that step—the first step of three hundred years is this very step today.

### Call to Response

I am making two calls:

**The first call**: You have believed in the Lord for many years, yet you admit that you have not walked seriously with God for a long time—your prayers have grown cold, you read Scripture less, you carry on with daily life, and God feels farther and farther away. If you are willing to start walking with Him again today, please stand up, and we will pray for you together. There is no shame in starting over; even Enoch was sixty-five years old before his years of walking with God were recorded.

**The second call**: You have not yet believed in the Lord, and today you are willing to know this God who desires to walk with man, and to walk with Him from this day forward—please raise your hand, and I want to pray for you. Also, parents who are willing to establish a habit of walking with God for your home, leaving this path for your children, please raise your hand as well.

(Wait for response, pray for those standing and raising their hands)

### Closing Response Prayer

Let us bow our heads together in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. In the midst of a genealogy filled with death, You placed the name of Enoch, showing us that death is real, yet it is not the only ending; those who walk with You, You take to Yourself.

Lord, we confess that we often only count our days and do not cultivate walking with You; we are busy having children, busy toiling, yet we push You to the margins of our days. Today, we ask that You give us the heart of Enoch: not seeking sensational experiences, but simply asking to walk with You day after day—walking at home, walking in the workplace, and walking through hardships.

May the footprints our generation leaves become the path beneath our children's feet; when our genealogy comes to us, may it not merely be one more name added, but an added record of walking with You.

In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## End-of-Chapter Notes

[1] *demuth*: Hebrew דְּמוּת (*demuth*), meaning likeness, similitude; Strong's number H1823; definitions according to BDB (*Brown-Driver-Briggs Hebrew and English Lexicon*) and HALOT (Koehler & Baumgartner, *The Hebrew and Aramaic Lexicon of the Old Testament*) entries.

[2] *tselem*: Hebrew צֶלֶם (*tselem*), meaning image; Strong's number H6754; see Gen. 1:26–27; definitions according to BDB and HALOT.

[3] *wayyamot*: Hebrew וַיָּמָת (*wayyamot*), "and he died," narrative form of the verb *mut* (to die, Strong's number H4191); appears repeatedly at the end of each generation in Genesis 5 (5:5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 27, 31).

[4] Sheth (Seth): Hebrew שֵׁת (*Sheth*), Strong's number H8352, related to the root meaning "to set, appoint, grant"; see Eve's naming explanation in Gen. 4:25.

[5] Enosh: Hebrew אֱנוֹשׁ (*Enosh*), Strong's number H583, meaning frail, mortal man.

[6] *halak* / walking with God: Hebrew הָלַךְ (*halak*), Strong's number H1980, to walk; Gen. 5:22, 24 uses the Hitpael form הִתְהַלֵּךְ (*hithallek*), denoting continuous, back-and-forth walking; definitions according to BDB and HALOT.

[7] *laqach*: Hebrew לָקַח (*laqach*), Strong's number H3947, to take; used in Gen. 5:24, "God took him."

[8] Methushelach (Methuselah): Hebrew מְתוּשֶׁלַח (*Methushelach*), Strong's number H4968; etymological interpretations have historically varied, and this manuscript does not adopt unverified popular explanations.

[9] Noach (Noah) and *nuach* (rest): Hebrew נֹחַ (*Noach*), Strong's number H5146; "rest" נּוּחַ (*nuach*), Strong's number H5117.

[10] *nacham*: Hebrew נָחַם (*nacham*), Strong's number H5162, to comfort, relent, have compassion; Gen. 5:29 "will comfort us" uses this root, forming a phonetic wordplay with Noah's name, which is a rhetorical device in the original text; this manuscript explains it according to the Chinese Union Version translation.

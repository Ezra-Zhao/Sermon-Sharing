# He Shall Not Go Out of the Sanctuary — I the LORD Do Sanctify Him

## A Sermon on Leviticus 21 (Full English Version)

- Text: Leviticus 21:1–24
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft, pending cross-review (sermon production batch, 22 October 2026; unpublished)

- Conventions: Paragraphs marked (Fact) state textual and historical matters; paragraphs marked (Application) state the preacher's interpretation and application. The two are kept distinct. ## Abstract

Leviticus 21 tightens the demands of holiness around those who minister: ordinary priests are bound by stricter rules than the people in mourning, in mourning customs, and in marriage; the high priest is bound more strictly still, barred even from defiling himself for his father or mother and commanded not to go out of the sanctuary; a priest with a blemish may not approach to offer, yet he may still eat of the most holy and of the holy things. The chapter closes with the divine self-declaration, "for I the LORD do sanctify them" (Lev 21:23, KJV), showing that separation is not self-exaltation but the act of the One who sanctifies.

## Sermon Text

### I. 00:00–08:00 Opening: Holiness Draws Closest to Those Nearest the Sanctuary

(Application) Brothers and sisters, by the time we reach Leviticus 21, the circle of holiness tightens. For many chapters the word has been addressed to all the people: you shall be holy, for I am holy. In this chapter the address narrows to a small group, the sons of Aaron, the priests. The demands do not relax; they become stricter and more detailed, covering attendance at burials, the expression of grief, and marriage. Why do the requirements grow more specific the nearer one stands to the sanctuary? The chapter's own answer is not that priests are nobler than others, but that their task is different: they offer the bread of their God, and they stand between the people and God. The nearer the place, the clearer the boundary of being set apart must be.

(Fact) First, the map of the chapter. It falls into three blocks. Verses 1–9 concern the priests in general: limits on defilement for the dead, forbidden mourning customs, marriage restrictions, and one severe regulation concerning a priest's daughter. Verses 10–15 concern the high priest, the one on whose head the anointing oil was poured, bound more strictly at every point. Verses 16–24 concern a priest who has a bodily blemish: he may not approach to offer, yet he may still eat the holy food. Verse 24 then closes the chapter (summary): Moses told these words to Aaron, to his sons, and to all the children of Israel. The regulations were not whispered privately; they were published before the whole congregation, so the people also knew what constraints their priests lived under.

(Fact) Note also the recurring first-person voice of God. Verse 6 says the priests offer the bread of their God; verse 8 declares that the LORD who sanctifies is holy; verses 12, 15, and 23 each close a unit with "I am the LORD" or its equivalent. These sentences are the nails that fasten every regulation to one foundation: the authority of the rules lies not in custom but in who the Speaker is. A reading of this chapter that leaves only a list of rules has lost its skeleton; its skeleton is the holiness of the Speaker and His active sanctifying of those who minister.

(Application) Two words will carry our reading: defilement and separation. To defile oneself is the Hebrew verb ṭāmēʾ (טָמֵא, Strong H2930; adjective ṭāmēʾ, H2931), to become unclean (see BDB, s.v. ṭāmēʾ). Separation belongs to the root qdš: to sanctify is qiddēš (קִדֵּשׁ, H6942, Piel stem; see BDB and HALOT, s.v. qdš). The whole chapter moves between these two poles: on one side, defilement to be avoided; on the other, a consecrated identity not to be surrendered. The priest's life is governed by two sentences: what must not be touched, and what must not be let go. What must not be touched is death and profanation; what must not be let go is the oil on his head and the ministry of the sanctuary.

(Application) One caution about method before we begin. This chapter was written for priests within the ancient Israelite sanctuary system; it is not a manual for church workers today. We read it to learn how God taught holiness within the symbolic order of the old covenant, and to learn reverence and self-restraint from the heart of the God who sanctifies. To lift any single rule out of it and impose it directly as a present-day personnel standard would ignore its historical setting and the New Testament's redefinition of ministry. We will return to this point, soberly, at the regulations on blemish.

### II. 08:00–24:00 The Priests: Defilement Permitted for Closest Kin, Pagan Mourning Forbidden

(Fact) The first block opens with burial. Verse 1 lays down the general rule: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Speak unto the priests the sons of Aaron, and say unto them, There shall none be defiled for the dead among his people:" (Lev 21:1, KJV). Verse 2 then opens the exception: "But for his kin, that is near unto him, that is, for his mother, and for his father, and for his son, and for his daughter, and for his brother," (Lev 21:2, KJV). Verse 3 adds one further case: "And for his sister a virgin, that is nigh unto him, which hath had no husband; for her may he be defiled." (Lev 21:3, KJV). Observe the proportion of the exception: it does not cancel the rule; it lodges family affection inside the rule. The priest is also a son, a father, a brother, and God does not ask him to be unmoved by the death of his nearest kin. What God asks is a boundary: for near kin, yes; beyond them, no. Verse 4 gives the reason in principle: "But he shall not defile himself, being a chief man among his people, to profane himself." (Lev 21:4, KJV). A man who is a chief among his people must mourn with restraint, because his conduct is seen and imitated.

(Fact) Verse 4 is a recognised textual difficulty in the Hebrew, and honesty requires that we say so. The Hebrew may be rendered literally along the lines of "he shall not defile himself for a relative by marriage among his people, to profane himself," and commentators are not agreed on the precise reference. The Chinese Union Version renders the sense "being a chief man among his people, he shall not defile himself after the common manner," which is the rendering followed in the KJV above. Whichever construal is adopted, the verse's function is clear: it restates and tightens the general rule and opens no new exception. Where scholarship is unsettled, this sermon marks the matter as unsettled and builds nothing on it; that is the rule applied throughout to every difficult word and phrase.

(Fact) Verse 5 turns to the outward customs of mourning: "They shall not make baldness upon their head, neither shall they shave off the corner of their beard, nor make any cuttings in their flesh." (Lev 21:5, KJV). Shaving the head, trimming the beard, and cutting the flesh were common mourning practices in the ancient Near East, expressing grief and at times carrying gestures toward the dead or toward foreign gods (compare Deut 14:1, where a similar prohibition is given to all the people). The prohibition is not a denial of grief — the priest may still defile himself for his nearest kin and attend their burial. What is forbidden is grief performed as pagan self-laceration. The priest's mourning may exist, but it may not cross the boundary of the body, because his body also serves in the sanctuary, and the marks on his body speak as well.

(Fact) Verse 6 is the hinge of the first block and one source of this sermon's title-theme: "They shall be holy unto their God, and not profane the name of their God: for the offerings of the LORD made by fire, and the bread of their God, they do offer: therefore they shall be holy." (Lev 21:6, KJV). Two key words appear here. To profane is ḥillēl (חִלֵּל, H2490, Piel), to treat as common, to strip of consecrated status (see BDB, s.v. ḥālal). Bread is leḥem (לֶחֶם, H3899); the offerings are called "the bread of their God," in Hebrew leḥem ʾĕlōhāw, "the food of his God." The offerings a priest handles daily are named God's food, and that naming fixes his identity: he is the man who presents food before God. If the one who presents the food is himself defiled and profanes God's name, the whole order collapses. The logic of the regulation is therefore identity first, conduct second: because they offer the bread of their God, therefore they shall be holy.

(Fact) Verse 7 concerns marriage: "They shall not take a wife that is a whore, or profane; neither shall they take a woman put away from her husband: for he is holy unto his God." (Lev 21:7, KJV). The priest's marriage is restricted, and the reason is still the same sentence: he is holy unto his God. Verse 8 places a corresponding duty on the congregation: "Thou shalt sanctify him therefore; for he offereth the bread of thy God: he shall be holy unto thee: for I the LORD, which sanctify you, am holy." (Lev 21:8, KJV). Holiness here is not the priest's private achievement; priest and people uphold one order together — the priest keeps himself, the people honour him, and both stand because of the LORD who sanctifies. Verse 9 is an exceptionally severe regulation and must be quoted without softening: "And the daughter of any priest, if she profane herself by playing the whore, she profaneth her father: she shall be burnt with fire." (Lev 21:9, KJV). (Fact) Its severity lies in the solidarity of identity: the priest's daughter, by profaning herself, profanes her father the priest. We state its severity as it stands, and we state equally that it belongs to the penal provisions of ancient Israel's law bound to sanctuary identity; it is presented here for textual statement and historical understanding, never as a pattern for any present-day action.

(Application) This block leaves today's reader with two reminders. First, family affection and office are not enemies. God permits the priest to defile himself for his nearest kin; being set apart was never meant to be cold-blooded. Within the boundary there are tears; beyond the boundary there is restraint; both are piety. Second, identity precedes conduct. The regulations keep saying, in effect, "for he is holy unto his God": first who he is, then what he does. We habitually try to earn an identity by conduct; Scripture runs the order the other way — first be set apart, then live in a manner worthy of that identity. With the order right, obedience is not performance; with the order reversed, even strict rules become a burden.

### III. 24:00–42:00 The High Priest: Not Even for Father or Mother, and Not Out of the Sanctuary

(Fact) The second block tightens every screw one turn further. Verse 10 identifies its subject: "And he that is the high priest among his brethren, upon whose head the anointing oil was poured, and that is consecrated to put on the garments, shall not uncover his head, nor rend his clothes;" (Lev 21:10, KJV). That description carries us back to Leviticus 8, where Aaron was anointed and consecrated, the oil poured on his head to sanctify him (see the consecration theme of Lev 8:12). Chapter 8 tells how he entered the office; chapter 21 tells how, having entered it, he must keep it day by day. Consecration is not a single day's ceremony; it is a lifetime of boundaries.

(Fact) Verse 11 is among the weightiest sentences in the chapter: "Neither shall he go in to any dead body, nor defile himself for his father, or for his mother;" (Lev 21:11, KJV). Set it beside the rule for ordinary priests and read carefully: ordinary priests may defile themselves for father and mother; the high priest may not. The same verb, ṭāmēʾ (H2930), receives an exception for near kin in the first block and has that exception withdrawn in the second. This is not a failure of divine sympathy toward the high priest's grief; it is a matter of the weight and concentration of the symbol. The high priest alone represents all Israel before God; were he to enter the defilement of death, what would be suspended is not one man's service but the whole people's channel of access to God. Verse 12 continues: "Neither shall he go out of the sanctuary, nor profane the sanctuary of his God; for the crown of the anointing oil of his God is upon him: I am the LORD." (Lev 21:12, KJV). "He shall not go out of the sanctuary" is the first half of this sermon's title. It does not mean the high priest could never physically leave the sanctuary precinct for the rest of his life; it means that when his parents die, he may not abandon the sanctuary and his office in order to attend their burial. The Hebrew word for the crown is nezer (נֵזֶר, H5145), the sign of consecration (see BDB, s.v. nezer). The crown of the anointing oil is on his head; therefore no bereavement, however great, outweighs the sign on his head.

(Fact) The high priest's marriage is stricter than the priests'. Verse 13: "And he shall take a wife in her virginity." (Lev 21:13, KJV). Verse 14: "A widow, or a divorced woman, or profane, or an harlot, these shall he not take: but he shall take a virgin of his own people to wife." (Lev 21:14, KJV). Verse 15 gives the reason: "Neither shall he profane his seed among his people: for I the LORD do sanctify him." (Lev 21:15, KJV). The concern remains the symbolic order: the high priest's household and offspring are drawn into the visible signs of consecration. We read this block acknowledging both its internal consistency within the ancient institution and its situatedness: these are regulations for the symbolic order of the sanctuary, not a judgement on the worth of any person in any circumstance today, and they must never be wielded to look down on anyone.

(Fact) This block also stands in structural dialogue with Leviticus 16, and the observation is worth making. Chapter 16 describes the summit of the high priest's office: the one day in the year when he enters within the veil to make atonement for all the people. Chapter 21 describes his ordinary days: no defilement even for parents, no going out of the sanctuary, a restricted marriage. Summit and ordinary day lie on one line: the man who may once a year pass within the veil must first, on ordinary days, keep the boundary of non-defilement. In Leviticus, holiness is never a performance staged for the festival day; it is the accumulation of ordinary days. The high priest's greatness lies not in the solemn moment of the Day of Atonement but in the fact that on every unremarkable day he was willing to pay, for the crown on his head, a price others were not required to pay — including restraining his tears at his own parents' death.

(Application) "He shall not go out of the sanctuary" still carries weight today. It reminds everyone who bears an entrusted charge that there are moments when private grief is real and the keeping of one's post is also real, and when the two collide Scripture offers no cheap comfort, only a clear order. But the boundary of application must be drawn with equal clarity: this is the regulation of the high priest's unique office, and it may not be universalised into a demand that anyone today surrender the right to bury their own family. Application may draw on its spirit — reverence, and fidelity to what is entrusted — and may not take its letter as a present-day rule. To say this plainly is what responsible preaching owes the text and the hearer alike.

### IV. 42:00–56:00 The Priest with a Blemish: He Shall Not Approach to Offer, Yet He May Eat the Bread of His God

(Fact) The third block begins afresh at verse 16, and the word of the LORD is addressed to Aaron himself: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Speak unto Aaron, saying, Whosoever he be of thy seed in their generations that hath any blemish, let him not approach to offer the bread of his God." (Lev 21:16–17, KJV; the quotation joins the speaking formula of v. 16 directly to its content in v. 17, each part quoted verbatim.) The central word of the block is blemish, Hebrew mûm (מוּם, H3971), a bodily defect or flaw (see BDB, s.v. mûm). The same word is used in Leviticus for sacrificial animals: what is offered to God must be without mûm (see the parallel regulations in Lev 22:20ff.). That priest and sacrifice share one word is the key to this block, as we shall see. Verses 18–20 then enumerate the cases: "For whatsoever man he be that hath a blemish, he shall not approach: a blind man, or a lame, or he that hath a flat nose, or any thing superfluous, Or a man that is broken-footed, or broken-handed, Or crookbacked, or a dwarf, or that hath a blemish in his eye, or be scurvy, or scabbed, or hath his stones broken;" (Lev 21:18–20, KJV). Two terms deserve exactness. "Any thing superfluous" renders śārûaʿ (שָׂרוּעַ, H8311), a limb extended too long, disproportionate (see BDB, s.v. śāraʿ). "He that hath a flat nose" renders ḥārum, related to the root ḥāram (H2763), a nose flattened or slit; its precise reference remains debated among scholars, and preaching should keep to its general sense without embellishing detail. Verse 21 restates the rule: "No man that hath a blemish of the seed of Aaron the priest shall come nigh to offer the offerings of the LORD made by fire: he hath a blemish; he shall not come nigh to offer the bread of his God." (Lev 21:21, KJV).

(Fact) But now mark the turn at verse 22 — the one sentence in this block, indeed in the whole chapter, that must never be omitted: "He shall eat the bread of his God, both of the most holy, and of the holy." (Lev 21:22, KJV). The regulation does not expel the priest with a blemish from the priestly house. His sphere of service is limited — he may not approach to offer — but his priestly identity and his provision are preserved entire: the bread of his God, the most holy and the holy, he may eat. The most holy things were the highest grade of offering, eaten only by priests in a holy place (see the pattern of Lev 6:16, 29); even from that grade he is not excluded. In other words, the regulation draws a line around an office, not around a person's worth; it restricts his post at the altar, not his seat in the house of God. Verse 23 then states boundary and reason together: "Only he shall not go in unto the vail, nor come nigh unto the altar, because he hath a blemish; that he profane not my sanctuaries: for I the LORD do sanctify them." (Lev 21:23, KJV). Attend to the final clause: the subject of sanctifying is still the LORD, and those sanctified include this priest with a blemish. The last word of the regulation is not "he is not good enough"; it is "I am the one who sanctifies them." This sermon's title joins that declaration to verse 12's "he shall not go out of the sanctuary": the one clause draws the boundary in, the other declares who makes holy, and both are the base text's own words.

(Fact) A further internal witness helps the reader weigh the symbolic reading. The first half of the next chapter, Leviticus 22, lays the same requirement of wholeness on the sacrificial animals (summary): an animal offered to the LORD must have no blemish; one with a blemish may not be offered and will not be accepted. Read side by side, the order of the two chapters is telling: chapter 21 treats the man who offers before chapter 22 treats the animal offered, because if the offerer is not set apart, the wholeness of the offering has no anchor. Both use the same word, mûm (H3971); this is no accident but deliberate correspondence — the symbolic order of the sanctuary is a single fabric in which altar, man, and offering refer to one another. Note, too, a detail of diction: throughout this block the restriction falls on verbs of approach — "approach," "go in," "come nigh." What is limited is the movement of drawing near to the core of the sanctuary. Not one sentence says that the man is no longer a priest, and not one sentence removes his right to eat the holy food. The text's own choice of verbs distinguishes an office-boundary from the preservation of identity with complete clarity — a precision that preaching must not blur.

(Application) How, then, should this block be understood in a way that is both faithful to the text and harmless to today's hearer? Scholarship offers two main lines of interpretation, and preaching should present both without rushing to a verdict. The first is the symbolic-order reading (reported here as a scholarly consensus in outline): the sanctuary is a densely symbolic space in which both the offerer and the offering must present wholeness at the symbolic level, pointing to the perfection and holiness of God; the unblemished sacrifice and the unblemished officiating priest are two faces of one symbolic language (Milgrom and Wenham broadly follow this line, differing in detail). On this reading the regulation concerns a symbolic office, not a person's worth — and the text itself immediately confirms the distinction by affirming that the priest with a blemish still eats of the most holy things. The second line comes from contemporary disability theology and ethics (reported here as scholarly discussion): some scholars warn that the ancient Near East commonly linked bodily impairment with impurity and divine displeasure, and that readers must not import that ancient cosmology into the present by judging anyone's spiritual standing from bodily condition; others point out, conversely, that within its ancient setting this regulation is restrained — the man with a blemish is neither expelled from the community nor stripped of provision — and that the preserved seat should not be minimised against the background of an age readier to marginalise. The two lines carry different evidential weight: the symbolic reading rests on the internal parallel structure of the text (one word, mûm, for sacrifice and priest alike) and is comparatively firm; the comparative-setting reading depends on reconstructions of ancient Near Eastern material, is softer, and may serve as reference but not as verdict.

(Application) On that basis, this sermon's position is a restrained threefold one. First, the symbolic level and the level of human dignity must be kept apart: the regulation limits the holding of one symbolic office within the old covenant sanctuary, just as the requirement of wholeness in a sacrifice is no judgement on the worth of an animal; likewise, the requirement for officiating is no judgement on any person's dignity or spiritual standing. Second, no verdict-style application may be drawn: this passage may not be used to support any exclusion of people with bodily impairments from roles, seats, or opportunities in the church today; the New Testament's picture of the church as the body of Christ, in which every member belongs to the one body (see the direction of 1 Corinthians 12), runs in exactly the opposite direction from exclusion. Third, return to the balance the text itself keeps: whoever expounds this block must read verse 22 aloud together with verse 23. Without "He shall eat the bread of his God," only exclusion remains; without "he shall not go in unto the vail," the symbolic seriousness of the text is erased. To present both sides together is simply to let this passage look like itself. We may wrestle with an ancient regulation, and find parts of it hard; we may not consume another person's body as a curiosity, and we may not quietly delete either face of the text.

### V. 58:00–60:00 Conclusion: It Is the LORD Who Sanctifies Them

(Fact) Looking back over the chapter, the two-tier structure stands out clearly. Ordinary priests may defile themselves for near kin, and for others may not; the high priest may not defile himself even for near kin. Grief is permitted; pagan self-laceration is not. Marriage is restricted, because the identity is holy unto God. The priest with a blemish is restricted in service, while his seat and his provision are untouched. Every section of the chapter finally lands on one sentence in its several forms: it is the LORD who sanctifies. Verse 23's "for I the LORD do sanctify them" (KJV) interlocks with the refrain "I am the LORD" that runs through the whole book, and with the command to all the people that they be holy because the LORD their God is holy. From first to last, holiness has exactly one source.

(Application) In the end, then, this chapter is not about how worthy the priests were; it is about how active God is. The priests keep boundaries because Someone first drew the boundaries for them and sanctified them. The priest with a blemish can sit at the table and eat of the most holy things, not because he fought for the seat, but because the LORD who sanctifies him left the seat for him. Every one of us who is in Christ has been called not on the ground of bodily wholeness or an unblemished record, but by the same God who actively sanctifies His own. May we, on the one hand, revere His holiness and keep the boundaries that must be kept; and may we, on the other, remember the seat He leaves for people, and never look down on anyone He has sanctified. To the LORD who sanctifies us be the glory. Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

The quietest sentence in Leviticus 21 is spoken over the priest with a blemish: "He shall eat the bread of his God, both of the most holy, and of the holy." (Lev 21:22, KJV). He may not approach to offer; his post is limited. But God does not withdraw his seat. Perhaps you are living today inside some limitation — of body, of circumstance, of opportunity. The limitation is real; there is no need to pretend otherwise. But hear the second half of the regulation: limitation is not abandonment, and a boundary around a post is not a boundary around love. The LORD who sanctifies him set the most holy food before him with His own hand, and He leaves a seat for you as well. Being set apart was never an identity purchased by flawlessness; it is God's own act: He sanctifies, and He gives those He sanctifies a share in His holiness even in the place of limitation. Consider, too, the high priest, who could not defile himself even for his parents and could not go out of the sanctuary: an office of holiness carries its cost, and fidelity can look like coldness to those watching. Yet in the same chapter God leaves both seat and food for the limited man. Cost and grace stand in one chapter — that is Leviticus' honesty: it neither glosses the weight of office nor retracts the breadth of grace. If today you have paid a price for fidelity, or quietly feel less because of a bodily or circumstantial limit, receive both halves together: the boundary that must be kept, keep by His strength; the seat that is given, receive by His strength. Be still, hand your limitation back to Him, and receive this one sentence: the seat is still there, and the food is still yours, for the One who sanctifies you is the LORD.

## Notes

1. The threefold division (Lev 21:1–9; 21:10–15; 21:16–24) follows the shifts of addressee (priests, high priest, Aaron's seed) and of topic (mourning and marriage; stricter mourning and marriage; bodily blemish). It is a structural observation, not the base text's own paragraphing.
2. ṭāmēʾ (H2930, verb; H2931, adjective): BDB, "to be or become unclean"; HALOT, s.v. ṭmʾ, likewise. Lev 21:1 and 21:11 both use this verb for defiling oneself.
3. ḥillēl (H2490): the Piel of ḥālal in BDB is "profane, defile, pollute." Lev 21:6 ("profane the name of their God"), 21:12, and 21:23 ("profane my sanctuaries") all belong to this root. The root also bears unrelated senses ("begin," "pierce"); the sermon uses only the Piel sense concerning the sacred/common distinction.
4. leḥem ʾĕlōhāw (Lev 21:6, 8, 17, 21, 22): leḥem (H3899) is bread, food. The KJV renders "the bread of his God"; the Chinese Union Version renders "the food of his God." Both refer to the offerings presented by, and in part eaten by, the priests.
5. nezer (H5145, Lev 21:12): BDB, "consecration, crown," the visible sign of consecration, also used of the Nazirite's separation.
6. qiddēš / məqaddēš (H6942): in Lev 21:8, 15, and 23 the sanctifying is in the Piel form with the LORD as subject throughout — the theological centre of the chapter.
7. mûm (H3971): BDB, "blemish, defect." Lev 22:17–25 applies the same word to sacrificial animals — the key internal evidence for the symbolic-order reading. Wenham and Milgrom both note the parallelism between sacrifice and priest, though they differ in how far its implications may be pressed; the sermon adopts their common ground and records their divergence.
8. śārûaʿ (H8311, Lev 21:18): the commissioning brief originally gave H5636; word-by-word verification on BibleHub shows the Strong number for this word in this verse to be H8311, H5636 being a different word. The sermon therefore cites the corrected number, and the correction and its source are recorded here. BDB, s.v. śāraʿ: "have a limb too long, be disproportionate."
9. ḥārum (Lev 21:18): related to the root ḥāram (H2763), as tagged in BibleHub's word-by-word analysis. Its precise reference (flattened, slit nose, or split lip) has no scholarly consensus; the sermon keeps to its general sense.
10. The intertextual reading of Leviticus 8 (consecration), Leviticus 16 (the Day of Atonement), and this chapter is structural: chapter 8 narrates entry into office, chapter 16 its summit, chapter 21 its daily keeping of boundaries. Read together, the three give the full contour of priestly holiness.
11. The penal provision of Lev 21:9: the sermon offers textual statement and historical setting only. Milgrom connects its severity with the representative place of the priestly household in the sanctuary's symbolic order; that is a scholarly explanation, cited at that level, and is no warrant for any present-day action.
12. Limits of evidence in the disability discussion: the symbolic-order reading is supported by the text's internal parallel structure; the ancient Near Eastern comparative reading rests on reconstruction of excavated material and is correspondingly more tentative. The sermon presents both without a verdict, and expressly rejects any use of this passage to exclude people with bodily impairments today; that stance is the preacher's application and is labelled as such, kept distinct from the factual layer.

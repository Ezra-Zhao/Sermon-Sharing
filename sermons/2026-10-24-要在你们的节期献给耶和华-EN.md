Title verification: The title is taken from the proposition of the whole book's summary sentence in verse 39 — "These things ye shall do unto the LORD in your set feasts." The chapter has four stations — the blowing of trumpets (29:1-6), the day of atonement (29:7-11), the seven days of the feast of tabernacles (29:12-34), and the solemn assembly of the eighth day (29:35-38) — plus the summary and the delivery (29:39-40). Candidates were checked verse by verse: "it is a day of blowing the trumpets unto you" (29:1) covers only the first station; "ye shall afflict your souls" (29:7) covers only the day of atonement; "ye shall keep a feast unto the LORD seven days" (29:12) covers only the feast of tabernacles. Only the sentence of verse 39, standing after the whole chapter and looking back over it, gathers all four stations into its "these things," and it is therefore the title. The English title follows the same verse in the KJV's own words: "Ye Shall Do Unto the LORD in Your Set Feasts."
Base-text notes (where another version or a common paraphrase differs, the base text is recorded as it stands): At 29:1 the date is "on the first day of the month" — the first day. At 29:2 the lambs are "seven lambs of the first year without blemish" — "without blemish," not "without defect." The closing sentence of 29:6 names two foundation layers together: "Beside the burnt offering of the month, and his meat offering, and the daily burnt offering, and his meat offering, and their drink offerings, according unto their manner, for a sweet savour, a sacrifice made by fire unto the LORD" — this day is both the new moon and the day of trumpets, so the word "beside" here carries two layers at once. At 29:7 the command runs "ye shall not do any work therein" — the prohibition here reads "any work," without the word "servile," differing from "ye shall do no servile work" at 29:1, 12, 35, and each is registered as it stands. At 29:8 the offering opens "But ye shall offer a burnt offering unto the LORD" — it opens with "But." At 29:11 the verse reads "One kid of the goats for a sin offering; beside the sin offering of atonement, and the continual burnt offering, and the meat offering of it, and their drink offerings" — its closing sentence names, beside the continual offering, one further layer, "the sin offering of atonement," that is, the offering of the day of atonement itself in Leviticus 16 (cross-reference layer), and it is recorded, not deleted. At 29:12 the seven days beginning on the fifteenth day of the seventh month are called only "a feast unto the LORD seven days"; nowhere in this chapter is the feast named "the feast of tabernacles" — the name stands at Leviticus 23:34 (cross-reference layer); this chapter records only dates and quantities of offering, and the name is not supplied. At 29:13 the offering is "a burnt offering, a sacrifice made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD." At 29:17 the Chinese base text reads 公绵羊两只, as it does on all seven days of the feast (29:13, 17, 20, 23, 26, 29, 32) without distinction; the KJV likewise reads "two rams" uniformly on all seven days — each manuscript registers its own text. At 29:35 the assembly of the eighth day is "a solemn assembly," distinct from the "holy convocation" of the other feasts. At 29:36 the offering is "a burnt offering, a sacrifice made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD: one bullock, one ram, seven lambs of the first year without blemish." At 29:39 the phrase is "beside your vows, and your freewill offerings" — "freewill offerings" recorded as it stands. At 29:40 the chapter closes "And Moses told the children of Israel according to all that the LORD commanded Moses"; this verse is numbered 30:1 in the Masoretic text (see Endnote 1).

## Summary

Chapter 28 arranged the calendar through the middle of the year (Passover, Unleavened Bread, Weeks; previous-text layer); this chapter continues with the second half of the year — and from its first sentence everything is crowded into the single month of the seventh month. The first day of the seventh month is both the new moon and the day of blowing the trumpets: a holy convocation, no servile work, one young bullock, one ram, and seven lambs offered as a burnt offering for a sweet savour, with the graded meat offering, and one kid of the goats for a sin offering besides — all of it beside the offerings of the new moon and the continual offering (29:1-6). The tenth day is the heaviest day of the chapter: a holy convocation, afflict your souls, no work at all; yet the quantity of offering is at its lightest — one young bullock, one ram, seven lambs, and one kid of the goats for a sin offering, beside the sin offering of atonement and the continual offering (29:7-11). From the fifteenth day the feast is kept seven days, and here is the chapter's centre of gravity: on the first day thirteen young bullocks, two rams, and fourteen lambs, and thereafter one bullock fewer each day — twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven — seventy bullocks in the seven days in all, the number of rams and lambs unchanged through the seven days, one kid of the goats for a sin offering added each day, every day beside the continual offering (29:12-34). After the seven days the assembly does not disperse: on the eighth day there is a further solemn assembly, no servile work, and the offering returns to a small round number — one bullock, one ram, seven lambs, and one goat (29:35-38). The last two verses gather up the whole book: verse 39 is the summary — these things are to be done unto the LORD in the set feasts, beside what each man vows and his freewill offerings; verse 40 is the seal of delivery — Moses told the children of Israel according to all that the LORD commanded him (29:39-40). Set this chapter beside the previous one and its character stands out: the feasts of the previous chapter each bear a name (the Passover, the feast of Weeks); the three feasts of this chapter are given no names anywhere in the chapter, only dates — the first, the tenth, the fifteenth. The names are in other scrolls; the dates are in this chapter. God would have His people nail the days down on the calendar first: as the year turns into its second half, first blow the trumpet and awake, then afflict the soul and make atonement, then spread the feast of the harvest in abundance, and close at last with a solemn assembly. The order cannot be shuffled: without the trumpet of the first day there are no tears on the tenth; without the tears of the tenth, the abundance of the fifteenth turns into indulgence.

## Main Text

### Part One: The First Day of the Seventh Month — the Day of Blowing the Trumpets (29:1-6) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) Verse 1, the date and the naming of this chapter's first station, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And in the seventh month, on the first day of the month, ye shall have an holy convocation; ye shall do no servile work: it is a day of blowing the trumpets unto you." (29:1) Registered item by item: the date — the first day of the seventh month, the same "first day" as every month's new moon (28:11; the previous chapter is the previous-text layer); the assembly — an holy convocation is to be held; the rest from work — no servile work is to be done; the day's own naming — "a day of blowing the trumpets": this chapter gives the day no other feast-name, marking it only by the "blowing of the trumpets." Verse 2, this day's burnt offering, from the base text: "And ye shall offer a burnt offering for a sweet savour unto the LORD; one young bullock, one ram, and seven lambs of the first year without blemish:" (29:2) The combination registered: one bullock, one ram, seven lambs — against the new-moon combination of the previous chapter (two bullocks, one ram, seven lambs, 28:11), the bullocks are reduced to one and the rest is the same. Verses 3-4, the proportions of the meat offering, from the base text: "And their meat offering shall be of flour mingled with oil, three tenth deals for a bullock, and two tenth deals for a ram, And one tenth deal for one lamb, throughout the seven lambs:" (29:3-4) Registered: the gradation is the same as the whole book's standing rule (three for a bullock, two for a ram, one for a lamb; 28:12-13). Verse 5, the atonement, from the base text: "And one kid of the goats for a sin offering, to make an atonement for you:" (29:5) Verse 6, this station's closing sentence, from the base text: "Beside the burnt offering of the month, and his meat offering, and the daily burnt offering, and his meat offering, and their drink offerings, according unto their manner, for a sweet savour, a sacrifice made by fire unto the LORD." (29:6) Registered: this verse carries the longest closing sentence of the chapter, because this day falls on the same day as the new moon and the layers multiply by one — first the daily continual offering, then the monthly new moon, then this station of the trumpets: three layers stacked, and not one layer abolishes another.

(Argument) First, where does this trumpet blast stand? The seventh month is the most crowded month of the year's feasts: the trumpet on the first, atonement on the tenth, the feast kept seven days from the fifteenth, and a solemn assembly on the eighth day besides — the year's peak of worship falls not at the year's opening but in its second half. And God lays the order out plainly: before the peak, first the trumpet. In Scripture the trumpet sound is never background music; it is a signal: it assembles the congregation (Num 10:3, cross-reference layer), it prepares the march, it announces the feasts. The blast on the first day says to the whole congregation: stop the work in your hands, come before the tabernacle — this month holds great matters: first pardon, then harvest; no one may be absent, and no one may bring an un-dealt-with sin to the feast. What we most lack in our lives today is exactly this trumpet blast: the days push us along, busy and busier, and a year slides past without even a signal to stop and examine ourselves. God blows the trumpet on the first day of the second half precisely to give His people an annual point of reminder: having come this far, awake first, assemble first, listen first. Is there in your life a "first day" nailed down like this — an unshakeable pause at which you bring out, one by one, the things accumulated over half a year and deal with them? If there is not, do not talk of harvest yet; supply the trumpet first.

(Argument) Next, this day's double identity. The first day of the seventh month is first of all a new moon — the station that comes every month (28:11-15); it is at the same time the day of trumpets — which comes only this once in the year. The closing sentence of verse 6 is therefore longer than anywhere else, naming both foundation layers, the new moon and the continual offering, one by one. This gives us a very practical reminder: a special day does not exempt us from ordinary lessons. Special as the day of trumpets is, the new moon's offering is offered as usual, the continual morning and evening lambs are offered as usual, nothing is dropped. Our common fault is to set the special against the ordinary: come back from a retreat, a special conference, a great meeting, and we think this month's devotions and Lord's Day worship can be let slack. Scripture's arithmetic runs the other way: the more special the day, the more carefully the ordinary layers underneath the "beside" must be counted — the continual offering is the root, the new moon the trunk, the feast the flower; however gloriously the flower blooms, it must not dig up the root. If you would add a special discipline to your life of faith, check first: are the foundations under the "beside" — the daily ones, the monthly ones — still standing? With the foundation standing, whatever you add stands firm; without it, whatever you add is excitement hanging in the air.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The original word behind "blowing the trumpets" is terûʿâ, denoting the sound of the trumpet blast, a shout; its Strong's number is marked in this sermon as pending verification, see Endnote 5, and only the word-form and the base text's rendering are registered. The word "set feasts" (môʿădêkem at 29:39) is the same word as the previous chapter's governing word môʿēd (H4150, verified at 28:2), denoting the appointed dates. "Continual" is tāmîd (H8548), "burnt offering" is ʿōlâ (H5930), "sweet savour" is rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ (H7381 + H5207), "sacrifice made by fire" is ʾiššeh (H801), "meat offering" is minḥâ (H4503), "drink offering" is nesek (H5262) — all verified in the previous chapter and registered here by continuation.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) This day's relation to the "new year" is set out in parallel, without adjudication. Later Jewish tradition takes the first day of the seventh month as the head of the year, the new year's day (tradition layer); the text of this chapter calls the day only "a day of blowing the trumpets," does not call it new year, and records no rite of the year's head; Leviticus 23:24 calls this day a sabbath, "a memorial of blowing of trumpets" (cross-reference layer), in wording again slightly different from this chapter. The reading runs more than one way: some hold that the meaning of the year's head was disclosed later and attached to this day; others that the day's own meaning lies only in the trumpet's assembling and in preparation for the day of atonement. This sermon registers only that the chapter's self-designation (a day of blowing the trumpets) and the traditional designation (new year) stand side by side, and makes no assertion at this chapter's fact layer that the day is the new year; the argument of the main text uses only the three meanings the chapter itself states — trumpet, holy convocation, and rest from work.

(Fact) This section closes by registering the list of the trumpets station: date, the first day of the seventh month; one holy convocation, one rest from work; added offerings — one bullock, one ram, seven lambs (burnt offering), one kid of the goats (sin offering), the meat offering by the gradation of three, two, one, the drink offerings after the manner; three layers stacked — continual offering, new moon, and this station (29:6). And one whole-chapter comparison is registered: this station's combination (one bullock, one ram, seven lambs, one goat) will recur exactly at the day of atonement (29:8, 11) and on the eighth day (29:36, 38) — the feast of tabernacles, the chapter's most solemn feast, uses another, larger combination; this small and complete combination is the standing pattern of the seventh month's feasts.

### Part Two: The Tenth Day of the Seventh Month — the Day of Afflicting Your Souls (29:7-11) (10:00-22:00)

(Fact) Verse 7, this station's date and atmosphere, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And ye shall have on the tenth day of this seventh month an holy convocation; and ye shall afflict your souls: ye shall not do any work therein:" (29:7) Registered item by item: first, the prohibition of work here reads "ye shall not do any work therein" — more absolute than the "ye shall do no servile work" of the chapter's other places (29:1, 12, 35), without even the qualifying word "servile," registered as the base text gives it; second, "afflict your souls" occurs only this once in the whole chapter and is this station's unique mark — at the trumpets the hand rests, at the feast the hand rests, but on this day alone the hand must rest and the soul must be afflicted even more. Verse 8, this day's burnt offering, from the base text: "But ye shall offer a burnt offering unto the LORD for a sweet savour; one young bullock, one ram, and seven lambs of the first year; they shall be unto you without blemish:" (29:8) Registered: the combination is the same as at the trumpets (one bullock, one ram, seven lambs), the smallest set of burnt offering in the whole chapter; the verse opens with "But ye shall offer" — the text itself has a sense of proportion about this day's light quantity. Verses 9-10, the proportions of the meat offering, from the base text: "And their meat offering shall be of flour mingled with oil, three tenth deals to a bullock, and two tenth deals to one ram, A several tenth deal for one lamb, throughout the seven lambs:" (29:9-10) Registered: the second full listing of the gradation (the first at 29:3-4). Verse 11, this day's atonement and closing, from the base text: "One kid of the goats for a sin offering; beside the sin offering of atonement, and the continual burnt offering, and the meat offering of it, and their drink offerings." (29:11) Two items registered: first, this verse's kid of the goats is yet one more laid upon this day's own "sin offering of atonement" — the text here lists as a separate layer the set of offerings belonging to the day of atonement itself in Leviticus 16 (cross-reference layer), and this chapter's own kid is added on top of it; second, this verse has no closing phrase "unto the LORD," a small difference from the closing formulas of other verses, recorded as it stands.

(Argument) The most striking contrast of this day is "the heaviest day, the lightest offering." In weight of days, the tenth is unique in the whole year: afflict your souls, do no work at all — all Israel is on this day to be atoned for the sins of the year (Leviticus 16, cross-reference layer). In display of offering, it is on a level with the first day: only one bullock. Five days later, at the feast of tabernacles, the first day alone has thirteen bullocks. Why is the day of atonement set out so lightly, and the day of harvest so heavily? Because the weight of atonement never lies in display: this day's heaviness is not in how much is heaped on the altar but in how much the soul is afflicted; not in how strong the animals are but in how deeply sin is dealt with. God will not have men cover shallowness with solemnity — the four words "afflict your souls" move the whole work of this day from the altar into the heart. Today we too often reverse the order: having transgressed, we make it up by doing a little more, giving a little more money, attending one more meeting, while the pass of the heart is never crossed. This chapter says: on the day of atonement God receives not the large but the true — one bullock with an afflicted heart outweighs thirteen bullocks with an untroubled heart. Keep a "tenth day" for yourself each year: stop every work, do nothing for show, do one thing only — lay out before God, one by one, the sins accumulated over the year, and deal with them clean.

(Argument) "Afflict your souls" must be taken one layer deeper. Affliction is not self-torment; it is not letting yourself off: ordinarily we are most lenient with ourselves — a fault has its reason, a loss its excuse, a delay its explanation. To afflict the soul is, on this day, to allow yourself no passage by reasons: a fault is a fault, a loss is a loss, and what must be confessed is confessed to the bottom. Scripture puts affliction and holy convocation in the same verse (29:7): affliction is not shutting the door and suffering alone; it is the whole congregation bowing together before God — my sin is not my affair only; it wounds the whole body, and in the body's atonement no one can stand aside. What the church today most lacks is precisely this corporate affliction: we are good at celebrating together and not good at confessing together; good at being busy at the feasts and not good at being quietly afflicted before God. The order of the seventh month is therefore all the more important: after the trumpet and before the feast there must be this pass of the tenth. Whoever skips the tenth and rushes to the fifteenth eats the feast noisily enough, but the account in his heart is still outstanding; whoever has passed the tenth knows why the abundance of the fifteenth is so sweet — because that abundance is eaten by people whose sins have been atoned for.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The "afflict" of "afflict your souls" belongs to the verb family of ʿinnâ (to afflict, to humble), here in a self-directed usage; "atonement" is the word kippurîm (the noun form of kāpar, H3722); the noun numbers of both words are marked in this sermon as pending verification, see Endnote 5, and only the word-forms and the base text's renderings are registered. This chapter does not call this day by the name "day of atonement" — the name stands in Leviticus 16 and Leviticus 23:27 (cross-reference layer); this chapter records only "the tenth day" and the four matters of affliction, rest from work, and offering, registered as recorded.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) How this chapter is to be read together with Leviticus 16 is set out in parallel, without adjudication. Leviticus 16 records the whole course of the day of atonement: the high priest entering the holiest place once a year, making atonement for himself and his house, the two goats (one for the LORD, one for Azazel), the blood sprinkled on the mercy seat (cross-reference layer). This chapter, for the same day, records only one set of congregational burnt offerings and one kid of the goats, and expressly says that this kid is offered "beside the sin offering of atonement" (29:11) — that is, this chapter itself owns that what it records is not the whole of the day of atonement, only the measure of the public burnt offering for that day. The reading runs more than one way: some hold that the two passages divide the labour complementarily (one recording the rite of the priest's entry into the holiest place, the other the quantity of the congregation's offerings); others that they come from different editorial layers. This sermon registers only what each passage carries and this chapter's own admission, and makes no statement about sources at the fact layer; the argument of the main text uses only the three items the chapter itself states — affliction, rest from work, and "beside the sin offering of atonement."

(New Testament Reading) Hebrews, treating of this day, says that the high priest entered "alone once every year" into the second part (Heb 9:7, key points), that the offerings "could not make him that did the service perfect" (Heb 9:9, key points), and sets against it Christ, who by His own blood entered in once for all (Heb 9:12, key points) and who "now once in the end of the world hath he appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself" (Heb 9:26); see also Hebrews 10:1-14 (cross-reference reading layer). One contrast is registered at the reading layer: the pattern of the tenth day in this chapter — affliction year by year, offering year by year — is read in the New Testament, by its very repetition, as pointing to a High Priest who offered Himself once and need never repeat it. The reading layer registers only how the New Testament reads this day's offerings; it does not fill "offered once" back in as this chapter's legislative fact. What this chapter establishes remains the rule the wilderness congregation was to carry out every year on the tenth day, its sequence and measures all as the original gives them.

(Fact) This section closes by registering the list of the day of atonement station: date, the tenth day of the seventh month; one holy convocation, one afflicting of souls, and the prohibition of work in the form "ye shall not do any work therein"; added offerings — one bullock, one ram, seven lambs (burnt offering, the chapter's smallest combination), one kid of the goats (sin offering), the meat-offering gradation fully listed for the second round, and two layers stacked — the sin offering of atonement and the continual offering (29:11). And a whole-chapter comparison is registered: this station is the only one of the four stations established by the "soul" — the others are established by date and offerings; this one alone, after the date, first commands affliction, and only then says what is to be offered.

### Part Three: Seven Days from the Fifteenth Day of the Seventh Month — Headed by Thirteen Bullocks (29:12-19) (22:00-36:00)

(Fact) Verse 12, this station's opening day and the feast's name, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And on the fifteenth day of the seventh month ye shall have an holy convocation; ye shall do no servile work, and ye shall keep a feast unto the LORD seven days:" (29:12) Three items registered: first, this station begins on the fifteenth day of the seventh month and is kept for seven days in succession — the longest station of the chapter (29:12-34, twenty-three verses in all); second, this verse calls it only "a feast unto the LORD," and nowhere in this chapter is a name given to these seven days — the name "feast of tabernacles" stands at Leviticus 23:34 (cross-reference layer), registered from the base text, and the name of another scroll is not filled back into this chapter; third, this verse commands a holy convocation and rest from servile work on the first day only; whether work is to rest day by day within the seven days this verse does not say, and it is recorded without supplement. Verse 13, the first day's burnt offering, from the base text: "And ye shall offer a burnt offering, a sacrifice made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD; thirteen young bullocks, two rams, and fourteen lambs of the first year; they shall be without blemish:" (29:13) Registered: thirteen young bullocks is the largest single-day quantity of burnt offering in the whole book (the previous chapter's largest single day is two bullocks, 28:11, 19); the rams rise to two and the lambs to fourteen — each twice the trumpets and atonement stations, while the bullocks differ thirteenfold: this station's "heaviness" lies wholly in the bullocks. Verses 14-15, the first day's meat offering, from the base text: "And their meat offering shall be of flour mingled with oil, three tenth deals unto every bullock of the thirteen bullocks, two tenth deals to each ram of the two rams, And a several tenth deal to each lamb of the fourteen lambs:" (29:14-15) Registered: the third full listing of the gradation, and, the quantities being amplified, this day's total of meat offering is the largest in the chapter. Verse 16, the first day's closing, from the base text: "And one kid of the goats for a sin offering; beside the continual burnt offering, his meat offering, and his drink offering." (29:16) Registered: the closing formula is fixed from here on, and every day of the feast of tabernacles hereafter, and the eighth day, follow this same form (29:19, 22, 25, 28, 31, 34, 38). Verse 17, the second day, from the base text: "And on the second day ye shall offer twelve young bullocks, two rams, fourteen lambs of the first year without spot:" (29:17) Registered, the beginning of the decrease: the bullocks fall by one (thirteen to twelve); the numbers of rams and lambs do not move. Verse 18, the second day's accompanying offerings, from the base text: "And their meat offering and their drink offerings for the bullocks, for the rams, and for the lambs, shall be according to their number, after the manner:" (29:18) Registered: the four words "according to their number, after the manner" from here replace the full listing of the gradation — the proportions do not change, they are simply reckoned by each day's quantities; this form occurs six times more hereafter (29:21, 24, 27, 30, 33, 37). Verse 19, the second day's closing, in the same form as verse 16: one kid of the goats for a sin offering, beside the continual offering (29:19, registered from the base text).

(Argument) First, on what day are these thirteen bullocks set out? The fifteenth day of the seventh month is the season when the harvest has been gathered in (Lev 23:39, "when ye have gathered in the fruit of the land," cross-reference layer): the threshing floor is full, the winepress is full, a year's sweat has become grain in the barn. Precisely when the people's hands are fullest, God issues the heaviest offering-list of the year: thirteen bullocks in one day, set out for seven days running. When a man's hands are full, he is most reluctant to let go; the better the harvest, the more easily the heart is occupied by the harvest — the land is mine, the grain is mine, and even in thanksgiving he wants to keep a margin. God's pattern runs against this temper: the first lesson of harvest is not enjoyment but offering; and offering heavier than in a lean year, more solemn than on an ordinary day. Thirteen bullocks on the altar declare publicly: this land is not ours, it is the LORD's; this grain is not what we earned, it is what He gave. We today should learn the same lesson: the month of the best income, the year of the smoothest enterprise, is exactly the time to increase offering and thanksgiving, not the time to remember Him only when circumstances turn and we need to ask. The offering made in abundance is faith's thermometer — this list measures whether, the more the hands hold, the more the heart turns toward God or toward the barn.

(Argument) Next, this chapter's "silence." Leviticus 23, recording these seven days, records booths, branches, and rejoicing (cross-reference layer); this chapter, recording these seven days, has in its twenty-three verses only numbers: how many bullocks, how many rams, how much flour, the drink offerings after the manner. No booths, no branches, not one sentence of "ye shall rejoice." This is not the chapter's deficiency but its duty: it is a table of offering-quantities, and all its care lies in the numbers — thirteen bullocks are not spoken casually; they are arranged by day, after the manner, prepared without break for seven days. Worship prepared to this degree is itself a form of godliness: not scraping together a number when the feast arrives, but counting out beforehand, one by one, the seventy bullocks and the ninety-eight lambs. When we hold meetings and keep feasts today, we most easily lose on preparation: the heart is warm but the affairs are in disorder, and when the moment comes we scramble. The numbers of these twenty-three verses say: a heart that loves God must land on preparation that can be counted. Thanksgiving that stays at a spoken "thank the Lord" is cheap; thanksgiving that lands on bullocks and lambs counted beforehand, on fine flour, oil, and wine prepared beforehand, is the sweet savour of these seven days.

The "manner" of "after the manner" refers, like the "manner" of "according unto their manner" at 29:6, to the whole book's established standing proportions for the accompanying offerings (the general rule of 28:14 and Numbers 15, cross-reference layer); this sermon registers only the base text's rendering and makes no further statement on the original. (Fact) This section closes by registering the account of the feast's first two days: the first day — thirteen bullocks, two rams, fourteen lambs, one goat, the meat offering fully listed (29:13-16); the second day — twelve bullocks, two rams, fourteen lambs, one goat, the accompanying offerings "according to their number, after the manner" (29:17-19). And the rule of the decrease is already formed: hereafter the bullocks decrease by one each day, the rams stand constantly at two, the lambs constantly at fourteen, the kid of the goats constantly at one — within the seven days the only number that slides is the bullocks; all the rest is nailed fast. The forming of this rule is in this section; its unfolding over the five days that follow is in the next.

### Part Four: The Third Day to the Seventh Day — One Fewer Each Day, Seventy in the Seven Days (29:20-34) (36:00-48:00)

(Fact) Verses 20-22, the third day, registered from the base text: eleven bullocks, two rams, fourteen lambs of the first year without blemish; the accompanying offerings "according to their number, after the manner"; and one goat for a sin offering, beside the continual burnt offering (29:20-22). Verses 23-25, the fourth day: ten bullocks, two rams, fourteen lambs, one kid of the goats, the accompanying offerings after the manner, the closing in the same form (29:23-25). Verses 26-28, the fifth day: nine bullocks, the rest the same (29:26-28). Verses 29-31, the sixth day: eight bullocks, the rest the same (29:29-31). Verses 32-34, the seventh day: seven bullocks, two rams, fourteen lambs, one goat, the accompanying offerings after the manner, the closing in the same form (29:32-34). The sameness and the difference in the five days' wording, registered item by item: the same — each day's three-verse group-pattern does not change (the main items, the accompanying offerings, the sin-offering closing), and the closing sentence occurs ten times in the whole chapter (29:6, 11, 16, 19, 22, 25, 28, 31, 34, 38; for the count see Endnote 4); the different — only the number of bullocks decreases day by day: eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven. The seven days' total of bullocks, added up again by hand: 13 + 12 + 11 + 10 + 9 + 8 + 7 = 70 (for the process of re-addition see Endnote 4). The seven days' total account: seventy bullocks, fourteen rams (2 × 7), ninety-eight lambs (14 × 7), seven goats; the meat offerings and drink offerings on all seven days are according to their number, after the manner, and are not listed again day by day. And one register of wording in parallel columns: in the Chinese base text the two rams are called 公绵羊 on the second day (29:17) and in the main items of the third to the seventh days alike (29:20, 23, 26, 29, 32) — the same two animals under one uniform wording, registered from the Chinese base text as it stands (an earlier distinction of wording between the days, once registered in the Chinese manuscript's collation note, was an error, corrected in this batch's Chinese review); the KJV reads "two rams" on all seven days without distinction, registered as it stands. The KJV has a distinction of its own at the sin offering: "one kid of the goats" on the first, second, and fourth days of the feast (29:16, 19, 25) and "one goat" on the third, fifth, sixth, and seventh days (29:22, 28, 31, 34), registered in parallel columns from the anchor.

(Argument) These five days of text read almost as repetition — the same sentence-pattern, the same closing, only the number of bullocks one less each day. Scripture is not afraid of repetition, because the lesson it would teach is hidden in the repetition: by the third and fourth days of the feast the freshness has passed and weariness comes, and a man is most apt to discount in his heart — prepare one fewer, arrive a little later, and turn "after the manner" into "making do." God writes these last five days so uniformly precisely to forbid the discount: the bullocks decrease by rule — that is God's arithmetic of subtraction; the fourteen lambs must not be one fewer, the goat each day must not be lacking, the accompanying offerings according to their number, after the manner, must not be economized. What God has ordained may decrease; what man steals may not decrease — this boundary these five days draw perfectly clearly. Our weariness in service mostly loses not on great matters but on "the fifth day": on the first day the enthusiasm is still there, on the second it still holds, but when the days are half spent the heart begins to find itself a way down. This chapter says: the fifth day's offering is of the same form, the same quality, and the same closing as the first day's; to keep ordinary days with the same seriousness as the solemn days is the true sweet savour of these seven days. If your seventh day still follows the first day's manner, your worship stands firm.

(Argument) The decrease itself also deserves a sentence. Why do the bullocks fall by one each day, down to the seventh day's seven? The text does not explain; it only arranges the numbers (for the readings at the reading level see this section's discussion layer). But the arrangement itself carries one plain lesson: abundance must have its gathering in. However grand the seven days' feast, there is a seventh day; the bullocks falling from thirteen to seven teach the people to learn restraint at the height of abundance — to gather in neatly, after the manner, and with a heart content. How many men's failure lies not in a lean year but in a fat one: having, they display, and displaying, they spend away both their substance and their reverence; the feast over, their days cannot return to the plainness of the continual offering. God writes the subtraction into the feast beforehand: after the grandest day, one fewer each day, down to the seventh day, then closed by the eighth day's small round number of one bullock (29:36), and then back to the continual offering's two lambs a day (28:3-8, previous-text layer). To walk step by step back from the solemnity of thirteen bullocks to the ordinariness of two lambs, each step after the manner and without blemish — that is the man who has truly kept the feast. Keeping a feast is not escaping the ordinary; it is bringing the feast's sweet savour back into the ordinary. The man who, back in the ordinary, can still offer those two lambs, has not kept the feast in vain.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. No new word can be registered in this section; two items are only recounted: in the Chinese base text the wording rendered "without blemish" stands in the feast's main items at 29:13, 17, 20, 23, 26, 29, 32, seven verses in all, present at the head verse of each of the seven days and lacking on no day; on the KJV letter the same seven places divide into "without blemish" five times (29:13, 20, 23, 29, 32) and "without spot" twice (29:17, 26), registered as the anchor gives them. "According to their number, after the manner" stands at 29:18, 21, 24, 27, 30, 33, six verses (the second to the seventh day), and once more on the eighth day at 29:37, seven occurrences in all. The bullocks' daily decrease by one is an arithmetical descent (first term 13, last term 7, seven terms in all), its sum 70; this sermon registers the process of re-addition at Endnote 4 and cites no other account.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The reading of the seventy bullocks is set out in parallel, without adjudication. The reading runs more than one way: one way looks to the feast's structure — the bullocks' decrease matches the feast's gradual approach to its close, falling to seven on the seventh day and gathered in to one on the eighth (29:36); the number itself is the feast's breathing. Another way looks to the total of seventy — in later Jewish tradition there is a reading that joins the seventy bullocks with the number of "the nations" (tradition layer: the list of nations in Genesis 10 is traditionally counted as seventy), holding that the feast's offerings are offered also on behalf of the nations. This sermon registers only that the two ways stand side by side: the text of this chapter gives only the number (one fewer each day, seventy in all) and gives no reason; the first way is a structural observation, the second is a later traditional reading, and neither is pronounced upon at this chapter's fact layer. The argument of the main text uses only the three items the text itself states — decrease, constancy, and after the manner.

(Fact) This section closes by registering the full account of the feast's seven days (every item already counted; the details are at Endnote 4): seventy bullocks (one fewer each day), fourteen rams, ninety-eight lambs, seven goats; a holy convocation is expressly stated on the first day (29:12), and the prohibition of work is expressly stated on the first day; the closing sentence "beside the continual burnt offering" occurs seven times within the seven days (29:16, 19, 22, 25, 28, 31, 34) — however grand the seven days, the continual offering was not abolished on a single day. And a contrast with the feast of unleavened bread in the previous chapter is registered: the seven days of unleavened bread follow the same pattern every day (28:24, after this manner daily, throughout the seven days), while the seven days of this feast decrease by one each day — the same seven days, one constant, one decreasing; constancy has its lesson (do not weary), decrease has its lesson (know how to gather in); read the two books together, and the pattern of the seven days is read in full.

### Part Five: The Solemn Assembly of the Eighth Day and the Summary of the Whole Book — All Beside Vows and Freewill Offerings (29:35-40, with the Whole Chapter) (48:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 35, the eighth day's assembly, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "On the eighth day ye shall have a solemn assembly: ye shall do no servile work therein:" (29:35) Registered item by item: first, this day is the eighth day after the feast's seven days — reckoned from the fifteenth day of the seventh month it falls on the twenty-second day of the seventh month (this date is a reckoning from the starting day; this verse does not state it expressly, see Endnote 8); second, the assembly's name is uniquely "a solemn assembly" — beside the three occurrences of "holy convocation" in the chapter (29:1, 7, 12) this day alone uses this name; third, the prohibition of work returns to the full form, "ye shall do no servile work." Verse 36, the eighth day's burnt offering, from the base text: "But ye shall offer a burnt offering, a sacrifice made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD: one bullock, one ram, seven lambs of the first year without blemish:" (29:36) Registered: the quantity of offering returns to the small round combination — one bullock, one ram, seven lambs, in the same form as the trumpets (29:2) and the day of atonement (29:8); "sweet savour" here reaches its fifth occurrence in the chapter (for the count see Endnote 4). Verse 37: the accompanying offerings after the manner (29:37). Verse 38, atonement and closing: one goat for a sin offering, beside the continual burnt offering (29:38) — the closing sentence's tenth occurrence in the chapter, and its eighth within the feast system. Verse 39, the summary of the whole book, from the base text: "These things ye shall do unto the LORD in your set feasts, beside your vows, and your freewill offerings, for your burnt offerings, and for your meat offerings, and for your drink offerings, and for your peace offerings." (29:39) Three items registered: first, the range of "these things" is not limited by this verse itself to this one chapter (for its precise reference see this section's discussion layer); second, the summary's landing point is still "beside" — the public offerings of the feasts are to be offered additionally, beside what each man vows and his freewill offerings, and the two do not offset each other; third, the word "peace offerings" occurs only this once in the whole chapter — everything recorded in the two chapters is public burnt offerings, meat offerings, drink offerings, and sin offerings, and peace offerings appear only in the summary under the heading of personal vows and freewill offerings, registered as recorded. Verse 40, the seal of delivery, from the base text: "And Moses told the children of Israel according to all that the LORD commanded Moses." (29:40) Registered: the previous chapter, chapter 28, opens with God's speaking (28:1-2), and the two chapters as one book close with Moses delivering the words exactly as spoken — the calendar's last stroke is not an offering but a delivery: only when the words have been delivered is the calendar promulgated.

(Argument) This stroke of the eighth day shows the greatest care. Seven days of feast are long enough; by man's reckoning, once the seventh day disperses, it disperses. God adds one day beyond the seven: a solemn assembly. Not another great spread — the offering-quantity is gathered back to the small round number of one bullock; it is the gathering back of the dispersing heart, assembling once more in solemnity before each returns to his own days. This gives us a most practical lesson: after a great matter there must be a gathering in. The special meeting has ended, the camp is over, the feast is past, and a man is most apt to collapse at once back into his old form, the heat cooling through in three days. The solemn assembly is God's last lock on the feast: do not hurry back; first stop solemnly for one day — recall before God what these seven days have received, and settle it firmly — and only then step toward home. When the church today has finished a great matter, it should learn this day: keep an assembly of gathering in, a quiet recollection, a concrete resolve, and do not let grace scatter in the noise of dispersal. The eighth day is not solemn in display, yet it cannot be lacking — how many men's spiritual peaks bear no fruit for just this: there was no eighth day.

(Argument) The "beside" of verse 39 must be spoken to all who would offset public duty with personal zeal. The offerings of vows and the freewill offerings are good — they are the special covenants and touches between a man and God. But the text states it flatly: these things of the feasts are to be done beside all that. That is: however great a vow you have vowed, however much you have freely offered, none of it can offset the feasts the congregation is to keep and the public offerings it is to present. Personal godliness, however warm, cannot offset the body's duty: you never appear at the prayer meeting, yet at the year-end thanksgiving you gave a large sum — the account does not balance that way. And the reverse is also true: feasts kept ever so completely in public cannot replace your personal vows and freewill offerings before God — the vow you personally must pay, the offering you personally must give, the feasts cannot pay for you. Verse 39 divides the two accounts perfectly clearly: the public to the public, the personal to the personal, each offered as its own, and neither may offset the other with its own book. Are both of your accounts clear?

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The original word behind "solemn assembly" is ʿăṣeret, denoting a solemn gathering, an assembly of gathering in; its number is marked in this sermon as pending verification, see Endnote 5. The "vow" of "your vows" is the word neder, and "freewill offerings" is the word nedābâ (an offering freely given); the numbers of both words are marked as pending verification, see Endnote 5, and only the word-forms and the base text's renderings are registered. The chapter's persons recounted: the only person named in the whole chapter is Moses (29:40); the only speaker is God (this chapter continues the speech of 28:1-2 without a new introduction, see Endnote 7); no place-name occurs anywhere in the chapter — the feasts of the seventh month record dates only, not places, registered as recorded.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters are set out in parallel, without adjudication. First, the range of "these things" (29:39): some hold that it refers only to the feasts of the seventh month in this chapter; others that it looks back over all the feast-offerings of chapters 28 and 29 as one book — this verse stands at the end of this chapter, immediately following the two chapters, and its word "set feasts" answers to the governing word môʿēd of 28:2. Both ways have their textual ground; this sermon registers only this verse's position and wording, and the title takes its meaning only from the verse's summary sentence-form, making no assertion of range at the fact layer. Second, one further register of this chapter's fullness and brevity against Leviticus 23: Leviticus 23 records for the feast of tabernacles the booths, the branches, and the rejoicing, calls the day of trumpets "a memorial of blowing of trumpets," and records for the day of atonement the affliction and the name of atonement; this chapter, for all three feasts, records only dates, holy convocations, rest from work, and quantities of offering, carrying neither names, nor rites, nor meanings. The reading of a complementary division of labour (one place governing feast-meaning and rite, the other the public quantities of offering) and the reading of editorial layers stand side by side; this sermon registers only that the two stand side by side, and makes no adjudication.

(New Testament Reading) The apostle Paul exhorts the believers: "I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service." (Rom 12:1, cross-reference reading layer) One contrast is registered at the reading layer: the public offering-list of the two chapters as one book — from the continual offering to the feasts, layer upon layer of "beside," every item accounted — is read in the New Testament as a shadow, whose substance is the believer's presenting of his own body as a living sacrifice: however completely the public feasts are kept, they must at last land on the individual's offering of himself. The reading layer registers only how the New Testament gathers up this system of offerings; it does not fill the doctrine of the living sacrifice back in as this chapter's rule. (Argument) The whole chapter closes with one sentence left for each of three kinds of people. First, for the man whose days have no point of reminder: nail a "first day" into your own year first — one trumpet blast, one assembling, one laying out of half a year's accounts in self-examination; without awaking underneath, abundance sooner or later turns to indulgence. ## Devotional Excerpt

In the seventh month God arranged four assemblies, and their order cannot be shuffled by a single step: first the trumpet on the first day, then affliction on the tenth, then the seven days of abundance from the fifteenth, and at the end one solemn assembly to gather it all up. "And ye shall have on the tenth day of this seventh month an holy convocation; and ye shall afflict your souls: ye shall not do any work therein:" (29:7) On this day the offering is at its lightest — only one bullock; but on this day the heart must be at its heaviest — afflict your souls. We so often reverse the order: we long for the abundance of the fifteenth and skip the affliction of the tenth; we want more blessing and do not want first to deal with sin. But without the tears of the tenth, the feast of the fifteenth has no sweetness to taste; with the account of sin still outstanding, however abundant the feast, it is only noise. Keep a "tenth day" for yourself today: stop every work, do nothing for men to see, only lay out before God, one by one, the transgressions accumulated over this year — confess them thoroughly and deal with them clean. Then you will discover that the abundance God has arranged behind it was waiting all along for an afflicted heart to enjoy it. And the solemn assembly of the eighth day reminds us: after abundance do not disperse at once; first, before God, solemnly gather in your heart, think through the grace received and settle it firmly, and then return into the ordinary days. And the root of all this stands in the summary sentence: "These things ye shall do unto the LORD in your set feasts" (29:39) — the days are appointed by Him, the order is arranged by Him; we have only to offer according to the dates and to keep according to the order. When the order is right, the feasts are all right, and the year's days are all right too.

English (KJV-based):

"These things ye shall do unto the LORD in your set feasts, beside your vows, and your freewill offerings, for your burnt offerings, and for your meat offerings, and for your drink offerings, and for your peace offerings." (Num 29:39, KJV) "And Moses told the children of Israel according to all that the LORD commanded Moses." (Num 29:40, KJV) God's order for the seventh month cannot be shuffled: first the trumpet, then the afflicted soul, then seven days of abundance, then one solemn day to gather it all up. Do not skip the tenth day. Stop every work, afflict your soul, and settle the year's account of sin before God — the feast that follows will taste entirely different to a heart that has been humbled first.

## Summary (about 100 words)

Moses questioned God about why He had mistreated the people, and God did not argue; He only declared: now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh. In the name of the LORD He reaffirmed the covenant, spoke seven "I wills" to accomplish redemption, and sent Moses to proclaim again. The people, discouraged by harsh labor, would not listen; Moses raised again his unskilled lips, and God still answered with a command and with Aaron.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The end of the previous chapter is the heaviest prayer in the whole Book of Exodus. Moses was sent to Pharaoh, and he thought that since God had spoken, things should at least move in a good direction. Instead Pharaoh said, who is the LORD, I do not know the LORD, and what followed was doubled labor: the supply of straw stopped, and the number of bricks to be delivered could not be reduced by one. The officers were beaten, the officers came and cried to Moses, and the people poured all their resentment onto Moses and Aaron. Moses returned to the LORD and said very heavy words: Lord, why have You mistreated this people? Why did You send me? Since I came to Pharaoh to speak in Your name, he has mistreated this people, and You have not delivered them at all.

This is the questioning of a preacher, of a man leading a team, of a man who had truly obeyed, at the moment when things became worse. It is not pretty and it is not dignified; it is simply real. Brothers and sisters, are there people among us who have lived through such a moment: you clearly walked according to God's word, yet your situation became harder than when you did not walk; you prayed, and things did not loosen but tightened further; you brought the problem before God, and everything that came out of your mouth was why. If so, this chapter today was prepared for you.

How did God answer Moses? He did not first explain the causes of suffering, He did not defend Himself, and He did not say you have misunderstood Me. The first sentence He spoke was: now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh. A little later He said again: I am the LORD. These two sentences — one telling what He will do, one telling who He is — stand at the head and the tail and hold up the whole chapter. The lesson we are to learn today is hidden right here: God's way of answering questioning is not an essay of explanation, but a fresh declaration of Himself.

Let us read together several key passages of today's Scripture:

"Then the LORD said unto Moses, Now shalt thou see what I will do to Pharaoh: for with a strong hand shall he let them go, and with a strong hand shall he drive them out of his land." (Exodus 6:1, excerpt, KJV)

"I am the LORD: I appeared unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob, by the name of God Almighty, but by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them. And I have also established my covenant with them, to give them the land of Canaan, the land of their pilgrimage, wherein they were strangers." (Exodus 6:2-4, excerpt, KJV)

"Wherefore say unto the children of Israel, I am the LORD, and I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians, and I will rid you out of their bondage, and I will redeem you with a stretched out arm, and with great judgments." (Exodus 6:6, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses spake so unto the children of Israel: but they hearkened not unto Moses for anguish of spirit, and for cruel bondage." (Exodus 6:9, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, some among us have come before You today carrying a why, and some are so tired they no longer even have strength to listen. Do not rebuke us first today; first set Your name again in our hearts. Let us know that while our circumstances have not yet changed, who You are has never changed. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: God Does Not Argue, He Only Declares — From God Almighty to I Am the LORD (6:1-5)

First look at the tone of God's answer. Moses asked three whys, and God answered not one of them directly. What He said was: now you shall see. Notice this "now." It does not mean, you will understand one day in heaven, nor does it mean, put your questions away first. He was moving Moses' eyes away from what Pharaoh was doing, onto what I will do to Pharaoh. In chapter 5, everything Moses' eyes were full of was Pharaoh: Pharaoh intensifying the oppression, Pharaoh saying he did not know the LORD, Pharaoh's taskmasters raising the stick. When a person is trapped in suffering, the eyes are most easily occupied by the side that is applying the pressure; looking and looking, all one sees is how heavy the other party's hand is, how hard the situation is, and how powerless one is oneself. God's first step was to take back Moses' right to see: do not only stare at what he is doing to you; begin to look at what I will do to him.

He also said that Pharaoh would, because of His strong hand, let Israel go, and even drive them out of his land. Today he lets them go; in the future he will even drive them out. Pharaoh's present hardness already has its ending written in God's word. This is not empty comfort; this is the declaration of the Sovereign. Brothers and sisters, faith often grows exactly like this: it is not that we first think all the reasoning through and then believe, but that God first sets His word before us, and we hold onto that sentence and walk forward step by step, and only later see it with our own eyes. When God said to Moses, now you shall see, He also meant: from today on, change the way you look — at events, and at Me.

Then God brought Moses back into the covenant. He said, I am the LORD; I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob by the name of God Almighty, but by my name the LORD I was not known to them. This must be explained clearly, so that it is not misunderstood. It does not mean that the patriarchs had never heard the name the LORD, for that name already appears in Genesis; it means that what the patriarchs mainly experienced and knew was God's appearing as the Almighty One — His acts of promising, blessing, and preserving — while the full manifestation bound up with the name the LORD, that once-for-all, covenant-keeping, large-scale work of deliverance, had not yet come. In the generation of the Exodus, God would let His people not merely hear that He is able, but experience with their own eyes what kind of covenant-keeping, mercy-showing God He is. A name here is not a label; a name is His self-revelation: He places His character, His faithfulness, and His acts together into the hands of His people.

God Almighty, in the original, is El Shaddai [2]. When Abraham was old and childless, God appeared to him by this name and said, I am God Almighty; walk before Me and be blameless; and when Jacob blessed Joseph at the end of his life, he also recalled God Almighty appearing to him at Luz and blessing him. In days of inability, of having no heir, of having no road to walk, what the patriarchs knew was this One: when human possibility ran out, His almightiness did not run out. Now God said to Moses, the Almighty One who appeared to the patriarchs and the LORD who speaks to you today are the same One. As I kept covenant with them in the past, so I will keep covenant with you today. This is what reaffirming the covenant means: it is not that God only now remembered the people, nor that Moses' questioning woke God up; it is that in the darkest moment God spread open again before Moses that covenant made hundreds of years earlier and let him look: the covenant is still there, and the One who made the covenant is still there.

God also spoke a very tender sentence: I have also heard the groaning of the children of Israel, whom the Egyptians keep in bondage, and I have remembered my covenant. Notice the order of the verbs: hearing comes first, remembering comes after. The people's groaning did not drift away into the air; God heard it, and having heard it, He bound it to His covenant: He remembered. In chapter 5 Moses said, You have not delivered them at all, and here God answered calmly: I have heard, I remember, and now I will act. Brothers and sisters, what a person fears most is suffering that no one knows about, tears that fall with nowhere to land. This passage tells us: not one groan of God's children is wasted. He may not act according to our timetable, but He has never been absent, and He has never forgotten. He remembers His covenant, and therefore He remembers the people in the covenant.

Notice also that in verse 1 God spoke of a strong hand, and in verse 6 He spoke again of a stretched out arm; both say the same thing: deliverance does not rest on the people being numerous enough, nor on Moses' eloquence being good enough, and still less on Pharaoh suddenly becoming kind-hearted, but on God's own acts personally intervening in history. People often place their hope in the wrong place: on circumstances loosening, on the other side conceding, on themselves trying a little harder — and in the end, once the hand slips, everything collapses. Here God set the foundation of hope straight: His hand will act, and the hand that once held up the patriarchs will now also hold up this generation. Moses' sentence at the end of chapter 5, You have not delivered them at all, spoke of the part the eyes could not yet see; God's answer in chapter 6 says that the progress of deliverance is never decided by human seeing. He has already heard, He has already remembered, He has already spoken; what follows is only the process of His words landing, sentence by sentence, on the ground. If a person remembers this order, he can stand firm even before he has seen, and even before Pharaoh lets the people go he will not misread Pharaoh's hardness as God's absence.

So in Part One we must grasp one order: God first declares who He is, and only then commands people what to do. Reverse the order, and a person collapses. If Moses stared at his own eloquence, he could only shrink back; if the people stared at their own brick kilns, they could only be discouraged. By putting His name first, God was, as it were, pouring the foundation of the whole matter again. With the foundation firm, the commands that follow can stand. This is also today's lesson for us: when your life is shaking, do not first ask how much longer you can hold on; first return to that sentence, I am the LORD, and first ask who the covenant-making God is. Who He is comes before, and is greater and longer-lasting than, how the situation looks.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Seven "I Wills" — The Pattern of Redemption Takes Complete Shape (6:6-8)

After God had declared His own name, He had Moses proclaim a set of words to the children of Israel. This set of words is one of the most orderly and most powerful declarations of redemption in the whole Old Testament. Count through it sentence by sentence and you will find that it is like a well-written pattern, ring linked to ring, with no step missing. It opens with, Wherefore say unto the children of Israel, I am the LORD, and at the end of verse 8 it is again, I am the LORD. At the head and at the tail, the same name locks every promise in between within His faithfulness.

The first "I will": "I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians" (6:6). Exodus was not the people escaping by themselves; it was God bringing them out. When the church speaks of salvation today, it must also remember this initiative: it was not that we first climbed up to God, but that God first stretched out His hand and brought us out from under the burden of sin and death.

The second "I will": "I will rid you out of their bondage" (6:6) — so that you will no longer do their labor [3]. Bringing out is not enough; the system of labor that weighed on them must also be broken off. When a person is saved, it is not merely a change of place, but a change of master and a change of identity: formerly doing forced labor for sin, now set free for righteousness.

The third "I will": "I will redeem you with a stretched out arm, and with great judgments" (6:6). That redemption is what finally delivers you from under their burdens. The word "redeem" is the central word of this passage [4]. In the Old Testament it carries the sense of buying back, of ransoming: the nearest kinsman pays the price and redeems back the person who was lost and the property that was lost. Here God calls Himself that Redeemer: He will pay the cost and redeem His own people back into His own hand. And judgment and redemption appearing in the same sentence remind us that God's deliverance is never a cheap smoothing-over: on one side He carries out righteous judgment, and on the other He accomplishes complete rescue. The expression "a stretched out arm" is also here [5], and it later became Israel's fixed way of remembering the Exodus for generation after generation: our God is the One who brought us out with a mighty hand and a stretched out arm.

The fourth "I will": I will take you to me for a people. Here redemption reaches the height of relationship. God saves people not merely to let them go, but to take them to Himself. A slave who is released, if no one takes him in, is still a drifter; but God said, you shall be My people. This is a move from having no share to having a share, from being another's forced laborers to being God's people. The goal of the Exodus was never merely to leave Egypt, but to belong to the LORD.

The fifth "I will": I will be to you a God. It pairs exactly with the previous sentence: you are My people, and I am your God. This is the most central sentence-form of the covenant relationship, appearing again and again later in the prophets and in the promises of the New Testament. What God wants is not a batch of released laborers, but a community that makes covenant with Him, knows Him, and worships Him. That we today call the church God's people has its root planted right here.

The sixth "I will": I will bring you in unto the land. Which land? The land He swore to give to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob. Deliverance has a direction; it is not aimless wandering after leaving Egypt. When God saves us, it is likewise never merely a matter of solving this one present difficulty and being done; He has a place He wants to lead us to, and purposes He wants to accomplish in us. Where the second half of a life is heading is not answered in Pharaoh's hand, but in the hand of the covenant-making God.

The seventh "I will": I will give it you for an heritage. To bring them in, and also to give it down as an inheritance, so that the people may dwell there in peace, receive it, and pass it to their descendants. At this step the pattern of redemption is complete: beginning from deliverance from burdens, passing through redemption, covenant, and belonging, all the way to receiving an inheritance. God does not do His work by halves. If He says I am the LORD, He will walk His people's road, stage by stage, from bondage to inheritance.

Brothers and sisters, please read these seven "I wills" through in one connected reading. Did they answer Moses' questioning in chapter 5? In terms of reasoning, they did not explain why that season of suffering had to come first; in terms of weight, they far exceed an explanation. They amount to God saying: you ask Me why; I first tell you what I will do, and I do it with My name as the guarantee. What a person in suffering sometimes needs most is not a manual of instructions, but a promise he can grasp. What God gave Moses was exactly such a promise: seven times "I will," without one shrinking back, and standing in the middle is the God with the outstretched arm.

These seven "I wills" also carry a weight that is easy to miss: they are all God's one-sided declarations, with no condition attached requiring the people to reach a standard first. He did not say, first become strong, and then I will bring you out; He did not say, first be able to listen, and then I will accomplish redemption; still less did He say, first prove that you deserve it, and then I will take you as My people. The order is exactly the opposite: because of the covenant, because of His name, He first established what He would do, and the people within it were slowly brought out, redeemed, taken in, and led. The flavor of grace is right here: God's people were not chosen only after they were first put in order, but after being chosen they are, generation by generation, put in order by Him into the likeness of His people. When we come before God today, it is likewise not that we first repair ourselves and only then dare to open our mouths, but that we first hear His I am the LORD, first admit that we are still standing in the brick kiln, and then let each of His "I wills" be fulfilled in us one by one. A person who has heard the promise need not pretend he is already standing in the promised land; the preciousness of the promise lies precisely in its being heard first while still in the land of Egypt.

We must also add one honest sentence: this passage was not spoken to people living in ease, but to people still in the brick kilns. God did not wait until the people had first left the harsh labor before declaring these seven "I wills"; He spoke the complete redemption aloud precisely when the labor was heaviest and the tally of bricks tightest. The order of faith is often like this: first there is God's word, and only later the change of circumstances; first one hears I am the LORD, and only later sees Pharaoh let the people go. Whoever waits until circumstances are completely good before he is willing to believe will forever wait past that stretch of road on which faith grows.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The People Who Would Not Listen, Moses Raising His Old Shortcoming Again, and a Quiet Genealogy (6:9-13, 14-27, 28-30)

What was the result when such a powerful set of words was proclaimed? The Scripture is very honest: Moses spoke so to the children of Israel, but they did not listen to Moses, because of anguish of spirit and cruel bondage. The proclamation did not fail, and the word did not fall empty, but those who heard could not take it in.

Here we must be gentle, and we must be honest. That the people would not listen, the original says, was because of anguish of spirit — literally, shortness of breath, a state of being pressed down so heavily that one cannot breathe evenly [6]. When a person has been pressed by harsh labor until he cannot even breathe evenly, telling him first to have faith, first to pull himself together, first to be positive is often useless: however right the words are, they cannot get in. This is not making an excuse for unbelief; it is admitting that suffering has its real weight. Some brothers and sisters, amid serious illness, amid unemployment, amid a broken family, for a time cannot take in any reasoning — not because they are hard-hearted, but because they are too tired. If the church only blames people at such a time for weak faith, it has learned this lesson wrongly. Here God did not immediately rebuke the people; He turned and continued speaking to Moses and continued pushing the plan forward. God understands people who are short of breath, and God has patience to wait until they have caught their breath. If today you are exactly that person who cannot take it in, do not first condemn yourself to despair: that God's word cannot get in today does not mean that God has left. The seed of the word is sown first, and He will watch over the hour when it awakens in your heart.

And Moses? Facing the people's not listening, he turned back to God and raised again his old problem: the children of Israel have not listened to me; how then shall Pharaoh hear me, who am of uncircumcised lips? The King James Version keeps the Hebrew wording here — "of uncircumcised lips" — and the literal meaning of the original is a man of uncircumcised lips [7] — that is to say, my lips are as if sealed by something, unclean, unfit, unable to speak fitting words. From the burning bush onward, this had always been Moses' knot. In chapter 3 he said, who am I; in chapter 4 he said, I have never been eloquent; and by chapter 6, after experiencing one failure, this knot was not only not untied, it was tied even tighter. Brothers and sisters, this too is a real person. A person's "I cannot" is often not humility; it is fear fed large by failure. What Moses said was the truth — his eloquence was indeed limited — but he placed this truth in front of God's name, letting "I cannot" block "I am the LORD."

How did God respond? He did not debate eloquence with Moses; He directly gave a command: charge Pharaoh to let the children of Israel go out of his land; and He had already arranged Aaron as Moses' mouth, to tell Pharaoh everything He commanded. God's way of responding was exactly the same as at the beginning: no arguing, only declaring; not entangling Himself in man's inability, only setting out the command and the companion. You say you cannot; I send Aaron to go with you. You say the people will not listen; the word must still be proclaimed again. You say Pharaoh will not listen; I charge you again to go and speak. When God uses a person, He never waits until the person is no longer weak before using him; He walks forward carrying man's weakness with Him. By the end of the chapter, in verse 30, Moses said the same sentence once more before the LORD: behold, I am of uncircumcised lips, and how shall Pharaoh hearken unto me? The Bible records it without begrudging the repetition, so that we may see: within one chapter, God had spoken so much of I am the LORD, and man still returns to his I cannot. Never mind — tomorrow the Scripture goes on, and God goes on speaking. Our lesson is not to guarantee that we will never shrink back again, but after every shrinking back to return again before His word.

Between Moses' raising this again and its repetition at the end of the chapter, the Bible quietly inserts a genealogy, writing from Reuben and Simeon down to Levi, and then to the generation of Moses and Aaron, even recording Aaron's wife, sons, and his grandson Phinehas [8]. At the tensest point of the narrative, why does it suddenly stop to report a family line? In the ancient world, before a messenger went into battle his family line had to be verified: whose house do these two men actually belong to, where do they come from, and are they qualified to stand and speak before the people and before Pharaoh? The line of Levi is written out in special detail because Moses and Aaron both came from this tribe; writing down to them amounts to handing the microphone back to these two messengers and saying to the reader: the ones who spoke before, and who must still go to see Pharaoh afterward, are these two men of this house. In the sermon we pass over it here; its function is not to seize the main line, but to set a seal of family origin upon the persons of the main line. The details we leave in the endnotes for slow reading; in the text we keep only one sentence: before God raises a person up to do His work, He first sets that person's origin within His narrative — not even a descendant of Levi raised in Egypt is an exception.

Looked at one layer deeper, the people's not listening and Moses' raising his old shortcoming again are in fact two forms of the same wound. The people's wound was in the ears, pressed down until they could not take words in; Moses' wound was in the lips, frightened until he could not speak words out. One below the platform, one upon it — it turns out that the valley of the spirit makes no distinction of position: the one who leads can also be short of breath, and the one who proclaims can also raise his old knot again. If the church understands this, it will not grade weakness, and will not think that only those sitting below need comfort while those standing above can only grit their teeth and endure. Chapter 6 records these two weaknesses side by side, and lets God's word continue walking forward between them — and that itself is a very great comfort: God's plan is not sustained by the completeness of either side, and His word still opens its own road between man's inability to take it in and inability to speak it out. Those of us doing the work in this generation must especially learn this lesson: when a companion cannot take it in, do not abandon him; when you yourself cannot speak it out, do not abandon yourself; to place people and matters back again under that sentence I am the LORD is more in line with the teaching of this chapter than mutual accusation.

So the passage for today leaves us with at least three sentences to carry away. The first, for those still in harsh labor: when your spirit is anguished, being unable to take words in is real; come first before God and breathe, and do not pretend to be strong — God remembers the covenant, and He also remembers you. The second, for those raising old shortcomings again: your "I cannot" may be entirely factual, but facts cannot be God; set I am the LORD in front of I cannot, and your feet can still step forward. The third, for proclaimers, for parents and leaders: in the moment when people will not listen, the word must still be proclaimed. That the people do not listen today does not mean God's word has fallen empty; we are responsible for proclaiming faithfully, and God is responsible for making the word sprout in His own hour. Do not, because of one refusal to listen, put away the words you ought to speak; and do not, because of one failure, conclude that Pharaoh will never let the people go. The story has not yet reached the night of the Passover.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we walked from Moses' why to God's I am the LORD. The questioning of chapter 5 did not receive an essay of explanation; it received a name, a reaffirmed covenant, and seven "I wills." God said: now you shall see. The people were discouraged and would not listen, Moses raised again the old knot of his lips, and God stopped for neither: the command remained, Aaron remained, and the word still had to be proclaimed. Brothers and sisters, please remember the order of this chapter: wherever I am the LORD appears, man's I cannot must step back one step. Stepping back is not disappearing; it is no longer ruling.

Before we close, add one sentence about the side of this chapter that reads Pharaoh: in chapter 5 Pharaoh asked, who is the LORD? I do not know Him. Chapter 6 is already answering that sentence — only the way of answering is not a debate, but the series of acts that follows. God would let Pharaoh know Him, would let Israel know Him, and would first let Moses know Him. Knowing God, in the Book of Exodus, is never a classroom concept, but a certainty slowly grown after a person has been grasped again and again by His word amid events. What we ask for today is also this kind of knowing: not understanding one more doctrine, but trusting more the God who has already spoken.

Let us pray together: Lord, who is of Himself and eternal, thank You that You did not fall silent before our questioning, and did not answer our discouragement with rebuke. You reaffirmed Your covenant; You said You have heard and You remember, and that You will redeem us with a stretched out arm. Help those still short of breath under harsh labor to catch their breath first in Your arms; help those who say again and again I cannot to set Your name first again. Let those of us who today cannot listen be able to listen another day, and those who today cannot speak it out be able to proclaim it another day. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who established the new covenant with His own blood and redeemed us from sin, Amen.

[1] ani YHWH: Hebrew אֲנִי יְהוָה, ani (אֲנִי, H589, I) plus YHWH (יְהוָה, H3068), forming God's self-declaration formula; in this chapter it appears at 6:2, 6, 7, 8, 29, of which 6:6-8 opens with it and closes the seven "I wills" with it, and 6:29 is its restatement when God charges Moses to speak to Pharaoh; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "wherever I am the LORD appears, I cannot must step back" is a homiletical exposition based on this declaration formula, stated separately from the lexical facts, as indicated in the context of the text.

[2] El Shaddai: Hebrew אֵל שַׁדַּי, El (אֵל, H410, God) plus Shaddai (שַׁדַּי, H7706), rendered "God Almighty" in the King James Version, at 6:3; see Genesis 17:1, where God appeared to Abram and called Himself God Almighty, and Genesis 35:11, where He appeared to Jacob under the same name. The etymology of the second element of this name is discussed in scholarship in several ways, deriving it from a word for mountain or from a word for supplying and nourishing, among others, and there is to date no settled conclusion; this manuscript, following BDB and HALOT, takes only the traditional rendering "God Almighty" and makes no etymological determination. The text's summary that the patriarchs knew God mainly by this name is a homiletical generalization about the emphasis of the appearing, distinct from the statement of 6:3 itself, and is exposition.

[3] Ridding out of bondage: in the latter half of verse 6 and the former part of verse 7, the text uses a group of verbs of bringing out from under burdens and ridding out of bondage to express deliverance from the Egyptian system of slavery; the second part of this manuscript, "you will no longer do their labor," is its homiletical paraphrase; the general sense of the words is according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[4] ga'al: Hebrew גָּאַל (H1350), to redeem, to buy back, to act as the kinsman-redeemer, at 6:6, "I will redeem you"; this word recurs in Leviticus in the kinsman's redemption of property and persons, and in Ruth where Boaz acts as redeemer, among other places; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "God calls Himself the Redeemer and pays the cost to redeem His people back" is a homiletical exposition based on this word, stated separately from the lexical facts.

[5] zeroa' netuyah: Hebrew זְרוֹעַ נְטוּיָה, zeroa' (זְרוֹעַ, H2220, arm) plus netuyah (נְטוּיָה, from the verb natah, נָטָה, H5186, to stretch out, to extend), "a stretched out arm," at 6:6; see Deuteronomy 26:8, "the LORD brought us forth out of Egypt with a mighty hand, and with an outstretched arm," a fixed expression in Israel's confessional recital; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[6] qotser ruach: Hebrew קֹצֶר רוּחַ, qotser (קֹצֶר, H7115, shortness, from qatsar) plus ruach (רוּחַ, H7307, breath, spirit), literally shortness of breath, extended to anguish of spirit and impatient discouragement, at 6:9; the KJV renders it "anguish of spirit"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "discouragement is not an excuse for unbelief but the real weight of suffering" is a pastoral exposition based on this word, indicated in the text as the sermon's position.

[7] aral sephatayim: Hebrew עֲרַל שְׂפָתַיִם, aral (עָרֵל, H6189, uncircumcised) plus sephatayim (שְׂפָתַיִם, H8193, lips, dual), "uncircumcised lips," at 6:12 and 30; the KJV renders it "uncircumcised lips"; its image is of lips as if sealed by a foreskin, unclean and unfit for use; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[8] Brief note on the genealogy (6:14-25): the genealogy begins with Reuben, Israel's firstborn, and Simeon the second son, and its weight falls on the line of Levi the third son: Gershon, Kohath, and Merari; Kohath begot Amram, and Amram married his aunt Jochebed, who bore Aaron and Moses; Aaron married Elisheba and begot Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar, and Eleazar begot Phinehas. Inserted at the climax of the narrative, this section verifies the family origin of Moses and Aaron and confirms the messengers' identity and tribal origin; its structural function is greater than its information itself. The treatment in the text is abbreviated; the names and generations follow the text of the King James Version.

[9] Brief note on the four cups of Passover: in later Jewish tradition the Passover meal includes the drinking of four cups, and rabbinic literature often associates the four cups with the fourfold expressions of redemption in 6:6-7 (I will bring you out, I will rid you out of bondage, I will redeem you, I will take you to me for a people) in recitation. This association is a reading of later rabbinic tradition and is not something the text of Scripture itself states explicitly; this manuscript mentions it only in this note and does not base any argument in the text upon it.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "I am the LORD, and I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians, and I will redeem you with a stretched out arm, and with great judgments." (Exodus 6:6, KJV) "And Moses spake so unto the children of Israel: but they hearkened not unto Moses for anguish of spirit, and for cruel bondage." (Exodus 6:9, KJV) Moses came back to God full of questions, and God did not begin with an explanation. He said: now you shall see, and He said: I am the LORD. This is how God answers our questioning: He first sets Himself before us. He has heard the groaning, He remembers His covenant, and seven times He says I will, from bringing His people out all the way to giving them an inheritance. If today discouragement has made you unable to listen, do not condemn yourself first. Remember the order: first comes I am the LORD, and only then does my I cannot step back. God understands the shortness of breath under harsh labor; He has not walked away, and His outstretched arm has not been withdrawn. Bring your why to Him just as it is, hear His name once more, and today His word will find its way back into your heart. Tonight before you sleep, there is no need to say much; just read Exodus 6:6-8 slowly once through, reading every I will aloud, all the way to I will give it you for an heritage, and then be quiet for a little while. You will find that your heart is not yet completely at ease, yet your breathing has first become steady, because before you, that God has already finished speaking.

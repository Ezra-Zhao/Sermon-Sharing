## Abstract (About 100 Words)

Moses is on the mount forty days and forty nights, and the people below cannot wait; they ask Aaron to make them gods to go before them. The golden calf is cast, an altar is built, and the people eat, drink, and play. On the mount, God speaks with Moses, and Moses intercedes on the ground of God's name and His covenant with the patriarchs. The tables are broken, the calf is ground to powder, the Levites take the sword, and Moses goes up again, willing that his own name be blotted out. This chapter asks every one of us: when you can no longer see God in your waiting, whom do you actually worship?

## (00:00-08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the last chapter we stopped at the highest point of Mount Sinai. God had finished giving the pattern of the tabernacle piece by piece, had called the craftsmen by name and filled them with the Spirit of God; He charged Israel again and again concerning the Sabbath, and at the end He put the two tables of testimony into Moses' hand. The word of God had been written on tables, and the foundation of Israel's covenant had been made ready. But please turn one page further in your Bible. The first sentence of chapter 32 is not a shout of joy; it is the sound of waiting disappointed: when the people saw that Moses delayed to come down out of the mount. On the mount is covenant-making; below the mount is covenant-breaking. On the mount are the tables written by God's own hand; below the mount is the calf cast by human hands; Moses has not yet come down, and the people below can no longer wait. The title of this chapter is taken from that proclamation over the golden calf: "These be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt." This sermon will treat that sentence as the mirror of the whole chapter: it is half right, and utterly wrong. It was indeed God who brought Israel up out of Egypt; but this calf, cast out of gold before their eyes, is absolutely not that God. To credit the exodus to a thing of one's own making — this is precisely the displacement of worship that this chapter exposes.

First, let us see the structure of the whole chapter clearly. Its thirty-five verses fall roughly into six acts. The first act is below the mount, verses 1-6: the people demand gods, Aaron casts the calf, builds an altar, and proclaims a feast to the LORD for the next day, and the people offer, eat, drink, and play. The second act is on the mount, verses 7-14: God calls them a stiff-necked people and proposes to consume them and make a great nation of Moses instead; Moses intercedes, and God repents, and does not bring upon the people the evil He had spoken. The third act is on the way down and before the camp, verses 15-20: Moses comes down with the tables written on both sides, enters the camp, sees the calf and the dancing, breaks the tables, and burns the calf to powder, strews it on water, and makes the people drink it. The fourth act is the reckoning, verses 21-24: Moses questions Aaron, and Aaron says that he threw the gold into the fire, and there came out this calf. The fifth act is the execution of judgment, verses 25-29: Moses stands in the gate of the camp and calls, the Levites gather to him, and at his command go through the camp with the sword, and about three thousand men fall. The sixth act is intercession renewed, verses 30-35: Moses goes up again, willing that his own name be blotted out of the book; God answers that whosoever has sinned against Him, him will He blot out, and closes the chapter with a plague. Read through all six acts, and we must see that this chapter does not settle the matter: the calf is destroyed, Moses is still on the mount, the people are still below it, and chapter 33 must go on to ask whether God Himself will go up with them. This is an unresolved chapter; this sermon, at its end, will offer only a structural pointer, and will not seize the content of the next chapter.

In preaching this chapter, this sermon first sets three rules. First, the level of fact and the level of appropriation are kept apart: what the text states directly, this sermon states directly, adding and subtracting nothing; observations drawn from the narrative are marked as structural observations or sermon appropriations, and are not passed off as the words of the text itself. Second, the severe passages are preached as they are, neither softened nor embellished: the sword of the Levites and the plague are the ethical difficulties of this chapter, and this sermon sets out the record, the narrative function, and the main scholarly treatments side by side, without handing the reader an easy conclusion. Third, the meaning of the original words rests only on the lexicons and commentaries: the key Hebrew words of this chapter — calf, molten image, play, repent, tables, and great sin — are given in the notes with the original text, transliteration, Strong's number, and the meaning according to BDB and HALOT, and the body of the sermon takes only what bears on its main line.

Let us read together a few key verses:

"And when the people saw that Moses delayed to come down out of the mount, the people gathered themselves together unto Aaron, and said unto him, Up, make us gods, which shall go before us." (Exodus 32:1, in part, KJV)

"And Aaron said unto them, Break off the golden earrings, which are in the ears of your wives, of your sons, and of your daughters, and bring them unto me. ... And he received them at their hand, and fashioned it with a graving tool, after he had made it a molten calf: and they said, These be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt." (Exodus 32:2, 4, in part, KJV)

"And the LORD said unto Moses, Go, get thee down; for thy people, which thou broughtest out of the land of Egypt, have corrupted themselves." (Exodus 32:7, KJV)

"And Moses besought the LORD his God, and said, ... Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, thy servants, ... And the LORD repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people." (Exodus 32:11, 13-14, in part, KJV)

"And it came to pass, as soon as he came nigh unto the camp, that he saw the calf, and the dancing: and Moses' anger waxed hot, and he cast the tables out of his hands, and brake them beneath the mount." (Exodus 32:19, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You brought us out of Egypt and made a covenant with us, yet in our waiting we so often make gods of our own first. Shine Your light on us through this chapter today, that we may recognize those self-made things on which we have fastened Your name, and learn to wait for You in delay, neither rushing to make a god nor hurrying to excuse ourselves. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## (08:00-24:00) Part One: Impatience Below the Mount — The Golden Calf, a Feast to the LORD, and Eating, Drinking, and Play (32:1-6)

The first key word of this chapter is delay. When the people saw that Moses delayed to come down out of the mount. Delay is not a comment added outside the text; it is the judgment the people formed with their own eyes. Align the timeline, and this sermon marks the point as a narrative observation: chapter 24 records that Moses went up and was in the cloud forty days and forty nights; for Moses on the mount, that was a time of communion with God and of receiving the tables; for the people below the mount, it was a time when their leader was nowhere to be seen, no news arrived, and the days were counted one by one. The same forty days and nights: on the mount, receiving; below the mount, wearing out. The text does not say that the people first denied God; the text says they could not wait for Moses. The leader's absence became their reason for making another guide: make us gods, which shall go before us. What they wanted, in the text's own terms, was specific: a guide who would go before them, visible and tangible. Fresh out of Egypt, the people had not yet learned to settle themselves under an unseen presence; they wanted to hand the work of guidance over to something they could see and touch. This sermon here states only the order in the text: it was not pagan doctrine that came first and a calf after; it was the anxiety of waiting that came first, and then the gathering to make a god. Hear also the action of gathering: the people gathered themselves together unto Aaron. Gathering is itself a verb of worship; Israel gathering before the tabernacle is worship. This time, gathering meant surrounding Aaron and pressing him. The same united action, with its direction reversed, becomes coercion. Everything Aaron does next happens under that pressure; the text offers no excuse for it there, and Moses' reckoning later names it directly. This sermon will deal with that in the fourth act; here we note only the order: the people gathered first, and Aaron spoke after.

Aaron's first sentence is not a refusal; it is compliance: break off the golden earrings, which are in the ears of your wives, of your sons, and of your daughters, and bring them unto me. Where the gold came from, the text does not develop here; it says only that the earrings were taken off in every household. Some interpreters connect the gold with the articles the people asked of the Egyptians in chapters 11 and 12; this sermon marks that as an intra-book connection and makes no final claim at this verse. What can be stated directly is this: the cost of the calf was borne by every family. All the people broke off the golden earrings and brought them to Aaron. No one is named as objecting, and no passage records anyone standing up to say no. Covenant-breaking in this chapter is collective from the very start: the idea came from the crowd, the gold came from the crowd, and the eating, drinking, and playing afterward belonged to the crowd as well. Verse 4 writes the manufacture in the briefest terms: he received them at their hand, and fashioned it, after he had made it a molten calf. Two words must be distinguished here, and the notes treat them in detail: calf, in the original 'egel (עֵגֶל, H5695) [1], denotes a young bull, a calf; the term for a molten image is connected in verses 20, 35 and elsewhere with masekhah (מַסֵּכָה, H4541) [2], an image made by casting. The calf was no unfamiliar figure in the ancient Near East; the bull was commonly associated with strength, fertility, and the mount or symbol of a deity, and the commentaries discuss this background at length. In citing that background this sermon takes only one point: the people did not choose an arbitrary shape; they chose the image of power that their world understood at a glance. To make God into the shape of power is to remake God after the trait human beings envy most. As soon as it was cast, the proclamation followed at once: they said, These be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt. Hear the grammar of the proclamation word by word: these — be thy gods. A single calf is matched with a plural form for the divine title; the Hebrew here uses a plural form of address, and versions through the ages have handled it variously; this sermon notes only this grammatical phenomenon and the proclamation itself, and forces no explanation here. Hear the content of the proclamation even more carefully: which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt. The history of salvation was not denied; the exodus was still loudly confessed; only, its credit was fastened onto the calf that had just been cast. This is the weight of this chapter's title: the most dangerous displacement is not the open denial of God's work, but crediting God's work to an account of one's own making. When Scripture later looks back on this scene, its verdict is consistent: Nehemiah 9:18 retells Israel's history and says that they made a molten calf and said, This is thy God that brought thee up out of Egypt, and had wrought great provocations; Psalm 106:19-23 says that they made a calf in Horeb and worshipped the molten image, and thus changed their glory into the similitude of an ox that eateth grass (Nehemiah 9:18; Psalm 106:19-23, cited here as Scripture's internal retrospect). Both retrospects put the weight in the same place: once the image was made, the glory had been exchanged. The body of this sermon keeps to the original setting of chapter 32; the two retrospects serve here only as comparison, without importing their details backward.

Seeing the calf proclaimed, Aaron did two things: he built an altar, and he appointed a feast. He built an altar before the calf, and proclaimed, To morrow is a feast to the LORD. This sentence is the most glaring in the whole passage, and it must be made plain at the level of fact: the name Aaron called out was the name of the LORD; what the people offered the next day were burnt offerings and peace offerings; the name used, the feast kept, and the sacrifices offered were all the language of covenant worship. But the altar was built before the calf, the image stood in the middle, and the object of worship had already been exchanged. On this basis this sermon states directly: this was a mixed worship — the lips called the name of the LORD, the knees bowed to a self-made image; the shell was a covenant feast, and the center was a god made by human hands. Sin, by this step, had learned to keep for itself the standing of devotion. The people did not say, We renounce the LORD from now on; they said, To morrow is a feast to the LORD. Much displacement of worship in life likewise does not appear in the guise of betrayal; it appears in the guise of keeping the name while replacing the center: the name is still called by the same name, the feast is still kept, the offering is still offered, only the thing standing before the altar is no longer God Himself.

The reality of the feast, in verse 6, is written in only a few lines, and every line is heavy: and they rose up early on the morrow, and offered burnt offerings, and brought peace offerings; and the people sat down to eat and to drink, and rose up to play. Offering first, eating and drinking in the middle, play last — the order itself is the curve of the fall. Peace offerings included a meal; eating and drinking in themselves are not sin. The problem is that this meal was set before the calf, and this gladness grew inside covenant-breaking. The word play, in the original letzacheq (לְצַחֵק, the infinitive form of H6711) [3], has a root connected with laughter and sport, and is of the same root as the name Isaac — Isaac's name in Genesis comes precisely from laughter — and commentators have often noted this shared root; this sermon marks it as a lexical observation: the same root that in Genesis is the laughter by which the child of promise was named has here become the revelry at a covenant-breaking feast. The lexical observation stops there, and does not let a root replace the context; the context itself is already clear enough: the people had just heard with their own ears in chapter 20 the commandment against making graven images, and had just said together in chapter 24, All that the LORD hath said will we do — and now they sat down and rose up before the calf, eating, drinking, and playing. Offering, feast, and meal were all gifts God gave to covenant life, and now all of them had been moved over to serve a calf. Moses on the mount was receiving detailed instruction on how to worship God; the people below the mount were holding a worship remodeled after their own will. The instruction had not yet come down the mountain, and the remodeling had already taken its seat at table — what chills the heart most in this chapter is that, when the feast broke up, the people still thought they had just worshipped the LORD.

## (24:00-42:00) Part Two: The Conversation on the Mount and the Breaking on the Way Down — Three Grounds of Intercession, God's Repentance, and the Calf Ground to Powder (32:7-20)

The camera cuts back to the mount. God speaks to Moses, and His first sentence is a command, His second a matter of belonging: Go, get thee down; for thy people, which thou broughtest out of the land of Egypt, have corrupted themselves. Thy people; thou broughtest them out — listen carefully to those two second-person claims. In the making of the covenant, God had called Israel His people, His own possession, again and again; now that the people have cast a calf, God in His speech hands the people back to Moses. Moses' answer deserves to be heard word by word just as much. Moses does not adjust his wording to God's; he beseeches Him: LORD, why doth thy wrath wax hot against thy people, which thou hast brought forth out of the land of Egypt with great power, and with a mighty hand? God says, this people; Moses says, thy people. God says, thou broughtest them out; Moses says, thou hast brought them forth. This sermon marks this exchange as a structural observation: the skeleton of the whole intercession is hidden in this tug-of-war over the terms of belonging. Moses does not deny that the people have corrupted themselves; he is not disputing the fact; he is disputing the belonging: fallen as they are, on God's account do they still count as Thy people? Intercession begins with the terms of address. This is not a word-game; it is the essence of the mediator's work: the one who stands between God and the people holds in one hand what God has said before, and in the other the people's present sin, and lets neither fact be erased. In verses 8-10 God states the grounds of judgment and His proposal to the end: they have turned aside quickly out of the way which I commanded them; they have made them a molten calf, and have worshipped it, and have sacrificed thereunto; and God says, I have seen this people, and, behold, it is a stiff-necked people: now therefore let me alone, that my wrath may wax hot against them, and that I may consume them: and I will make of thee a great nation. Let me alone; a great nation instead — for Moses personally, this proposal was promotion; for the people, it was the end. Had Moses kept silent, he would have been the father of a great nation. Moses did not keep silent; he besought the LORD his God, and the word besought in verse 11 is the hinge of the whole passage.

Moses' intercession, arranged in the order of the text, rests on three grounds, and this sermon marks the arrangement as a structural ordering drawn from the text itself. The first ground is what the Egyptians will say. Moses says the Egyptians will say, For mischief did he bring them out, to slay them in the mountains, and to consume them from the face of the earth. Moses does not mean that God needs to save face; his meaning is plain only against the narrative of the exodus: from the beginning the exodus was a work God did for His own name, watched all along by Pharaoh and the Egyptians, and if the people were destroyed in the wilderness just after leaving Egypt, the Egyptians' telling of this God would end in malice. The second ground is the coherence of God's name with God's work; Moses continues, Turn from thy fierce wrath, and repent of this evil against thy people. This ground is joined to the first, and this sermon reads them together as concerning God's witness before the nations: as God deals with His own people, so the nations will know Him. The third ground is remembrance of the covenant with the patriarchs. Moses says, Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, thy servants, to whom thou swarest by thine own self, and saidst unto them, I will multiply your seed as the stars of heaven, and all this land that I have spoken of will I give unto your seed, and they shall inherit it for ever. This ground carries the greatest weight, because it moves the basis of intercession from the people's present performance to God's past promise: the people are unworthy, and Moses does not say they are worthy; he says, Thou swarest. Of the three grounds, not one is that the people are innocent, not one is that the punishment is too heavy; all three are about God Himself: Thy name, Thy work, Thy covenant. This is the difference between intercession and pleading for leniency: leniency looks for human pitiableness; intercession looks for God's faithfulness. Verse 14 closes the scene in a single sentence: And the LORD repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people.

The word repent must be faced squarely. Repent, in the original nicham (נִחַם, the corresponding form of H5162) [4], means to repent, to turn in purpose, and can also mean to be comforted; here the text states directly that God turned, and did not bring the evil He had spoken. A common scholarly treatment reads such language as anthropopatheia — figurative, human-affect language describing God's action, so that readers can grasp God's turning, without equating it with human regret born of ignorance. Another interpretive tradition stresses that the word should be read within this chapter's narrative relationship: that God turns in response to intercession shows precisely that intercession is a real element in God's governance, not a pageant performed for onlookers. At the same time the tension must be marked honestly, without a final verdict: 1 Samuel 15:29 declares plainly that the Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent, for He is not a man, that He should repent; yet Samuel, in that same chapter, also records that God repented that He had made Saul king. Set side by side, interpreters through the ages have handled the two differently: some distinguish by context — in one place God turns from bringing the evil He had spoken, in another His purpose and faithfulness do not change; others gather both under anthropopatheia, holding that the word repent carries different descriptive functions in different contexts. This sermon sets the treatments side by side and renders no final verdict in this chapter; it holds only to the two points this chapter can state directly. First, verse 14 does use nicham to describe God's turning, and neither translator nor preacher should preach the word away. Second, this turning happens after Moses intercedes by appealing to God's name and His covenant with the patriarchs; the narrative order joins intercession and turning together, and that order itself is a lesson the text wants its readers to see.

The conversation on the mount ends, and Moses turns and goes down. Verses 15-16 deliberately slow the camera on what is in his hands: And Moses turned, and went down from the mount, and the two tables of the testimony were in his hand: the tables were written on both their sides; on the one side and on the other were they written. And the tables were the work of God, and the writing was the writing of God, graven upon the tables. Tables, in the original luchot (לוּחֹת, H3871) [5], denotes flat tablets, of stone or wood; here it refers specifically to the tables of testimony. Written on both sides; the work of God; the writing of God — the text sets down three clauses in a row so that the reader sees clearly, before the breaking, exactly what is about to be broken: not Moses' notebook, but God's own work. On the way down there is first a sound. In verses 17-18 Joshua hears the noise of the people as they shouted, and says to Moses, There is a noise of war in the camp; Moses answers, It is not the voice of them that shout for mastery, neither is it the voice of them that cry for being overcome: but the noise of them that sing do I hear. Joshua could hear that something was happening, but not what kind of thing it was; Moses knew at once that it was the sound of worship — of worship aimed at the wrong object. This sermon marks this as a narrative observation: when sin reaches its public stage, it is often first heard as a sound and first noticed as excitement; to discern what that excitement actually is takes familiarity with the worship of God. Moses knew the sounds of worship, and so he could hear that the singing in the camp was not singing offered to God. In verse 19 Moses drew near the camp and saw: he saw the calf, and the dancing, and Moses' anger waxed hot, and he cast the tables out of his hands, and brake them beneath the mount.

The breaking of the tables must be preached with its levels marked. At the level of fact: Moses broke the two tables beneath the mount, and this is the direct statement of the text. At the level of sermon appropriation: this sermon reads the breaking as a symbolic act signifying that the covenant had already been broken — the tables are the text of the covenant, the people had already torn up the covenant in their conduct, and Moses' breaking made the already-ruptured covenant relationship into a visible proclamation; this reading is common in the interpretive tradition, and this sermon adopts it and marks it as a sermon appropriation, not as something the text states in so many words. The place of the breaking also matters: beneath the mount, before the camp, in the people's sight; Moses let the rupture be public. Verse 20 follows at once with Moses' disposal of the calf, lacking no step: he took the calf which they had made, and burnt it in the fire, and ground it to powder, and strawed it upon the water, and made the children of Israel drink of it. Burn, grind, strew, drink — in four steps the calf was not stored away, not buried, but utterly unmade into powder, and those who had made it and worshipped it were made to drink it with their own mouths. This sermon here cites one structural parallel and marks it as such: Numbers 5 records the bitter-water rite for a wife suspected of unfaithfulness, in which the priest washes the written curses into water and makes the woman drink it, to make plain guilt or innocence; the water of powder in this chapter and the bitter water share a structural similarity — in both, matter bound up with sin is dissolved in water and given to drink — but their subjects, occasions, and functions differ, and this sermon marks the comparison as a structural observation only, without equating the two, and without claiming that Moses here performed the rite of Numbers — in narrative time that rite had not yet been given, so the parallel is a structural resemblance seen in retrospect, not a law executed at the time. With the disposal done, the calf is gone, the tables are broken, and the singing has stopped: the god human beings made has become powder, and the tables God wrote have become fragments. What Moses does next is reckoning and calling; the accounts must be settled one by one.

## (42:00-56:00) Part Three: Self-Defense, the Sword, and Intercession Renewed — Aaron's Fire, the Levites' Sword, and the Name in the Book (32:21-35)

The reckoning begins with Aaron. Moses says to Aaron, What did this people unto thee, that thou hast brought so great a sin upon them? Great sin, in the original chata'ah gedolah (חַטָּאָה גְדֹלָה, H2403 plus H1419) [6], literally a great sin, a grave transgression. Moses' way of asking sets the order of responsibility plainly: he does not first ask what the people did; he asks Aaron, thou hast brought them into it. The people are guilty, and Moses does not excuse the people; but the leader's responsibility stands in Moses' account as a separate entry. Aaron's answer falls into two parts, and at the level of fact this sermon states only what the text gives, without psychological assertions. In the first part Aaron says, Let not the anger of my lord wax hot: thou knowest the people, that they are set on mischief. For they said unto me, Make us gods, which shall go before us: for as for this Moses, the man that brought us up out of the land of Egypt, we wot not what is become of him. What Aaron reports the people as saying agrees in substance with the record in verse 1; only one matter of order is worth noting: he first shifts responsibility onto the people's nature, and only then repeats the people's demand. As the text presents it, so this sermon relays it, neither supplying Aaron's motives nor pronouncing a diagnosis on him. The second part is the famous sentence: And I said unto them, Whosoever hath any gold, let them break it off. So they gave it me: then I cast it into the fire, and there came out this calf. Listen carefully to the grammar of this sentence: the subjects are the gold and the calf, the verbs are throwing and coming out, and the craftsman has disappeared. Fashioned with a graving tool, made a molten calf — that is the narrator's record in verse 4; there came out this calf — that is Aaron's own record. The same event, two tellings: one has a craftsman, a mould, and a process; the other has only a fire and a calf that walked out by itself. This sermon marks the contrast as the literary presentation of an evasive narrative: the text offers no comment and no explanation; it simply lays Aaron's words before the reader as they are, and lets the words show what they are. Preaching here does not cross the line into psychological analysis of an ancient figure; it holds only to the contrast the text supplies: how verse 4 writes it, and how verse 24 says it — set side by side, the hearer will understand. Note also where Moses' questioning lands: he does not first ask where the gold came from; he asks about the great sin. The technical question of the casting is a small account; bringing the people into a great sin is the large one. Aaron's entire answer talks about process and circumstance, and not one word touches the great sin. The place where question and answer miss each other is the whole tension of this scene.

Verse 25 turns to the execution of judgment. When Moses saw that the people were naked — for Aaron had made them naked unto their shame — he stood in the gate of the camp and said, Who is on the LORD'S side? let him come unto me. And all the sons of Levi gathered themselves together unto him. The gate of the camp was its entrance and exit, and also the place of public gathering and of judgment; standing in the gate to make the call, Moses set the choice before everyone: at this point, no one could any longer leave ambiguous the side on which he stood. Having gathered, the Levites received a command severe enough to take the breath away: Thus saith the LORD God of Israel, Put every man his sword by his side, and go in and out from gate to gate throughout the camp, and slay every man his brother, and every man his companion, and every man his neighbour. Verse 28 records the result of its execution: And the children of Levi did according to the word of Moses: and there fell of the people that day about three thousand men. This sermon states this as it is, neither softening nor embellishing: those slain were their own brothers, companions, and neighbours, about three thousand men; that is the number and the relationships the text gives, and this sermon neither shrinks the number nor evades the relationships. The ethical difficulties of this passage must be set out squarely and side by side. The first is the difficulty of collective judgment: why a collective act of covenant-breaking was dealt with by the sword within the camp is a heavy question for readers of every age, and interpreters have read it either from the setting of the covenant community's survival — holding that the calf incident was a fundamental overthrow of the covenant just established, and that without such dealing the community would have turned wholesale to idol worship on the very first stage out of Egypt — or from its narrative function, holding that the passage means to show the real cost of covenant-breaking, that sin is not something a single apology can turn the page on. The second is the difficulty of a priesthood established by the sword: verse 29 says that Moses said, Consecrate yourselves to day to the LORD, even every man upon his son, and upon his brother; that he may bestow upon you a blessing this day — the Levites afterward bore the service of the tabernacle, and their separation is developed later in this book and beyond, and this narrative records their gathering and obedience as the starting point of their consecration to the LORD; yet scholarship has also voiced reserve about joining bloodshed and consecration, some stressing that this was a one-time command for a particular moment of judgment and cannot be generalized into a standing principle, others emphasizing the loyalty to the covenant that the tribe of Levi showed here. This sermon sets these treatments side by side, and marks the narrative function of verse 29 — consecrated to the LORD this day — as this chapter's own closure, without reading the passage as a pattern of action that any age may copy: the gate of the camp, the covenant, Moses present, and God's declared judgment are four conditions peculiar to this chapter, and none may be transferred by analogy without them all. In preaching this passage, the pulpit's duty is to let the hearer grow heavy together with the text, not to find the text a facile way out.

In verse 30 Moses says to the people, Ye have sinned a great sin: and now I will go up unto the LORD; peradventure I shall make an atonement for your sin. Note Moses' wording: to the people he says, ye have sinned a great sin; toward God he goes up to make atonement. Condemnation and intercession are two faces of the same ascent in Moses. Verses 31-32 are the emotional summit of the whole chapter. Moses returned unto the LORD, and said, Oh, this people have sinned a great sin, and have made them gods of gold. He confesses first, and confesses cleanly: no mention that Aaron misled the people, no mention of his own absence, only that this people have sinned a great sin. Only after confessing does he make his request: Yet now, if thou wilt forgive their sin—; and if not, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book which thou hast written. If not, blot me out — this is the last sentence of Moses' intercession, and it is all he has to stake: if the people's sin will not be forgiven, he would rather his own name be blotted out of God's book. The book is the book in which God has written names; to be blotted out is to be removed from it; Moses stakes his own place in the covenant so that the people may remain in the book. This sermon here treats only this chapter's text, without unfolding the whole biblical theology of the book in advance; it marks one level only: these are Moses' own words of intercession, and the text records his heart, but does not say that God acted on his proposal. God's answer, in verses 33-35, comes in three layers. The first layer is sovereignty over the blotting of names: And the LORD said unto Moses, Whosoever hath sinned against me, him will I blot out of my book. Moses was willing to substitute; God does not permit substitution; the adding and removing of names rest in God's hand alone. The second layer is that the road still goes on: Therefore now go, lead the people unto the place of which I have spoken unto thee: behold, mine Angel shall go before thee. The people are not consumed, the journey is not cancelled, and Moses' office is not revoked — after judgment, God still hands this people back into Moses' hand to lead onward. The third layer is that the account is not cancelled: Nevertheless in the day when I visit I will visit their sin upon them. The day of visitation is judgment deferred; part of this chapter's sin was dealt with, and part of it was written down. Verse 35 closes with a plague: And the LORD plagued the people, because they made the calf, which Aaron made. The text gives no number for the plague; the chapter thus ends in an unresolved state: the calf destroyed, three thousand fallen, the plague come, Moses still in the place of intercession, the people still to go forward, and whether God Himself will go up with them is a question chapter 33 must still contend for. This sermon stops here, offering only a structural pointer: the door of chapter 32 does not close; it opens onto Moses' prayer in chapter 33, If thy presence go not with me, carry us not up hence. For today, let us first see this chapter's account clearly: the sin is great, the judgment is real, the intercession is deep, and the outcome still hangs upon God's answer.

## (58:00-60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus 32. Remember its opening: when the people saw that Moses delayed to come down out of the mount. The doorway of sin is often not open defiance, but impatience in waiting. Remember that proclamation: These be thy gods — the lips called the name of the LORD, the knees bowed to a self-made image; mixed worship is this chapter's most glaring mirror. Remember also the terms of address on the mount: God said this people, Moses said thy people, and intercession begins with refusing to change the wording; of Moses' three grounds, not one speaks of the people's pitiableness, and all three speak of God's name, God's work, and God's covenant. Remember the two broken things below the mount: the tables God wrote were broken, and the calf human beings cast was ground to powder — the covenant was broken, the image destroyed, and the cost of sin was not discounted by a fraction. Remember at last the name in the book: Moses was willing that his own name be blotted out, and God said, Whosoever hath sinned against me, him will I blot out. Deep as intercession is, it cannot replace each person's own account before God; severe as judgment is, God still commands Moses to lead this people forward. May we learn today to wait in delay, to make no god and offer no self-defense, to take our stand at the gate of the camp, and to belong to the LORD alone.

Let us pray together: Lord, we confess that we too, in our waiting, have made calves of our own, crediting Your works to the account of self-made things, and keeping for ourselves the standing of devotion. Forgive us our great sin, and shatter every self-made image in our hearts; teach us still to wait for You in what seems Your delay, and still to stand on Your side when the crowd gathers and presses. Thank You that in judgment You have still left us a place of intercession, and cause each of us to examine his own name and his own sin, and to turn to You while it is today. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[1] 'egel: Hebrew עֵגֶל (H5695), a young bull, a calf, a calf-like animal. In Exodus 32:4, 8, 19, 20, 35 and elsewhere it denotes the calf that Aaron cast and the people worshipped; Psalm 106:19, looking back on this event, also uses this word. In the ancient Near East the bull symbolized strength and fertility, a background discussed at length in the commentaries; the body of the sermon takes only its association with the image of power, without developing comparative religious history. Meaning according to BDB and HALOT.

[2] masekhah: Hebrew מַסֵּכָה (H4541), an image made by pouring or casting, a molten image, from the root for pouring and casting. Exodus 32:4 narrates the casting of the calf with the verb of casting and the mould, 32:8 has God charge the people with making themselves a molten calf, and Leviticus 19:4, Deuteronomy 27:15 and other passages likewise use this word for cast idols. The distinction drawn in the sermon body between the calf's shape ('egel) and the nature of a molten image (masekhah) rests on this word. Meaning according to BDB and HALOT.

[3] letzacheq: Hebrew לְצַחֵק (H6711, the Piel infinitive of the verb tzachaq), to laugh, to make sport, to play, to jest. Exodus 32:6, the people rose up to play, uses this word; the same root appears in the name Isaac (יִצְחָק, Yitzchaq), and Genesis 21:6 and other passages record Sarah laughing at the birth of her son, a shared root often noted in the commentaries. The sermon body marks this as a lexical observation: a shared root does not mean an identical sense, and the specific meaning is fixed by context; the play in this verse stands after offering, eating, and drinking, within the setting of covenant-breaking. Meaning according to BDB and HALOT.

[4] nicham: Hebrew נִחַם (H5162, the Niphal form of the verb nacham), to repent, to turn in purpose, to alter the dealing that had been announced, and also to be comforted and to have compassion. In Exodus 32:12 Moses asks God to turn and repent, and 32:14 records that God repented and did not bring the evil He had spoken. Scholarship most often treats this as anthropopatheia, figurative human-affect language describing God's action; its tension with the "will not repent" of 1 Samuel 15:29 is set out side by side in the sermon body, with the various treatments presented and no final verdict rendered. Meaning according to BDB and HALOT.

[5] luchot: Hebrew לוּחֹת (singular luach, H3871), a flat tablet or board, of wood or of stone. In Exodus 32:15-16, 19 it denotes the two tables of testimony that Moses brought down from the mount and broke, which the text is careful to record as written on both sides, the work of God, and the writing of God. Exodus 24:12, 31:18, 34:1 and other passages also use this word for the tables. Meaning according to BDB and HALOT.

[6] chata'ah gedolah: Hebrew חַטָּאָה גְדֹלָה (H2403 plus H1419), a great sin, a grave transgression. Chata'ah denotes sin, an offence, and also a sin offering; gedolah is the feminine form of the adjective great, grave. In Exodus 32:21 Moses charges Aaron with bringing a great sin upon the people, and in 32:30-31 Moses uses the words great sin again to the people and to God; its three occurrences form the chapter's own defining term for the calf incident. Meaning according to BDB and HALOT.

[7] Scripture's internal retrospect on the calf incident: Nehemiah 9:18 looks back on the event with the making of a calf and great provocations against God, set within the prayer of confession over Israel's history of covenant-breaking; Psalm 106:19-23 records the making of a calf in Horeb and the changing of their glory into the similitude of an ox that eateth grass, and records Moses standing in the breach to turn away God's wrath from destroying them. The sermon body cites both as comparisons; the standing in the breach in the Psalm answers to Moses' intercession in this chapter, but this sermon does not use the Psalm's exposition to rewrite the narrative order of this chapter.

[8] The setting of the call and the sword (32:25-29): the gate of the camp is the public and judicial space of an ancient camp or city, and Moses' standing in the gate to make his call is understood in the sermon body accordingly, as a public choice of sides. The tribe of Levi gathered and obeyed here, and verse 29 calls this their consecration to the LORD that day; the Levites' later bearing of the tabernacle office is found in Numbers 3-4 and 8, and the blessing of Levi in Deuteronomy 33:8-11 also looks back on loyalty that did not acknowledge kin. Scholarly treatments of this passage's ethical difficulties are set out side by side in the sermon body: the reading from the survival of the covenant community, the reading from narrative function, and the reserved judgment that it may not be generalized into a standing principle are all presented, with no single conclusion imposed.

[9] The book and the blotting out of names (32:32-33): the book in "blot me out of thy book" denotes the book God has written, the image of the register of the names of the living; Psalm 69:28, Daniel 12:1 and other passages use a similar image, and Revelation 3:5 takes it up again. The sermon body treats only this chapter: Moses was willing to give his own name in the people's place, and God answered that sovereignty over the blotting out rests with Him and follows each person's own sin; this chapter does not unfold a complete doctrine of the book, and this note only marks where the parallels are found.

[10] The water of powder and the bitter-water rite (32:20 and Numbers 5:11-31): both have the act of dissolving matter bound up with sin in water and giving it to drink, and are structurally similar; they differ in that the rite in Numbers concerns an individual's suspected case, is conducted by the priest, and takes place at the tabernacle, whereas this chapter is Moses' disposal of an image already made and a manifestation before all. This sermon marks the comparison as a structural observation, without equating the two or interpreting one by the other; interpreters are divided on whether to adopt the comparison, and this sermon adopts it while keeping the boundary.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And when the people saw that Moses delayed to come down out of the mount, the people gathered themselves together unto Aaron." (Exodus 32:1, KJV) "Yet now, if thou wilt forgive their sin—; and if not, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book which thou hast written." (Exodus 32:32, KJV)

Meditate today on two things. The first is waiting. The people's sin did not begin with hatred of God; it began with impatience. The leader was out of sight, no news came, the days dragged, and there was gold at hand — so they made something visible for themselves. Our own lives have such moments at the foot of the mountain, when prayers seem unanswered and nothing changes, and we hurry to fashion a reassurance of our own. Ask the Lord to teach you quietness in delay, and not to rush to make a god. The second is intercession. Moses confessed the sin without excuse, asked for pardon without conditions, and at last staked his own name. When you pray for family, for children, for the church, learn this heart that will not walk away: to stand in the breach not because you are sinless, but because you will not leave those you love to face judgment alone. Today, take one person you cannot put down in your heart, set that name before the Lord, and intercede quietly, in no hurry to walk away. Not making a god in waiting, and not walking away in intercession — these two lessons are enough for a lifetime, and may the Lord, by His grace, keep our names secure in His book.

## Summary (about 100 words)

Israel set out for Beersheba, offered sacrifices first, and traveled afterward. In a vision of the night God called him: fear not to go down into Egypt, for I will go down with thee, and I will also surely bring thee up again. Then the Bible names, one by one, the seventy persons who went down to Egypt, and records Joseph embracing his father and weeping in Goshen, and preparing the whole family to meet Pharaoh as shepherds. Before a great migration there is first an altar; God's "going with thee" covers the downhill road of life, and of the names He counts, not one is missing.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

At the end of the previous chapter, Jacob saw the wagons Joseph had sent, and his spirit revived. An old man who for twenty-two years had believed his son was dead and would not be comforted suddenly heard that his son was still alive, had become ruler in Egypt, and wanted to bring the whole family to enjoy good things. By common sense he should have boarded the wagon at once, without a day's delay. Yet at the very opening of this chapter, the Bible records that he stopped first — stopped at Beersheba, built an altar, and offered sacrifices. The wagons were waiting at the door, the future was spread out in Egypt, but he first asked God: should I make this journey or not? Am I going with You, or are You going with me?

This was the greatest migration of the life of a man one hundred and thirty years old. To leave the promised land and move to foreign Egypt — and not as a guest, but with the whole household of seventy persons uprooted. Was this step blessing or calamity? In a famine his father Isaac had once wanted to go down to Egypt, and God Himself stopped him, saying, go not down into Egypt. His grandfather Abraham had gone down to Egypt and stumbled there. Now it was his turn to stand at the same crossroads. Brothers and sisters, before a great migration in life, the most important thing is not packing luggage; it is first making this clear: is God going with me?

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several key passages:

"And Israel took his journey with all that he had, and came to Beersheba, and offered sacrifices unto the God of his father Isaac. And God spake unto Israel in the visions of the night, and said, Jacob, Jacob. And he said, Here am I." (Genesis 46:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"I am God, the God of thy father: fear not to go down into Egypt; for I will there make of thee a great nation: I will go down with thee into Egypt; and I will also surely bring thee up again: and Joseph shall put his hand upon thine eyes." (Genesis 46:3-4, excerpt, KJV)

"And Joseph made ready his chariot, and went up to meet Israel his father, to Goshen, and presented himself unto him; and he fell on his neck, and wept on his neck a good while. And Israel said unto Joseph, Now let me die, since I have seen thy face, because thou art yet alive." (Genesis 46:29-30, excerpt, KJV)

"That ye shall say, Thy servants' trade hath been about cattle from our youth even until now, both we, and also our fathers: that ye may dwell in the land of Goshen; for every shepherd is an abomination unto the Egyptians." (Genesis 46:34, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, many among us are standing at a crossroads of migration: changing work, moving cities, sending children far away, growing old and frail ourselves, about to walk a completely unfamiliar downhill road. Speak to us tonight through Jacob's Beersheba, and let us hear clearly that promise: fear not, I will surely go down with thee. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Altar at Beersheba and the Call in the Night (46:1-4)

First look at the setting out. "And Israel took his journey with all that he had, and came to Beersheba." Please note that the text here does not use "Jacob" but "Israel." This is the name God gave him, the covenant name. The narrator seems to remind us: this journey is not simply a family fleeing famine to seek refuge with a relative; it is the departure of a nation, the preface to the great book of Exodus. The seventy persons who go down today will come out, more than four hundred years later, as a congregation beyond counting. So every step of this journey carries the weight of the covenant.

Where is Beersheba? At the southernmost edge of the promised land, the last stop on leaving Canaan to go down to Egypt. The place itself is a monument: Abraham planted a tamarisk tree there, made a covenant with Abimelech, and called on the name of the LORD, the everlasting God; Isaac built an altar there, and God appeared to him in the night and said, fear not, for I am with thee. Jacob himself, fleeing to Haran in his youth, had passed nearby. Now he was old and about to leave the border, and he chose to stop at Beersheba — not by chance, but deliberately returning to the place where his ancestors had met God. When a person makes a major decision, he needs to return to the place where God once spoke to him, and realign himself. The farther a person travels, the more he must remember the altar at the starting point.

The first thing he did there was to offer sacrifices. "And offered sacrifices unto the God of his father Isaac." "Sacrifices," in the original zevach (זֶבַח, H2077), a sacrifice offered by slaughtering an animal [1]. This was not a casual prayer before leaving; it was a solemn slaughtering, a shedding of blood, a laying on the altar. Why was a sacrifice indispensable? Because there was a thorn in Jacob's heart: Isaac had been stopped by God at this very place and forbidden to go down to Egypt. Now the son was about to walk the road his father had been stopped from walking, and he had to confirm: had God changed His mind because times had changed, or was he, greedy for Egypt's grain and his son's glory, acting on his own authority? To offer a sacrifice was to spread the decision out before God and ask God to declare Himself. Before a great migration, first an altar — this order is still our lesson today. We often sign the contract first, buy the ticket first, promise people first, and only at the end ask God to bless and to endorse our decision. Jacob's order was exactly the opposite: the wagons were at the door, but he knelt first at the altar; if God did not speak, he dared not take one step beyond the border. Brothers and sisters, the mark of obedience is not walking fast; it is being willing to stop and wait for God to speak.

God did speak, and in the night. "And God spake unto Israel in the visions of the night." "Visions of the night," in the original mar'ot hallaylah (מַרְאֹת הַלַּיְלָה, H4759 + H3915), visions of the night, scenes seen in the night [2]. Several great matters of Jacob's life God spoke of in the night: the dream at Bethel was in the night, the dream at Laban's house was in the night, and now the vision at Beersheba was also in the night. This is no coincidence. At night, a person has stopped his hands and quieted his heart, and only then can God's voice be heard; at night is also when an old man about to leave his homeland is most afraid and least able to sleep. God draws near precisely in the hour of a person's greatest weakness. He did not wait for daylight, nor wait for Jacob's emotions to settle; He came in the night, like a considerate father who sits by his son's bed and speaks first.

How did God begin? "Jacob, Jacob!" He called the name twice. There is meaning in this name: when God called him, He did not use his great covenant name "Israel," but his own name "Jacob." The moments in the Bible when a name is called twice are never ordinary: God called "Abraham, Abraham" on Mount Moriah at the moment the knife was raised; God called "Moses, Moses" in the burning bush; the Lord called Martha, and called Simon, also twice. Calling twice is urgency, intimacy, and solemnity: what follows, you must hear word by word. And Jacob's answer is the most beautiful answer of the people of Israel: "Here am I." His grandfather had said this on Mount Moriah, and his descendant the prophet Isaiah would say it later. A man one hundred and thirty years old, awakened by God in the middle of the night, did not complain and did not panic, but said, Here am I. Brothers and sisters, this is the instinct trained by a lifetime of following God: however old one is, however dark the road ahead, when God calls, first answer "Here am I," and then listen to the command.

Then God's words grew heavier sentence by sentence. First He named Himself: "I am God, the God of thy father." By introducing Himself as the God of Isaac, God wanted Jacob to remember: the God who stopped your father and the God who lets you go today are the same God; He had His reasons for stopping, and His season for letting go, and His sovereignty had not changed — you need not suspect you have knocked on the wrong door. Then came the heart of the whole chapter: "Fear not to go down into Egypt." Please think: why did God first say, fear not? Because Jacob was afraid. He feared leaving the promised land, feared dying in a foreign country, feared the whole family being assimilated and swallowed up in Egypt, feared that by this going, the inheritance of his ancestors and the covenant of God would be broken in his own hands. God did not rebuke his fear; God dealt with his fear first. True comfort is never "there is nothing to fear"; it is first admitting that you truly are afraid, and then taking the reasons for fear apart one by one. God next did exactly that, one by one: afraid the descendants would be cut off? "I will there make of thee a great nation" — not in Canaan, but in Egypt, the greatness of a nation would be accomplished. Afraid of dying a stranger in a foreign land? "I will also surely bring thee up again" — your ending is not in Egypt; God Himself takes responsibility for bringing you back. Afraid that once gone you cannot return, lonely and without support? "I will go down with thee into Egypt" — note, God does not stand at the border of Canaan waving farewell; God Himself accompanies him down. Afraid of dying with eyes unclosed? "Joseph shall put his hand upon thine eyes" — literally, Joseph's hand shall be placed on your eyes: the son you loved most and believed long dead will with his own hand close your eyes. Four fears, four promises, each fitted to its symptom. This God is not a God who hands over a road map and walks away; He is a God who personally dismantles every fear, one by one.

Here we must chew on that key word: go down. In the original yarad (יָרַד, H3381), to go down, descend [3]. In Genesis 46:3-4, this word "down" and the word "up" form a vivid contrast: fear not to go down into Egypt; I will go down with thee into Egypt; and I will also surely bring thee up again. "Down" is geographically from the highlands toward the lowlands of the Nile; it is also, in circumstance, from the promised land toward a land of sojourning; and it is even more the downhill road in an old man's heart: leaving home, aging, sickness, death, each step lower than the last. Everyone loves "up": promotion, rising, flourishing; no one loves "down." But the God of the Bible promises precisely this: on the downhill road, I will walk with you. He is not only the God of the mountaintop but also the God of the valley; not only the God of your young, strong, and prosperous days, but also the God of your old, dim-eyed days when you must be supported as you walk. The most precious part of God's "going with thee" is right here: He does not guarantee that you will not walk a downhill road; He guarantees that on the downhill road you will not be alone. After this night, Jacob's heart was settled. The sacrifice on the altar was finished, the words in the night were heard, and the next day he calmly boarded the wagons Pharaoh had sent. Brothers and sisters, if your great migration does not yet have an altar and a vision beneath it, please do not hurry to board; if you have already heard clearly God say, "I will surely go down with thee," then go down without hesitation, because at the end of the descent is the "bring thee up again" for which God takes responsibility.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Seventy Names — God Counts Every Person (46:5-27)

Only after the matter of the altar was finished did the narrative truly get on the road. "And Jacob rose up from Beersheba: and the sons of Israel carried Jacob their father, and their little ones, and their wives, in the wagons which Pharaoh had sent to carry him." The journey passes without comment, but at verse 8 the Bible suddenly slows down, slowing as if to a roll call, naming those who went down to Egypt one by one: all the souls of Israel came into Egypt, Jacob and his sons, and the names of those who came into Egypt are recorded below. Many people, reaching a genealogical list, want to skip it as dry. Brothers and sisters, it is exactly this passage that most reveals God's heart. A book of salvation, at the pass of a nation's great migration, is willing to spend a full twenty verses only to write down every name. This is not an administrative record; this is theology: the list is a mark of sovereignty — this company is not a band of refugees who wandered into Egypt on their own, but a covenant people whom God personally counted and personally led down. God counts every name, and not one is missing.

Let us look slowly at the structure of this list. First, those born to Leah: the firstborn Reuben and his sons, Hanoch, Phallu, Hezron, and Carmi; the sons of Simeon; the sons of Levi; the sons of Judah — here is a painful detail: Judah's sons Er and Onan had already died in the land of Canaan, and the list records them all the same; the dead are not removed from the register. Then come Perez, "the sons of Pharez were Hezron and Hamul," passed over in a single stroke, utterly inconspicuous, yet the Bible will not omit even the smallest grandsons of this branch. Brothers and sisters, please pause on this name: Perez was born to Judah by Tamar, one of the children of most complicated origin in this family, and now he stands in the register alongside the sons born in wedlock. God's list does not assign qualification by origin, nor rank by human respectability; whoever belongs to this covenant is on the register. The list continues: the sons of Issachar; Gad and Asher, born to Zilpah, Leah's handmaid; even Serah, Asher's daughter, is named — in the registers of that era women were often not counted, yet Serah's name is written clearly in the Scripture. Leah's line totaled thirty-three persons.

Next, those born to Rachel: Joseph and Benjamin. Joseph was already in Egypt, and his two sons Manasseh and Ephraim, born in Egypt, are counted in the number; Benjamin's ten sons are named one by one, and not even the youngest grandsons are omitted. Then Dan and Naphtali, born to Bilhah, Rachel's handmaid. At last the text sums up: "All the souls that came with Jacob into Egypt, which came out of his loins, besides Jacob's sons' wives, all the souls were threescore and six. And the sons of Joseph, which were born him in Egypt, were two souls: all the souls of the house of Jacob, which came into Egypt, were threescore and ten." Seventy persons, in the original shiv'im nefesh (שִׁבְעִים נֶפֶשׁ, H7657 + H5315), seventy lives, seventy persons [4]. Here an academic fact must be honestly stated: in the reckoning of the number, the Hebrew Masoretic Text and the Greek Septuagint differ; in Joseph's household the Septuagint records several more persons, making the total seventy-five, and in Acts 7 Stephen's sermon uses precisely the reckoning of seventy-five [4]. This manuscript only notes this difference honestly and draws no conclusion — behind the two readings lie different methods of reckoning: whether Joseph's descendants and daughters-in-law are included was not handled identically by the ancient copies and translations. What matters is not the dispute on the abacus, but the theological fact the text repeatedly declares: God Himself counted, and not one is missing. Deuteronomy later recalls: thy fathers went down into Egypt with threescore and ten persons, and now the LORD thy God hath made thee as the stars of heaven for multitude. From seventy to as many as the stars and the sand, what lies between is God's faithfulness across more than four hundred years.

Why is a list worth speaking of at such length? Because to people who are drifting, a list is identity. Today we live in an age that measures people by numbers: employee numbers, student numbers, rankings, follower counts — people are compressed into a line of data, replaceable and deletable at any time. But the way God governs His people is by calling names. When He called Abraham He counted the stars; when He numbered Israel He registered them by name; when His Son came He said, I know my sheep and call them by name; even the hairs of our head He has counted. A God willing to call the roll name by name is a God who treats no one as scrap. If you feel you are, in the genealogy, of the kind of Perez — passed over in a stroke, noticed by no one, of complicated origin, ranked last — please listen well: on God's register, to be passed over in a stroke is still to have been written in; and once written in, it is never blotted out. The list is also for descendants: four hundred years later, the Israelites in Egypt multiplied greatly and suffered bitterly, and Pharaoh did not know Joseph, but God knew every name. The deliverance of the Exodus began to be paid out from this very list. To be counted is to be remembered; and for the person who is remembered, deliverance will sooner or later arrive.

Notice also the tears and grace hidden in the list. Judah stands in the list; he was one of those who had proposed selling his brother, and now God still records him in the register of the covenant people, and in days to come the King would come from him. Simeon stands in the list; he and Levi had massacred Shechem, and Jacob at his end would scatter them, yet God still kept him with the whole family. Whatever a person's past, the list neither whitewashes nor condemns; the list only declares one thing: these are the people God will personally bring down and personally bring up. Today's church rolls, baptismal registers, and intercession lists continue precisely the theology of this list: we pray name by name not because our memories are good, but because our God numbers His people one by one in exactly this way. When elders and deacons name a weak member in visitation, and when a mother names a distant child with tears late at night, they all take part in the same sacred act — handing names back into the hands of the Lord who never omits one. At the end of this passage, the text says Jacob's whole household "came into Egypt." No losses, no stragglers. At the setting out it was seventy; at the arrival it was still seventy. The One who led them counted them personally all the way through.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Meeting in Goshen and the Beginning of Being Set Apart (46:28-34)

At the border of Egypt, the pace of the story suddenly quickens, and warms. Jacob first sent Judah before him to Joseph, to direct his face unto Goshen — note that the one sent was Judah, not the firstborn Reuben. In the preceding chapters Judah had step by step come to the place of pledging himself for his brother and bearing responsibility for his father; the position of a leader grows in bearing burdens, it is not seized by birth order. Judah went ahead, and Joseph followed, making ready his chariot: the chariot of the ruler, harnessed with the best horses, driven personally to Goshen to meet his father. Twenty-two years. The one Jacob believed was in the grave, and the one Joseph believed he would never see again, met face to face on the land of Goshen.

The Bible writes this reunion with only the plainest few movements: "And Joseph presented himself unto him; and he fell on his neck, and wept on his neck a good while." He fell on his neck, embraced his neck, and wept a good while. Joseph was the ruler of Egypt, second only to one man and above ten thousand — what scenes had he not seen? Yet before his father he was not a ruler; he was the seventeen-year-old child whose coat of many colors his brothers had stripped off, whom they had thrown into a pit and sold far away. All the grievance, longing, glory, and loneliness of these twenty-two years were in this weeping. The Bible is not ashamed to record the tears of men: Esau wept, Jacob wept at the well, Joseph several times turned away from his brothers and wept, and now in his father's embrace he wept a good while — the words "a good while" are time measured personally by the Holy Spirit. Tears are not weakness; tears are the evidence love leaves on the body. Brothers and sisters, if in your family there is someone long separated, someone out of contact, someone you believed you would never see again in this life, please do not belittle this scene in Goshen: God has the power to lead a relationship people believed already buried back into an embrace. Of course, not every relationship can be reunited in this life in this way; but this scene at least tells us that God is a God who gathers the scattered, and His script is often one chapter longer than our despair.

Jacob's words are the emotional summit of the chapter: "Now let me die, since I have seen thy face, because thou art yet alive." This sentence has an ancient echo: many years later, in the temple at Jerusalem, old Simeon took the infant Jesus in his arms and said, Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, for mine eyes have seen thy salvation. The same state of heart: the wish fulfilled, even death is acceptable. Jacob had wanted too many things in his life: the birthright, his father's blessing, Rachel, his sons' safety, news of Joseph's coat. At the last station he wanted only one thing: to see Joseph's face once. When a person's desires reach their end, they become very simple. To see the face of the beloved, to know he is still alive, to have one's accounts with God and with people settled — then death is no longer terror, but completion. Here too lies the Christian's confidence before death: it is not that we hold on to something and will not let go, but that we have already seen the One we needed to see — by faith we have seen the face of the Lord Jesus — and so the sentence "now let me die" can be learned slowly, a whole lifetime in advance.

After the emotion, Joseph immediately switched to the rhythm of handling affairs, and this is precisely Joseph's loveliness: he could weep, and he could also arrange. He prepared, and taught his brothers word by word, the wording for meeting Pharaoh: if Pharaoh asks what your occupation is, you shall say, thy servants' trade has been about cattle from our youth until now, both we and our fathers. This arrangement had three layers of purpose. The first layer was honesty: they truly were shepherds; they did not impersonate and did not embellish, and before a king they did not change their story. Before any power or rank, the Christian's first strategy is always the truth. The second layer was settlement: every shepherd was an abomination to the Egyptians, in the original to'evah (תּוֹעֵבָה, H8441), an abominable thing, a thing detested and avoided [5]. Hearing that they were shepherds, Pharaoh would instead settle them in the land of Goshen, far from the royal city and near the border — where water and grass were abundant, exactly the right place for pasturing. Human detestation, in God's hand, became an instrument of settlement. The third layer was the deepest: the beginning of being set apart. The whole family living in Goshen kept a distance from Egyptian society and were not rapidly assimilated by court culture and idolatry, so that across four hundred years they could multiply into a distinct nation and finally be led out by God. God's way of protecting His people is sometimes not to take them away at once, but first to give them a Goshen: a place where they can settle, raise children, and maintain their own worship and way of life. Today's families and churches are the same: we live and work in the world, yet we need Goshen's boundary — the setting apart of the Sabbath, the persistence of the family altar, the priority of children's nurture — not to escape the world, but first to stand firm, so that in days to come we have strength to go out. These words Joseph taught his brothers seem to be diplomatic wording; in fact they were God, through his mouth, preparing a cradle for a nation's four hundred years.

Following this passage, we come to today's application, and will speak only of the three most essential things. First, if you are about to walk the downhill road of life — retirement, old age, sickness, moving away from familiar places — please go back to Beersheba: offer sacrifices first, listen to God first, and set out only after you have taken "I will surely go down with thee" into your heart. A downhill road is not frightening; walking it alone is what is frightening. Second, if you feel you are the most inconspicuous one on the list, please remember Perez: God's register omits no one, and even those of the most complicated origin and the last rank are within the count. What you must do is not to squeeze to the front of the list, but to confirm that you are on the register, and to give thanks for it. Third, if God has given you a Goshen — a settled home, a stretch of years in which to breathe, a church in which to worship — please cherish this grace of being set apart, and also learn from Joseph to weep for your family and to arrange thoughtfully for your family: tears and planning side by side, feeling and responsibility side by side — this is precisely mature faith.

One more word for those who are caring for the elderly and accompanying the sick: Joseph's prepared wording, his harnessed chariot, and his embrace that arrived in time are all lessons in caregiving. In accompanying a person on the downhill road, tears alone are not enough; there must also be arrangement: prepare the chariot, think the words through carefully, settle the dwelling properly, so that the one accompanied suffers a little less panic and receives a little more dignity. Through Joseph's hands God personally demonstrated what considerate caregiving is — he embraced his father's neck and wept, and he also planned out the dwelling place of the whole family for the next four hundred years. As we accompany parents on the last stretch of the road and accompany members through days of sickness, we should do the same: prayer and practical matters side by side, tears and responsibility side by side — this is living out "I will surely go down with thee" for the people beside us to see.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Genesis 46: an altar at Beersheba, a list of seventy names, an embrace in the land of Goshen. Before a great migration, first an altar and a vision — this is the order of the whole chapter; God's "going with thee" covers the downhill road of life — this is the promise of the whole chapter; of the names that are counted, not one is missing — this is the comfort of the whole chapter. Jacob went down, and God went down with him; Jacob's descendants later came up, and God personally brought them up. That sentence of that year, "I will go down with thee into Egypt, and I will also surely bring thee up again," was paid out in full at the Red Sea in the Exodus. Today this promise comes to us as well: everyone who sets out by faith, however low the place he goes down to, has God going with him, and will surely have God bringing him up.

Let us pray together: God who goes with people, thank You; You did not stand at the border of the promised land watching Jacob go, You personally accompanied him down, and You personally brought his descendants up. Comfort those among us who are walking the downhill road, strengthen those who are about to migrate and are in fear, and remind us to build the altar first and listen to You first before setting out. And let us be certain that our names are on Your register, and not one is missing. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[4] shiv'im nefesh: Hebrew שִׁבְעִים (shiv'im, H7657, seventy) + נֶפֶשׁ (nefesh, H5315, life, person, soul); Genesis 46:27, "all the souls of the house of Jacob, which came into Egypt, were threescore and ten." The reckoning of the number must be honestly noted: the Masoretic Text (MT) counts 66 persons who came with Jacob (46:26, not including Joseph's household already in Egypt) plus Joseph and his two sons and others, totaling 70 persons; the Greek Septuagint (LXX) at 46:20 records more of Joseph's descendants, making the total 75 persons, and the 75 persons cited by Stephen in Acts 7:14 agree with the LXX reckoning; the seventy is stated explicitly in Exodus 1:5 ("all the souls that came out of the loins of Jacob were seventy souls") and Deuteronomy 10:22 ("thy fathers went down into Egypt with threescore and ten persons"); Deuteronomy 26:5 says only that the fathers were "a few in number" when they went down, and Joshua 24:4 gives no number. The difference arises from differing methods of reckoning and manuscript traditions, scholarship has reached no settled conclusion, and this manuscript only notes the difference and draws no conclusion; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[5] to'evah: Hebrew תּוֹעֵבָה (H8441), an abominable thing, a detestable thing; Genesis 46:34, "for every shepherd is an abomination unto the Egyptians," and the custom in Genesis 43:32 that the Egyptians could not eat with the Hebrews is also related to this notion; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "human detestation, in God's hand, becomes an instrument of settlement and of being set apart" is a homiletical exposition based on the outcome of the narrative, and is marked separately from the lexical facts.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "I am God, the God of thy father: fear not to go down into Egypt; for I will there make of thee a great nation: I will go down with thee into Egypt; and I will also surely bring thee up again: and Joseph shall put his hand upon thine eyes." (Genesis 46:3-4, KJV)

In his one hundred and thirtieth year, Jacob had to leave everything familiar and go down into Egypt. He was afraid, so he stopped first at Beersheba and offered sacrifices; God understood his fear, so in the night He called his name twice and took his fears apart one by one: afraid the line would end, God said He would make him a great nation; afraid of dying in a foreign land, God said He would surely bring him up again; afraid of being alone, God said, I will go down with you. Brothers and sisters, on the downhill road of your life God does not stand at the top watching you go; He walks down with you step by step. God counted the seventy names on that list one by one, and today He is counting your name too. Fear not to go down — build the altar first and place the decision before God; once you have heard clearly, "I will surely go with you," set out with a settled heart. At the end of the descent there is the road up that God Himself has prepared. Your name is on the register, not one is missing, and He will walk with you the whole way, until you see His face and can say, now let me die. May you tonight also hear Him call your name twice, answer first with "Here am I," and then, carrying His going-with-you, sleep in peace and set out tomorrow with a settled heart.

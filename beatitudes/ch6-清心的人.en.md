## I. The Scripture

"Blessed are the pure in heart, For they shall see God." (Matt. 5:8, NKJV)

This beatitude is short, so short that it is easily read past. Pure in heart, they shall see God, with no other condition in between. Yet the more one reads it, the more one feels that it asks a very deep question: Why do people so often not see God?

It is not that God is absent. When praying, one feels He is far away; when reading Scripture, the heart is in turmoil; whether days go smoothly or not, only one's own calculations remain — very often, the problem is not outside, but that the heart inside is too mixed. When the heart is mixed, the eyes blur. The Sixth Beatitude speaks of precisely this: when the heart is pure, a person can see God.

## II. Exegesis of the Original Text

The Greek text: Μακάριοι οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ, ὅτι αὐτοὶ τὸν θεὸν ὄψονται.

**1. "The pure in heart" (οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ, hoi katharoi tē kardia)**

"Pure" or "clean" is καθαρός (katharos, Strong G2513), whose basic meaning is clean, pure, unmixed. This word has two layers of meaning in the Bible, and in reading this beatitude, both layers are needed.

One layer is being washed clean. The related verb is καθαρίζω (katharizō, Strong G2511), meaning to cleanse, to wash clean: cups and dishes washed clean (Matt. 23:26 uses precisely this verb), lepers cleansed, sins forgiven — all move from "having defilement" to "having been cleansed." Acts 15:9 says that God, through faith, "purified their hearts" (the original uses precisely καθαρίζω, G2511). Purity of heart is not a character a person cultivates out of himself; it is first having been washed.

The other layer is unmixedness, singleness. Wine not diluted with water, gold not adulterated, a heart with only one Master. James 4:8 states the opposite very bluntly: "purify your hearts, you double-minded!" "Double-minded" is δίψυχος (dipsychos, Strong G1374), literally a person with "two minds, two souls." Wanting God on one side, and unwilling to give up the benefits of the world on the other, refusing to let go of either side — such a heart the Bible does not call pure.

"Heart" is καρδία (kardia, Strong G2588). The heart spoken of in the Bible does not refer only to feelings; it is the center of a person: thoughts issue from there, the will decides there, desires take root there. Proverbs says, "Keep your heart with all diligence, For out of it spring the issues of life" (Prov. 4:23, NKJV), and Jesus also said that what is spoken by the mouth and done in conduct all come out of the heart (Matt. 15:18–19). So "purity of heart" purifies not the surface, but the source. However respectable things look on the outside, if the source is adulterated, what flows out will sooner or later change in flavor.

One small point also deserves attention: τῇ καρδίᾳ uses the dative case, which can be read as "those who are pure in heart" or as "those who are pure as to the heart." Whichever reading is taken, the emphasis is not on external rules, but on the inner person. In Matthew 23, Jesus rebukes the Pharisees for cleaning the outside of the cup and dish, while inside they are full of extortion and self-indulgence (Matt. 23:25), rebuking precisely this point: the shell of religion polished until it shines, while the heart has never been touched.

The rendering of this verse in the Lü Zhenzhong Translation is broadly close in meaning to the Chinese Union Version; both place the emphasis on cleanness of heart and seeing God.

English versions for comparison:
- KJV: "Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God."
- NIV: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God."
- NRSV: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God."

**2. "They shall see God" (τὸν θεὸν ὄψονται, ton theon opsontai)**

"See" is ὄψονται (opsontai), from ὁράω (horaō, Strong G3708), in the future tense: they shall see. In the Bible, this "seeing" is not only seeing an object with the physical eyes; it also carries the sense of recognizing, understanding, and experiencing personally.

This promise must be read from both ends together; reading only one end will skew it.

One end is the future. On this earth, no one can see God in full. "For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face" (1 Cor. 13:12, NKJV). John states the end even more fully: "we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is" (1 John 3:2, NKJV), and at the end of Revelation it also says that God's servants "shall see His face" (Rev. 22:4, NKJV). Seeing God is first an ultimate, complete promise; it is the blessing of the pure in heart in the new heaven and new earth.

The other end is the present. The "seeing" of the present is, through the eyes of faith, tasting beforehand in still-incomplete light. Recognizing God's hand in one's circumstances, suddenly understanding while reading Scripture that this is spoken to oneself, experiencing His real presence in prayer — these are not illusions, nor self-consolation; they are a little light of that future, complete "seeing" leaking into today beforehand. Hebrews says that Moses "endured as seeing Him who is invisible" (Heb. 11:27, NKJV), speaking of precisely this present seeing.

With the two ends joined, the order becomes clear: the more truly one sees in this life, the more hope one has for seeing in the life to come; the more mixed the heart is in this life, the less that light can enter. Purity of heart is not merit exchanged for seeing God; it is the very condition of the eyes. When the eyes are dirty, it is not that one does not want to see; it is that one cannot see.

## III. The Place and Logic of This Beatitude in the Sermon on the Mount

As the Beatitudes reach the Sixth Beatitude, there is a thread within them.

The first four beatitudes (poor in spirit, mourning, meekness, hungering and thirsting for righteousness) speak of how a person is emptied before God and fed. The Fifth Beatitude, mercy, turns the camera to person-to-person relations for the first time. The Sixth Beatitude turns back again, speaking at a deeper level: if acts of mercy are to last long and not change in flavor, there must be a root in the heart. That root is purity.

This order is not arbitrary. A person whose hands are willing to show mercy to others, but whose heart is full of calculation — helping people in order to be seen, giving alms in order to exchange for reputation — such mercy will not last long, nor will it be genuine. What Jesus warns against immediately afterward in the Sermon on the Mount is precisely this performative piety: when giving alms, do not let the left hand know what the right hand is doing; when praying, do not stand in the synagogues to be seen by people (Matt. 6:1–6). The Sixth Beatitude and chapter six are in fact two sides of the same matter: one side is the promise, that the pure in heart shall see God; the other side is the warning, that when motives are dirty, even the most spiritual things will rot.

Looking further on, the Seventh Beatitude, the peacemakers (5:9), is the fruit of purity of heart. Only a person with one Master in his heart can serve as a peacemaker; a person with two masters in his heart will first contend for himself wherever he goes. The Eighth Beatitude, being persecuted for righteousness' sake (5:10), is the cost of all this. First make the heart pure, then go out to live among people, and finally be prepared to pay the cost — this is roughly the road the Beatitudes take.

For this reason, purity of heart is like a pivot in the Beatitudes. It roots downward in the poverty of spirit and mourning before it: a person who has never wept over his own sin will not truly seek a clean heart, because he does not feel at all that there is any problem with his heart. It bears upward the fruit of mercy and peace. A heart that has been washed and is single is the living source of all the character that follows.

## IV. Cross References

- Ps. 24:3–4 — "Who may ascend into the hill of the LORD? Or who may stand in His holy place? He who has clean hands and a pure heart, Who has not lifted up his soul to an idol, Nor sworn deceitfully." (NKJV) This is the most direct Old Testament root of the Sixth Beatitude. The one who can draw near to God is not the person with the most complete rituals, but the person whose hands and heart are both clean. "Not lifted up his soul to an idol" means not giving one's heart to those empty, bubble-like things.
- Ps. 51:10 — After David sinned, he prayed: "Create in me a clean heart, O God, And renew a steadfast spirit within me." (NKJV) The word "create" reminds people: a clean heart is not patched up; it is newly created by God. Only when one knows one cannot create it oneself will one open one's mouth to ask.
- Ps. 73:1 — "Truly God is good to Israel, To such as are pure in heart." (NKJV) When the psalmist Asaph wrote this psalm, his heart was in fact very unsettled: the wicked prosper, the righteous suffer. The whole psalm is his process from blurred eyes to clear eyes. In the end, the turning point he saw was not in his circumstances, but in seeing God afresh in the sanctuary (Ps. 73:17, in substance). This is precisely the live version of the Sixth Beatitude: when the heart is settled, the eyes become clear.
- Ezek. 36:25–27 — God promises to sprinkle clean water on people, so that they are cleansed from all filthiness, and to give them a new heart and a new spirit. In the Old Testament, purity of heart was always something God took the initiative to do; what a person can do is not refuse the water sprinkled down.
- Matt. 23:25–27 — Jesus rebukes the Pharisees for cleaning only the outside of the cup and dish. If outward piety replaces inward cleanness, a person is not nearer to God, but farther, because he will mistakenly think he is already clean enough.
- 2 Tim. 2:22 — Paul exhorts Timothy to pursue righteousness, faith, love, and peace "with those who call on the Lord out of a pure heart." (NKJV) Purity of heart is not a matter of shutting one's door alone; it needs companions. Being together with a group of people who pray with a pure heart, the heart does not easily become dirty; mixing all day with calculating people, the heart can hardly avoid being led astray.
- Heb. 12:14 — "without holiness no one will see the Lord" (in substance; NKJV: "Pursue peace with all people, and holiness, without which no one will see the Lord."). This sentence is almost a synonym of the Sixth Beatitude: the qualification for seeing the Lord is not in education, seniority, or years of service, but in holiness, in that heart set apart and belonging solely to God.
- James 4:8 — "Draw near to God and He will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners; and purify your hearts, you double-minded!" (NKJV) The order is worth noting: it is drawing near that brings nearness; cleansing and drawing near to God are two steps of the same matter, not first washing oneself clean and only then being qualified to come and see God.

## V. Life Application

**Scene One: In service and work, pass the motive through once first.** The same matter, done with different motives, comes out with completely different flavors. Teaching a class, leading a small group, doing a piece of business, writing a piece of code — is it to build people up and do the matter well, or to be seen, to be affirmed, to surpass others? It cannot be seen from the outside, but one knows most clearly in one's own heart. In the ministry training of River of Life Christian Church, there is a reminder often mentioned (paraphrased in substance from Pastor Liu Tong's sermons): God seems to be speaking all the time; not hearing is often not because God's voice is too small, but because our hearts are too mixed — hearts crowded with calculation, panic, and ambition, like a pool of muddy water that cannot reflect the moon. This analogy is rough, but accurate. The concrete practice is not difficult: before doing an important matter, first ask one question in the heart — Am I doing this to please God, or to prove myself? Asking before acting, much inner friction is reduced on the spot by half.

**Scene Two: At home, do not treat family members as tools.** Impure motives are hardest to hide at home. Being good to a spouse — is it because one loves him or her, or because one expects repayment, fears he or she will leave, or is performing for outsiders to see? Disciplining children — is it accompanying them to grow according to their own gifts, or for one's own face before relatives and friends, comparing one's child with other people's children? Caring for the elderly — is it sincere service, or performing for relatives to see, calculating what will be divided in the future? These questions are all unpleasant to ask, but the Sixth Beatitude asks precisely to this level. Treating family members as tools means the heart is mixed; when motives are slowly cleared, and family members are no longer treated as means to satisfy one's own sense of security, the pressure the other party feels will loosen, and many frozen relationships begin to move from here. Purity of heart may not immediately change the other party, but it first changes the flavor of the air at home.

**Scene Three: In the age of the mobile phone, leave the heart a place not occupied.** There is a very concrete reason why the hearts of this generation are mixed: they are too full. On opening one's eyes, one first swipes through messages; layoffs, price increases, conflicts, comparisons all pour in in a flood, the heart is muddy all day long — what talk is there of seeing God? The Danish thinker Kierkegaard has a book whose title itself is a piece of exegesis: purity of heart is to will one thing (his work is commonly translated in Chinese as 《清心要专一》 and the like, in English as Purity of Heart Is to Will One Thing). A heart can truly hold only one Master. In practice it can be very small: for example, for the first period after waking each day, do not touch the phone; first read a passage of Scripture, say a short prayer, first make the source of the day clear, then let the voices of the world enter. This is not aloofness; it is order — a person who first sees God, then reads the news, and a person who is first filled full by the news and then remembers God, live the day with different eyes.

## VI. Response Prayer

Lord, I confess that my heart is often mixed. On one side I want to draw near to You, on the other side I am unwilling to lay down those calculations, vanities, and panics; on the outside I do spiritual things, on the inside I am planning for myself. When I read Your words rebuking the Pharisees, I always feel they speak of others; in fact, many times they speak of me.

As David prayed, so I ask today: please create in me a clean heart. By myself, I cannot wash it clean, nor can I be single. Please sprinkle clean water on me, wash away, little by little, the falseness mixed in; replace that double-minded heart with a heart single toward You.

You have promised that the pure in heart shall see You. In this life, please open my eyes, let me recognize Your hand in ordinary days and understand Your words; in the life to come, please let me see Your face frankly, without needing to shrink back. I am willing for my heart first to become pure, and then my eyes will be clear. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ I pray, Amen.

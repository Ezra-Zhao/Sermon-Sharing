## Summary (about 100 words)

Moses' father-in-law Jethro heard all that God had done for Israel, brought back Zipporah and Moses' two sons, rejoiced and blessed the LORD as greater than all gods, offered sacrifices, and ate with the elders. The next day, seeing Moses judge alone from morning to evening, he said plainly that the thing was too heavy, and counselled a tiered order: Moses would devote himself to representing the people before God and teaching the statutes, while able men who feared God, were truthful, and hated covetousness would be chosen to judge in ranks. Moses listened, and his father-in-law returned home, his task complete.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Today we come to Exodus chapter 18. This chapter has no earth-shaking parting of the Red Sea, no wonder of manna falling from heaven, and no swords flashing on a battlefield. It tells of a family visit, a meal, a conversation, and a set of arrangements. Yet do not underestimate this chapter on that account. When God redeems a people, He does not only snatch them out of a slave master's hand; He also organises them and teaches them, so that a crowd of runaway slaves slowly becomes a people with order, with statutes, and with the ability to bear one another's burdens. The exodus is the beginning of redemption, the covenant at Sinai is its climax, and chapter 18 stands right between the two, like a threshold: before crossing it, a father-in-law stands here first, teaching Moses a lesson every leader must learn for a whole lifetime — when the thing is too heavy, do not carry it alone by sheer force.

Let us first note where this chapter stands. The Scripture says Jethro came to the mountain of God, where Moses and the people were camped, at a time when the Ten Commandments had not yet been given and the law had not yet been formally proclaimed at Sinai. That is to say, this arrangement of tiered judgment happened before the giving of the law. This does not mean organisation is more important than the law; it means that when God prepares His people, He begins with the order of daily life. In Deuteronomy chapter 1, Moses in his old age looked back and also spoke of appointing judges to share the heavy load; that was his recollection many years later. How the sequence in the two passages corresponds is discussed differently in scholarship, and we will state that honestly later, without preaching a conjecture as a settled conclusion. Today let us first walk honestly with the narrative of Exodus itself, and see what this chapter itself wants to teach us.

The main line of this chapter is in fact very clear; it is a line of four steps: first, hearing what God has done; then, blessing God; then, offering counsel; and finally, listening to the counsel. From hearing to blessing, from blessing to counsel, from counsel to listening — every step is natural, and every step also reveals the character of the people involved. The one who gives counsel is an outsider, yet he keeps his place; the one who hears the counsel is the leader, yet he is willing to be taught. In this sermon I do not intend to turn this chapter into a management lecture. This chapter does indeed contain wisdom about organisation and the division of labour, and very practical wisdom at that; but if management technique is made the protagonist, the Scripture is preached off centre. The true protagonist of this chapter remains the God who did great things, and who then gave wisdom through the mouth of a father-in-law. With that lens, let us read the text.

Let us read several key passages together:

"When Jethro, the priest of Midian, Moses' father in law, heard of all that God had done for Moses, and for Israel his people, and that the LORD had brought Israel out of Egypt." (Exodus 18:1, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jethro said, Blessed be the LORD, who hath delivered you out of the hand of the Egyptians, and out of the hand of Pharaoh, who hath delivered the people from under the hand of the Egyptians. Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods: for in the thing wherein they dealt proudly he was above them." (Exodus 18:10-11, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses' father in law said unto him, The thing that thou doest is not good. Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee: for this thing is too heavy for thee; thou art not able to perform it thyself alone." (Exodus 18:17-18, excerpt, KJV)

"Moreover thou shalt provide out of all the people able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness; and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands, and rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens." (Exodus 18:21, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, thank You that You do not only save us out of bondage, but also teach us how to bear one another's burdens and how to live in order. Through Jethro's blessing, remind us to tell of Your deeds first in everything; through his plain words, remind us that admitting a thing is too heavy is not weakness; through Moses' listening, remind us that those who lead must all the more be willing to be taught. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: A Father-in-Law Brings the Family, Hears All God Has Done — First Telling, First Blessing (18:1-12)

The very first sentence of this chapter introduces Jethro plainly: "Jethro, the priest of Midian, Moses' father in law." He has two identities. By family, he is Moses' father-in-law, one of the household; by religious background, he is the priest of Midian [1] — a man holding office in a foreign religion, not a Hebrew, and still less an elder in Israel's camp. These two identities laid over each other create the tension of the whole chapter: an outsider is about to walk into the camp of God's people; a relative is about to criticise his son-in-law's work. The reason his later words carry weight is precisely that he is neither a stranger meddling from outside, nor a subordinate currying favour; standing where father-in-law and outsider meet, he speaks with both the weight of family affection and the clarity of an outsider.

This time Jethro did not come empty-handed. He brought Moses' wife Zipporah and his two sons back with him. The Scripture explains expressly that Zipporah had earlier been sent back by Moses, and now the family was reunited in the wilderness. After Exodus chapter 4, Zipporah and the children fade out of the narrative; in chapter 18, the father-in-law personally brings his daughter and his grandsons back to Moses' side. This stroke looks homely, yet it matters greatly: Moses led a million people out of Egypt, while his own little household was separated. Those who work for God also need their family back beside them; the tent of the one who leads the crowd must not stay empty forever either. The first thing Jethro did was not to give counsel, but to send people home. The wisdom of governance had not yet opened its mouth; family wholeness had already gone first. A gentle application belongs right here: when we help a person, often the first thing is not to give him a pile of opinions, but to supply the missing piece in his life, and to bring the people he longs for back to his side.

The names of the two children take a full two verses of the Scripture to explain — the narrative habit of the Bible reminding us: there is something here to hear. The elder son was called Gershom, and Moses said: I have been a stranger in a strange land. Gershom's name records Moses' forty years of wandering — a prince of Egypt by upbringing, overnight a fugitive, herding his father-in-law's flocks in the wilderness of Midian, so that even the son born to him carried the word "sojourner" in his name. The second son was called Eliezer, and Moses said: the God of my father was my help, and delivered me from the sword of Pharaoh. This name records something else: the escape was not accomplished by the speed of his own feet; God's hand preserved him under the sword. One name speaks of sojourning, the other speaks of help; one remembers affliction, the other remembers deliverance. Brothers and sisters, put together, these two names are the faith autobiography of the first half of Moses' life. He needed no memoir of his own; his sons' names are the memoir. He needed no written reminiscence; call a child's name, and all the past is there. This also reminds parents: the names we give our children, and the stories of the past we tell them, are silently declaring what we believe. Moses did not erase the affliction from the names, nor did he let affliction be the whole of the names: the sojourning is true, and the help is also true — and the help is greater than the sword, because the child's name finally rests on "delivered me." Affliction as background, deliverance as conclusion — this is how a believer looks back on a life.

Next comes the first climax of the chapter: telling and blessing. Jethro heard all that God had done — that is the general statement of verse 1. Then, in the camp, Moses told his father-in-law all that the LORD had done to Pharaoh and to the Egyptians for Israel's sake, and all the travail that had come upon them by the way, and how the LORD delivered them. Note the order: what Moses told was not only victories, but also hardships; not only miracles, but also the troubles along the road. If a testimony reports joys and hides sorrows, it becomes propaganda; Moses' telling had difficulties and deliverances both, so what Jethro heard was a complete and truthful story of redemption. When we tell others what God has done, we should be just as honest today: not exaggerating our own merit, not concealing the bends in the road we walked, so that the hearer knows clearly that the thing succeeded because God delivered, not because we were so capable.

When Jethro had heard, his response had three layers, each rising higher. The first layer was joy: the Scripture says Jethro rejoiced for all the goodness which the LORD had done to Israel, whom He had delivered out of the hand of the Egyptians. Rejoicing over someone else's grace is not a generosity everyone has. How many people, hearing that another has been blessed, first feel a sourness in the heart? Jethro was an outsider; Israel's deliverance brought him no direct benefit of his own, yet he rejoiced as if it were his own affair. To rejoice sincerely over what God has done for others is a sign that a person's heart has been opened. The second layer was blessing: he said, Blessed be the LORD, who has delivered you out of the hand of the Egyptians and out of the hand of Pharaoh. Joy is a feeling; blessing is a declaration. He was not only glad in his heart; he opened his mouth and ascribed the glory to the LORD, crediting the deliverance, every bit of it, to God's name. The third layer, and the weightiest, was his confessional declaration: "Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods: for in the thing wherein they dealt proudly he was above them." How this sentence should be understood is read differently in scholarship: some take it as Jethro's confession of conversion to the one true God, from now on acknowledging the LORD as the only true God; others take it as still, against a polytheistic background, an acknowledgement that the LORD surpasses all gods and is supreme — a major step in worshipping the one true God, yet not necessarily a complete statement of conversion in the later doctrinal sense. Both readings have scholarly support, and this manuscript presents them side by side without settling the question. What we can be sure of is what the text itself says plainly: Jethro confessed with his own mouth that in delivering Israel, the LORD had shown His power surpassing every so-called god, and that Pharaoh's pride had been broken by God Himself. This was already no polite formula; it was the heartfelt bowing down of a foreign priest after he had heard what God had done.

After the blessing came, immediately, sacrifice and a meal. Jethro took a burnt offering and sacrifices for God, and Aaron came, and all the elders of Israel, to eat bread with Moses' father-in-law before God. Let this scene pause in our minds for a moment: sacrifice is worship, and eating is fellowship. A foreign father-in-law offers sacrifice, Israel's elders come to the table, and everyone eats together before God. Before Israel's formal covenant, this is a rare picture of unity in the camp: whoever has heard what God has done, whatever his origin, can worship before God and can eat at the same table. Let the homiletical point be stated clearly here: true fellowship begins with telling together and blessing together what God has done. Many relationships break down not because people never ate together, but because at the table only what people did wrong was ever discussed, never what God had done. On that day Jethro first listened, first rejoiced, first blessed, first sacrificed — and only then, on the next day, did he open his mouth to talk about work. That order is itself a spiritual discipline: be a worshipper first, then a counsellor; put God at the centre first, then talk about human arrangements. Advice without blessing underneath easily turns into accusation; advice that has passed through worship carries the warmth of blessing.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Judging Alone from Morning to Evening, a Father-in-Law Says Plainly: Too Heavy — Counsel Given with a Sense of Place (18:13-23)

The next day, the scene changed completely. Yesterday was reunion and worship; today was crowding and exhaustion. The Scripture says that on the morrow Moses sat to judge the people, and the people stood by Moses from the morning unto the evening. Let us see this picture clearly. Moses sat; the people stood. Moses was one man; the people were there all day. From morning to evening, the queue had no end. Why did the people come? Moses himself explained: the people come to me to enquire of God; when they have a matter, they come to me, and I judge between one and another, and I make them know the statutes of God and His laws. That is to say, everything Moses was doing was itself good: enquiring of God is good; discerning right and wrong is good; teaching the statutes is also good. The problem was not that the work was wrong; the problem was that it all rested on one man — and even good work will crush a man when it all rests on him.

Jethro watched from the side for a whole day, and then he spoke. His first sentence was not polite at all: "The thing that thou doest is not good." Note carefully: the ground for the father-in-law's words was not that his office was great, nor that his years were many, but a fact he had seen with complete clarity: "Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee: for this thing is too heavy for thee; thou art not able to perform it thyself alone." This wearing away, in the original, carries the sense of withering, of fading — like a flower slowly drying up away from water. When a person overdraws himself, at first he does not crash down with a bang; he withers quietly like a flower: first tired, then irritable, then numb, and at last even his original love for the people is worn away. Jethro's sentence pointed out the loss on both sides at once: Moses would wear away, and the people would wear away. When a leader forces himself to hold on, it is not only the leader who suffers; the people stand from morning to evening, wait until they are exhausted, a case drags on for a whole day, justice arrives late, and resentment grows. So monopolising the work is not diligence; in the end, monopolising is itself a debt owed to the very people one serves. This is a lesson many who are leaders, parents, or the mainstay of others must hear today: admitting that the thing is too heavy is not denying one's calling; it is protecting the calling from being destroyed by the wrong way of carrying it.

Still more important is the way Jethro gave his counsel. He said: "Hearken now unto my voice, I will give thee counsel, and God shall be with thee." This expression, giving counsel, uses in the original precisely the word for advising, consulting, and counselling [2]; its tone is that of an adviser, not a king. He did not say you must do exactly this; he said I will give you counsel. Then, at the end of his proposal, he added a sentence of the utmost importance: "If thou shalt do this thing, and God command thee so, then thou shalt be able to endure, and all this people shall also go to their place in peace." Do you see it? The father-in-law himself handed the final decision back to God. He offered a plan, but he did not dress his plan up as God's command; he proposed a scheme, but he left one condition attached: if God commands you so. This is the sense of place for an outsider giving counsel, and it is a lesson everyone who advises others should learn: advice is advice; however confident we are, we must allow the other person to verify it before God, to pray over it, to seek confirmation. To package one's own opinion as the word of God is the most dangerous kind of overstepping. Jethro, as the honoured elder and the guest of honour of the whole camp, was fully entitled to be more forceful; yet he deliberately stopped his words at the position of an adviser. When the counsellor does not overstep, the hearer can actually take the counsel in; this boundary is kept spotlessly clean in this chapter.

Jethro's proposal had two main parts. The first was to redefine Moses' own post. He said: you be for the people toward God, bringing the causes of the people to God; and you shall teach them ordinances and laws, and show them the way wherein they must walk, and the work that they must do. Please circle these two things: one is being for the people before God — the service of prayer and mediation, bringing the people's difficulties before God; the other is teaching — explaining God's statutes and ordinances clearly, so that the people know the way they should walk. These two things no one else could do in Moses' place. Bringing the people's causes before God requires the man who communes with God; teaching the statutes and ordinances requires the man who receives the revelation. Jethro's brilliance was that he did not simply tell Moses to do less; he helped Moses distinguish: what can only be done by you, and what can be handed over. A leader's true post is not grasping every small matter in his own hand, but guarding those positions that only he can guard — representing the people before God, and teaching before the people. Only when the core post is guarded is one qualified to divide the rest out; if the core post is lost, no matter how finely the work is divided, it is empty.

The second part was choosing helpers and governing in tiers. Jethro said: you shall provide out of all the people able men, and place them as rulers of thousands, rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens over the people, and let them judge the people at all seasons. There are three points in this passage; let us look at them one by one.

First, the criteria for choosing come before the offices. The text lists four: fearing God, men of truth, hating covetousness — together with the general heading, able men. The order of the four is very deliberate: character first, arrangements afterwards; look at the man first, confer the office after. Fearing God [4] means this man has in his heart a God greater than Moses and greater than the favour of the people; when he judges a case, he first thinks of how God sees it. Men of truth [5] — the original speaks of truth, of reliability; this man's words stand up, and his work can bear examination. Hating covetousness [6] means he loathes the unjust gain that comes by abusing an office; when money is placed before him, his hand does not reach out and his heart does not move. Brothers and sisters, at a time when the Ten Commandments had not yet been given and the law had not yet been written down, the standard Jethro set for judges already struck at the lifeline of judicial fairness: fear God, speak truth, keep clean hands. When we choose people for work today, we should return to the same order: ability decides how great a thing a person can do; character decides how far his work can go. Ability without character beneath it — the greater the office, the greater the harm.

Note also a detail of language: the word the KJV renders "provide" (Ex 18:21) means in the original first of all to see, to look at, to observe [3]. That is to say, choosing is not decided by a résumé, by a recommendation, or by a snap impression; it is done by watching carefully among the people — seeing how this man treats others day by day, how he speaks, how he faces money — and only choosing him when one has seen clearly. A leader choosing people must first open his eyes, and let enough days pass. Hastily appointed commanders are often people who know how to perform; slowly observed ones are people who can stand up under real matters.

Second, tiers are not ranks of worth, but a sharing of the load. Rulers of thousands, of hundreds, of fifties, and of tens: the number of people under them differs, and the weight of responsibility differs, but they do the same one thing — judging the people at all seasons, so that the people do not have to queue before Moses for everything. From ten people to a thousand, every level has someone responsible, and every matter has someone to receive it. The wisdom of this arrangement is that it lets disputes among neighbours be settled among neighbours, and keeps the small matters of ten people from having to disturb the leader who oversees a thousand. Levels of governance are not meant in themselves to grade people into high and low; they are meant to let responsibility land where the problem is nearest.

Third, the principle for dividing cases is crystal clear: every great matter they shall bring to Moses, but every small matter they shall judge themselves. Jethro stated the result beautifully: "So shall it be easier for thyself, and they shall bear the burden with thee." Easier — that is for Moses. Bearing the burden with you — that is for the judges. And going to their place in peace — that is for the people. A good division of labour benefits all three sides: the leader does not collapse, the fellow workers have a share, and the people receive timely justice. Please remember especially these words: bear the burden with thee. Sharing is not throwing the leader's burden away; it is letting more shoulders carry it together. It is not that Moses from now on had nothing to do; it is that Moses carries the portion he should carry, and the others carry the portion they should carry. Among God's people, a burden was never designed to be carried in full by one man. Galatians says: bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ. The tiered governance of this chapter may be called an early form of that sentence in the governing life of the Old Testament. Of course, this is a homiletical connection at the level of exposition, not a claim that Jethro quoted the New Testament in his day; it is that the principles by which the same God works are consistent from beginning to end.

Let us gather this section together. At the level of fact, Jethro's counsel can be listed as a checklist: the posts are separated, with representation and teaching belonging to Moses; helpers are chosen by character; governance is tiered by number; cases are divided by size. All of these are observable, verifiable facts of the text. But the spiritual weight of this chapter is more than a checklist: that an outsider could offer such counsel was because he had first heard what God had done, and had first blessed God; that a leader could receive such help was because God had brought the father-in-law into his camp. Organisational method is the outer shell; God's perfecting of people through one another is the core. If we take away only the shell — learning a set of meeting and division techniques — without learning to tell of God first, to fear God, and to hate covetousness, we have learned only the skin, and the weightiest thing in the text has leaked away.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Moses Willing to Listen, Jethro Willing to Leave — Teachability and Withdrawal (18:24-27)

The end of the text is only four short verses, yet it lights up the character of both men.

First, look at Moses. The Scripture says Moses hearkened to the voice of his father-in-law, and did all that he had said. This sentence is plain and unadorned, yet it weighs a thousand pounds. Who was Moses? The leader who with his own hand had brought the Israelites out of Egypt; the man who lifted up the rod beside the Red Sea; the man who spoke with God day after day. And who was Jethro? By spiritual credentials, a foreign priest; by standing in the camp, a guest. This great leader was willing to listen to a foreign father-in-law's criticism of his way of working — and not only to listen: he acted on it. He chose able men out of all Israel and made them heads over the people, rulers of thousands, rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens, and they judged the people at all seasons; hard causes they brought to Moses, but every small matter they judged themselves. Brothers and sisters, in this matter of hearing advice, listening reveals character more than speaking does. When a person reaches a certain position, the hardest thing is not that no one dares to tell him the truth, but whether, when the truth comes, he is willing to bow his head. If Moses had stood on the leader's dignity, he could perfectly well have said: what do you know? God called me. But he did not. He could tell the difference: his father-in-law was challenging not his calling, only his method. The calling must not be shaken; the method can be changed completely. A leader who is willing to change his method, willing to listen to an outsider, willing to admit that his original way of doing things would drag both himself and the people down — this is not losing face; this is precisely his credential for being a leader. For a leader to be unteachable is the most dangerous defect of character. In this chapter Moses set an example for all who lead: the higher the position, the wider the ears must open.

Then look at Jethro. When the matter was done, the Scripture says Moses let his father-in-law depart, and he went his way into his own land. The counsellor did not stay to hold power. If Jethro had wished to remain in the camp, by his standing as father-in-law and by the merit of his counsel he could perfectly well have sought a prominent position, directing affairs every day. But he did not. He said the words that needed saying, did the things that needed doing, and went back to his own land. He came bringing the family, and blessed a household; he left taking no power with him, leaving no strings behind. Brothers and sisters, this is the rarest sense of place in a counsellor: helping without grasping power; perfecting others without controlling them. How many kind-hearted helpers, helping and helping, turn someone else's work into their own territory; Jethro helped and then left, leaving the stage entirely to Moses and the Israelites. He knew deeply that the one God had called to lead this people was Moses, not himself; his role was a passer-by who blesses, not a resident overlord. Churches and families both need such people: at the key moment, speak one right word, reach out a hand to steady someone — and then step back to one's own place, making room for the other person's growth. Willingness to come is love; willingness to leave is wisdom.

Finally, we must set this chapter in its larger scriptural context and make two honest points of positioning. These are statements at the level of fact, not homiletical elaboration. First, the question of sequence. The narrative of this chapter stands after the people's arrival at the mountain of God and before the giving of the Ten Commandments, and by the narrative order of Exodus the establishment of tiered judgment precedes the giving of the law. In Deuteronomy 1:9-18, Moses in his old age, looking back, also spoke of appointing heads to share the judging; that passage is his recollection many years later, and its scene echoes this chapter. The correspondence between the two passages in chronological sequence and in detail has long been discussed in scholarship: some understand them, following the narrative order, as two related arrangements, one before and one after; others hold that what the two passages record overlaps and differs in emphasis. This manuscript simply presents both honestly side by side: Deuteronomy is the retrospect, this chapter is the narrative of the time itself; how exactly to harmonise them is left to scholars, and the message of this chapter does not depend on that harmonisation to stand. Second, a forward look at the theme. This lesson of sharing a heavy load does not end with this chapter. In Numbers chapter 11, under still greater pressure, Moses cried out to God again: the burden of all this people is too heavy for me; I am not able to bear it myself alone. That time, God Himself took of the Spirit that was upon Moses and gave it to the seventy elders, so that they would bear the burden of the people with Moses. The same words — too heavy — and two responses: the first time, God gave the wisdom of tiered governance through the mouth of a father-in-law; the second time, God Himself, by distributing His Spirit, brought the sharing to completion. It may be said that Exodus chapter 18 is the starting point of this theme-line, and Numbers chapter 11 is its deepening: human arrangements need the Spirit of God to fill them, and the Spirit of God delights to work through sound arrangements. This is a homiletical exposition drawn by this manuscript from the intertextuality of the two passages, stated separately from the facts of the text, and offered for your examination and comparison.

Gather the application together, and I leave a reminder for three kinds of people. The first is for those who are forcing themselves to hold on: please learn from Moses, and first admit that it is too heavy. Today, list the things in your hands and divide them: which few are the representing and the teaching that no one else can do for you, and which can be handed over as small matters? Handing over is not shirking; it is perfecting others so that they bear the burden with you. The second is for those who love giving opinions: please learn from Jethro — tell of God first, bless first, and only then speak; when you speak, be a counsellor only, not a commander; leave the final decision to God and to the other person's prayer; when you have helped, step back to your own place, without grasping power and without controlling. The third is for those who hold the authority to choose people: please return to those four criteria — fearing God, men of truth, hating covetousness — together with the patience to observe with open eyes. Better to choose the right person slowly than to hand an office quickly to someone whose hands are not clean and whose words cannot be trusted. The wisdom of governance, in the end, does not lie in how beautifully the organisational chart is drawn, but in whether the person in every position fears God.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 18. Jethro heard what God had done, rejoiced and blessed, offered sacrifice and shared the table; he saw the weariness of Moses judging alone, said plainly that it was too heavy, and counselled a tiered order; Moses hearkened to his father-in-law's voice and chose God-fearing men to bear the burden with him; Jethro, his task complete, withdrew and returned to his own land. One chapter of Scripture, two admirable men: one who could speak, and one who was willing to listen; one who dared to speak plainly, and one who could be taught; one who perfected another, and one who laid down his dignity. And above it all is the God who gave wisdom through an outsider, and ordained that heavy burdens should have others to share them. May we remember the general account of this chapter: first tell of what God has done, then talk about human arrangements; and when a burden is too heavy, sharing it is not failure — it is the way out God has prepared long beforehand.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You for teaching us through a father-in-law. Forgive the pride with which we monopolise and force ourselves on, and heal our wearing, withering hearts. Teach us, like Jethro, first to hear of Your deeds, first to bless You, and then to help others with a sense of place; and teach us, like Moses, to be willing to hear a true word, willing to change our method, and willing to divide the burden out. Prepare for us fellow workers who fear You, are truthful, and hate covetousness, and make us ourselves such people, bearing the burden together with all, and walking the road ahead in peace. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee: for this thing is too heavy for thee; thou art not able to perform it thyself alone." (Exodus 18:18, KJV) "Moreover thou shalt provide out of all the people able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness; and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands, and rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens." (Exodus 18:21, KJV)

Some burdens crush us not because they should never be carried, but because we try to carry them entirely alone. Moses judged from morning to evening, doing nothing but good work, yet he was wearing away. His father-in-law Jethro woke him with one sentence: this thing is too heavy for you; you are not able to perform it yourself alone. Today God asks us through the same words: can that heavy thing in your hands be shared? Which part must you bring before God in prayer yourself, and teach yourself — and which part can be entrusted to God-fearing, truthful people with clean hands, to bear the burden with you? Admitting that it is too heavy is not surrender; it is how God's prepared help gets in. Lay the burden out, invite others in: you will be eased, they will have a share, and the work will travel farther. May we learn from Moses today — willing to hear one true word, and willing to let go of one thing we were never meant to carry alone. And if someone like Jethro, willing to tell you the truth, stands beside you, treasure him: hear him out first, then verify it before God. Many a help begins exactly with one unwelcome piece of plain speech. Loosen your hands a little, and your heart will be eased a little, and the road will be walked longer.

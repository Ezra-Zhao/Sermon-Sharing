# Keeping the Charge of the Lord — A Sermon on Numbers 9

- Text: Numbers 9:1-23
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending cross-review — initial translation, awaiting proofreading (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Numbers 9; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations; verses not checked word for word are given in summary only, never as quotations.
- Note on the title: The tentative directions — "Passover," "the second-month observance," "the pillar of cloud" — were each checked against the Chinese base text of Numbers 9 and set aside. The unbroken phrases "pillar of cloud" and "pillar of fire" do not occur in this chapter at all: the chapter speaks only of "the cloud" and of an appearance "as it were fire" (vv. 15, 16); the pillar wording is that of Exodus 13:21. The four-character phrase "second-month re-observance" likewise does not occur; the chapter says only that they shall keep the Passover "the fourteenth day of the second month" (v. 11). "Keep the Passover" occurs repeatedly but covers only the chapter's first half. The base text's closing unbroken wording, at verse 23 — in the Chinese, "they kept that which the Lord had commanded" — gathers the whole chapter, keeping the feast in its first half and following the cloud in its second. The KJV at verses 19 and 23 renders the same Hebrew charge-word as "kept the charge of the Lord," and the English title takes that KJV phrase: "Keeping the Charge of the Lord." See Endnote 2.
- Translator's independent check: In preparing this English manuscript, the Chinese manuscript's factual claims were re-checked independently against the KJV and the Hebrew text as carried on wd.bible (BHS), not carried over on trust. Four divergences between the two base texts were found and are recorded in Endnote 13 — most materially at verse 11, where the Chinese text expressly adds "and eat it with the Passover lamb," a phrase the KJV of that verse does not contain; this English manuscript follows the KJV there and flags the difference rather than importing the Chinese wording.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Chapter 9 has only twenty-three verses, yet it holds two great matters. Its first half (vv. 1-14) is the Passover: in the first month of the second year after the Exodus, the LORD commands Israel in the wilderness of Sinai to keep the Passover at its appointed season, and the people do so. But certain men, defiled by a dead body, cannot keep it on that day; instead of quietly absenting themselves, they come before Moses and Aaron and ask why they are kept back from offering an offering of the LORD in its appointed season among the children of Israel. Moses will not answer on his own authority: stand still, he says, and I will hear what the LORD will command concerning you. There follows one of the most consequential case-rulings in Israel's law: a man unclean by reason of a dead body, or in a journey afar off, may keep the Passover on the fourteenth day of the second month at even, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, leaving none of it unto the morning and breaking no bone of it; but the man who is clean and not in a journey, and forbears to keep it, shall be cut off from among his people and shall bear his sin; and a stranger who will keep the Passover does so under one and the same ordinance as the home-born. The second half (vv. 15-23) is the cloud: on the day the tabernacle was reared up, the cloud covered it, and by night there was upon it as it were the appearance of fire — so it was always. When the cloud was taken up, Israel journeyed; where the cloud abode, there they pitched — a few days, from evening to morning, two days, a month, a year — and they kept the charge of the LORD and journeyed not, until the cloud was taken up. This sermon reads the chapter in order: the appointed season and the timeline of the second year's first month; the defiled men's question and Moses' waiting; the second-month ruling item by item, with its penalty and its one ordinance; and the rhythm of the cloud's rising and abiding — one lesson throughout: to keep the feast is to keep what was commanded, and to follow the cloud is to keep what was commanded. Remembrance has a fixed date; the road has none; both are governed by commandment, not by ourselves.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 The appointed season: keeping the Passover in the first month of the second year, in the wilderness of Sinai

(Fact) Chapter 9 opens by reporting its time and place; in the KJV: "And the Lord spake unto Moses in the wilderness of Sinai, in the first month of the second year after they were come out of the land of Egypt, saying," (Num 9:1, KJV) The command follows at once: "Let the children of Israel also keep the passover at his appointed season." (Num 9:2, KJV) "In the fourteenth day of this month, at even, ye shall keep it in his appointed season: according to all the rites of it, and according to all the ceremonies thereof, shall ye keep it." (Num 9:3, KJV) In these two verses "appointed season" stands twice — verse 2 and verse 3 again. The Hebrew word behind it is môʿēd (Strong's H4150), an appointed time, a set season; its occurrences in this chapter checked for this sermon in the BHS text as carried on wd.bible are verses 2, 3, 7, and 13 — all four in the first half (a checked selection, not an exhaustive count). The "passover" itself is pesach (Strong's H6453); its checked occurrences include verses 2, 6, 11, 12, and 14. Keeping the feast, in this passage, is nailed fast to two pins: a set day, and a set manner — "according to all the rites of it, and according to all the ceremonies thereof." (Translator's note, recorded in full at Endnote 13: where the Chinese base text at verse 3 pairs the words "statutes and ordinances," the KJV pairs "rites" and "ceremonies"; each manuscript quotes its own base text, and neither wording is imported into the other.)

(Fact) The timeline must be set out side by side exactly as it stands, because between the date of chapter 9 and the date of chapter 1 there is a plain precedence that the preacher must not blur. Chapter 1 opens in the KJV: "And the Lord spake unto Moses in the wilderness of Sinai, in the tabernacle of the congregation, on the first day of the second month, in the second year after they were come out of the land of Egypt, saying," (Num 1:1, KJV) Set the two side by side: the events of chapter 9 belong to the first month of the second year (9:1), and its Passover falls on the fourteenth day of that month (9:3, 5); the census of chapter 1 belongs to the first day of the second month (Num 1:1). By the calendar, this chapter's Passover precedes chapter 1's census by about half a month; by the order of the scroll, chapter 9 stands after chapters 1-8. The narrative order of Numbers, that is, is not a strict calendar order: first come the census, the camp's arrangement, the cleansing of the camp, and the Levites' charges (chs. 1-8), and only then does the scroll turn back to record the keeping of the Passover in the first month. This sermon sets out the two dates, each in its own words and with its own figure, altering neither; why the material is so arranged is treated below only at the level of scholarly discussion, and is not decided here.

(Fact) The command reaches the people, and is carried out without ado. Moses speaks to the children of Israel that they should keep the Passover (v. 4, Summary), and the keeping stands in the KJV: "And they kept the passover on the fourteenth day of the first month at even in the wilderness of Sinai: according to all that the Lord commanded Moses, so did the children of Israel." (Num 9:5, KJV) Mark this verse against chapters 1-8: the tabernacle had only just been reared up, on the first day of the first month of the second year (Exod 40:17, Summary), and the offerings of chapter 7 and the Levites' induction of chapter 8 belong to that same season (Summary) — and with the tabernacle newly standing, the first great act is the Passover. One full year after the Exodus, in the same month and at the same evening hour, Israel keeps again what that first night began: then it was in the land of Egypt, house by house under the sign of blood, rising in haste to depart; now it is in the wilderness of Sinai, the camp in order and the tabernacle pitched, keeping it at leisure according to all its rites and ceremonies. The text settles the account in one sentence: "according to all that the Lord commanded Moses, so did the children of Israel." The feast's soundness lies not in the scale of its ceremony but in those words — so did they: the day, so; the rites, so; the ceremonies, so.

(Application) The words "appointed season" are this chapter's first lesson to every act of remembrance. Men remembering great things easily fall to one of two extremes: remembrance by impulse — we remember when we happen to feel like it, and the day is set by mood; or remembrance by custom — we keep the day everyone else keeps, and the day is set by surroundings. The Passover is neither. It has an appointed season — the fourteenth day of the first month, at even, year by year, kept when the day comes. Why does God nail remembrance to a date? Because forgetfulness is man's constant state: a deliverance as great as the Exodus can fade within a year into a mere story. The date is the nail that fixes remembrance to the calendar, so that year by year, when the point comes round, it is rehearsed again, and is not left to drift with feeling. And more: the date is not a good day Israel picked for itself; it is the LORD's appointment — the initiative in remembrance lies with the One who showed the grace, not with those who received it. We keep the feast not because we happen to be moved that day, but because He commanded that day to be kept. Much of the collapse of a life of faith collapses not over great matters but over dates: prayer with no fixed day, Scripture with no fixed hour, assembly left to mood — and when the mood sinks, everything stops together. This chapter sets its rule on the date first: fix the date, and then speak of feeling; keep the date, and the remembrance can be kept.

(Application) Notice, too, who keeps this feast: all Israel — not the priests only, and not the Levites only. Chapters 1-8 have spoken much of men in special office — priests offering, Levites bearing, tribes encamping each under his standard. At the Passover there is no special office: the whole people keep it together. For the Passover remembers the whole people's Exodus: on that night it was every house's firstborn who was sheltered under the blood, not one house's only; and the feast kept again this year belongs, therefore, to every house. Worship's order has its divisions of labour; grace's remembrance has no outsiders. That line lies buried deep in this chapter's first half, and everything that follows grows out of it — the defiled men's question, and the stranger's one ordinance alike: because this is the whole people's feast, anyone missing from it makes the feast incomplete; and the reason the later ruling opens a door in the second month for a handful of men is precisely that this feast was never allowed to lose anyone silently.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) This precedence of chapter 9's date over chapter 1's has long been noticed by commentators, who have offered more than one reading: some hold that Numbers is arranged by theme rather than by calendar — first the camp's order (census, encampment, cleansing, charges), then the ordinances of worship and journey, with the Passover and the cloud forming a combined account before the departure, reaching back to the first month; some, that the command of 9:1 and the census of 1:1 belong to one and the same season, and that the editor deliberately places the Passover after the Levites' induction and before the setting forward of chapter 10, so that keeping the feast, following the cloud, and setting forward form one narrative movement; some, that this is the familiar retrospective arrangement of ancient editing, and no strict chronology need be forced upon it. This sermon sets out the existence and drift of these discussions only, and decides for none of them; nor does its argument rest on the arrangement. The two dates are each clear in themselves: 9:1 is the first month of the second year; Num 1:1 is the first day of the second month; and this sermon preaches each passage from its own words.

### II. 08:00-24:00 The men defiled by a dead body: why are we kept back — and Moses waiting

(Fact) The feast is kept in order, and the problem surfaces from the least expected quarter. Verse 6 stands in the KJV: "And there were certain men, who were defiled by the dead body of a man, that they could not keep the passover on that day: and they came before Moses and before Aaron on that day:" (Num 9:6, KJV) In the Hebrew phrase rendered "defiled by the dead body," "defiled" is a plural form of ṭāmēʾ (Strong's H2931), and "dead body" joins nepeš (Strong's H5315) with "man" — literally, the corpse of a man; the two words stand together at verses 6, 7, and 10, checked verse by verse in the BHS for this sermon (a selection, not an exhaustive list). Under the rules given elsewhere in this book, a man who touched a dead body was unclean seven days and had to cleanse himself on the third and seventh days, failing which he could not approach the sanctuary (the cleansing rules of Numbers 19 belong later in the book and are noted here in Summary only, by way of reference, without reading their verse numbers back into this chapter; chapter 9 itself says only that these men "could not keep the passover on that day"). Why these men had become defiled — whether burying kin, or carrying a dead companion — this chapter nowhere records; and this sermon keeps the text's silence, and invents no history for them.

(Fact) These men's response is the backbone of the passage. Their question stands in the KJV: "And those men said unto him, We are defiled by the dead body of a man: wherefore are we kept back, that we may not offer an offering of the Lord in his appointed season among the children of Israel?" (Num 9:7, KJV) Read their words layer by layer; there are three. First, they own their condition at the outset: "We are defiled" — they do not dispute the fact of their defilement, and they do not ask to be reckoned clean by exception. Second, what they ask is, "wherefore are we kept back?" — what keeps them back is the ordinance itself, and their question is not "can an exception be made for us?" but "what, in the end, is this ordinance for?" Can the ordinance for keeping the feast really mean to shut the men who long to keep it outside the feast? Third, what they want is not exemption but participation: "that we may not offer an offering of the Lord in his appointed season among the children of Israel" — the weight of the whole question lies in "among the children of Israel." They do not ask for a private feast of their own, kept apart; they ask to be back inside the congregation, offering alongside everyone else. And set their case steadily against its background: these are men defiled precisely by having done their duty to the dead — burying the dead is love and righteousness, yet it has cost them their seat at the feast in point of ritual. The text speaks not one word of blame against them; it simply records their question as they asked it.

(Fact) Moses' answer stands in the KJV: "And Moses said unto them, Stand still, and I will hear what the Lord will command concerning you." (Num 9:8, KJV) The verse is short, and must be read word by word. "Stand still" — Moses neither pronounces judgment on the spot nor comforts them on the spot; he first bids them wait. "I will hear what the Lord will command concerning you" — Moses holds in his hand the whole body of the feast's rites and ceremonies (v. 3); no man knows the Passover ordinances better than he. But among those ordinances there is no such article: whether a man defiled by a dead body may keep the feast, and how, cannot be found in the existing text. Moses does not legislate on the spot by virtue of his leadership, does not reason by analogy from experience, and does not make an exception out of a kind heart — he goes to hear. A leader's authority shows its most important face exactly here: not that he can answer everything, but that he knows which question he must not answer himself. Moses carries the question, exactly as asked, before the LORD, and waits for His command — and that waiting is no delay; it puts the source of the ruling back where it belongs.

(Application) These defiled men preach a lesson to every absentee. They might lawfully not have come at all: defilement was an express ground in the text, their absence an absence the law allowed, and no one could have blamed them — they might even have comforted themselves with the ground: it is not that I will not keep it; the ordinance will not let me. But they came, and they asked. In their hearts they could not put the feast down: everyone else is offering, and why am I standing outside the camp? This refusal to be content with absence is a precious thing in a life of faith. Many a man's drifting away from assembly, from the feast, from prayer begins not in rebellion but in a lawful ground — busy, ill, far away, defiled by his circumstances. Used long enough, the ground becomes a habit; and habit, long enough, makes a man truly a man outside the camp. These men would not let a lawful ground ripen into a permanent absence: they brought the ground with them, came before Moses, and asked the question out loud — and asking is itself the evidence of refusing to give up. God's later answer is as rich as it is precisely because someone was willing to ask: the door of the second month was opened first for these few men who came and asked, and only afterwards written down as a ruling for all generations. If you stand outside the camp today, the first thing to learn is not self-reproach but asking: bring your condition, exactly as it is, before God, and ask Him — His answer is very often wider than you expect.

(Application) Moses' waiting preaches a second lesson, to everyone who must decide. Had Moses wished to display his competence, he could have ruled on the spot — permit, or forbid — and either answer could have been dressed in plausible reasons: to permit is compassion; to forbid is fidelity to the ordinance. He chooses neither; he chooses to wait. Why? Because he knows this is not a choice between compassion and fidelity; it is a question the ordinance itself has not finished answering — and where God's command has not finished speaking, man's best answer is, "Stand still, and I will hear." How many disputes in church and household arise from someone snatching at the right to answer for God: wrapping his own judgment up as God's command, calling his own indulgence God's grace, and his own severity God's holiness. Moses' example is cold and clear: what you have not heard, say you have not heard; what must be waited for, bid everyone wait for together. Through those days of waiting, these men's case hung unresolved, and Moses' authority hung with it — and it is exactly that willingness to leave the matter honestly hanging that makes the command, when it comes, a ruling for the whole congregation for all generations, and not one personal disposition of Moses. The man who can say "I will hear" is the man fit to relay the words, "Thus the LORD commands."

### III. 24:00-42:00 The fourteenth day of the second month: the ruling for keeping it later, the penalty of being cut off, and one ordinance for the stranger

(Fact) The command comes, and first fixes the ruling's scope: "And the Lord spake unto Moses, saying," (Num 9:9, KJV) "Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, If any man of you or of your posterity shall be unclean by reason of a dead body, or be in a journey afar off, yet he shall keep the passover unto the Lord." (Num 9:10, KJV) Read the ruling's boundaries item by item. First, its subjects are widened: not these few men only, but "any man of you or of your posterity" — this is no one-time pardon; it is an ordinance laid down for descendants. Second, its cases are two: being "unclean by reason of a dead body," which is these men's case; and being "in a journey afar off" — a man away from the camp who cannot get back in time, a different case from the defiled men's, yet gathered onto the same road of later keeping. This sermon states the fact exactly: the journey case does not appear in the question of verses 6-7; it is added when the command itself comes at verse 10 — the text is reported as it is written, and the widening is not credited to the questioners. Third, the verb does not change: such a man "shall keep the passover" — the later keeping is still a keeping, not a release. The door God opens is a door of keeping it late, not a door of being excused from keeping it at all.

(Fact) The date and manner of the later keeping stand in the KJV: "The fourteenth day of the second month at even they shall keep it, and eat it with unleavened bread and bitter herbs." (Num 9:11, KJV) The date is the fourteenth day of the second month, at even — exactly one month behind the fourteenth day of the first month, at even (vv. 3, 5), the day and the hour differing not a hair: the same fourteenth, the same evening. (Independent check, flagged here as Endnote 13 records: the Chinese base text of this verse expressly adds a third item to the eating — "and eat it together with the Passover lamb." The KJV of verse 11 contains no such phrase; its "it" is the Passover itself, carried from verse 10. This English manuscript follows the KJV and does not import the Chinese addition; the Chinese manuscript, which quotes its own base text, contains it there as a matter of course. The difference belongs to the two base texts, and neither manuscript corrects the other by it.) The items as the KJV gives them are unleavened bread and bitter herbs: the Hebrew words, checked in the BHS at this verse, are maṣṣôt (Strong's H4682) for the unleavened bread and mərōrîm (Strong's H4844) for the bitter herbs, standing together in the one verse. Unleavened, remembering that night's haste in departing, when there was no time for dough to rise; bitter, remembering the bitterness of slavery in Egypt — for the man who keeps it late, neither act of remembrance is abridged: a month late, and the content of the remembrance is not reduced by one fraction. Verse 12 follows in the KJV: "They shall leave none of it unto the morning, nor break any bone of it: according to all the ordinances of the passover they shall keep it." (Num 9:12, KJV) Three rules, set out item by item. First, "leave none of it unto the morning" — the lamb is eaten that night, with nothing kept over. Second, "nor break any bone of it" — the Hebrew word for the bone is ʿeṣem (Strong's H6106), standing in this verse, checked for this sermon; this rule already stood in the original Exodus ordinance (Exod 12:46, Summary), and here, for the later keeping, it is restated without yielding a word. Third, the governing sentence: "according to all the ordinances of the passover they shall keep it" — the later keeping is no abridged edition and no makeshift edition: apart from the date's moving back one month, every ordinance stands as before. The Hebrew word rendered "ordinances" belongs to the ḥuqqâ family (Strong's H2708); its checked occurrences in this chapter's first half are verses 3, 12, and 14 (a selection, not an exhaustive list) — the first month is kept by its rites and ceremonies (v. 3), the second-month keeping by all its ordinances (v. 12), and the stranger by the same ordinance (v. 14): one measure, laid across the whole first half of the chapter.

(Fact) The ruling's other face is its penalty, in the KJV: "But the man that is clean, and is not in a journey, and forbeareth to keep the passover, even the same soul shall be cut off from among his people: because he brought not the offering of the Lord in his appointed season, that man shall bear his sin." (Num 9:13, KJV) This verse must be read against verses 10-12 before its boundary can be seen. The man of verse 10 could not keep it — defilement, or a distant journey, stood in his way by circumstance, and God opens the second month's door for him. The man of verse 13 can keep it and does not: he is "clean, and is not in a journey" — neither hindrance is his — and yet he "forbeareth." Forbearing is not inability; it is a choice. Both men are alike absent from the feast on the fourteenth day of the first month, and one receives the grace of a later keeping while the other falls under the penalty of being cut off — and the difference lies not in the fact of absence but in its cause: one could not; the other would not. The penalty's content is stated from the text only: he "shall be cut off from among his people," removed from the congregation; and "that man shall bear his sin," the guilt remaining his own to carry. This sermon states those two sentences as the text gives them and does not expand the manner of the cutting off's execution, on which this passage itself does not elaborate.

(Fact) The ruling's last verse opens the door one inch wider still, in the KJV: "And if a stranger shall sojourn among you, and will keep the passover unto the Lord; according to the ordinance of the passover, and according to the manner thereof, so shall he do: ye shall have one ordinance, both for the stranger, and for him that was born in the land." (Num 9:14, KJV) The "stranger" is the Hebrew gēr (Strong's H1616) — a sojourner, not of Israel's house, yet dwelling among Israel. Such a man, if he "will keep" the feast, is received how? Not at a separate table, and not at a lower grade: "ye shall have one ordinance" — the same ordinance and the same manner: the same date, in its season, and the same manner of eating. The stranger's door at the Passover has a root already in Exodus (Exod 12:48-49, Summary: the sojourner, circumcised, may keep it, under one law for the home-born and the stranger alike), and this chapter restates it, setting it alongside the ruling for later keeping. Read verses 10-14 together, and the ruling's shape is complete: for the man who cannot keep it, a road of keeping it later (vv. 10-12); for the man who can and will not, the penalty of being cut off (v. 13); for the man outside the bloodline who is willing, one and the same ordinance (v. 14). Grace, righteousness, and openness — all three present, none missing, in five verses.

(Application) The fourteenth day of the second month is one of the most tender dates in Scripture, and deserves to be preached slowly. God might have said: a feast missed is a feast missed; let the unclean man wait for next year — the law could have been that strict, and strict beyond reproach. He did not say it. For the absent He appoints a whole second month — not any day after the feast when a man happens to remember, but the fourteenth day of the second month, at even: a fixed day, a fixed hour, a fixed manner of eating, and fixed ordinances, as solemn in every particular as the first month. That tells us: God's grace is never slipshod. The door He opens for later keeping is built as carefully as the front door — because in His sight, worship kept late is still worship, and a feast kept in arrears is still a feast, and neither deserves a makeshift rite to fob it off. And precisely because He opens it so solemnly, the later keeping can never become indulgence's excuse: the door of the second month is opened for the man who could not, not for the man who would not. Verse 13's cutting off stands one verse away from verse 11's later keeping; grace and penalty stand that close together, to teach a man to tell the two apart: hindrance of circumstance, God bears with; refusal of the heart, God calls to account. Many a man mistakes forbearance for permission, and the second month for the standing rule — putting off year by year, always reckoning that there will be a chance to make it up. The text speaks first: the road of later keeping is walked only by those who cannot walk the first road; for those who can walk it and do not, what waits is not the second month but being cut off.

(Application) One ordinance for the stranger is this passage's final light. The Passover is Israel's most intimate remembrance — it remembers "our fathers" and "our Exodus" — and by man's reckoning such a feast, of all feasts, ought to be kept behind closed doors. The ordinance says the opposite: the sojourning stranger who will keep it, let him keep it, under one ordinance with the home-born. The moment the remembrance of grace becomes a patent of blood, the remembrance dies: it turns from thanksgiving into boasting, and from witness into a wall. Here, at the second Passover kept by the first generation, God takes that wall down in advance: the feast's centre is not "who we are" but "what the LORD has done" — and whoever will may come in, eat the same unleavened bread and bitter herbs, and keep the same ordinances. Every remembrance the church keeps today must stand this verse's examination: is our door open to the one who will? Is our one ordinance truly one — or is it one measure inside and another outside? Only when the stranger can sit at the same table is the feast alive.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The cloud covering, fire by night: taken up, and they journeyed; abiding, and they pitched

(Fact) The chapter's second half changes scene: from the feast to the journey, from dates to the cloud. Verse 15 stands in the KJV: "And on the day that the tabernacle was reared up the cloud covered the tabernacle, namely, the tent of the testimony: and at even there was upon the tabernacle as it were the appearance of fire, until the morning." (Num 9:15, KJV) Read it item by item. First, its starting point is "the day that the tabernacle was reared up" — the tabernacle reared up on the first day of the first month of the second year (Exod 40:17, Summary), and the cloud covering it that same day: it is not that the tabernacle stood a long while before the cloud arrived; the tabernacle is reared, and the cloud is there — God's presence and His dwelling arrive on one day. Second, the object the cloud covers is named with care: "the tabernacle, namely, the tent of the testimony" — the tent of the testimony is the tabernacle's other name (the sense given in Summary), and where the cloud rests is exactly where the testimony is. Third, its two appearances, by day and by night: the covering cloud is the day appearance; "as it were the appearance of fire, until the morning" is the night appearance — one and the same Presence, a cloud to look at by day, and as fire by night. The Hebrew for "cloud" is heʿānān (Strong's H6051), its checked occurrences in this chapter including verses 15, 17, 19, and 22 (a selection, not an exhaustive list); the word for "fire" is ʾēš (Strong's H784), standing at verse 15 in the construct phrase "the appearance of fire," checked for this sermon. Verse 16 makes the day-and-night pattern a standing rule, in the KJV: "So it was alway: the cloud covered it by day, and the appearance of fire by night." (Num 9:16, KJV) (Independent check: the KJV of this verse states "by day" expressly, where the Chinese base text of the same verse carries no separate word for daytime and lets the covering cloud itself imply the day; see Endnote 13. Each manuscript quotes its own base text.) "So it was alway" takes what verse 15 records of one day and spreads it over all the days of the wilderness: not on the first day only, but every day — the cloud never absent, the fire never out.

(Fact) The cloud's relation to moving and resting stands in the KJV: "And when the cloud was taken up from the tabernacle, then after that the children of Israel journeyed: and in the place where the cloud abode, there the children of Israel pitched their tents." (Num 9:17, KJV) The verse is a rule exact as a mechanism: taken up — they journeyed; abiding — they pitched. There is no third option: for the people to move while the cloud abides will not do; for the people to linger when the cloud is taken up will not do. Verse 18 follows in the KJV: "At the commandment of the Lord the children of Israel journeyed, and at the commandment of the Lord they pitched: as long as the cloud abode upon the tabernacle they rested in their tents." (Num 9:18, KJV) Mark this verse's attribution: outwardly the people watch the cloud, yet the text says they move and rest "at the commandment of the Lord" — the cloud is the commandment's visible shape, and the commandment's source is the LORD, not the cloud itself. As long as the cloud abode, so long they rested: the decision over duration lies neither with the people nor with the cloud, but with the One who commands the cloud.

(Fact) In verses 19-22 the text then draws out the lengths of abiding, one grade at a time; this sermon copies them item by item from the KJV, each checked, and lets none of them be carried in a blur. The first grade, many days, in the KJV: "And when the cloud tarried long upon the tabernacle many days, then the children of Israel kept the charge of the Lord, and journeyed not." (Num 9:19, KJV) — when the tarrying stretches long, the trial arrives with it: they "kept the charge of the Lord, and journeyed not" — not journeying, too, is a kind of keeping: keeping the "stay" inside the commandment. The Hebrew word rendered "charge" here is mišmereṯ (Strong's H4931), standing at verses 19 and 23, checked verse by verse in the BHS for this sermon (see Endnote 9). The second grade, a few days, in the KJV: "And so it was, when the cloud was a few days upon the tabernacle; according to the commandment of the Lord they abode in their tents, and according to the commandment of the Lord they journeyed." (Num 9:20, KJV) The third grade, a single night, in the KJV: "And so it was, when the cloud abode from even unto the morning, and that the cloud was taken up in the morning, then they journeyed: whether it was by day or by night that the cloud was taken up, they journeyed." (Num 9:21, KJV) — camp pitched for one night only, and at dawn the cloud is taken up, and the camp must move again: a stay of less than a full day, and still they journey. The fourth grade, two days, a month, a year, in the KJV: "Or whether it were two days, or a month, or a year, that the cloud tarried upon the tabernacle, remaining thereon, the children of Israel abode in their tents, and journeyed not: but when it was taken up, they journeyed." (Num 9:22, KJV) Set the four grades side by side: many days; a few days; from evening to morning; two days, or a month, or a year — the text lays out both extremes of length: as short as one night, as long as a year; and whatever the length, the rule is one only: while the cloud is there, the people rest; when the cloud is taken up, the people journey. In preparing this sermon the time-expressions of verses 19-22 were counted individually: five occurrences in all — "many days" at verse 19, "a few days" at verse 20, "from even unto the morning" and "by day or by night" at verse 21 (two occurrences within the one verse), and "two days, or a month, or a year" at verse 22; the count and its method are stated here in the open, in place of any vague "many times."

(Application) The cloud's moving and resting preach this chapter's deepest lesson to everyone who must "wait" and everyone who must "go." The hard thing is neither going nor staying; the hard thing is that neither going nor staying is ours to choose. When the cloud tarries a year, a man's heart grows weeds: every road round about has been scouted, the tent is lived old, and the promised land still lies at the sky's edge — and in that year the greatest trial is no outward enemy but weariness: the sense that the days have stalled, that God has forgotten us, that we ought to make some movement of our own. But the text says they "kept the charge of the Lord, and journeyed not" — keeping the stay itself as a charge: in the staying, pitch the camp firmly, keep the feasts well, teach the children, and live the year as a year of preparation rather than a year of complaint. When, on the other hand, the cloud is taken up after a single night, the hard thing is letting go: the camp only just pitched, the water only just drawn, the first whole sleep only just slept — and at dawn the cloud rises, and the camp must move. In that night the greatest trial is lingering: wait a little, watch a little, see whether the cloud settles back. But the rule allows no bargaining: taken up, they journeyed. Staying has its lesson and going has its lesson, and the two lessons share one root: the sovereignty is not mine. Most of life's anxiety comes from wanting to set the cloud's timetable ourselves — begging it to move quickly while it stays, and to move slowly while it goes. This chapter takes the timetable out of man's hand, and leaves him one thing only: eyes to watch the cloud, and feet to follow it — while the cloud is there, rest in peace; when the cloud is taken up, journey at once; and the rest belongs to the One who commands the cloud.

(Application) Notice further: the cloud rests on the tabernacle, not on each man's tent. When the people watch the cloud, they are not watching the sky over their own doors; they are watching the movement of the One at the congregation's centre — the tent of the testimony. That is why the whole camp can rise and rest together: there is one signal only, and one centre only. If each man watched his own cloud, this house's rising today and that house's not rising, the camp would be in chaos in a moment. The journeying of church and household is the same: the thing most to be feared is not slowness but a multitude of signals — each man going by his own feelings, his own news, his own little cloud — until people of one camp can no longer walk together. This chapter's order is quiet and firm: one God, one tabernacle, one cloud, one commandment — and what all the people keep is one and the same commandment. Draw the eyes back from our several little skies, and fix them on His tabernacle, and unity has its foundation, and moving and resting their agreement.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: keeping the charge of the Lord — pitching and journeying alike at His commandment

(Fact) The whole chapter is sealed at verse 23, in the KJV: "At the commandment of the Lord they rested in the tents, and at the commandment of the Lord they journeyed: they kept the charge of the Lord, at the commandment of the Lord by the hand of Moses." (Num 9:23, KJV) This verse is the chapter's seal, and this sermon's English title is taken from it. Read it sentence by sentence: resting, at the commandment; journeying, also at the commandment — the whole cloud narrative of the second half is here gathered into the word "commandment": the cloud is its outward shape, the commandment its substance. The last clause, "at the commandment of the Lord by the hand of Moses," gives the commandment's order of transmission: the LORD commands by the hand of Moses, Moses delivers it to the people, and the people keep it — the same structure as the first half's ruling: the people ask (v. 7), Moses waits and goes to hear (v. 8), the LORD speaks (v. 9), Moses declares it (v. 10), and the people do it. The chapter's two halves run on one line: commandment descending from above, obedience rising from below, and between them a Moses who will wait, and who will relay what he hears unaltered. The whole chapter can now be gathered in three lines. First, remembrance keeps its date: the Passover is kept in its appointed season (vv. 2, 3, 5), the fourteenth day of the first month at even, year by year, its rites and ceremonies lacking nothing. Second, absence has its later keeping: the man who cannot keep it keeps it on the fourteenth day of the second month at even (vv. 10-12); the man who can and will not is cut off from among his people (v. 13); the stranger who will keep it stands under one ordinance (v. 14). Third, the road follows the cloud: taken up, they journeyed; abiding, they pitched (vv. 17, 18); for two days, a month, a year, all at the commandment (vv. 19-22); and the whole is gathered up in "they kept the charge of the Lord" (v. 23). The date is fixed, and the journey is not fixed — what is fixed, man must not alter; what is not fixed, man must not fix for himself; both are governed by the commandment. That is chapter 9.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, keep exactly: the Passover's date, its manner of eating, and its ordinances are appointed by God in plain words — faith's first lesson is not ardour but exactness: the appointed day is that day, not a bone is to be broken, none of it is to be left unto the morning; to hear the commandment accurately and keep it accurately is worship's foundation. Second, be able to wait: Moses could say "Stand still," and the people could abide a full year beneath the cloud — waiting in this chapter is no passive waste, but an active keeping: keeping silence until one has heard clearly, and keeping the camp unmoved until the cloud is taken up. Third, be able to follow at once: when the cloud is taken up in the morning, journey in the morning; when the ruling arrives, keep it in the second month — when the commandment comes, the movement comes with it, without delay and without discount. May our whole lives have remembrance with its date, and a road that follows the cloud: the feast that must be kept, never missing a year; the road that must be walked, never lingering a step; and the years that must be stayed, stayed quietly keeping the charge, until the day the cloud is taken up.

(New Testament reading) Two New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter's first half; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. The first is 1 Corinthians 5: there it is said, "For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us:" (1 Cor 5:7, KJV), and believers are exhorted to keep the feast with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth (1 Cor 5:8, Summary) — the lamb of this chapter, still to be kept a month late, with its unleavened bread and bitter herbs, points in the New Testament reading to the One Passover already sacrificed: on one side, a lamb kept year by year, and made up a month late; on the other, a Lamb offered once, never to be lacking again. The second is John 19: at the Lord's suffering, the soldiers did not break His legs, and the Gospel records that these things were done that the Scripture should be fulfilled, "A bone of him shall not be broken." (John 19:36, KJV) — the inconspicuous rule of verse 12 of this chapter, "nor break any bone of it," answers in the New Testament reading to that scene at the cross: the bone that those who kept the feast were careful, year by year, not to break found its fulfilment, later, in the true Lamb. This sermon states these two New Testament passages' own records, and their correspondence, and does not read the sense of either back into the other. (Application) But the layers must be kept straight: Numbers 9 is first of all itself — a feast on the fourteenth day of the first month at even, a ruling for the fourteenth day of the second, and a cloud over the tabernacle that is cloud by day and fire by night. The New Testament reading is the light in which the church, in Christ, looks back on it; it must not be used to dissolve this chapter's rules. The more solid this chapter's rules — its dates checkable verse by verse, its ordinances countable item by item, its lengths of tarrying quotable grade by grade — the more weight the reading layer's correspondence carries: the door of the second month, opened in that day for absentees, shows how willing the Lord who opens doors is that all men should keep this feast together; and He Himself is the Lamb this feast remembers.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's own hands. You may be standing where those defiled men stood: a season of your life has cost you your absence — the feast has passed, the camp has moved, and you fear you have been left behind for good. This chapter says to you: come and ask; do not slip quietly away. For the man who could not keep it, God has kept the fourteenth day of the second month; a keeping that comes late is still a keeping. You may be standing beneath the cloud: the days have stayed a long while — a year has passed, and the camp is still in the same place, and you wonder whether the staying is a being-forgotten. This chapter says to you: staying, too, is a commandment; to keep the charge and journey not is itself a keeping of the charge of the Lord; in the days while the cloud is not taken up, pitch the camp firmly and keep the feast well. Or you may be hearing the cloud taken up in the morning: only just settled, and the camp must move again, and your heart cannot bear to leave. This chapter says to you: taken up — then journey; the commandment's substance was never our comfort, but our walking with Him. May each of us, in the feast, in the camp, and on the road, be a keeper of the charge of the Lord: when the feast comes, keep it; when the cloud abides, rest; when the cloud is taken up, journey — all our lives at His commandment, and not at our own.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): What touches me most in Numbers 9 is the men defiled by a dead body. They had the most lawful of reasons to be absent — the ordinance itself barred them, and no one could have blamed them for staying away. Yet they came, and they asked: wherefore are we kept back, that we may not offer an offering of the Lord in his appointed season among the children of Israel? In their question I see how rich God's answer can be: the fourteenth day of the second month at even, the same passover, unleavened bread and bitter herbs, none of it to be left unto the morning, nor any bone of it broken — a feast kept a month late, and not one whit less solemn than the first month. With God, to be late is not to be shut out; a hindrance of circumstance is not a rejection of the heart; for those who could not keep it, He appointed a whole second month. The other half of the chapter touches me in the cloud: when it tarried a year, Israel abode in their tents a year; when it was taken up in the morning, they journeyed in the morning. What I fear most is exactly this not-being-my-own-master: the staying seems too long, the going too sudden. But the text says they kept the charge of the Lord and journeyed not, and at the commandment of the Lord they journeyed — staying and going were both a keeping. So I pray today: Lord, for the feasts I have missed, bring me back through the door of the second month; for the seasons when I am stayed, make me able to keep the days of not journeying, without haste and without complaint; and when the cloud is taken up, make me able to follow, without lingering and without delay. All my life, at Thy commandment: when the feast comes, to keep it; when the cloud abides, to rest; when the cloud is taken up, to journey.

2. Title: the tentative directions fail the base text or fail the whole chapter, for three reasons. First, the unbroken phrases "pillar of cloud" and "pillar of fire" occur in no verse of Numbers 9 in either base text: the chapter's word for the cloud is simply "the cloud" (vv. 15, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23), and for the night appearance, "as it were the appearance of fire" (vv. 15, 16); the pillar wording belongs to Exodus 13:21 (KJV: "the Lord went before them by day in a pillar of a cloud... and by night in a pillar of fire"), a verse checked for this sermon and cited here in Summary for comparison only. To title this chapter with Exodus' wording would be to crown one book's chapter with another book's phrase. Second, the phrase "second-month re-observance" as a set phrase occurs nowhere in the chapter; the text's wording is a date and an act: "The fourteenth day of the second month at even they shall keep it" (v. 11). Third, "keep the Passover," though it recurs (vv. 2, 5, 11 and elsewhere), covers only the first half (vv. 1-14) and not the cloud. The closing verse's wording — "they kept the charge of the Lord" (v. 23, KJV; the same phrase stands at v. 19) — is both the base text's own unbroken wording and a wording that gathers the whole chapter: keeping the feast in the first half is a keeping of the charge (v. 5, "according to all that the Lord commanded Moses, so did the children of Israel"), and following the cloud in the second half is the same keeping (v. 19). The Chinese title takes the Chinese base text's closing unbroken phrase; the English title takes the KJV's rendering of the same Hebrew charge-word at the same verses.
3. The textual basis of the side-by-side timeline: the first month of the second year — Num 9:1 (quoted); the Passover on its fourteenth day at even — 9:3, 9:5 (quoted); the census on the first day of the second month — Num 1:1 (quoted). The difference: the keeping recorded in chapter 9 falls on the fourteenth day of the first month, the census recorded in chapter 1 on the first day of the second month; by the calendar the keeping precedes the census by about half a month (the exact interval between the fourteenth of the first month and the first of the second depends on the first month's length, and this sermon states "about half a month" as an approximation only, making no exact day-count claim); by the scroll's order, chapter 9 stands after chapter 1. Section I sets the two out side by side; the three kinds of reading kept at the (Scholarly and historical discussion) level there — thematic arrangement, narrative linkage, and retrospect — are given in drift only, and this sermon decides for none of them.

Subjects: "any man of you or of your posterity" (v. 10), not the questioners of that day only.

## Summary

The land east of the Jordan has just been divided (chapter 32, previous-text layer), yet Moses does not look forward at once; he first looks back and writes down, station by station, the road travelled over forty years. The chapter states its own origin at its head: "And Moses wrote their goings out according to their journeys by the commandment of the LORD: and these are their journeys according to their goings out." (33:2) In the more than forty verses that follow, almost everything is in one and the same sentence-form: they departed from one place, and pitched in another. From Rameses, through Succoth, Etham, and Pi-hahiroth, across the sea to Marah, Elim, the Red sea, the wilderness of Sin, Dophkah, Alush, and Rephidim, to the wilderness of Sinai (33:5-15); then through Kibroth-hattaavah and Hazeroth, station by station northward, through a long string of camps of which only the names remain, to Ezion-gaber and Kadesh (33:16-36); then to mount Hor — and here the list halts and records the chapter's only death notice with a complete date: "And Aaron the priest went up into mount Hor at the commandment of the LORD, and died there, in the fortieth year after the children of Israel were come out of the land of Egypt, in the first day of the fifth month." (33:38) Aaron was a hundred and twenty-three years old when he died (33:39). After that, through Zalmonah, Punon, Oboth, Ije-abarim, Dibon-gad, Almon-diblathaim, and the mountains of Abarim, to the plains of Moab by Jordan near Jericho (33:41-48), the camp's two ends being Beth-jesimoth and Abel-shittim (33:49). There the list closes, and the voice turns sharply toward the future: the LORD speaks to Moses in the same place — when they pass over Jordan into the land of Canaan, "Then ye shall drive out all the inhabitants of the land from before you, and destroy all their pictures, and destroy all their molten images, and quite pluck down all their high places:" (33:52) The land is to be inherited by lot (33:54); "But if ye will not drive out the inhabitants of the land from before you; then it shall come to pass, that those which ye let remain of them shall be pricks in your eyes, and thorns in your sides, and shall vex you in the land wherein ye dwell." (33:55) The first half of this chapter is a ledger, the second half a commandment: only when the road behind has been recorded clearly is a man fit to hear the word for the road ahead.

## Main Text

### Part One: A Record Made by Commandment — the Journeys Are Set Down Below (33:1-4) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) Verse 1, the general heading, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "These are the journeys of the children of Israel, which went forth out of the land of Egypt with their armies under the hand of Moses and Aaron." (33:1) Registered item by item: first, the chapter's genre declares itself in its first verse — what is recorded is "the journeys"; the Chinese base text carries a parenthetical alternative, "or, stations," the two renderings standing side by side in that manuscript, and the counting basis of this sermon is set out under the two reckonings in Endnote 4; second, the character of the company is "with their armies" — the exodus was no scattered wandering but a march in formed companies; third, the leadership is "under the hand of Moses and Aaron," the two names side by side, recorded as they stand. Verse 2, the origin of the record, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And Moses wrote their goings out according to their journeys by the commandment of the LORD: and these are their journeys according to their goings out." (33:2) Registered: the record is not Moses' spontaneous recollection in old age; it was written by commandment; and the object recorded is "their goings out" — the subject is the whole camp, not Moses' road alone. Verse 3, the date and posture of the departure, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And they departed from Rameses in the first month, on the fifteenth day of the first month; on the morrow after the passover the children of Israel went out with an high hand in the sight of all the Egyptians." (33:3) Registered: the date is exact to the day — the fifteenth day of the first month, the morrow after the passover; the starting point is Rameses; the posture is "with an high hand," and that in the sight of all the Egyptians, recorded as it stands. Verse 4, the background of the day of departure, registered in its main points: at that time the Egyptians were burying all their firstborn, which the LORD had smitten among them, and upon their gods also the LORD executed judgments — departure and burial set side by side on the same day; this is the first background note in the list, and one of the few in the whole chapter; for its details the Exodus account itself governs (previous-text layer), and this chapter takes only its outline.

(Argument) This is the chapter most easily skipped, and the one that least should be. If the forty years' road is not recorded, what the next generation hears is only a few great stories: the crossing of the Red sea, Mount Sinai, the spies' evil report. What Moses did by commandment was to write down also the days between the great stories that have no story — Dophkah, Alush, Rissah, Kehelathah: what happened behind those names this chapter does not say a word about; they were simply written down. The recording itself is a form of godliness: what God values is not only the great things He did for His people, but also every ordinary stretch of road His people walked step by step. A family, a church, that remembers only its high moments has a hollow foundation; write down the ordinary days as well, and you will know how you were brought, step by step, to today. At the gate of Canaan Moses looks back and settles the account; what he records is not a roll of merit but the evidence of leading: every station counted, no step missing.

(Argument) Note also the timing of the record: it was not made at the foot of Sinai, nor at Kadesh, but on the plains of Moab, by Jordan, before entering Canaan. The backward look is placed at the point of departure — first count up the road behind, then receive the word for the road ahead; the chapter's very arrangement is teaching. A departure without a backward look easily turns presumptuous: a man thinks he was brave all along, and forgets that the water at Marah was bitter and that at Rephidim there was no water to drink. Looking back makes a man honest, and it also makes him bold: honest, because the list contains places where the camp was pitched in failure; bold, because through every station, bitter or sweet, the whole camp came through. Today, when we stand at the gate of any new stage — a new job, a new city, children leaving home, a church's new year — we should first do what Moses did: write the road behind down station by station, to show our children, and to show ourselves in some future day of weakness. Men forget; writing does not. Memory alters; a ledger does not.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Journeys / stations": massāʿ (H4531, the number marked in this sermon as pending verification, see Endnote 5), denoting a stage of a journey or a station; the Chinese base text keeps the two renderings side by side in a parenthesis at the first verse. The verb behind "wrote" is kāṯaḇ (H3789), to write, to record; the "wrote" of verse 2, where Moses wrote, shares its root with the many occurrences of "write" in this book. "Armies" is ṣāḇāʾ (H6635); "with their armies" at verse 1 denotes arrangement in companies. "With an high hand" renders an idiom of a raised hand (bĕyāḏ rāmâ), which the Chinese base text renders freely as going out boldly and without fear; only this correspondence is registered, and it is not expanded.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The nature and source of this chapter's list are set out in parallel, without adjudication. The reading runs more than one way: one way takes this as an archival reading — the list derives from a record of itinerary character, verse 2 itself states that it was written by commandment, its genre is close to the march and caravan logs of the ancient Near East, and its value lies precisely in its plain, unadorned statement; another way takes it as a liturgical retrospect — the list's arrangement has been edited, its choice of stations does not wholly agree with the Exodus narrative (see the discussion layers of Parts Two and Three), and its function is closer to restating the whole journey to the new generation before entering Canaan, a memorial text rather than an original log; a third way fastens on the alternative rendering "stations" at verse 1 in the Chinese base text — read as stations, the whole chapter is a list of camps; read as journeys, it is an itinerary; the two readings affect the counting basis, and this sermon lists the two reckonings separately in Endnote 4 and does not adjudicate here. This sermon registers only the points of the three readings and the chapter's own statements (written by commandment, one consistent sentence-form, very few notes), and the argument of the main text uses only the order and content the list itself states, without adjudicating its process of composition.

(Fact) This section closes by registering the account of the first part: the general heading, one verse (journeys, or stations in the Chinese parenthesis; with their armies; under the hand of Moses and Aaron); the origin of the record, one verse (by the commandment of the LORD); the date of departure, one place (the fifteenth day of the first month, the morrow after the passover); the starting point, one place (Rameses); the background note, one place (the Egyptians burying their firstborn, the LORD executing judgments on their gods). And it is registered: from verse 5 the body of the list begins, its sentence-form turning to the repeated register "removed from —, and pitched in —" as far as verse 48, with brief notes carried only at verses 8, 9, 14, 38, 39, and 40, each recorded in its place.

### Part Two: The First Stretch Out of Egypt — from Rameses to Sinai (33:5-15) (10:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 5, the first stretch of the journey, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And the children of Israel removed from Rameses, and pitched in Succoth." (33:5) The stations that follow are registered in summary only, their names and order registered verse by verse from the base text: Verse 6, they departed from Succoth, and pitched in Etham, which is in the edge of the wilderness. Verse 7, they removed from Etham, and turned again unto Pi-hahiroth, which is before Baal-zephon, and they pitched before Migdol — this verse carries two reference place-names with one station (Baal-zephon, Migdol), registered as references only, with no separate station set up; for the basis see Endnote 4. Verse 8, they departed from before Pi-hahiroth, and passed through the midst of the sea into the wilderness, and went three days' journey in the wilderness of Etham, and pitched in Marah — this verse carries two itinerary notes: passing through the midst of the sea, and a journey of three days, recorded as they stand. Verse 9, they removed from Marah, and came unto Elim, and in Elim were twelve fountains of water, and threescore and ten palm trees, and they pitched there — two numerical notes (twelve, threescore and ten) recorded as they stand. Verse 10, they removed from Elim, and encamped by the Red sea. Verse 11, they removed from the Red sea, and encamped in the wilderness of Sin. Verses 12-13, they took their journey out of the wilderness of Sin and encamped in Dophkah, and departed from Dophkah and encamped in Alush. Verse 14, they removed from Alush, and encamped at Rephidim, where was no water for the people to drink — one note of lack, recorded as it stands, standing in direct contrast with the abundance of fountains at verse 9. Verse 15, they departed from Rephidim, and pitched in the wilderness of Sinai. Recounted by hand in this sermon: this section has eleven stretches of journey in all, and eleven encampments (Succoth, Etham, Pi-hahiroth, Marah, Elim, the Red sea, the wilderness of Sin, Dophkah, Alush, Rephidim, the wilderness of Sinai); the details are at Endnote 4.

(Argument) In the first stretch of the road the list is at its densest and its notes at their most numerous: the crossing of the sea and the three days' journey (33:8), Elim with its twelve fountains of water and threescore and ten palm trees (33:9), Rephidim with no water to drink (33:14). These three notes are exactly the three deepest marks of the first year out of Egypt: saved, fed, and thirsty. When Moses wrote the list, he did not distribute the notes evenly to every station; he left them only at these places — not because the other stations were unimportant, but because the water and the thirst at these places are the starting lessons of the whole camp's faith. In the forty years that followed, the people again and again came to grief at water, and again and again were supplied, and the source of it all lies in this stretch. Our own remembering should have the same selectivity: it does not matter if the stations of a lifetime cannot all be recorded, but those few places — how God brought you through the sea, how after bitter water He gave you fountains, how in the place with no water to drink He did not forsake you — must be remembered more clearly than anything else. When the next generation asks about your faith, you may not be able to answer with systematic theology, but these stations you must be able to answer for.

(Argument) Note also the honesty of the list: the one sentence at Rephidim, "where was no water for the people to drink," is recorded plainly, without a word of the complaint rehearsed and without a word of explanation of how God supplied — it registers the lack itself alone. In the Exodus account that stretch has quarrelling and the striking of the rock (previous-text layer); the list here keeps only those few words. Looking back over the road behind, we commonly fall into two faults: either we record only the supply and erase the lack, editing history into a promotional film; or we record only the lack and magnify the grievance, reading history as an indictment. Moses' list does neither: Marah of the bitter water is named, Rephidim of the lack is recorded in fact, Elim of the supply is recorded by number. Only such a ledger is trustworthy, and only such a ledger is fit to be opened at the gate of Canaan. When a church writes its own history and a family tells its own past, they should learn this proportion: do not beautify, do not blacken; let the station-names and the facts stand together.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Removed / departed" is nāsaʿ (H5265), to pull up camp and set out; the list of this chapter uses this verb for every stretch, paired with "pitched / encamped," ḥānâ (H2583) — one pulling up, one pitching — forming the rhythm of the whole chapter; see Endnote 5. The "Red sea" of "encamped by the Red sea" is yam-sûp (a compound of H5488 and H3220), the same name as the place of the crossing in Exodus; at verse 8 of this chapter the wording is "passed through the midst of the sea" without a name, and at verse 10 "by the Red sea"; the two places are entered separately, and their precise reference is not forced into identity. The numerical note at "Elim" (twelve fountains of water, threescore and ten palm trees) registers only the base text's words and numbers, without symbolic interpretation.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The agreements and differences between this section's list and the Exodus narrative are set out in parallel, without adjudication. The reading runs more than one way: one way holds that the two complement each other — the Exodus narrative is full on events and brief on stations, this chapter's list is full on stations and brief on events; Dophkah and Alush, for instance, appear only in this chapter's list and their names are not seen in the Exodus narrative, and only read together do the two give the whole journey; another way holds that there is tension between the two — the order of individual stations does not wholly agree with the itinerary described in the narrative (as with the order of the wilderness of Sin and the Red sea), showing that the list and the narrative each have their own logic of arrangement and should not be forced into station-by-station alignment; a third way fastens on the distribution of the notes — the notes of this chapter are concentrated in the first stretch and in the mount Hor stretch, while the middle stretch has almost none, and some readings take the list's fullness and brevity to reflect the nearness and distance of memory. This sermon registers only the points of the three readings and the textual facts (Dophkah and Alush appear in this chapter alone; the notes are unevenly distributed), and does not adjudicate the source relation of the two records at the fact layer; the argument of the main text uses only the station order and notes the list itself states.

(Fact) This section closes by registering the account of the second part: eleven stretches of journey, eleven encampments; two reference place-names (Baal-zephon, Migdol, with no separate station set up); four notes (passing through the midst of the sea, the three days' journey, Elim's twelve fountains of water and threescore and ten palm trees, Rephidim's lack of water to drink); the section's end-point, one place (the wilderness of Sinai) — the whole camp stayed here for nearly a year (previous-text layer of this book), while the list records only the one verse of arrival; the length of the stay is not recorded in this chapter, and this blank is registered as a blank.

### Part Three: The Long List North of Sinai — the Years of Which Only the Place-Names Remain (33:16-36) (24:00-34:00)

(Fact) The stations of this section are registered in summary only, their names and order registered verse by verse from the base text: Verse 16, they removed from the desert of Sinai, and pitched at Kibroth-hattaavah. Verse 17, they departed from Kibroth-hattaavah, and encamped at Hazeroth. Verses 18-19, they departed from Hazeroth and pitched in Rithmah, and departed from Rithmah and pitched at Rimmon-parez. Verse 20, quoted verbatim in full from the base text as a specimen of the sentence-form: "And they departed from Rimmon-parez, and pitched in Libnah." (33:20) Verse 21, they removed from Libnah, and pitched at Rissah. Verses 22-23, they journeyed from Rissah and pitched in Kehelathah, and went from Kehelathah and pitched in mount Shapher. Verses 24-25, they removed from mount Shapher and encamped in Haradah, and removed from Haradah and pitched in Makheloth. Verses 26-27, they removed from Makheloth and encamped at Tahath, and departed from Tahath and pitched at Tarah. Verses 28-29, they removed from Tarah and pitched in Mithcah, and went from Mithcah and pitched in Hashmonah. Verses 30-31, they departed from Hashmonah and encamped at Moseroth, and departed from Moseroth and pitched in Bene-jaakan. Verses 32-33, they removed from Bene-jaakan and encamped at Hor-hagidgad, and went from Hor-hagidgad and pitched in Jotbathah. Verses 34-35, they removed from Jotbathah and encamped at Ebronah, and departed from Ebronah and encamped at Ezion-gaber. Verse 36, they removed from Ezion-gaber, and pitched in the wilderness of Zin, which is Kadesh — this verse, with the words "which is Kadesh," makes an identification note for the wilderness of Zin, recorded as it stands. Recounted by hand in this sermon: this section has twenty-one stretches of journey in all, and twenty-one encampments (Kibroth-hattaavah, Hazeroth, Rithmah, Rimmon-parez, Libnah, Rissah, Kehelathah, mount Shapher, Haradah, Makheloth, Tahath, Tarah, Mithcah, Hashmonah, Moseroth, Bene-jaakan, Hor-hagidgad, Jotbathah, Ebronah, Ezion-gaber, Kadesh), and not one event note in the whole section; the details are at Endnote 4.

(Argument) What most shakes the reader in the whole chapter is precisely the emptiness of this section. Twenty-one stations, and every station has only a name: no event, no date, not one sentence of memory. Yet we know that hidden in these twenty-one stations is the longest stretch of Israel's years — after setting out from Sinai, after the spies' evil report and the sentence into the wilderness, that generation walked back and forth over this ground for nearly forty years (previous-text layer of this book). Forty years are compressed into twenty-one place-names, and they take barely two minutes to read. That is the list's severity and its mercy at once: severe, in that it leaves not one further word for that generation's years, as if their days deserved to be only the connecting lines between place-names; merciful, in that these station-names were in the end written down, and not one station was deleted — that generation fell in the wilderness, but the road they walked did not fall; Moses stored it station by station into Scripture, to become the map in the hands of the next generation as it entered Canaan. A man's days may be exhausted by his own failure, but they will not be discarded by God's record.

(Argument) This section also corrects our contempt for "uneventful years." Rithmah, Libnah, Rissah, Tahath, Tarah — these stations never appear anywhere else in Scripture, and no event hangs on their names. Yet it was exactly by walking station after such a station that the whole camp got from Sinai to Kadesh. Most of the life of faith is in fact stations of this kind, with no story: day after day of prayer with no particular sense of being touched, week after week of meetings at which nothing great happens, year after year in the same post with no breakthrough arriving. We easily think such days are blank, are waste, and even doubt whether God is still present. The list says: a station with no story is also part of the journey, and is also within the record. God did not skip a station because it was uneventful; He commanded Moses to write its name down. On the day the new generation of Israel entered Canaan and looked back, the wilderness years were not forty years deleted, but forty years recorded in full. However uneventful your present station is, first live it well and record it well — the departure of the next station begins from the encampment of this one.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. Most of the place-names of this section are transliterated proper names whose Hebrew forms and etymologies are much disputed; this sermon registers only the base text's renderings throughout, without etymological assertions. Among them, "Kibroth-hattaavah" has in chapter 11 the narrative origin "the graves of lust" (previous-text layer); this chapter registers its name only, without reading its meaning back in. The name "Kadesh" is used at verse 36 in the construction "the wilderness of Zin, which is Kadesh" together with the wilderness name, recorded as it stands. The verbs remain the pair nāsaʿ, to depart (H5265), and ḥānâ, to encamp (H2583), repeated in pair; in this section's twenty-one stretches there is not one exception; see Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The modern identification of the stations of the middle stretch is set out in parallel, without adjudication. The reading runs more than one way: one way holds that the route can be reconstructed — from clues for individual station-names in other passages of Scripture and in ancient geographical material (Ezion-gaber, for instance, near the northern end of the Red sea, and Kadesh south of Beersheba), the whole course can be drawn in outline as a northward journey from Sinai, winding round to Kadesh; most of the stations cannot be verified, but the general course holds together; another way holds that most of the middle stations cannot be identified — more than ten stations from Rithmah onward have no documentary corroboration outside this chapter, the proposed identifications of their sites differ widely from one another, and forced location only manufactures a false certainty; a third way fastens on the number — there is a traditional reading of the whole chapter's stations as "forty-two stations" (for the basis see Endnote 4), set in structural comparison with such things as the sectional numbers of the New Testament genealogy; this is a structural observation at the reading level, not stated by this chapter itself. This sermon registers only the points of the three readings and the textual facts (the middle stretch's twenty-one stations have no notes, and many stations have no corroboration elsewhere), and does not settle the modern site of any station at the fact layer; the argument of the main text uses only the order the list itself states.

(Fact) This section closes by registering the account of the third part: twenty-one stretches of journey, twenty-one encampments; one identification note (the wilderness of Zin, which is Kadesh); zero event notes — this blank is registered in contrast with the second part's four notes, and no reason for it is extrapolated. And it is registered: the two end stations of this section (Kibroth-hattaavah, Kadesh) each have major events in this book's narrative (previous-text layer); the list here records the station-names only, the events are governed by their own accounts, and this chapter does not retell them.

### Part Four: The Pause at Mount Hor and the Last Stretch — Aaron Died There (33:37-49) (34:00-48:00)

(Fact) Verse 37, mount Hor, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And they removed from Kadesh, and pitched in mount Hor, in the edge of the land of Edom." (33:37) Verse 38, the death notice, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And Aaron the priest went up into mount Hor at the commandment of the LORD, and died there, in the fortieth year after the children of Israel were come out of the land of Egypt, in the first day of the fifth month." (33:38) Registered item by item: first, the time is "in the fortieth year after the children of Israel were come out of the land of Egypt, in the first day of the fifth month" — the chapter's only complete date besides the day of departure (33:3), exact to year, month, and day; second, the dead man is "Aaron the priest," named by his office, recorded as it stands; third, the manner of his death is that he "went up into mount Hor at the commandment of the LORD, and died there" — the ascent was by commandment, and he died on the mountain, recorded as it stands. Verse 39, his age, registered: Aaron was a hundred and twenty and three years old when he died in mount Hor — the chapter's only note of a person's age. Verse 40, a note of news, registered: king Arad the Canaanite, which dwelt in the south in the land of Canaan, heard of the coming of the children of Israel — here the list inserts one stroke of an outside reaction, recorded as it stands; for its relation to the battle with Arad in chapter 21 the account itself governs (previous-text layer), and this chapter records only the hearing. Verses 41-48, the last stretch of the journey, registered in summary only: they departed from mount Hor and pitched in Zalmonah, departed from Zalmonah and pitched in Punon, departed from Punon and pitched in Oboth, departed from Oboth and pitched in Ije-abarim, in the border of Moab, departed from Iim and pitched in Dibon-gad, removed from Dibon-gad and encamped in Almon-diblathaim, removed from Almon-diblathaim and pitched in the mountains of Abarim, before Nebo, and departed from the mountains of Abarim and pitched in the plains of Moab by Jordan near Jericho. Verse 49, the extent of the final camp, registered: they pitched by Jordan, from Beth-jesimoth even unto Abel-shittim in the plains of Moab — the final camp is bounded by the place-names at its two ends, recorded as it stands. Recounted by hand in this sermon: this section has nine stretches of journey in all (the one stretch from Kadesh to mount Hor, and the eight stretches from mount Hor onward), and nine encampments; the details are at Endnote 4.

(Argument) After more than forty verses of one sentence-form, at mount Hor the pace suddenly slows. Everywhere else it is one verse, one station, passing the gate without entering; only here does the list halt and give one man a complete death notice: which year, which month, which day, which office, and at what age. Aaron is the whole camp's priest, the one who stands side by side with Moses in the chapter's opening words, "under the hand of Moses and Aaron" (33:1); when he died, the list sent him off with the chapter's most solemn strokes. This slowing down teaches us how to regard the departure of a fellow traveller: the company must still go forward and the camp must still be pulled up, but Scripture does not allow us to process a man's death as a footnote symbol in an itinerary — it deserves a pause, it deserves the recording of a date. In a church and in a family, everyone who finishes his road deserves this stroke for which the list slows down: record his office, record his years, record that he finished "at the commandment." Hurrying travellers cannot settle this account; Moses settled it clearly: of those who died in the forty years, beyond counting, only Aaron is halted for and recorded in detail in the list — not because the others were unimportant, but because the one who leads the company must first learn to pause for a fellow traveller's finishing point.

(Argument) Note further the change in the place-names of the last stretch: Ije-abarim is noted as "in the border of Moab," the mountains of Abarim as "before Nebo," the plains of Moab as "by Jordan near Jericho." After forty years of walking, the list's notes for the first time begin to measure distance by "before" and "near" — Jericho is just across, the promised land can be seen across the river. Every station of this stretch was walked in a place from which the end-point could be seen. To be able to see it and yet not have crossed is the hardest stretch of faith to endure: homesickness sharpens near home, changes multiply near the finish, and both king Arad's hearing and Aaron's death happen in these days when the far bank can be seen. We too mostly fall — not away in Egypt, far off, but in the place where Jericho can be seen, where we slacken. The list records this stretch with an unusual number of references and boundaries, as if reminding the new generation: on the last stretch, not one station may be recorded with one missing, and not one step may be walked with one missing; the camp is already pitched by the river, and the river has not yet been crossed.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Border / edge" is gĕḇûl (H1366): mount Hor at verse 37 is "in the edge of the land of Edom," and Ije-abarim at verse 44 is "in the border of Moab" — the same word twice. The plural form of "plain," ʿărāḇâ (H6160), is at verses 48 and 49 "the plains of Moab," denoting the lowland east of the Jordan. The verb "died" is mûṯ (H4191): Aaron's "died there" at verse 38 and "when he died in mount Hor" at verse 39 are the same word twice; in the whole chapter only this one man's death is entered into the list; see Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The textual difference in the departure place-name at verse 45 is set out in parallel, without adjudication. The facts are registered first: the encampment at verse 44 in the base text is "Ije-abarim," and the departure place at verse 45 in the base text is "Iim" (registered from the anchor, key points) — in the KJV the two verses do not carry the identical full form; in the Chinese base text both verses read the full name alike. The Septuagint tradition and some modern English versions (for example the ESV) read "Iim" (Iyim) at verse 45; one reading holds that "Iim" is a shortened form or variant writing of "Ije-abarim," the two names referring to one station; another holds that the two names were originally two neighbouring camps, merged into one in the list's arrangement. This sermon registers the names as the respective base texts give them, counts them as one station (see Endnote 4), sets the two readings side by side, and does not adjudicate which is the original reading. Registered in addition: Aaron's death on mount Hor has its own account elsewhere in this book (chapter 20, previous-text layer); the correspondence of the two places in date and detail is governed by that account, and this chapter registers only the year, month, day, and age the list itself states, without collation or adjudication here.

(Fact) This section closes by registering the account of the fourth part: nine stretches of journey, nine encampments; one complete date (the first day of the fifth month in the fortieth year after the coming out of Egypt); one age note (a hundred and twenty and three years old); one news note (king Arad heard); one sentence bounding the final camp (from Beth-jesimoth even unto Abel-shittim). With this, the whole chapter's list account is complete: forty-one stretches of journey in all (eleven in the second part, twenty-one in the third, nine in the fourth), forty-one encampments, and, with the starting point Rameses, forty-two station-names in all — for the itemized details and the basis see Endnote 4.

### Part Five: The Word After the List — Crossing, Driving Out, and the Lot (33:50-56) (48:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 50, the place of the speaking, registered: the LORD spake unto Moses in the plains of Moab by Jordan near Jericho — the place where the commandment at the chapter's end is issued is precisely the list's terminal station; the two matters share one place, recorded as it stands. Verse 51, the occasion of the commandment, registered in its main points: speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, When ye are passed over Jordan into the land of Canaan — the commandment takes "passed over Jordan" as its time boundary; the crossing is at hand and the word arrives first, recorded as it stands. Verse 52, the three items of commandment, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "Then ye shall drive out all the inhabitants of the land from before you, and destroy all their pictures, and destroy all their molten images, and quite pluck down all their high places:" (33:52) Registered item by item: drive out the inhabitants, destroy the pictures and the molten images, quite pluck down the high places — the order is first the inhabitants, then the objects, then the sites, recorded as it stands. Verse 53, the ground for taking the land, registered in its main points: ye shall dispossess the inhabitants of the land, and dwell therein, for I have given you the land to possess it — the ground for taking the land is "for I have given you the land," gift first, taking after, recorded as it stands. Verse 54, the method of dividing the land, registered in its main points: divide the land by lot for an inheritance among your families; to the more give the more inheritance, and to the fewer give the less inheritance; every man's inheritance shall be in the place where his lot falleth; inherit according to the tribes of your fathers — three items of method: the lot decides the land, the measure follows the numbers, the belonging follows tribes and families, recorded as it stands. Verse 55, the warning, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "But if ye will not drive out the inhabitants of the land from before you; then it shall come to pass, that those which ye let remain of them shall be pricks in your eyes, and thorns in your sides, and shall vex you in the land wherein ye dwell." (33:55) Registered: the object of the warning is "those which ye let remain of them"; the images are two (pricks in your eyes, thorns in your sides); the consequence is one (they shall vex you in the land wherein ye dwell), recorded as it stands. Verse 56, the close of the warning, registered in its main points: moreover it shall come to pass, that I shall do unto you, as I thought to do unto them — the whole chapter closes with this sentence; no response of the people is recorded, and this blank is registered as a blank.

(Argument) The order of list and commandment is the first lesson this chapter leaves the new generation. God did not let them enter Canaan with a blank: before entering the land, they first go through forty years of station-names. That means remembering is not nostalgia; it is equipment — only a man who remembers the bitterness of Marah, the thirst of Rephidim, the stay at Sinai, and the circling from Kadesh knows what kind of people they themselves are, and knows that the scene after the crossing is not a matter of course. At a handover in a church or a family today, a ledger should likewise be set up before rules are laid down: first let the next generation recognize the road behind in full, and only then hand them the commandment for the road ahead. Teaching that skips the remembering and goes straight to the rules leaves the rules hanging in the air; a commandment with a ledger under it has weight.

(Argument) Note further the proportion of the commandment itself: what the commandment points to first is the system of idols — pictures, molten images, high places, listed without one missing (33:52) — while what the warning points to is the consequence of "letting remain" — the pricks and thorns are not far away; they are "in the land wherein ye dwell" (33:55). Read together, the two verses give a logic that allows no bargaining: the new land cannot hold two systems of worship; what is let remain today will vex tomorrow. Applied to us, what has to be dealt with is not conduct only, but also the "high places" in our surroundings that shape us day by day — the things that make us live, without noticing, by another set of values. Moses speaks this absolutely at the river's edge not because he is unfeeling, but because he has just proved it with forty years of list: the price of compromise was already paid once by the wilderness generation.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Drive out" and "inherit / possess" are the two faces, causative and basic, of the one root yāraš (H3423): at verses 52 and 55 "drive out" uses its causative form (to cause the other side to lose the land), and at verses 53 and 54 the gift and inheritance of the land use its basic form (to obtain land as a possession); one word, two faces, and only the word and its occurrences are registered. The "lot" of "divide by lot" is gôrāl (H1486); the dividing of the land by lot at verse 54 uses the same method as the principle of dividing the land in chapter 26 (previous-text layer). "High places" is bāmâ (H1116), one of the objects to be plucked down at verse 52. The numbers of the word-forms behind "pricks," śikkîm, and "thorns," ṣĕnînîm, are marked in this sermon as pending verification (see Endnote 5); only the base text's renderings are registered.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Later readings and evaluations of the commandment to drive out the inhabitants are set out in parallel, without adjudication. The reading runs more than one way: one way holds that this commandment has its covenant context — the sin of Canaan's inhabitants and their idol system are the commandment's own stated focus (33:52 lists pictures, molten images, and high places first), and the commandment's range is limited to that land, that time, and that covenant arrangement, and may not be transplanted directly as a principle of conduct for any age; another way holds that within the canon itself this commandment is tested by the later narrative — the records in Joshua and Judges of how it was actually carried out (following-text layer) and the warning of verse 55 answer each other, and this reading fastens on the historical echo of "letting remain will surely vex"; a third way fastens on the ethical difficulty — modern readers' moral questioning of a commandment of expulsion is itself part of the history of reading, and the several responses (the historical-setting account, the progressive-revelation account, the account of limits internal to the text, and others) each have their arguments. This sermon registers only the points of the three readings and the original wording of this chapter's commandment (the three items of commandment, the ground, the method, and the warning all registered from the base text above), and makes no adjudication for ancient or modern times at the fact or argument layers; the application of the main text takes only the logic of worship this chapter itself states (the new land cannot hold two systems of worship), and does not extend to transplants at the interpersonal or political level.

(New Testament Reading) The apostle Paul, writing to the church at Corinth about Israel's wilderness road, says that these things were our examples, and again that they are written for our admonition (1 Cor 10:6, cross-reference reading layer; see also 10:11, where these things are said to have happened unto them for ensamples and to be written for our admonition). One contrast is registered at the reading layer: this chapter's list is the ledger-form of the admonition — it stores the forty years' road as a checkable record, so that later readers need not pass through the wilderness in person to read warning and comfort from the station-names and notes; the New Testament's word fixes the use of this ledger: it was written down not for nostalgia, but for admonition. The reading layer registers only the contrast of the two texts; it does not fill the New Testament's admonition saying back in as a fact of this chapter's list. The list's origin still stands as verse 2 itself states it (written by the commandment of the LORD).

(Fact) The whole chapter's data gathered in, every item already counted (details at Endnote 4): the chapter has 56 verses; the five parts' verse counts in order are 4 (33:1-4), 11 (33:5-15), 21 (33:16-36), 13 (33:37-49), 7 (33:50-56), totalling 56. Forty-one stretches of journey, forty-one encampments, and with the starting point Rameses forty-two station-names; eight places carrying notes (33:3 the date, 4 the background, 8 the crossing of the sea and the three days, 9 the fountains and palm trees, 14 no water, 36 the identification, 38-39 the death notice and the age, 40 the hearing; the details are at the several sections); two complete dates (the fifteenth day of the first month; the first day of the fifth month in the fortieth year after the coming out of Egypt); three items of commandment at the chapter's end, two images in the warning. This chapter's joins with the chapters before and after: it follows chapter 32's division of the land east of the Jordan (previous-text layer) — the land divided, the road is recorded first; it leads into chapter 34's boundaries of Canaan (following-text layer) — whose existence is registered only: the list closes on the plains of Moab, the commandment already points across the river, the drawing of the boundaries is in the next chapter, and it is not read back into this chapter.

## Devotional Excerpt

"And Moses wrote their goings out according to their journeys by the commandment of the LORD: and these are their journeys according to their goings out." (33:2) Forty years of road, and Moses wrote it down station by station, not omitting even the stations with no story. Try today to record your own journey too: where you set out from, where you pitched, at which station the water was bitter, at which station there were twelve fountains, at which station you thought you could go no further and yet set out again. It does not need to be written well; it only needs to be written whole. When you write it down you will discover that the stretches you thought at the time were detours are all, after all, within the journey, and that the years you thought were wasted all, after all, have names station by station. What we fear has never been that the road is long; it is that our days are remembered by no one. Moses' list says: every station of yours God commanded to be written down. And do not fear to write down the unseemly stations either: at Rephidim there was no water to drink, and Moses wrote it all the same; Kibroth-hattaavah is the graves of lust, and Moses wrote it all the same. An honest ledger is what lets a man stand firm; write the stations of failure in as well, and the future you will know how you were brought through step by step. Tonight, take ten minutes and write down this year's stations of yours, for the future you, and for your children — men forget, writing does not; memory fades, a ledger endures.

English (KJV-based):

"And Moses wrote their goings out according to their journeys by the commandment of the LORD: and these are their journeys according to their goings out." (Num 33:2, KJV) Forty years of road, and Moses wrote it down stage by stage — even the stations with no story were not skipped. Write down your own journey tonight: where you set out, where you camped, where the water was bitter, and where the springs were found. You do not need to write it well; you only need to write it whole. The God who commanded the record has not lost a single stage of your road.

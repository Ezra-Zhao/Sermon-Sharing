Summary: Leviticus chapter 3 treats the peace offering. Among the five offerings, the burnt offering is wholly burned and the remnant of the meat offering belongs to the priests; the peace offering alone is an offering at which the offerer himself may also sit down and eat: the fat and the inward parts are burned upon the altar to God, the breast and the right shoulder belong to the priest, and the rest of the flesh is shared by the offerer and those who sit with him, in holiness. Yet the chapter closes with a solemn perpetual statute: neither fat nor blood shall be eaten — a reminder that the centre of the feast is God, not man's appetite.

## I. The Only Offering at Which One May Sit Down and Eat 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, when we read Leviticus we are often stopped by words like blood, fat and inward parts, and feel that it is far from our own lives. Coming to chapter 3, pause first at a very plain picture: a family leads a bullock, or carries a lamb, to the door of the tabernacle; the animal is killed, the blood is sprinkled round about the altar, the fat is burned upon the altar and the smoke rises; and after a while this family does not go home empty-handed — they sit down and eat the flesh of the sacrifice, and at table with them are neighbours and kin, perhaps also the stranger and the poor. Worship, at the last, has become a meal. This is the most striking thing about the peace offering among the five offerings: it is the only offering of which the offerer himself also receives a portion, and may sit down and eat.

Let us state the factual level clearly. Leviticus chapters 1 to 7 treat in order the burnt offering, the meat offering, the peace offering, the sin offering and the trespass offering. The burnt offering is wholly burned upon the altar, and the offerer does not eat of it; of the meat offering a handful is burned upon the altar and the remnant belongs to the priests, and the offerer does not eat of it; the sin offering and the trespass offering are, according to their grades, either burned or given to the priests, and the offerer likewise does not eat of their flesh. Of the peace offering alone — reading chapter 3 together with the detailed rules of chapter 7 — the greater part of the flesh belongs to the offerer, to be eaten by him and by those who sit at table with him. This is not an embellishment added by the preacher for effect; it is a fact set out by the manner of distribution: the flesh of the peace offering has three destinations — a portion for the altar, a portion for the priest, and a portion for the offerer and his companions. Three parties share one sacrificial animal together; this pattern is unique in the whole sacrificial system. The title, At the Same Table, comes from this pattern: God receives His portion at the altar, and men receive theirs around the table; worship and feast are joined together and not separated.

The distinctiveness of the peace offering does not lie only in the eating. Among the five offerings it is also not appointed for some specific sin. The sin offering deals with sins committed unwittingly; the trespass offering deals with wrongs that carry restitution; each has its definite offence. The peace offering has no such corresponding offence. The common reasons for offering it are thanksgiving, the payment of a vow, and free willingness. Chapter 7 later divides the peace offering into three kinds: the sacrifice of thanksgiving, offered for grace received; the votive offering, offered when a vow made in trouble is now to be paid; and the freewill offering, offered for no special reason, simply out of willingness. Taken together, the tone of the peace offering is not that a man has caused trouble and comes to make amends, but that, the relation between man and God being already sound, a man comes gladly to draw near, to give thanks, and to celebrate. What it presupposes is not a rupture awaiting repair, but reconciliation already present. This matters greatly: Israel's life of faith does not consist only of the gravity of confession and atonement; it also has the brightness of thanksgiving and feasting. God did not design worship only as a courtroom; He also designed it as a banquet.

Corresponding to this tone is the voluntary character of the peace offering. It is of course offered according to ordinance, with procedures that allow no carelessness — the seventeen verses of this chapter are those procedures; but whether to offer, and when to offer, for the most part arise from the offerer's own thanksgiving, vow and willingness, and are not forced upon him by some charge of sin. If a man has thanksgiving in his heart, he may come and offer; if he has made a vow, he comes to pay it; if he simply wishes to draw near to God, he may also come and offer. Voluntary does not mean careless, and willingness does not mean disorder. This offering is at once the freest and yet written with the most meticulous procedures, specifying even which parts of the fat are to be burned and from where the tail is to be cut off. Freedom and order go together; that is the temper of the peace offering: the heart is willing, but the hand must act earnestly according to God's word.

We should also mark out in advance the scope of this chapter, lest we fault it for what it does not contain. Chapter 3 sets out only the main body of the peace offering's procedure: what is to be offered, how it is killed, how the blood is handled, and which parts are burned upon the altar. It does not state within this chapter what portion the priest receives, nor within how many days the flesh must be eaten; those details are in chapters 7 and 19. When this sermon speaks of distribution and of the time limit for eating, it will identify its cross-references, and will not pass off the words of later chapters as the words of chapter 3. Distinguishing the chapter itself from its cross-references is basic honesty in reading Leviticus: chapter 3 sets up the frame of the procedure, chapter 7 supplies the details of distribution, and the system is complete only when the two are read together.

Finally, let us point out first the difficulty of this offering's name, lest our later usage be vague. "Peace offering" — the Hebrew name of the offering belongs to the same word-family as the word for peace, and the Chinese rendering "peace" makes it easy to think we have understood it. In fact, scholars still hold differing views on the etymology of this offering's name; the notes will set side by side the view that connects it with the root for peace and wholeness, and the view that connects it with the payment of vows, without settling the question. The sermon text uses the established rendering "peace offering," taking only its received sense in the biblical tradition, and does not quietly present the etymological dispute as settled. The name may be used for now, but the exposition must be honest: what we know for certain is the procedure and distribution of this offering; where the etymology is in doubt, let the doubt stand.

The road this chapter will take us along is this: first, how the three kinds of livestock are offered — the common acts of slaughter, sprinkling of blood and burning of fat; then, how the flesh is distributed among God, priest and men, and the rules for eating together; then, the solemn perpetual statute at the chapter's end, that neither fat nor blood shall be eaten; and finally, in its own clearly marked place, the New Testament reading, hearing how Paul and Ephesians speak in the language of peace and reconciliation. The feast is warm and the perpetual statute is solemn; the two stand together in one chapter, and therein lies the chapter's weight.

## II. Cattle, Sheep and Goats: Three Kinds of Livestock, One Set of Acts 08:00-24:00

The ordinance of chapter 3 is divided by livestock into three sections: one form for cattle, one for sheep, and one for goats. The preaching takes only the main line and does not spread out every item. The main line is one common set of acts, appearing again and again in the three sections like the refrain of a song: the offerer brings the animal before the LORD, lays his hand on the head of the victim, and kills it on the spot; the priest receives the blood and sprinkles it round about the altar; the fat, together with the appointed inward parts, is taken out and burned upon the altar. The differences among the three sections lie chiefly in the size of the animal, the character of its covering, and the treatment of the tail; the common frame does not vary by a word. See the frame clearly first, and the details will have their place.

First, the offering of cattle: "And if his oblation be a sacrifice of peace offering, if he offer it of the herd; whether it be a male or female, he shall offer it without blemish before the LORD" (Lev 3:1, KJV). There is one point here that differs from the burnt offering and deserves notice: the cattle of the burnt offering must be male, but the cattle of the peace offering may be either male or female. This loosening in one place and strictness in another is not arbitrary: the burnt offering expresses an offering wholly given to God, and uses the strongest bullock; the peace offering is an offering of shared table fellowship, and the ordinance widens the rule on sex, so that more of the livestock a household actually has can enter into this offering. The widening is itself compassion: the feast of the common table should not be open only to households that can raise a bullock. That an animal with blemish may not be offered, however, remains the common bottom line: what is offered to God in sacrifice may not be fobbed off with something damaged. Widening where widening is possible, holding fast where no concession may be made — the ordinance's sense of proportion lies precisely here.

The acts begin with the laying on of the hand. The offerer lays his hand on the head of the victim at the door of the tabernacle. We have already seen this act in chapter 1, and the several views of its meaning were set out in the notes there; this chapter does not repeat the dispute, but takes only the most secure level: the laying on of the hand joins the offerer to the victim — this offering is not an animal unrelated to him, but one he has brought himself, claimed himself, and presents before God as his representative. After the laying on of the hand, he kills the animal: "And he shall lay his hand upon the head of his offering, and kill it at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation" (Lev 3:2, KJV). The slaughter is still performed by the offerer's own hand; it is not that he hands over a living animal and stands by to watch the bustle. He must face the ending of this life under his own hand, and know in earnest that drawing near to God is never a weightless affair: under the feast of thanksgiving, too, there lies the cost of a life. However glad the peace offering may be, it is not cheap gladness; it begins with slaughter, and its joy carries gravity within it.

Next comes the blood. Aaron's sons, the priests, sprinkle the blood round about upon the altar. The sprinkling of the blood is the priest's office; the offerer may not intrude upon it, and the priest may not be absent from it. The blood is sprinkled round about the altar, not splashed at random, but so that the blood, which represents life, surrounds the altar, and the relation among offerer, offering and altar is joined in the manner God appointed. The full theology of the blood is left for the fourth section, to be unfolded with the prohibition of verse 17; here we record the fact of the act: every peace offering has blood sprinkled upon the altar. There is no bloodless peace offering, just as there is no feast without slaughter. From the very beginning, this offering reminds man that his being able to sit down and eat in peace rests on life first having been solemnly delivered at the altar, not carelessly wasted.

Then comes the step most characteristic of the peace offering: the fat and the appointed inward parts are taken out and burned upon the altar. The parts named in the text are, in the main, the fat that covereth the inwards, the fat upon the inwards, the two kidneys and the fat upon them, and the caul above the liver, taken together: "And he shall offer of the sacrifice of the peace offering an offering made by fire unto the LORD; the fat that covereth the inwards, and all the fat that is upon the inwards, And the two kidneys, and the fat that is on them …" (Lev 3:3-4, KJV, excerpted). The form for sheep has in addition the fat tail, of which we shall speak below. These parts are not eaten; they are wholly burned. The text says the priest shall burn them upon the altar, upon the wood of the burnt offering, and calls this an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD. In quantity, what is burned is only a part of the whole animal; in position, this part goes up to the altar first and belongs to God first. Man has not yet sat down, and God has already received His portion; the flesh has not yet been divided, and the fat has already been burned. It is not that man remembers God after he has eaten his fill; it is that God is served first, and only then do men eat. Order — this is the quietest and most powerful lesson in the whole theology of the peace offering.

Next, the form for sheep. A sheep offered as a peace offering must likewise be without blemish, and the laying on of the hand, the slaughter, the sprinkling of the blood and the burning of the fat are all the same in the main line. There is one separate rule alone: the whole fat tail is to be taken off hard by the backbone, and offered together with the rest: the text directs that he shall take away "the fat thereof, and the whole rump" (Lev 3:9, KJV). One kind of sheep in the ancient Near East is the fat-tailed sheep, whose tail is large and laden with stored fat; it is the most concentrated store of fat in the whole sheep, and in the shepherd's eyes one of the best parts of the animal. The ordinance names this tail expressly, precisely so that the offerer may not keep the best fat for himself: good as the tail is, it must be cut off whole and burned upon the altar. This cut is made very concretely — concrete as to the part, concrete as to cutting by the backbone. Reverence often hides in such concreteness: it is not saying with the mouth that the best belongs to God, but truly, when the flesh is divided, putting the fattest piece on the altar first. The form for sheep, by means of one tail, preaches once again the old principle that the meat offering's fine flour already taught with its best grade, and the burnt offering with its unblemished animal: the good goes to God first; it is not the leftovers that go to God.

The form for goats is the shortest, and its ordinance is almost a fresh confirmation of the common frame: an unblemished goat is offered, the hand is laid on, it is killed, the priest sprinkles the blood round about the altar, and the fat and inward parts are likewise taken out and burned upon the altar, for an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour. The text adds no new device for the goat, and this very repetition is teaching: whether a man offers cattle, sheep or goat, there is only one road by which he comes before God, and only one set of acts. The values of the animals are high and low, but the order of drawing near has no high and low. The man who leads in a bullock has spent more money and cannot gain thereby one whit more of God's acceptance; the man who carries in a goat has spent less and need not be ashamed of himself at the door of the tabernacle. God wrote the three sections so uniformly precisely in order to set the three kinds of men within one and the same set of acts: the same laying on of the hand, the same slaughter, the same sprinkling of blood, the same burning of fat. Before the feast of the peace offering has even begun, equality is already established in the acts.

Taken together, the three sections also show one repeated form of words: offered before the LORD, killed before the LORD, the blood sprinkled and the burning done, all before the LORD. The audience of this offering from beginning to end is God, not man. The neighbours will see whose household has offered a bullock, and kin and friends will come to eat the flesh together, but the offering is not a display made for them to see; it is a presentation set before God. The honour men give one another tempts a man: to offer a large animal to show his means, to spread a wide table on paying a vow to win a name. The text, by its repeated "before the LORD," draws this offering back from mere display: what God looks at is not how large a table you have spread, but whether, according to His word, you have brought the animal before Him, burned the fat to Him first, and solemnly sprinkled the blood upon the altar. The honour men give is for men; acceptance is God's to give; the two must not be confused.

To close this section: three kinds of livestock, one set of acts — brought before God, the hand laid on, killed by the offerer's own hand, the blood sprinkled by the priest, the fat and inward parts burned upon the altar for a sweet savour. When the acts are finished, the greater part of the animal's flesh remains: it has not been burned, and it has not yet been distributed. How this remaining flesh is divided, to whom it is given to eat, and within how many days it must be eaten, is the theme of the next section — and the most moving part of the peace offering: the smoke of the altar has gone up, and men are about to sit down.

## III. God's at the Altar, Man's at the Table: The Threefold Division of One Victim 24:00-42:00

The division of the peace offering must be read with chapter 3 and chapter 7 together. Chapter 3 records only the altar's portion: the fat and the inward parts burned to God. What the priest receives and what the offerer eats are given in detail in Leviticus 7:31-34: the priest shall burn the fat upon the altar, but the breast shall be Aaron's and his sons', first waved before the LORD as a wave offering; and the right shoulder shall be given to the priest as a heave offering. The text states expressly that this is the portion taken from the peace offerings of the children of Israel and given to the priests as their due for ever — an apportionment appointed by the LORD Himself. The whole picture is then complete: the fat goes up on the altar, to God; the breast and the right shoulder belong to the priest, God's gift out of the sacrifice to those who serve; the rest of the flesh belongs to the offerer, who eats it with his household and with those who sit at table with him. This threefold division is not a folk custom but an arrangement of the ordinance: one animal, three parties, each receiving its portion, each in its place.

First, the altar's portion. Why the fat belongs to God will be treated in detail in the fourth section together with the perpetual statute; here we grasp its place in the division: it is divided off first. As soon as the slaughter is done, the fat is first taken and put on the altar; only then is the priest's breast and shoulder spoken of, and only then do men speak of eating the flesh. The order must not be reversed. If men picked the flesh first, the priest took the shoulder first, and only what remained — the fat nobody wanted — were then burned, that would not be sacrifice at all, but a dividing of spoils. The logic of the peace offering is exactly the reverse: God first, the priest next, man last. The first lesson a man learns in this division is to set the order right — not God standing behind men to gather leftovers, but man receiving his own portion after God and after those who serve. And when he receives it, he knows clearly that this portion is what remains after the division, yet that he is not thereby ill-treated: for the whole order is appointed by God, and the man who receives last still receives a portion given by God.

Next, the priest's portion. The breast is first waved and the shoulder heaved — two acts called the wave offering and the heave offering, ritual acts by which that portion of flesh is first presented before God and then delivered into the priest's hand. Their meaning is this: what the priest receives is not wages paid by the offerer, but provision transferred by God out of His own sacrifice. The offerer may not treat the breast and shoulder as a favour he bestows upon the priest, nor may the priest treat them as a right he exacts; both parties stand under God's apportionment. This level agrees with the principle that the remnant of the meat offering belongs to the priests as a thing most holy: God binds worship and provision together — when the people offer willingly, the priests receive food and serve at the tabernacle with undivided attention. Because the peace offering includes the eating of flesh, the weight of this provision shows more fully still: the day on which the people eat flesh is also a day on which the priests have flesh to eat. Joy is shared, and provision is naturally accomplished within that sharing, without need of another heading.

Next, man's portion — the high point of the peace offering. The offerer takes the remaining flesh home, or spreads it in a clean place, and invites those who are to sit at table with him to eat. One may picture the company at that table: the offerer's household and children, the near neighbours, the people of his clan, and also the stranger and the poor, whom the law again and again charges men not to forget. When Deuteronomy later speaks of eating flesh of this kind in the place which the LORD shall choose, it says expressly that a man shall rejoice together with the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless and the widow; that guest list belongs to a later unfolding, yet it is of one line with the spirit of the common table in the peace offering. The meaning of sitting at table together lies not in the eating of flesh itself, but in the togetherness: people who ordinarily belong to different stations — those with possessions and those without, those of the tribe and those who sojourn, the one who offers and the ones who are invited — sit around the flesh of one and the same victim, eat of one and the same offering, and are blessed by one and the same God. Sitting at table together is the very shape of reconciliation: if relations have not been reconciled, men cannot sit down at one table and eat in peace; that they can sit together shows that the estrangement has been removed and fellowship established. The peace offering makes reconciliation into something that can be seen and tasted.

The eating has a time limit, and the limit is found in the cross-references, which must be cited as what they are. Leviticus 7:15-18: the flesh of a peace offering offered for thanksgiving shall be eaten the same day it is offered; none of it shall be left until the morning. What is offered in payment of a vow, or as a freewill offering, may also be eaten on the second day; what remains on the third day must be burned with fire, and if it is eaten on the third day it shall not be accepted, it shall be an abomination, and the person who eats of it shall bear his iniquity. Leviticus 19:5-8 restates the same rule and adds severe words: if it is eaten at all on the third day, it profanes the holy thing of the LORD, and the person who eats it shall be cut off from among his people. Why should the eating of flesh have a time limit? The text does not state its reasons item by item, and later readers have held several views, which may be set side by side: first, a view from preservation — in the wilderness and in ancient conditions flesh kept long would surely spoil, and the limit is a practical protection of the people; second, a view from reverence — the flesh of the sacrifice is a holy thing and may not be consumed at leisure like household leftovers; it must be eaten solemnly within the time God appointed; third, a view from the composition of the feast — the limit compels the offerer to invite company widely on that same day: since the flesh cannot be kept, enough people must be invited to finish it, solitary eating becomes impossible under the system, and sharing is driven into flesh and blood by the limit itself. The three views do not exclude one another, and this sermon sets them side by side without suppressing any. Most striking is the combination of the third view with the first two: a rule seemingly about preservation in fact creates a community of sharing — because the flesh cannot be finished and cannot be kept, a man naturally invites neighbours, the poor and the stranger to sit at his table. By a time limit, the system turned generosity into a necessity.

The limit also shows the solemnity of eating together: this meal may not drag on indefinitely into snacks of ordinary days. It has a holy window: that day, or at most the second day. Eaten within the window, it is accepted fellowship; eaten outside the window, thanksgiving turns into profanation. The same piece of flesh changes its character when the time of eating is wrong. The weight of this teaching is not light: even thanksgiving has a time and a boundary appointed by God, let alone anything else. Joy is not another name for licence, and the feast too has the rules of the sanctuary. If a man thinks that, having received grace, he may do as he pleases, the words of chapter 19 are waiting for him: freedom within grace still lives inside the borderline of holiness.

To gather up this section: one victim — the fat up on the altar to God, the breast and shoulder waved and heaved to the priest, the remaining flesh at table to men; the thanksgiving offering finished the same day, the votive and freewill offerings at most by the second day, and eating on the third day is profanation. By the division of flesh and by a time limit, the peace offering arranges the life of a reconciled community into practicable daily form. Every mouthful at that table preaches the same truth: God first receives His portion, and men then share theirs; reconciliation is not a slogan, but sitting down around one and the same offering. This is the warmest place in this offering. Yet at the very end of the chapter the tone turns suddenly severe — the warmer the feast, the more there is a boundary that may not be crossed. In the next section we shall look at that boundary: fat and blood, for ever not to be eaten.

## IV. Neither Fat nor Blood Shall Ye Eat: The Boundary Behind the Perpetual Statute 42:00-56:00

In verse 16 chapter 3 first sets down a settled word: "all the fat is the LORD's" (Lev 3:16, KJV). Verse 17 follows immediately: "It shall be a perpetual statute for your generations throughout all your dwellings, that ye eat neither fat nor blood" (Lev 3:17, KJV). When words reach "for your generations" and "throughout all your dwellings," this is no longer a temporary working rule within the court of the tabernacle, but a boundary cut into the life of Israel for generation after generation: wherever they dwell, and however many generations pass, blood shall not be eaten, and fat shall not be eaten. However glad the feast, these two things are never on the menu.

First, the fat. That the fat belongs to God was already established in the division of the earlier sections; here the question is why. Several levels may be stated at the factual level. First, the fat is the richest part of the animal. The ancients viewed the fat just as they viewed fine flour among grains and the firstfruits among harvests: it is the best portion of the whole. That the best belongs to God is a principle running through all five offerings, and the peace offering sets it down upon the fat: a man who offers cattle or sheep may not keep the essence for himself and push the lean and the second-rate to the altar. "All the fat is the LORD's" settles the ownership of the essence once and for all: whether it is a time of sacrifice or not, the fat has never been man's portion. Second, the fat burns readily, and on the fire of the altar it burns with thick, fragrant smoke, exactly fitting the image of an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour: it turns into smoke and ascends, and men can see it and smell it; the offering has a perceptible shape. Third, in the language of Scripture fat is often associated with abundance and excellence; God's taking the symbol of abundance under His own name is a reminder to the people, every time they slaughter and divide flesh, of this: from whom abundance comes, to him its essence belongs. Read together, these three levels show that the fat's belonging to God is not a strange dietary taboo, but the extension, at the table, of the sacrificial principle: God receives the essence, men eat the rest — and as they eat, they remember that the essence is not their own. This also explains the scope of the prohibition: what is chiefly forbidden is the fat of cattle, sheep and goats, the sacrificial animals — that portion of abundance belonging to the altar — and not every oil and grease swept wholesale into the forbidden zone. A prohibition must be read with its object read accurately: it may not be enlarged at will, nor reduced at will.

Next, the blood. Verse 17 joins blood to fat in a single prohibition: "that ye eat neither fat nor blood" (Lev 3:17, KJV). This prohibition is rooted extremely deep in Scripture, and its root system must be set out clearly at the factual level. Its earliest source is Genesis 9:4, where after the flood God makes a covenant with Noah and says plainly: "But flesh with the life thereof, which is the blood thereof, shall ye not eat" (Gen 9:4, KJV). Blood and life are there directly equated: the blood is where the life is, and to eat blood is to appropriate another's life by swallowing it; God does not permit it. In Leviticus the prohibition of blood is restated again and again: chapter 17 gives its reason most fully — the life of the flesh is in the blood, and God has given the blood upon the altar to make an atonement for men's souls; and blood is life, so that man may not eat the blood of any creature. The prohibition of this chapter's verse 17, together with the covenant of Genesis and the explanation of chapter 17, forms a complete chain: blood is life; life belongs to God; God has appointed the use of blood at the altar, for atonement, and not in man's mouth. Men may eat flesh; they may not eat blood. A man receives the animal's flesh as food, but its life is to be returned, in the form of blood, and solemnly disposed of; he is not permitted to swallow it down as food. The theological foundation of this boundary may be put in one sentence: the sanctity and the ownership of life. Life is not property that man may dispose of at will; even before an animal's life a man must stop at the boundary of the blood — how much more before the life of man. When Deuteronomy later speaks of eating flesh, it restates that flesh may be eaten as usual, only blood shall not be eaten, and says that the blood is the life, and that a man may not eat the life with the flesh; and it connects this boundary with doing what is right in the sight of God — an unfolding of the same tradition.

The placing of blood and fat side by side has a further structural depth. Both are the parts of the victim to which man's heart most easily reaches: the fat is the most fragrant, and the blood most concerns life. In taking both away together, God set up two boundary stones at the very centre of the feast: one says, the best is not yours; the other says, life is not yours. Men sit at table and eat the portion that is God's gift, within the boundary; reaching across the boundary changes the character of the feast. This answers, at a distance, to the picture of Eden — a piece of homiletical exposition, which must be marked as such: in the garden every fruit might be eaten, and only one tree might not; here the whole table may be eaten, and only blood and fat may not. What may be eaten is much, and what may not be eaten is drawn clearly — God's generosity has always stood together with a boundary, and man's fall has often begun precisely with disbelieving that boundary and reaching, after all, for the one forbidden thing. Having marked the exposition, we must still return to the fact: the perpetual statute of this chapter itself does not mention Eden; the correspondence is one the preacher reads out, not an explicit statement of the chapter.

Notice also the manner of the statute's wording: it does not say, ye shall not eat in the tabernacle; it says, throughout all your dwellings. That sentence carries the prohibition from the sanctuary into daily life. When Israel has in time entered Canaan and lives scattered in its cities and villages, slaughtering and eating flesh at home, not beside the tabernacle, blood and fat are still not to be eaten. Devotion holds true not only in the sanctuary but also at one's own table; the boundary is to be kept not by priests only, but by every household. "For your generations" lifts it again from the habit of one generation to a household teaching handed down from age to age: as the father teaches the son, so the son teaches the grandson — neither blood nor fat shall be eaten; that is how it is handed down, generation by generation. Chapter 3 of the peace offering begins with the feast at the door of the tabernacle and ends at the tables of every household, in the table rules of generation after generation — the shape of faith, in the end, is the shape of daily life.

To close this section: the fat belongs to God, because the essence has always been His; blood shall not be eaten, because life has always been His. The perpetual statute stands at the end of the feast not to spoil the joy, but to give joy its root: men can sit together in peace precisely because on the table there is a portion God has first received, and a boundary that may not be crossed; remove those two things, and the peace offering is left as a table of meat only, and no longer counts as worship. Carrying this boundary with us, we enter the last section, where, as our rule requires, the New Testament reading is brought out separately and its level marked, before we return to this chapter to conclude.

## V. He Is Our Peace: The New Testament Reading and Sitting Together Today 58:00-60:00

What follows belongs to the level of New Testament reading and homiletical use, and is not to be read back into Leviticus chapter 3 as its plain original meaning. The New Testament speaks of peace and reconciliation in three places that answer this chapter at a distance. Romans 5:1 says: "Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 5:1, KJV). To have peace with God is precisely that state of sound relation toward which the peace offering looks, declared in the New Testament to be a fact accomplished through faith. Ephesians 2:14 says it still more directly: "For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us" (Eph 2:14, KJV). Paul is there speaking of Gentiles and Jews being made one in reconciliation, and its picture lights up the spirit of the common table in mutual illumination: people who once could not sit at one table now sit at one table together. Colossians likewise says that God, "having made peace through the blood of his cross," reconciled all things unto himself, whether things in earth or things in heaven (Col 1:20, KJV). The saying that peace is made through blood forms a New Testament interpretive correspondence with the place of blood upon the altar in the old covenant; but it must be marked: this is the apostles' rereading in the light of the event of Christ, not something Leviticus chapter 3 is already literally preaching about the cross. Only when the levels are marked is the correspondence true, and only then is neither passage of Scripture treated unjustly.

The homiletical use of the shared dimension of the Lord's Supper belongs to this same level. The table of the Lord's Supper is the centre of the New Testament community's eating together: one bread, one cup, received by all in distribution. The picture of the peace offering's common table — God first receiving His portion, and men afterwards eating together — can help believers to grasp, in the Supper, the order of God first and men after, and of receiving together and sharing together; but it may not be reversed into saying that the peace offering is the Old Testament version of the Lord's Supper, or that chapter 3 already presupposes the whole meaning of the Supper. A use whose boundary is kept is a use that can be used long.

Returning to this chapter itself to conclude: Leviticus chapter 3 gives us not only an ancient set of sacrificial procedures, but a picture of a reconciled life: the animal brought before God, the blood sprinkled upon the altar, the fat first burned to God, the breast and shoulder divided to those who serve, the remaining flesh set upon the table, the whole household with neighbours, the stranger and the poor sitting together, eaten up within the boundary of that day and the next, and remembering for generation after generation: neither fat nor blood shall be eaten, the essence and life alike belong to God. May our lives today have this same order: first give the best to God, then set all we have on the table for others; thanksgiving not delayed, vows not left owing, joy not overstepping its boundary. For such a person, there is a feast at home, and peace in the heart. Amen.

## Notes

1. shelamim: Hebrew שְׁלָמִים, transliteration shelamim, noun in plural form, peace offering, peace offerings, Strong's number H8002. The sense follows BDB: peace-offering, sacrifice for alliance or friendship; HALOT: peace offering, communal sacrifice, and related senses. The views on its etymology are set side by side, without a settled conclusion: first, the view connecting it with shalom — the root sh-l-m carries the senses of completeness, wholeness, peace and reconciliation, and the name of the offering is taken from the wholeness and reconciliation of man's relation with God and with man; Wenham broadly expounds the fellowship character of this offering along this line. Second, the view connecting it with the payment and fulfilment of vows — the verb of the same root also carries the senses of repaying and fulfilling; Milgrom and others point out that the votive offering holds an important place within the peace offering, and that the name may be connected with repaying and discharging what was vowed. Third, some scholars connect it with the background of covenant meals in the ancient Near East, holding that the name stresses the common meal of the covenant community. Each view has its support; a root connection alone cannot settle the case. The sermon text uses the established rendering "peace offering," and honestly sets the points of etymological doubt side by side.

2. zevach: Hebrew זֶבַח, transliteration zevach, noun, slaughtering, sacrifice of slaughtering, sacrificial victim, Strong's number H2077. The sense follows BDB: sacrifice, feast of sacrifice; HALOT: slaughtering, sacrifice. It is of the same origin as the verb to slaughter, and stresses the act by which the victim is slaughtered; hence the full name of the peace offering is often given as the sacrifice (zevach) of peace offerings. The Hebrew construction of Lev 3:1 joins the two words in exactly this way — a peace offering presented by slaughtering — in contrast to the meat offering, in which no animal is slaughtered.

3. chelev: Hebrew חֵלֶב, transliteration chelev, noun, fat, fatty part, choicest portion, Strong's number H2459. The sense follows BDB: fat, choicest portion; HALOT: fat, best part. In Scripture the word denotes both the fat of animals and, figuratively, the choicest part of land and harvest. In this chapter it repeatedly denotes the fat covering the inwards and attached to the inwards and the kidneys; Lev 3:16 sums it up: all the fat is the LORD's — the textual foundation both for the fat's belonging to God and for the prohibition of eating fat in 3:17.

4. dam: Hebrew דָּם, transliteration dam, noun, blood, Strong's number H1818. The sense follows BDB: blood; HALOT: blood. Cross-references on the prohibition of blood: Genesis 9:4 — flesh with its life, that is, its blood, shall not be eaten; Leviticus 17:10-14 — whoever eats blood shall be cut off, with the express statement that the life of the flesh is in the blood and that blood makes atonement; Deuteronomy 12:23 — the blood is the life. The "eat neither fat nor blood" of Lev 3:17 is one of the summary statements of the blood prohibition within the sacrificial ordinances of Leviticus, and together with the passages above forms the complete chain of the theological foundation that blood is life — the chain unfolded at the factual level in the fourth section of the sermon text.

5. alyah: Hebrew אַלְיָה, transliteration alyah, noun, fat tail, Strong's number H451. The sense follows BDB: fat tail of sheep; HALOT: fat tail. It denotes the large tail of the Near Eastern fat-tailed sheep, rich in stored fat and one of the richest parts of the whole animal. Leviticus 3:9 directs that, in offering a sheep as a peace offering, the whole fat tail shall be taken off hard by the backbone and offered together with the fat; see also Lev 7:3, 8:25 and 9:19. The second section of the sermon text argues from this that the best is given to God first.

6. Without blemish: Lev 3:1, 3:6 and 3:12 repeatedly require that the victim be without blemish, in the Hebrew belonging to the word-field of completeness and freedom from defect. Unlike the burnt offering, the cattle and sheep of the peace offering may be either male or female; completeness alone allows no concession. Wenham treats this difference of widening and strictness in connection with the different characters of the two offerings, and Hartley likewise notes that the widening as to sex shows the side on which this feast is opened to the wider people.

7. The division of labour in laying on the hand, slaughter and sprinkling of blood: Lev 3:2, 3:8 and 3:13 run in parallel — the offerer lays on his hand and slaughters, and the priest sprinkles the blood round about upon the altar. For the several views of the meaning of the laying on of the hand, see the notes to the first sermon of this series; this chapter holds to the most secure reading, representation and joining. The Hebrew expression for sprinkling the blood round about the altar stresses that the blood surrounds the body of the altar; Milgrom treats its connection with the cleansing of the altar and with atonement, and Hartley its fixed place in the ritual order.

8. An offering made by fire, of a sweet savour: Lev 3:5, 3:11 and 3:16 call the burned portion of the peace offering an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD, in a Hebrew expression parallel to the corresponding summary sentences of the burnt offering and the meat offering. "Sweet savour" is an anthropomorphic expression denoting that the offering is accepted by God; see the discussion of Milgrom set out in the note on reach nichoach in the first sermon of this series. In this chapter the sweet-savour sentences are used only of the portion burned upon the altar, not of the portion eaten at table; the linguistic boundary of the distribution must be kept clear.

9. The priest's portion: Leviticus 7:31-34 — the fat is burned upon the altar, the breast belongs to the priests as a wave offering, and the right shoulder belongs to the priest as a heave offering; the text states expressly that this is the portion the LORD has taken from the peace offerings of the children of Israel and given to the priests as their due for ever. Wave offering and heave offering in the Hebrew denote offerings first waved and lifted up in presentation before the LORD; BDB and HALOT both list this ritual sense. This passage is a cross-reference and does not lie within chapter 3 itself; the sermon text identified its source when citing it.

10. The time limit for eating the flesh: Leviticus 7:15-18 — the thanksgiving offering is to be eaten the same day, the votive and freewill offerings may be eaten until the second day, what remains on the third day must be burned, and whoever eats on the third day bears his iniquity; Leviticus 19:5-8 restates the rule, and eating on the third day is a profaning of the holy thing, the eater to be cut off from among his people. Thanksgiving, votive and freewill are the three classes of motive for the peace offering, see Lev 7:11-21. Wenham and Hartley both treat the limit as serving at once the functions of holiness, preservation and the promotion of sharing; this sermon sets those readings side by side.

11. Perpetual statute: Lev 3:17, a statute for ever, in the Hebrew an ordinance continuing through generations, in the same form of words as the generational ordinances of Passover and Unleavened Bread in Exodus and Leviticus. The phrase "throughout all your dwellings" extends the scope of the prohibition from the sanctuary to the people's daily life at home; Milgrom lays particular weight on this wording, noting that the prohibitions of blood and of fat are dietary boundaries for all Israel, not working rules for priests only.

12. Sources at the level of New Testament reading: Romans 5:1 — justified by faith, peace with God; Ephesians 2:14 — he is our peace, having broken down the middle wall of partition; Colossians 1:20 — having made peace through the blood of his cross. See also 1 Corinthians 10:16-21 on the shared language of partaking of one bread and one cup. All of these belong to the New Testament's interpretation and use of the language of peace, reconciliation and eating together; when cited in preaching their level must be marked, and they are not to be read back into Leviticus chapter 3 as its plain original meaning.

## Devotional Excerpt

What moves me most in the peace offering is the table. The fat is first burned to God, the breast and the thigh are given to the priest, and what remains, I eat together with my family and neighbours around the table. Reconciliation, it turns out, is not merely a doctrine; it is being able to sit down and eat together. If there is thanksgiving in my heart today, I will not put it off until tomorrow: thanks should be given in time, vows paid in earnest, and guests gladly brought to the table. The table has its boundary — the best belongs to God first, and matters of life are not to be treated lightly; the table also has its warmth — God does not let me eat alone, but brings companions to my side one by one. Lord, teach me to give You the best first, and then to set all I have on the table for others, so that my thanksgiving does not stay on my lips only, but falls upon the table and into my days, becoming a peace that others, too, can taste.

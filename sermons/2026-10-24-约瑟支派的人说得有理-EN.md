## Main Text

Introduction (00:00–05:00). (Fact) The last chapter of Numbers brings no new person onto the stage; it tells the sequel of an old case. In chapter 27 the five daughters of Zelophehad came to the door of the tabernacle and presented their case, and received an inheritance; by chapter 36 the chiefs of the family of Gilead come before Moses, and what they speak of is still that same inheritance — only this time the question is not "can daughters receive an inheritance?" but "once daughters have received an inheritance, will the inheritance follow the marriage out?" The whole chapter has only thirteen verses, yet its structure is complete: the case presented in four verses (vv. 1–4), the ruling in five verses (vv. 5–9), the obedience in three verses (vv. 10–12), and the closing formula in one verse (v. 13).

(Argument) A book that begins with the numbering of the people (Numbers 1) ends with a marriage provision for one inheritance, and that ending deserves attention: Israel's forty years in the wilderness are walked out, they stand on the east bank of the Jordan, and the last thing to be settled before entering the land is not warfare, not marching, but how an inheritance is kept within a marriage and not lost. Receiving the land is a great matter; keeping the land is a long matter; what this chapter establishes is precisely the law for keeping the land.

(Fact) One further fact of form is registered first: in this chapter God has not one sentence of direct speech. The ruling of chapter 27 was, in the settled text, spoken by God in person (the sermon on Numbers 27 registers three passages of God's speech); the ruling of this chapter has Moses, at verse 5, commanding the children of Israel "according to the word of the LORD", and verse 6 restates it as "the LORD doth command" — the words still come from the LORD, but they come out through Moses' mouth and are reported by the narrator. This difference of form is registered only, and no rank is inferred from it (see Endnote 11 for the discussion).

(Summary) The governing line of the chapter can be put this way: in chapter 27 God stood on the daughters' side and said "right", and gave the inheritance to a household that had no son; in chapter 36 God stands on the chiefs' side and says "right" again, and keeps the inheritance in the tribe to which it belongs. Between the two sentences of "right" lies one complete rule: the inheritance may be given to daughters, but a daughter may not carry the inheritance out of the family. The five parts below take it part by part.

(Fact) The chapter's place in the structure of the whole book is also registered clearly: after the second numbering (chapter 26), what is established in succession on the plains of Moab is the precedent of the inheritance case (chapter 27), the full record of the journeys (chapter 33), the four borders of Canaan (chapter 34), the Levitical cities and the cities of refuge (chapter 35), and last of all this chapter's marriage rule. These commands have one thing in common: in every case there is as yet no land, but there is already law — the people have not yet entered the land, and the way the land is to be divided, the way the cities are to be used, the way life is to be reckoned, and the way the inheritance is to be kept have all been written out one by one. This chapter stands last not because its weight is lightest but because it is the ring that closes the set most tightly: the preceding chapters distribute the inheritance downward, and this chapter answers how, once the inheritance has been distributed and the first generation of marriages arrives, the map is not to be redrawn.

(Argument) It can also be said that the narrative centre of Numbers has by now turned completely: the first half of the book records how this generation failed in the wilderness; the second half records how the next generation is prepared on the plains — they are numbered, they are cleansed, they are taught to divide the land, to keep the inheritance, and to live their days. This chapter is the last lesson of that preparation: the end of preparation is not a charge sounded on a trumpet, but a detailed rule for living ordinary days.

### Part One: The Chiefs Come Before Moses — The Presentation First Acknowledges the Old Verdict (36:1–2; 05:00–15:00)

(Fact) Verse 1 first reports the identity of those presenting the case; the settled text says (in the KJV wording): "And the chief fathers of the families of the children of Gilead, the son of Machir, the son of Manasseh, of the families of the sons of Joseph, came near, and spake before Moses, and before the princes, the chief fathers of the children of Israel:" (36:1) This chain of identity is counted link by link by hand: the general designation "of the families of the sons of Joseph" stands at its head, and beneath it three links — Manasseh, Machir, Gilead — and below that it joins to "the chief fathers" among Gilead's descendants. Set it against the genealogy at 27:1, when the five daughters presented their case (registered from the surrounding text): that chain has five links — Manasseh, Machir, Gilead, Hepher, Zelophehad. The two chains come from one and the same family of Gilead: the five daughters who presented their case in chapter 27 are the daughters of Zelophehad, and Zelophehad belongs to the generation of Gilead's grandsons; the chiefs who present the case in chapter 36 are chiefs among the descendants of Gilead. That is to say, this is not an outsider coming to lodge a complaint; it is the second presentation from inside one and the same family — the first presented by the daughters of the family whose father was gone, the second presented by the family's chiefs. The occasion of the presentation is also registered clearly: it was not a private approach to Moses, but a coming "before Moses, and before the princes, the chief fathers of the children of Israel" — spoken before the whole leadership layer of Israel.

(Fact) Verse 2 is the opening of the presentation, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "And they said, The LORD commanded my lord to give the land for an inheritance by lot to the children of Israel: and my lord was commanded by the LORD to give the inheritance of Zelophehad our brother unto his daughters." (36:2) Note the method of this opening: the chiefs begin by citing two commands already issued — the first, the giving of the land by lot (agreeing, in the surrounding text, with the commands to divide the land at 26:55, 33:54, and 34:13, registered); the second, the very verdict of chapter 27 that assigned the inheritance of Zelophehad to his daughters. They did not say that verdict was wrong; on the contrary, they twice made plain that it was a command of the LORD — first acknowledging the source and force of the old verdict, and only then speaking of the consequence the old verdict had not covered. The words "my lord" occur twice in verse 2; they are the chiefs' respectful form of address to Moses. And the phrase "Zelophehad our brother" calls the dead man a brother — there is no hostility in the tone of the presentation, and it is registered on the record.

(Argument) This opening is the art of presenting a case, and also the way institutions grow: a supplementary rule very seldom begins by overturning the precedent; it mostly begins from "the precedent is right, only it is not yet enough". That daughters receive an inheritance is right; that the land is given by lot is right; only, when the two right things are set side by side, they give birth to a new question that neither of them had spoken of alone. The chiefs bring the question up, without accusation and without reopening old accounts — what they present is a consequence, not a grievance.

(Fact) The channel of presentation is also worth registering: when the people of this book have a matter, there is a road by which it can be brought before Moses — in chapter 27 the five daughters came to the door of the tabernacle and stood before Moses, and Moses brought their cause before the LORD (registered in paraphrase from the surrounding text). The chiefs of this chapter walk the same road: they come before Moses and the chief fathers and speak their words in the open. They did not go themselves to block the five daughters' marriages, did not privately put pressure on the family, and still less did they act first and report afterwards — a matter inside the institution walks through the institution's door.

(Argument) This stroke matters especially for those who keep what has been entrusted: when a gap in the rules is discovered, the easiest thing to do is to bypass the rules and patch it privately — block a marriage, exert pressure, plug the hole with personal influence. The chiefs do precisely the opposite: they present the gap before the lawgiver and let the rule grow its own supplement. A private patch can plug a hole for a time; the general rule established by presentation (v. 8) plugged every later hole of the same kind.

(Scholarly & Historical Discussion Layer) On the sequence of the two presentations, the commentary tradition holds one layer of discussion, stated side by side: one reading takes chapter 36 as the "implementing detail" of the chapter 27 precedent — once the precedent stands, the boundary questions that arise in carrying it out surface with it, and legislation answers them with a second case. Another reading takes the juxtaposition of the two chapters as an arrangement of the editing: whatever the actual historical sequence of the two cases, the present canonical order obliges the reader to read the two chapters together, the freedom of chapter 27 and the boundary of chapter 36 interpreting each other. The two readings stand side by side; this manuscript registers them only and does not adjudicate.

(Summary) Governing sentence for the part: those presenting the case first acknowledge the old verdict and only then state the consequence, and the hearing is public and formal — the last case of Numbers stands from its first sentence on the foundation of "The LORD commanded", not on anyone's grievance.

### Part Two: If They Marry into Another Tribe — Two "Diminishings" and the Long Account of the Jubilee (36:3–4; 15:00–27:00)

(Fact) The chiefs' argument has only two steps, both in verses 3 and 4 of this chapter. The first step is the immediate one: verse 3 in the settled text says (in the KJV wording): "And if they be married to any of the sons of the other tribes of the children of Israel, then shall their inheritance be taken from the inheritance of our fathers, and shall be put to the inheritance of the tribe whereunto they are received: so shall it be taken from the lot of our inheritance." (36:3) The inheritance goes with the person — a daughter carries her inheritance into marriage, and the inheritance is "put to" the account of her husband's tribe; the share this tribe received by lot is thereby diminished by one portion. The second step is the long-range one: verse 4 in the settled text says: "And when the jubilee of the children of Israel shall be, then shall their inheritance be put unto the inheritance of the tribe whereunto they are received: so shall their inheritance be taken away from the inheritance of the tribe of our fathers." (36:4) The two occurrences of "diminished" (in the Chinese base text's wording; the KJV renders the outcome as the inheritance being taken from / taken away from this tribe) are counted by hand: there are only these two in the whole chapter, both in the chiefs' presentation (vv. 3, 4). Note in particular the place of the jubilee in the second step: when the chiefs reckon the account, they say that when the jubilee arrives this inheritance is not returned but still "be put unto" the husband's tribe — in other words, in the premise of their argument the jubilee settles sales, not marriages; an inheritance transferred out by marriage is not among the things the jubilee returns, and when the time comes it is instead as though the seal were set on the transfer for good.

(Fact) The standing of the jubilee institution in this chapter is registered clearly here: the word "jubilee" (yôbēl, H3104) occurs in the whole book only at this verse (36:4); the institution itself is not established in this chapter but in Leviticus 25 (registered from the surrounding text: that chapter commands the fiftieth year as the jubilee, in which sold land is to return to its original owner in the jubilee). This chapter only has the presenters cite the institution in their argument, and the manner of the citation is: when the jubilee arrives, it still cannot recover this inheritance.

(Scholarly & Historical Discussion Layer) Inside this citation lies a real tension, and scholarship and the commentary tradition hold two readings side by side; this manuscript states them side by side and does not adjudicate. One reading holds that the chiefs' statement is itself a premise of the legal system: transfer by inheritance and transfer by sale are two different kinds — a sale is a temporary alienation, and so the jubilee can redeem and return it; an inheritance is a transfer of status and name, and the inheritance a daughter receives passes, together with her descendants, into her husband's house, so there is no jubilee return to speak of; when the chiefs cite the jubilee, they are precisely making the point that "even the jubilee cannot stop it". The other reading holds that the chiefs' words are the argument of a presentation, not a complete statement of the jubilee provisions — standing on their own tribe's side, they state the consequence at its heaviest; the ruling Moses transmits (vv. 6–9) nowhere mentions the jubilee and establishes only the marriage rule, which amounts to going around this layer of tension rather than harmonizing it head-on, and the reader should not take the premise of the presentation directly as the content of the ruling. At the Fact layer only two things can be settled: the chiefs' argument used the jubilee, and the ruling did not use the jubilee; how the jubilee provisions of Leviticus 25 connect with the chiefs' premise, the text itself does not explain.

(Argument) Leaving the tension aside, the chiefs' way of reckoning deserves to be learned: what they reckon is not one marriage but a line of loss that grows by itself. If the five daughters marry out, that is five portions; when the five daughters' descendants grow up in their husbands' houses, those five portions are the husbands' houses' for ever. The first generation is marriage, the second generation is inheritance, and at the jubilee comes the sealing of the account — they reckon the total account of fifty years later, item by item, into today, and present it before Moses. Those who keep an inheritance must have this kind of sight: an inheritance is never lost in one sudden collapse; it is lost by one outward flow that "looks like nothing", plus the compound interest of time. What the presentation presents is not a loss that has already happened — the five daughters have not yet married out (the obedience of vv. 10–12 still lies in the future) — but a loss that has not yet happened and that, under the rules as they stand, will necessarily happen. To mend the rule before the loss happens: this is the first lesson this chapter teaches everyone who keeps an entrusted charge.

(Fact) The direction of this account is to be seen clearly as well: on the Chinese base text, the two verbs of the chiefs' presentation form an out-and-in pair — what is "put to" (added to) the husband's tribe is increase; what is "diminished" from this tribe is loss (the KJV renders the same movement as the inheritance being taken from this tribe and put to the other). An inheritance carried away by marriage flows in one direction only: the husband's house only increases and never diminishes, this house only diminishes and never increases, because the belonging of the inheritance follows the belonging of the descendants — the descendants belong to the husband's house, so the inheritance belongs to the husband's house; there is no offset and no payment back. This is not the gain and loss of one marriage; it is a one-way valve: once it is fitted, every generation's marriages discharge in one direction only. And the timing matters still more: the five daughters' marriages have not yet taken place, the first loss has not yet been entered in the account, and what the chiefs present is not a recovery claim but a prevention.

(Argument) The account of a one-way valve costs more the later it is reckoned. If one waited until the five daughters had married, the children had grown, and the jubilee had set its seal, and only then sought recovery, what would be recovered would not be a rule but another family's established property — at that point, whichever side were found "right", bones and sinews would be hurt. In this chapter the law chooses to act before the first loss occurs, at the lowest cost and with the least harm to anyone: the rule stands before the marriage, and after the marriage no one suffers loss. Keeping an inheritance, keeping the faith, keeping any entrusted charge at all — the timing is learned here: the boundary is to be set before the first loss, not patched after the account shows red.

(Summary) Governing sentence for the part: the chiefs' presentation has only two verbs — "put to" and "diminished"; one house's increase must be another house's decrease, and if the sound of the jubilee cannot call back an inheritance carried away by marriage, this account can only be intercepted by a rule made beforehand, not by a return made afterwards.

### Part Three: Said Well — First "As They Will", Then "Only" (36:5–7; 27:00–39:00)

(Fact) At verse 5 the ruling comes down; the settled text says: "And Moses commanded the children of Israel according to the word of the LORD, saying, The tribe of the sons of Joseph hath said well." (36:5) Set this verdict sentence back into the verdict account of the whole book (the count recounted by hand): "said well / speak right" in this verdict form occurs once in this chapter and once at 27:7, and the two occurrences form a pair. At 27:7 God speaks to Moses about the five daughters: "The daughters of Zelophehad speak right:"; at 36:5 Moses, according to the word of the LORD, announces outwardly: "The tribe of the sons of Joseph hath said well." Two presentations, two opposing parties — on one side the daughters seeking an inheritance, on the other side the chiefs guarding an inheritance — and yet the adjudication gives each of them one sentence of "right". This is not splitting the difference: look closely at the two chapters and it is clear that the two sentences of "right" judge two different things. What chapter 27 judges "right" is the claim that "the daughters of a household without a son shall receive an inheritance"; what chapter 36 judges "right" is the claim that "an inheritance shall not pass across tribes with a marriage". The two claims are both true, and so the rule must hold both at once: the daughters still receive their inheritance (the verdict of chapter 27 is not withdrawn), and marriage has a further boundary set for it (the rule of chapter 36 is newly supplied).

(Fact) Verse 6 is the rule itself, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "This is the thing which the LORD doth command concerning the daughters of Zelophehad, saying, Let them marry to whom they think best; only to the family of the tribe of their father shall they marry." (36:6) Within one sentence there are two halves in sequence: the first half, "Let them marry to whom they think best" — whom they marry is for them to decide themselves; the law does not assign marriages and does not arrange them for them; the second half, "only to the family of the tribe of their father shall they marry" — the range is drawn inside their own father's tribe and family. The phrase "the family of the tribe of their father" (on the Chinese base text, the key phrase "of the same family and tribe") occurs three times in the chapter (vv. 6, 8, 12) and is the chapter's key phrase: at the level of the original language, "family" (mišpāḥâ, H4940) is the clan level inside the "tribe" (maṭṭeh, H4294); "tribe" occurs fifteen times in the chapter and "inheritance" (naḥălâ, H5159) fourteen times — for the accounts of the two words' occurrences see Endnotes 4 and 5. The object of the rule is named at verse 6 as "the daughters of Zelophehad", the five of them — first a rule made for a particular case; its extension into a general rule is the business of verse 8 in the next part.

(Fact) Verse 7 gives the rule's reason sentence, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "So shall not the inheritance of the children of Israel remove from tribe to tribe: for every one of the children of Israel shall keep himself to the inheritance of the tribe of his fathers." (36:7) The reason does not lie in the five daughters but in the whole structure: the map of the inheritance was drawn by tribes (the four borders of chapter 34 and the giving of the land by lot, registered from the surrounding text); if marriage could freely carry inheritances across the boundary, the map would be redrawn generation by generation with the marriages. The words "shall keep himself" (on the Chinese base text, "each keep") occur twice in the chapter (vv. 7, 9) and are the general action of this rule — not snatching back, but keeping hold.

(Fact) The occasions on which the two verdict sentences were announced also hold a comparable difference, registered here: the verdict of 27:7 was spoken by God to Moses alone; the five daughters were not among its hearers at the time, and the verdict was conveyed and carried out through Moses (registered in the sermon on Numbers 27). The verdict of 36:5 was announced by Moses in the presence of the children of Israel — verse 5 reads "commanded the children of Israel", and its hearers are the whole body. The first case was judged for five persons; the second case's verdict carries, from the moment it leaves the mouth, the specification of a general rule: what it judges is not only one presentation but a standing law about to be established, announced beforehand to all. This difference of occasion answers exactly to the word "every" in verse 8 of the next part: the ruling's hearers were enlarged first, and the scope of the provision's application is enlarged after them.

(Argument) There is a further layer of meaning: the chiefs presented their case in the presence of the chief fathers (v. 1), and the ruling too was announced in the presence of the children of Israel (v. 5) — the presentation public, the adjudication public, with no transaction in a private room in between. An inheritance is the common property of the whole tribe, and its rule must therefore be established in the open: anyone may come and present, anyone may hear the verdict, and the verdict speaks one sentence of "right" to each of the two parties with impartiality.

(Argument) This one sentence of verse 6 is one of the most condensed samples in biblical law of freedom and boundary spoken together: "as they will / to whom they think best" comes first, "only" comes after, and the order cannot be reversed. If there were only the freedom and no "only", the five daughters' freedom would be paid for by the collapse of the whole tribe's map of inheritance; if there were only the "only" and no freedom, the boundary would become a chain that assigns marriages. The law sets the two words in one and the same sentence: the freedom is real freedom — within the boundary, whom to choose and whom not to choose, no one decides for them; and the boundary is a real boundary — it does not move for an individual's wishes. It is worth noting that the boundary is drawn at the level of "the family", not spread boundlessly over the whole tribe and not narrowed harshly to their own house: their father's brothers' sons (v. 11) stand squarely inside this range, and within the range there is still a life that can be chosen.

(Fact) Look one layer more closely at the freedom half of the sentence: the KJV renders this half of verse 6 as "Let them marry to whom they think best" (the wording is registered as it stands) — the seat of judgment is in the women's own hearts: not whom the chiefs think best, not whom Moses thinks best, but whom they think best. The Chinese base text reads "they may marry as they will" — the willing follows their own intent. Read the two settled texts side by side and the shape of the rule is clearer still: the law asks only "is he a man of the family of their father's tribe?", and does not ask "which one is he?"; it guards the range and does not manage the choice of person.

(Argument) This layer of proportion is the tenderness of the chapter most easily read past. If God's intent had been to take back the standing chapter 27 gave the daughters, He could simply have assigned their marriages, or commanded them never to marry so that the inheritance be preserved; He did neither. He drew only one boundary line of the family, and the lifelong matter inside the boundary line He handed back entirely to themselves. This is also exactly how the obedience record of verses 10–12 is to be read: the five daughters chose, within the range, their father's brothers' sons — the text says "they were married", and the subject of the sentence is they; it does not say "they were married off". The boundary was established by God, the choice was made by them, the obedience was walked by them; the three verbs belong each to its own subject, and nowhere does the chapter write a person as an implement of the rule.

(New Testament Reading Layer) This Old Testament principle that "the inheritance shall not remove" is registered at the reading layer only in its New Testament echoes: the apostolic letters say that believers have in Christ "obtained an inheritance" (Eph. 1:11, 14, registered in paraphrase), and 1 Peter 1:4 calls this inheritance one "incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away" (registered in paraphrase). The Old Testament keeping of the inheritance keeps an earthly map from being redrawn by marriages; the New Testament inheritance is spoken of as a kind that fundamentally cannot be lost — read in contrast, all the reading layer can register is this: from beginning to end Scripture treats "inheritance" as something given by God, something to be kept, and something that in the end can be kept; as for a theological joining-through of the two Testaments' views of inheritance, this manuscript registers the textual echoes only and makes no assertion of a joining-through.

(Summary) Governing sentence for the part: the second sentence of "right" is judged to the chiefs, and the first sentence of "right" is not taken back; the rule opens the door with freedom and sets the boundary with "only" — the daughters' inheritance is preserved, and the daughters' freedom is preserved too, and the way both are preserved is to let marriage remain within the map of the family.

### Part Four: From Five Persons to All Persons — The General Rule of Verses 8–9 (36:8–9; 39:00–49:00)

(Fact) Verse 8 raises the particular case into a general rule, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "And every daughter, that possesseth an inheritance in any tribe of the children of Israel, shall be wife unto one of the family of the tribe of her father, that the children of Israel may enjoy every man the inheritance of his fathers." (36:8) Compare it word by word with verse 6: verse 6 says "concerning the daughters of Zelophehad" — its object is five persons; verse 8 says "every daughter, that possesseth an inheritance" — its object is a class of persons: every one. Within the space of four chapters (chapter 27 having established its general rule on inheritance beforehand), this is the second time the book walks the whole legislative road of "particular case — verdict — general rule": chapter 27, from the one case of the five daughters, established the five-level order of inheritance as a general rule (registered in the sermon on Numbers 27); chapter 36, from the follow-on presentation of the same case, establishes the general rule for the marriages of daughters who receive an inheritance. The purpose clause of the general rule is also written out: "that the children of Israel may enjoy every man the inheritance of his fathers" — "every man enjoy" and verse 7's "shall keep himself / each keep" answer each other: keeping is the action, enjoying (receiving and holding) is the result; what the provision is to preserve is that every household can carry its fathers' inheritance onward.

(Fact) Verse 9 restates verse 7 almost word for word, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "Neither shall the inheritance remove from one tribe to another tribe; but every one of the tribes of the children of Israel shall keep himself to his own inheritance." (36:9) The two verses are collated by hand: the subject narrows from "the inheritance of the children of Israel" to "the inheritance" in its bearing on them, and the object kept narrows from "the inheritance of the tribe of his fathers" to "his own inheritance", while the skeleton sentence "shall not remove from tribe to tribe" and "shall keep himself" stand unchanged at both occurrences. A principle spoken twice — once appended after the rule for the particular case (v. 7), once appended after the general rule (v. 9) — restatement is sealing: the principle is not diluted as its scope of application widens; it is nailed down once more at the very place of the widening.

(Fact) The sentence form of verse 8 itself is also registered: "every … shall …" is the fixed sentence form by which the law establishes a general rule — "every" gathers a class of persons into one sum, and "shall" sets down a disposition without exception. In the legal provisions of this book and of the Pentateuch this form is seen repeatedly (a registering statement; the instances are not quoted one by one); when the reader meets the word "every", the pen is known to have left the particular case: from then on, whenever a daughter receives an inheritance because she has no brother, there is no need to present a case again and no need to wait for a verdict again — the standing law applies directly. The value of the five daughters' one case is fixed at this point: their father had no son, their presentation is without parallel, but the provision they left behind is to govern every later household of the same kind.

(Argument) This also explains why Scripture registers the five daughters' names three times (26:33, 27:1, 36:11): the general rule is nameless, but its origin has names. The law book refuses to let the origin of its legislation become a nameless clause — it lets later generations, every time they open this general rule, first see five concrete persons: their presentation, their verdict, their obedience. A rule is established because of persons, and persons are made imperishable because of the rule; this is one deep meaning of Scripture's recording of names.

(Scholarly & Historical Discussion Layer) On the relation of chapter 36 to chapter 27, the discussion layer registers two readings side by side and does not adjudicate. One reading reads it as a "narrowing": chapter 27 gave daughters an unprecedented standing in inheritance, and chapter 36 at once takes back part of the actual freedom with a marriage restriction; between the two chapters there is an oscillation of right and restriction, and later readers' evaluations differ with their own standpoints. Another reading reads it as a "matching pair": the two chapters guard one and the same principle — that the inheritance endures by tribe and family and that the name of the dead is not cut off (the burden of the presentation in chapter 27, registered in the sermon on Numbers 27); chapter 27 guards the inward face of this principle (a household without a son is not left without an heir), and chapter 36 guards its outward face (a daughter with an inheritance does not carry it out); and the two cases were both triggered by a presentation and both received a verdict of "right", so that in the canonical order they look more like a matched pair of provisions than like provisions overturning each other. At the Fact layer this manuscript registers only the textual facts both readings acknowledge in common: the inheritance verdict of chapter 27 is not revoked by one word in chapter 36, and the marriage rule of chapter 36 does not rewrite chapter 27 in return; all further evaluation is left below, at the discussion layer.

(Fact) The boundary of the general rule is likewise registered clearly, lest the sermon overshoot: the general rule of verse 8 reaches only "every daughter, that possesseth an inheritance"; for the marriages of daughters who have not received an inheritance this chapter has no provision; and the rule's range reaches only to "the family of the tribe of her father" — within the family, choice is restricted no further. What this chapter has not said, this manuscript in every case does not say.

(Argument) This step, from five persons to all persons, is the chapter's most practical teaching for later generations: however justly a particular case is handled, if it is not raised into a general rule, the next case of the same kind must be presented once more and judged once more; raised into a general rule, matters of the same kind have law to stand on from then on. Many provisions of the Pentateuch grew in exactly this way — not by a complete code existing first and waiting for cases to be fitted to it, but by the case arriving first, the verdict following, and the general rule sealing it up. The Israelites had not yet entered the land, and the law had already grown two provisions tall on the plains of Moab.

(Summary) Governing sentence for the part: one word "every" in verse 8 writes the household matter of five daughters into a general rule for every daughter who receives an inheritance; verse 9 nails the principle sentence down once more — a particular case has an end point, a general rule has none, and the way an inheritance is kept is from now on written into standing law.

### Part Five: So Did They — The Obedience, the Names, and the Closing of the Whole Book (36:10–13; 49:00–57:00)

(Fact) Verse 10 is the obedience sentence, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "Even as the LORD commanded Moses, so did the daughters of Zelophehad:" (36:10) This sentence form is the fixed obedience-register form in the legal narrative of this book and of the Pentateuch (registered from the surrounding text: Exodus and Leviticus in many places close a section of commands with a sentence of the kind "even as the LORD commanded Moses, so did they", registered in paraphrase, not quoted one by one). This chapter uses it of the five daughters: the subject of the commanding is the LORD, the one through whose hand it passed is Moses, and the ones who walked it out are the five women.

(Fact) Verse 11 sets down the names, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "For Mahlah, Tirzah, and Hoglah, and Milcah, and Noah, the daughters of Zelophehad, were married unto their father's brothers' sons:" (36:11) The five daughters' names are all registered, not one missing — but the order differs from 27:1 (registered separately at the head of this manuscript): 27:1 reads Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, Tirzah, while this chapter reads Mahlah, Tirzah, Hoglah, Milcah, Noah — Tirzah advanced from fifth to second, Noah moved from second to last. The text does not state the reason for the different order, and this manuscript registers the separate records only and does not speculate. The persons they married are "their father's brothers' sons" — the sons of their father's brothers, that is, paternal male cousins, falling squarely in the middle of the range of verse 6, "the family of the tribe of their father". Verse 12 re-checks the result, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "And they were married into the families of the sons of Manasseh the son of Joseph, and their inheritance remained in the tribe of the family of their father." (36:12) Its two clauses each close one account: the persons were married into their own family; the inheritance remained in their own family and tribe. The word "remained" is the landing point of the whole chapter — the "diminishing" the chiefs feared in verses 3 and 4 did not happen, and the inheritance is unmoved, exactly as it was.

(Fact) One entry for the later carrying-out is added (surrounding-text layer): Joshua 17:3–6 registers that the five daughters later came before Eleazar the priest and Joshua and the chiefs, and, under the precedents of this chapter and chapter 27, received an inheritance among their father's brethren (registered in paraphrase) — the rule established on the plains of Moab was carried out as established when the land was divided; the precedent is valid across books.

(Scholarly & Historical Discussion Layer) On the five daughters' marrying their paternal male cousins, the discussion layer registers one entry concerning degrees of kinship, registered only and not developed: among the prohibited degrees of kinship listed in Leviticus 18 there is no class of cousins (a registering statement); the marriages of this chapter stand outside that list, and between the two texts there is no express contradiction. Scholarship has much discussion of the customary background of marriage within the clan in ancient Israel (marriage within the kin group, to preserve the inheritance and the wholeness of the family, was a common arrangement in the ancient Near East); this manuscript states the background side by side only and establishes no new thesis.

(Argument) Notice how the whole book is closed: not with a victory, not with a list of names, but with five women of whom it is said, "so did they". In Numbers chapter 1 God commands the numbering of the people, and the people do as commanded; in the last chapter God commands a boundary for a marriage, and the five daughters do as commanded. Across forty years in the wilderness this book has recorded a great deal of "would not" — the spies' report, the congregation's murmuring, Korah's company in rebellion, Moses' own striking of the rock — and at the place where the book closes, the narrator lets the whole book land on one quiet sentence of obedience. The grand narrative is gathered up into the daily keeping of the covenant: the inheritance was kept, the names were left behind (the five daughters' names are registered three times, at 26:33, 27:1, and in this chapter, verifiable in the surrounding text), and it was done not by great events but by five ordinary persons being willing, in the lifelong matter of marriage, to walk as commanded.

(Fact) Read the head and the tail of the whole book against each other once more (registered from the surrounding text): chapter 1 opens with the scene in the tabernacle in the wilderness of Sinai, God commanding Moses to number the males of twenty years old and upward who are able to go to war (registered in paraphrase); chapter 36 closes with the scene on the plains of Moab, and its content is a marriage rule and the obedience of five women. The opening numbers those who can fight; the closing guards the inheritance that can endure; the opening's objects are the males, the closing's protagonists are women. This is not to say that war is unimportant — in the registers of the numbering of the males, the males' totals are numbered out twice (chapters 1 and 26), and the second generation has already taken over the staff; it is to say that a book that opens with a war establishment has, at the end, something beyond the establishment to give an account of: inheritance, names, marriage, ordinary days.

(Argument) The English name of Numbers counts heads of people; its Hebrew name, however, is taken from its opening word, "in the wilderness". One book with two names answers exactly to these two strokes at its head and tail: God numbers His people in the wilderness, and at the wilderness's end He teaches them to keep the inheritance. That heads can be numbered clearly does not mean an inheritance can be kept; what this chapter supplies is precisely the lesson beyond the numbers.

(Fact) Verse 13 is the closing formula of the whole book, quoted verbatim in full from the settled text: "These are the commandments and the judgments, which the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses unto the children of Israel in the plains of Moab by Jordan near Jericho." (36:13) This verse supplies the scene in full at the book's very end: the plains of Moab, by Jordan, near Jericho — the same place as the command scenes registered at 35:1, 26:3, and elsewhere (registered in paraphrase from the surrounding text). Set against the closing formula of Leviticus 27:34 (registered): that book closes with words commanded to Moses "in mount Sinai"; this book closes on the plains of Moab — from Sinai to Moab is exactly the road this generation walked out. As for the scope the closing formula sums up, scholarship holds two readings — some take it for the whole book, some for the commands given on the plains of Moab (from chapter 26 onward) — stated side by side, and this manuscript does not adjudicate (see Endnote 12).

(Summary) Governing sentence for the part: the obedience sentence, the names, the result sentence, and the closing formula — four strokes close the book: the persons married within their own family, the inheritance remained within its own tribe, the words came from the LORD, and the book closes on the plains of Moab; in the last chapter of Numbers there is nothing left with its echo unfinished — the five daughters did as commanded, and the whole book is closed on that.

### Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

(Argument) Look back at these two chapters: one family, two presentations, two sentences of "right". The daughters present their case, God says right, and the inheritance is given to a household that has no son, so that their father's name is not cut off; the chiefs present their case, God again says right, and the inheritance remains in the tribe to which it belongs, and the five daughters' freedom is drawn with a boundary that can be kept. God did not stand on the daughters' side to press the chiefs down, nor stand on the chiefs' side to take the daughters' inheritance back — He stood on the side of the principle that the inheritance endures, and twice judged those presenting their case to be right, because what the two parties sought was in fact two faces of one and the same matter. Readers today, reading these two chapters, are often in haste to choose a side: either cheering for the breakthrough of chapter 27, or frowning at the restriction of chapter 36. The order of the text instead invites us to read the two chapters through together: freedom without a boundary, and the inheritance scatters in one generation; a boundary without freedom, and the law becomes a chain. "Let them marry to whom they think best; only to the family of the tribe of their father shall they marry." — may our families, our churches, and our lives learn both halves of this one sentence: within the boundary God has drawn, to be free to the full; and at the end of that freedom, to be willing to keep the boundary. This is how an inheritance is kept, how a name is left behind, and how a person walks the wilderness out and closes the book on the plains.

(Argument) One last stroke is left for the preacher himself: no one in this chapter does anything earth-shaking. The chiefs only reckoned the account clearly and presented the case plainly; Moses only transmitted the words and the verdict according to the word of the LORD; the five daughters only married as commanded, and kept as commanded. The closing of a whole book does not rest on heroes; it rests on a company of people willing to take detailed rules seriously. Of those who fell across the forty years in the wilderness, most were defeated not by great matters but by "what does this small thing matter?"; of those who stand firm on the plains, most win not in great matters but in "as it was commanded, so we do". Numbers leaves its last chapter to a marriage rule in order to nail this sentence to the lintel of the whole book: only those who can keep the detailed rules can enter the promised land.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotional use)

"Let them marry to whom they think best; only to the family of the tribe of their father shall they marry." (Num. 36:6, KJV) God's answer to the five daughters of Zelophehad speaks freedom first and the boundary second. The freedom is real: whom they marry is for them to decide themselves; no one assigns their marriages for them. The boundary is real too: the inheritance is to remain in their own family, and their father's name is to be carried on. The five did not complain about this boundary; the only sentence the text records is this: "Even as the LORD commanded Moses, so did the daughters of Zelophehad:" (Num. 36:10, KJV). In the end they married within their own family, the inheritance remained, and their names are recorded three times in the scroll, so that even today we can still read them out: Mahlah, Tirzah, Hoglah, Milcah, Noah. We often suppose that freedom and obedience must always sacrifice one of the two. God's order is in fact this: first He gives you the freedom — "to whom they think best" — and then He hands you the "only" — the choice inside the boundary is freer than drifting without a boundary. Today there is also in your hands an "inheritance" to be kept: a faith, a name, a family. Do not fear the boundary; the boundary is not there to take yours away from you, but to keep yours for you. Prayer: Lord, thank You for speaking freedom to us first, and thank You for speaking the boundary next. Teach us to make our choices gladly inside the boundary You have drawn — not envying the roads outside the boundary, not treating lightly the inheritance in our hands; may our obedience be as quiet and as whole as those five women's, and may what You have entrusted to us be, in our hands, not one piece less. Amen.

### Devotional Extract (English, KJV-based) — original English segment of the Chinese manuscript, reproduced verbatim

Read Numbers 36:6–7 (KJV): "This is the thing which the LORD doth command concerning the daughters of Zelophehad, saying, Let them marry to whom they think best; only to the family of the tribe of their father shall they marry. So shall not the inheritance of the children of Israel remove from tribe to tribe: for every one of the children of Israel shall keep himself to the inheritance of the tribe of his fathers." God's answer gives freedom first — "to whom they think best" — and the boundary second. The daughters obeyed, married within their tribe, and kept both their inheritance and their father's name. True freedom is not the absence of boundaries; it is a willing heart choosing, gladly, inside the lines God has drawn to keep what He has entrusted to us.

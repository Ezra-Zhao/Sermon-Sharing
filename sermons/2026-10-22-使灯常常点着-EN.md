## Summary (about 100 words)

The blueprint unfolds outward from the holy place: the first step for a man entering the court is the bronze altar — atonement comes before nearness; hangings of fine linen enclose a court, drawing set-apartness in the middle of the camp; and at the chapter's end the people bring beaten pure olive oil, and the priests tend the lamp from evening to morning, so that the lamp burns continually. The light in God's house is maintained together by the offering of the whole people and the keeping of the priests.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Let us first join the order of these chapters together in our hearts. In chapter 25, God commanded the making of the ark, the table of the bread of the Presence, and the golden lampstand — the furnishings innermost in the holy place; in chapter 26, He commanded the making of the tabernacle itself — how the curtains were to be woven, how the coverings were to be laid, how the boards were to stand, and how a veil was to divide the holy place from the most holy. By chapter 27 the camera has moved a large step outward: it no longer speaks of the furnishings inside the tabernacle, but of the court outside it — first an altar is set up in the court, then a circle of hangings is built around it, and at the very end, in just two verses, a seemingly small matter is commanded: bring oil, and light the lamp. This manuscript states the order first, because in the tabernacle chapters the order itself is the argument: God's blueprint is drawn from the inside out — first fix the centre, then fix the dwelling, then fix the court, layer by layer outward; but man's road of drawing near to God runs exactly the other way, from the outside in. A man comes from the camp, enters the court gate first, comes to the altar first, and only then — past the laver — comes to the holy place, and to the most holy behind the veil. In the building, the drawing runs from inside out; in the walking, the road runs from outside in; and these two directions, laid together, are precisely the lesson this chapter is to teach us.

Hold the contents of this chapter in hand first. Verses 1 to 8 are the bronze altar: an altar made of acacia wood, foursquare, five cubits long, five cubits broad, and three cubits high, with horns rising from its four corners, the whole altar overlaid with brass, its pans, shovels, basins, fleshhooks, and firepans all made of brass, a network of brass beneath it, rings of brass at its four corners, poles passed through the rings, and the altar hollow, made with boards. Verses 9 to 19 are the court of the tabernacle: on the south side and the north side, hangings of fine twined linen one hundred cubits long, pillars standing on sockets of brass with hooks and fillets of silver; fifty cubits on the west and fifty on the east; on the east a gate hanging of twenty cubits, of blue and purple and scarlet and fine twined linen, wrought with needlework; and all the vessels and pins of the court made of brass. Verses 20 to 21 are only two verses, yet they are the eye of the whole chapter: the people are to bring pure olive oil beaten for the light, to cause the lamp to burn always; Aaron and his sons, from evening to morning, are to order the lamp in the tabernacle of the congregation, outside the veil that is before the testimony; and this is to be a statute for ever to the generations of the children of Israel. The furnishings are finished, the court is finished, and at the last it lands on oil and lamp, on the word "always" — and so this manuscript's title is taken from here: Keep the Lamp Burning Continually.

In preaching this chapter, this manuscript still keeps the three rules laid down in the previous chapters. First, grasp the main line and do not spread out every inch: where the altar's brass rings were set, how many cubits apart the court's pillars stood — Scripture records these in fine detail, but this manuscript takes only the work and materials that carry the main line and preaches them through; it does not unfold every cubit. Second, symbolic uses must be identified by their level: the horns of the altar, the gate of the court, the pure oil — how later ages and the New Testament read them, this manuscript will mention, but every instance is identified as a later narrative, a New Testament reading, or homiletical exposition, and exposition is never passed off as the original, on-the-spot meaning of this chapter. Third, facts are distinguished from exposition and identified as such: what the text plainly says belongs to the text, structural observations belong to structure, applications belong to application — the three levels are never mixed into one. Holding these three rules, we can preach this chapter solidly, without stuffing our own impressions into the mouth of the text.

Let us read several key passages of today's text together:

"And thou shalt make an altar of shittim wood, five cubits long, and five cubits broad; the altar shall be foursquare: and the height thereof shall be three cubits. And thou shalt make the horns of it upon the four corners thereof: his horns shall be of the same: and thou shalt overlay it with brass." (Exodus 27:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And thou shalt make the court of the tabernacle: for the south side southward there shall be hangings for the court of fine twined linen of an hundred cubits long for one side." (Exodus 27:9, excerpt, KJV)

"And thou shalt command the children of Israel, that they bring thee pure oil olive beaten for the light, to cause the lamp to burn always. In the tabernacle of the congregation without the vail, which is before the testimony, Aaron and his sons shall order it from evening to morning before the LORD: it shall be a statute for ever unto their generations on the behalf of the children of Israel." (Exodus 27:20-21, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You prepared a dwelling for Yourself, and You also prepared for us the road of drawing near to You. Open our eyes today to see that altar standing in the court, and teach us not to talk of nearness while bypassing atonement; to see that enclosed court, and teach us that being set apart is no empty phrase; and to see that lamp burning continually — its oil brought by the people, its flame kept by the priests — and teach us each to do our part, so that the light in Your house does not go out. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Bronze Altar — The First Station on Entering the Court, Atonement First (27:1-8)

This section speaks of the altar first, because the altar is the first thing a man meets on entering the court. A man comes out of the camp, passes through the east gate of the court, and the first thing he sees is not the curtains of the tabernacle, nor the golden lampstand in the holy place, but a foursquare altar overlaid with brass, with fire on it and blood beside it. Fix this position clearly: the altar stands in the court; it is the first station for one who enters. The people could not bypass the altar and go straight for the tabernacle, and the priests could not skip the altar and enter the holy place directly. The first step on the road inward is not at the innermost point, but in the court; not in the place of shining, but in the place of bleeding. This summary is a structural explanation drawn from the spatial fact of the altar's standing in the court in this chapter, together with the general order in the later sacrificial ordinances, in which the offering was first presented at the altar and only then did the priest enter the holy place to serve; its root lies in the layout of the text, and it is identified here. With the order stated clearly, what follows can stand firm: altar, laver, holy place — the laver is commanded only in chapter 30, standing between the tabernacle and the altar, where the priests, when they went into the tabernacle or came near the altar to minister, were first to wash their hands and their feet (Exodus 30:17-21, cited in sense; this manuscript mentions it here only to give the order, and does not develop the laver). That is to say, a man's road of drawing near to God is set out by Scripture in full clarity: first through the altar, then through the washing, and only then into the holy place. Atonement comes before nearness — this is the order, and it is also the fact, and this manuscript faces it directly and does not soften it: without sacrifice, without covering, a man stops in the court and cannot go in. These are not frightening words invented by preaching; they are the words the floor plan of the tabernacle itself speaks.

Now look at the making of the altar. The text says: thou shalt make an altar of shittim wood[1]. Acacia is still the local wood of the wilderness, the same material as the ark and the table; the altar was to be foursquare, five cubits long, five cubits broad, and three cubits high. Five cubits is roughly two metres twenty today, and three cubits roughly one metre thirty — a foursquare altar, not small, yet not extravagant either, its height just fit for a man standing at its side to offer sacrifice. On its four corners horns were to be made, of the same piece with the altar. "Horn," in the original, is qeren (קֶרֶן, H7161), a horn, the horn of an animal [2]. These four horns were not ornaments nailed on; they were made of one piece with the body of the altar, rising up from its four corners. The whole altar was to be overlaid with brass; "brass," in the original, is nechoshet (נְחֹשֶׁת, H5178) [3]. Note the distinction in materials: the furnishings inside the holy place were mostly of pure gold, but the altar in the court was overlaid throughout with brass, and even the altar's utensils — the pans to receive the ashes, the shovels, the basins for the blood, the fleshhooks, and the firepans — were all of brass. Brass endures fire, brass stands burning; gold shines in the Holy of Holies, brass endures the fire in the court. This is the factual layer of material fitted to place, and this manuscript states only the fact: the text plainly says that the inner things were mostly gold and the altar outside was brass; the many likenings of gold and brass to nearness and distance between God and man drawn in later preaching are a common homiletical use which this manuscript does not adopt.

For the lower half of the altar, the text commanded a network, like a grating, of brass, with four rings of brass at its four corners; the poles were to be made of acacia wood, overlaid with brass, and put into the rings, with which the altar was to be carried. And of the altar's interior the text gives a particular instruction: the altar was hollow, made with boards. Why hollow? The text gives no further explanation, and this manuscript presents the common views on the workmanship together without deciding: hollow, it was easier to carry — when the wilderness journey began, two men could lift it and go; some commentators also say that a hollow altar, once set in its place, could be filled with earth or stones and a fire kindled within it. This manuscript takes the factual layer of the carrying view — poles always ready and a hollow altar were both provision for the wilderness journeys — and presents the other view alongside as the commentary tradition. Whichever view is taken, one sentence the text states plainly must not be missed: it was to be made with boards, after the fashion shown on the mountain; verse 8 says, as it was shewed thee in the mount, so shall they make it. Even the altar in the court was not left to the craftsmen's own discretion; the rule of building according to the pattern governs from the Holy of Holies all the way out to the court — this is the consistent line of this chapter with the two before it, and this manuscript marks it here.

Once the altar was made, what was it to do in the court? This chapter commands only its making; it does not yet command the ordinances of sacrifice — those will not be fully stated until Leviticus. But the altar's use was no stranger to the reader within the book: as early as Exodus chapter 20, God commanded that an altar be built for Him, with burnt offerings and peace offerings offered upon it; and in chapter 24, at the cutting of the covenant, Moses first built an altar and sprinkled half of the blood of the covenant upon it. From the beginning the altar was the place of sacrifice and atonement, the place where blood was sprinkled and offerings were burned. This manuscript makes here only an intertextual note within the book: the altar of this chapter replaces the earlier temporary altars of earth and stone with a brass altar made according to the pattern, portable, its place fixed in the court of the tabernacle, becoming from then on the formal place of all sacrifice. Once an offering lay on the altar, the people in the court knew one thing: a man does not come before God empty-handed, and still less may he rush in bearing his sin; the sin must first be dealt with at the altar before he can walk further in. This sentence is homiletical exposition drawn from the altar's position and the general order of sacrifice, and is identified here; but its root is firm — on the floor plan the altar stands first, and the order itself is preaching this sentence.

The meaning of the horns of the altar — this manuscript will handle it with particular care, distinguishing this chapter from what came later. Back to this chapter: the horns were simply part of the altar, made of one piece with it, and the text in this chapter does not explain their use; only later, in the sacrificial ordinances, is it stated plainly that the blood of the sin offering was to be put upon the horns of the altar (Leviticus 4, cited in sense) — the horns were the most prominent part of the altar, and the part most touched by blood. Later still, in the days of the kings, the horns grew another layer of meaning in the narratives: a man who had sinned and was pursued would flee into the sanctuary and lay hold on the horns of the altar, and that was a plea for refuge, a plea for mercy. Adonijah, his plot exposed and fearing Solomon, arose and went and caught hold on the horns of the altar, and Solomon pardoned him and told him to go to his house (1 Kings 1:50-53, cited in sense); Joab fled to the tabernacle of the LORD and caught hold on the horns of the altar, and afterwards was still executed according to his sin (1 Kings 2:28-34, cited in sense). In bringing these two matters together, this manuscript must identify the level: laying hold on the horns of the altar for refuge is a use that appears only in the later narratives; it is not something Exodus 27 says of itself on the spot. In this chapter the altar is simply the altar, and the horns are simply its four horns. Yet read in both layers together, a line can still be seen: when a man had nowhere left to run, the direction of his flight was the altar, and nothing else; and what he could grasp was the horn of that altar over which blood had flowed and atonement had been made. A man who caught hold on the horns of the altar had settled one thing in his heart — that in the very place of judgment there is, of all places, mercy; and in the place where he deserved to die under sentence there is, of all places, a way to live. This layer is a homiletical use drawn from the later narratives, preached with its level identified, and not read back as this chapter's original meaning. The New Testament nowhere directly cites the horns of the altar as teaching, and this manuscript will not force a Christological likeness here either; it keeps only the proportion: the order of the altar is the original meaning, refuge at the horns is the later narratives' use, and the reflection that mercy is found in the place of judgment is homiletical exposition — three levels kept apart, and the altar is preached with weight, and without excess.

The altar is finished; now let us walk around the circuit of the court. However rightly the altar stands, if no court encloses it, the boundary line of a man's road from the camp to the altar remains blurred; and God's next command is precisely to draw that boundary line clearly.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Court of the Tabernacle — Set-Apartness Drawn in the Middle of the Camp (27:9-19)

This section speaks of the court. The court was an open space enclosed by a circle of hangings, one hundred cubits long, fifty cubits broad, and five cubits high, with the tabernacle standing in its western half and the bronze altar in its eastern half, between the court gate and the door of the tabernacle. Set the dimensions out first: one hundred cubits is roughly forty-five metres today, fifty cubits roughly twenty-two or twenty-three metres, and five cubits high roughly two metres twenty — a little higher than a man; standing outside the court, one could see the upper half of the tabernacle, but could not see the altar or the door of the tabernacle within. This height is a fact the text gives; this manuscript adds one structural observation in passing: the court was not a city wall, and was not made to resist an enemy; the court was a boundary line, separating seeing from entering — those outside knew that what lay within was holy, but could not simply step across and in. The use of a boundary lies not in holding off an army but in declaring a separation; this sentence is homiletical exposition, rooted in the material fact that the hangings were only five cubits high and woven of fine linen — identified first, and then preached.

The making of the hangings the text gives side by side, beginning with the south. On the south side the hangings were one hundred cubits long, made of fine twined linen. "Hangings," in the original, is qela'im (קְלָעִים, H7050), hangings, curtains [4]. The south hangings had twenty pillars, the pillars standing on twenty sockets of brass, and the hooks and fillets of the pillars made of silver. The north side was exactly the same as the south: one hundred cubits, twenty pillars, sockets of brass and hooks and fillets of silver. The west side was the court's back wall: hangings of fifty cubits, ten pillars, ten sockets of brass. The east side was likewise fifty cubits, but divided into three parts: on each side hangings of fifteen cubits with three pillars each, and in the middle twenty cubits left open for the gate of the court. Look at the provision for the four sides as a whole: the pillars all stood on sockets of brass, the hooks and fillets were all of silver, and only the hangings themselves were white cloth of fine linen — white, silver, and brass: these three colours were the whole palette of the court's circuit. This manuscript takes only one point tied to the main line: the altar in the court was of brass, and the sockets and bands of the court were mostly of brass; the further in one went, the more costly the metal; this layer, the court, was a world of brass, the first layer of separation for a man coming in from the camp. This observation is a structural explanation drawn from this section's list of materials, identified here, and it does not spread out the numbers pillar by pillar.

Only the gate hanging had needlework — this is a distinction in this section at which we must pause. The text says that the hanging for the gate of the court was to be of blue and purple and scarlet and fine twined linen, wrought with needlework, twenty cubits long and five cubits high, the same as the court's hangings, with four pillars and four sockets of brass. All around, the hangings were the natural colour of fine linen; only at the gate, in one place, were the colours of the tabernacle's curtains used, and needlework used. Why was the gate alone given needlework? The text states no reason plainly, and this manuscript offers homiletical exposition with its level identified: the gate is the place of coming and going, the most used and most conspicuous place in the whole court; to set the finest workmanship at the entrance is to place a mark at the opening of the boundary — here one may enter, but this is no casual gap; this is the proper gate, an entrance left open according to the rule. A man entering would first see the colours and the needlework, and his heart would first be prepared before he crossed the threshold. This reading is exposition, not something the text states plainly; the fact the text states plainly is only one: white plainness all around, and needlework at the gate alone. Hold that fact fast, and the exposition will not drift.

The meaning of the court must be seen against the whole panorama of the camp. Once the tabernacle was set up, the camp of Israel was arranged in this way: the tents of the twelve tribes were pitched all around, in the middle were the tents of the Levites, and in the middle of the Levites was the court enclosed by hangings, and in the middle of the court, the tabernacle (the arrangement of the camp in Numbers chapters 2-3, cited in sense; this manuscript gives it here as an intertextual panorama). That is to say, from the door of any Israelite's tent, the gaze passed through his own tribe's camp, through the Levites' camp, and stopped at last at that circle of white hangings. God's court was drawn in the very middle of the camp. This manuscript joins this line to Exodus chapter 19: at the foot of Sinai, God had commanded Moses to set bounds round the mountain, and the people were not to touch the mountain; whoever touched it was to be put to death (Exodus 19:12-13, cited in sense). Then, the boundary was drawn at the foot of a mountain, so that the people would know that God's holiness could not be approached carelessly, could not be offended. Now the boundary has been drawn into the middle of the camp — and what is used is not ropes and warnings, but a circle of fine linen hangings and a gate: the boundary remains, the holiness remains, yet a gate has been opened, and inside the gate stands an altar. The same line of set-apartness has moved from the foot of the mountain into the camp, from "do not touch" to "enter by the proper gate, through the altar." This contrast is exegetical explanation drawn from the two chapters: the fact of a boundary is present in both places; the opening of a gate is the new fact of this chapter; read together, they show that God's heart was never to keep men outside for ever, but to lead men in according to the order. Being set apart was never a matter of shutting the world outside the gate and being done; being set apart is to enclose a piece of ground in the very middle of the people's daily life, marking that this ground belongs to God, and that here men are to come and go, to act, and to worship according to God's rule.

The gate of the court faced east — a gate hanging of twenty cubits on one side — and this too is a structure the text sets out plainly. Around the whole court — one hundred plus fifty, fifty plus fifty — this one place in the middle of the east side alone was a gate; the south, north, and west sides were all whole hangings, with no second gate, no back gate, no side gate. Stating the structure honestly, it stops here: there was only one place of entry. For exposition this manuscript keeps restraint, adding only one sentence and identifying it as such: God opened only one way into the court, not because He was grudging, but because the order can have only one line — if the road of altar first, laver next, holy place last had many entrances, the order would fall into confusion, and men would choose a shortcut that bypassed the altar and come in that way. Only this one sentence of exposition, and no further: the gate of the court is not directly equated here with any New Testament teaching on the door and preached at length; later, the Lord Jesus said, I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved (John 10:9, excerpt) — that is the New Testament's own plain teaching, and this manuscript notes it here in one sentence of contrast, without mixing the two passages into one level: Exodus records the floor plan of a court; the New Testament speaks of the door of salvation; each level belongs to its own level, and the reader may himself feel the correspondence between them.

At the end of this section, the text gathers up the court's smaller pieces in one closing sentence: all the vessels of the tabernacle and the court, and all the pins of the court, were to be made of brass. The pins were the stakes driven into the ground, holding fast the cords of the hangings and the tabernacle; the most inconspicuous things of all, yet they decided whether the hangings stood steady and whether the tabernacle was pitched true. Even the pins had their material fixed and were counted in the blueprint — of this detail this manuscript notes only one sentence: in God's blueprint there is no part that does not count, just as in God's house there is no person who does not count. This sentence is homiletical exposition, rooted in the textual fact that the vessels and the pins are listed together in the command, and is identified here. The court is enclosed, the altar is standing, and by rights the blueprint could close here; but at the last God added two more verses, commanding a small thing that had to be done every day — bring oil, light the lamp. These two verses are the eye of the whole chapter, and we will take them slowly.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Oil for the Ever-Burning Lamp — The Offering of the Whole People and the Keeping of the Priests (27:20-21)

This section has only two verses, yet it can carry the whole chapter — because it stretches time out long. The eighteen verses before it all speak of making: make once, and it is done; an altar well made could serve for many years, and hangings well woven could hang for many years. But these last two verses speak of every day: oil was to be brought continually, the lamp tended night by night, from evening to morning, not one day allowed to break. The blueprint, at its very end, lands on daily life, lands on "always" — this is the first reminder the chapter's structure gives us, and this manuscript identifies it first as a structural observation: the whole chapter begins with furnishings made once and finished, and closes with a work to be done daily; the word "always" is precisely where the eye of the chapter lies.

The text says: And thou shalt command the children of Israel, that they bring thee pure oil olive beaten for the light, to cause the lamp to burn always. Note first where the oil came from: the oil was brought by the people. It was not prepared by Moses himself, nor produced by Aaron's house; the command was to the children of Israel — to the whole people — to bring the oil. The lampstand was commanded in chapter 25, hammered out of a talent of pure gold, its seven lamps standing in the holy place; however fine the lampstand, without oil it could not be lit; and however much oil there was, without a man to tend it, the lamp would still go out. Here God assigned the duty of supplying the oil to the whole people. This manuscript first sets out the straight statement of the text's structure: the lamp was not self-supplied by the priests; it was the people's oil. One step further is homiletical exposition, and this manuscript identifies it separately here: the light in God's house is maintained together by the offering of the whole people and the keeping of the priests — if the people do not bring oil, the priests have no oil to light; if the priests do not tend it, the people's oil cannot burn through one night either. The light of the church was never the business of a few, nor a duty the many can shrug off once pushed away; some give oil, some keep the lamp, and neither can be lacking. This layer of application is exposition read directly out of this section's division of labour between supplying oil and tending, its root in the text's own assignment of duties — it may be preached with confidence, but its level must still be identified.

Now look at the quality of the oil: pure oil olive beaten. The pure oil, in the original phrase, is shemen katit (שֶׁמֶן כָּתִית, shemen, H8081, oil; katit, H3795, beaten) [5] — referring specifically to the first oil gained by beating and crushing the olives and letting the oil seep out of itself, not oil pressed out by heavy pressing in a press. Beating and pressing are different ways of gaining oil, and the clearness of the oil differs too: beaten oil was the purest, with the least sediment, burning with little smoke and a steady light. Why was the text so particular about the oil? The text states no reason plainly, and this manuscript offers only an explanation of the workmanship's facts, and restrained exposition: the holy place had no windows; if the lamp smoked heavily, it would smoke first at the priests serving inside, and then at the veil and the furnishings; to use the purest oil was so that the light would stay bright for long, and the holy place not be wrapped in smoke all day. The exposition adds only one sentence: of the things offered to God, what God wanted was not the most, but the purest — when the people brought oil, they were not competing over who brought the most, but over whether the oil was pure. This sentence is homiletical exposition, rooted in the workmanship fact of the word katit, and is identified here. The proportion must also be stated clearly: this manuscript does not turn the beaten olives into a hard likeness of a suffering heart, of the kind common in later preaching; such likenesses are occasionally used in sermons, but this manuscript does not adopt them, lest the likeness cover over the lexical fact.

To cause the lamp to burn always. The ever-burning lamp, in the original, is ner tamid (נֵר תָּמִיד, ner, H5216, a lamp; tamid, H8548, continually, without ceasing) [6]. This word tamid is no stranger in the tabernacle's setting: the bread of the Presence always set out on the table used this same word "always"; and the high priest's breastplate and plate had their own "always" requirements. "Always," here, is not a matter of often remembering in feeling; it is a matter of institution, of never ceasing: oil was to be replenished, wicks were to be trimmed, and the lamp was to have a man tending it night by night. Verse 21 fixes the tending's duty, person, place, and time all firm: the place — in the tabernacle of the congregation, without the veil that is before the testimony, that is, in the holy place, where the golden lampstand stood, outside the veil and before the ark of the testimony; the persons — Aaron and his sons, the priestly house that in the next chapter would be set apart and clothed in the holy garments; the time — from evening to morning, they were to order it before the LORD. "Order it" — the original word is 'arakh (עָרַךְ), to set in order, to arrange, to tend; every evening the priest set the lamp in order, added oil, and trimmed the wick, so that the lamp would not go out through the night, and at daybreak would still be burning. A lamp does not stay lit by being lit and left alone; a lamp is tended into burning: whether enough oil was added, whether the wick was trimmed even, whether the lamp was wiped clean — all of it lay in the hands of the man who tended it. This manuscript notes one sentence of exposition and identifies it: behind a lamp that burns always stands a man who is never absent, night by night; the light in God's house looks continual, but underneath it is laid this: that someone did the ordinary work of tending for a whole lifetime. Later, when Eli was old and the lamp of God had not yet gone out, on the night Samuel lay in the temple of the LORD (1 Samuel 3:3, cited in sense), it is often read as this lamp's echo in history: the lamp had not yet gone out, because the rule of keeping the lamp still stood, and the oil was still being replenished. This manuscript mentions it here in one intertextual sentence only, identified as a later narrative echo, and does not develop it as this chapter's original meaning.

At last, the closing sentence of these two verses must be read word by word: it shall be a statute for ever unto their generations on the behalf of the children of Israel. "A statute for ever" — in the original, a formulation for an ordinance of lasting validity. Altars would grow old, hangings would tear, the tabernacle was later replaced by the temple, and the temple later fell as well; but this matter of bringing oil and lighting the lamp God fixed as a statute for all generations — meaning: as long as God's house still stands, the lamp must not go out and the oil must not fail. Here this manuscript gathers the whole chapter together: from chapter 25 to chapter 27, by this point in the blueprint the furnishings are complete, the dwelling is complete, the court is complete, and even the oil that makes the holy place shine and the hands appointed to keep the lamp are complete. What is still lacking? People are lacking — people clothed in holy garments, standing between the altar and the lamp to serve. And so the next chapter, chapter 28, opens by speaking no longer of furnishings but of the garments of the priests: ephod, breastplate, robe, and plate, put piece by piece upon Aaron. The structural observation is identified here: God first prepares the place, and then prepares the people who will serve; first the altar and the lamp, then the man standing between altar and lamp. This order still speaks audibly to the church today, and that belongs to homiletical exposition: do not rush first to find people and arrange a scene; ask first whether the place is prepared — whether the altar of atonement stands true, whether the boundary of set-apartness is clear, whether the lamp still burns; when the place is right, a man clothed in the garments can stand within it, and the service will stand firm. The blueprint, at the end of chapter 27, pauses on the words "to burn always," and leaves the people of the next chapter for the next lesson.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today, from the altar in the court, to the hangings of the court, and at last to the lamp in the holy place, we have learned three things. First, the order of drawing near: a man's first station on entering the court is the bronze altar — altar, laver, holy place — atonement comes before nearness; the order may not be reversed, and may not be bypassed; the altar's four horns were made of one piece with the altar, and that men later caught hold on the horns of the altar pleading for refuge shows precisely that the place of judgment keeps mercy. Second, the boundary of separation: hangings of fine linen enclosed the court in the very middle of the camp, and set-apartness moved from the boundary at the foot of Sinai into the centre of the people's daily life; white plainness all around, needlework at the gate alone, and only one place of entry, on the east — the boundary was clear, the way in was open, and the way in was also the only one. Third, the ever-burning lamp: the oil was the beaten pure oil the people brought, the lamp was tended by the priests from evening to morning, and the light in God's house was maintained together by the offering of the whole people and the keeping of the priests, fixed as a statute for ever to all generations. The furnishings, the dwelling, and the court are all complete; the next step, God will prepare the people who serve. May our generation also each stand in our own place: those who should bring oil, bring pure oil; those who should keep the lamp, keep it through the night — so that the lamp burns continually, until the morning.

Let us pray together: Lord, we thank You that You have drawn the road of drawing near to You so clearly, step by step. Teach us not to bypass the altar — willing first to confess sin before You and receive covering, and only then to walk further in; teach us not to pull down the boundary — willing in the very middle of life to be set apart for You; and teach us not to despise that small thing that must be done every day — willing to bring pure oil, willing to tend night by night, and to keep that lamp. If our oil is mixed with dregs, cleanse it; if our wick is crooked, trim it; if we who keep the lamp are drowsing, wake us. Let the light in Your house, in our generation, still burn continually. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And thou shalt command the children of Israel, that they bring thee pure oil olive beaten for the light, to cause the lamp to burn always." (Exodus 27:20, excerpt, KJV) "It shall be a statute for ever unto their generations on the behalf of the children of Israel." (Exodus 27:21, excerpt, KJV)

Why could the lamp in the holy place burn always? The answer does not lie in how costly the lampstand was — it was of pure gold, that is true; the answer lies in oil that never failed and a keeper who never absented himself. The oil was brought household by household, and not just any oil pressed in haste, but the first oil that seeped, drop by drop, from olives beaten and crushed — so pure that it burned without black smoke. The lamp was kept night by night, from evening to morning: oil added, wick trimmed, all set in order, so that when morning came the lamp was still burning and the night could be called kept. Your life of prayer is, in truth, just such a lamp: it will not burn for a whole year on one great meeting's emotion; it burns because of that little pure oil each day and that one tending each day. What is the pure oil? The small, unmixed honesty of the heart — coming before God without acting, without pretence, saying as much as there is, confessing sin cleanly, giving thanks cleanly. What is the tending? Spending a few minutes before sleep laying the day out before God — what must be confessed, confessed; what must be handed over, handed over; the wick trimmed short, so that tomorrow it can burn again. If you feel your lamp is going out, do not blame the lampstand first; look and see whether the oil is still being brought and the lamp still being tended. You can begin again tonight: bring a little pure oil, tend the lamp once — for of the man who keeps the lamp burning always, God has said: it is a statute for ever, to all generations; and in your generation, it counts too.

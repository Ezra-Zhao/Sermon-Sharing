## Summary (About 100 Words)

As Jesus went to Jerusalem, passing between Samaria and Galilee, ten men with leprosy stood at a distance and cried aloud for mercy. The Lord did not lay His hand on them at once; He only told them to go and show themselves to the priests, and as they went, they were cleansed. One of them, seeing that he was healed, came back, praising God with a loud voice, and fell down and worshipped with thanksgiving. Jesus asked where the nine were, and told this man who had returned: your faith has saved you. This sermon looks at three things: the cry of those who stood at a distance, the obedience of being cleansed on the road, and the deeper grace received only by the one who came back to give thanks.

## (00:00 - 06:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you on Thanksgiving!

In today's story, ten people were healed, but only nine are remembered simply as having been healed. The remaining one is remembered for something beyond his name: he came back. The words "came back" are ordinarily so common: coming back from work, coming back from a trip, a child coming home. But in this passage, coming back is an act of worship, an act of acknowledgment, and a receiving that is deeper than healing. At Thanksgiving we count our blessings across a whole table. Today, first count this: among those who have received grace, how many have come back to give thanks? And which group do we ourselves belong to?

Let us read the whole passage aloud:

"And it came to pass, as he went to Jerusalem, that he passed through the midst of Samaria and Galilee. And as he entered into a certain village, there met him ten men that were lepers, which stood afar off: And they lifted up their voices, and said, Jesus, Master, have mercy on us. And when he saw them, he said unto them, Go shew yourselves unto the priests. And it came to pass, that, as they went, they were cleansed. And one of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, and with a loud voice glorified God, And fell down on his face at his feet, giving him thanks: and he was a Samaritan. And Jesus answering said, Were there not ten cleansed? but where are the nine? There are not found that returned to give glory to God, save this stranger. And he said unto him, Arise, go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole." (Luke 17:11-19, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, the grace we have received this year is more than we can count. Today, please stop our feet as we hurry forward, and teach us to be the one who comes back: to come back and give glory to You, to come back and fall down and thank You, and to come back and receive Your deeper salvation. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (06:00 - 17:00) Part One: Those Who Stood at a Distance — Crying for Mercy, and a Command Whose Effect Had Not Yet Been Seen (17:11-14)

Jesus was going to Jerusalem. Luke deliberately reminds us of the direction: this journey was toward the cross. And it was on this very road that He still stopped to listen to the cries of ten sick men. He passed through the borderland between Samaria and Galilee, a marginal zone in Jewish eyes — mixed, awkward, and not respectable. Grace often stops in just such a zone: not at the center, but at the border; not in the living room of respectable people, but at the entrance of a village.

Ten men with leprosy came to meet Him, standing at a distance. Standing at a distance was required by the law, and it was also the shame of their disease: they could not draw near to people, could not go home, could not enter the synagogue; they could only live together at the edge of the crowd. Among the ten there were Jews and also a Samaritan. Ordinarily the two peoples had no dealings with each other, but disease had formed them into one company. Suffering sometimes has this effect: it tears down the walls between people and lets them first stand together in the identity of the sick. They cried with a loud voice: Jesus, Master, have mercy on us! In this cry there were no conditions and no bargaining. It meant only this: we have no way left; You are the only way. They called Him Master; they had not yet called Him Lord. Yet this one cry, "have mercy on us," was already the beginning of faith: they were convinced that this Jesus could hear, and that He was willing to have mercy.

Jesus saw them and said to them: Go, show yourselves to the priests. Please notice the order in which the Lord worked: He did not first declare them healed, He did not lay His hand on them, and He gave no immediate evidence. He first gave a command, and a command that sounded premature. According to the law, a person with leprosy was to be examined by a priest after being cleansed; the priest would inspect and declare him clean, and only then could he return to the community. But at this moment they still bore the disease on their bodies, and already they were to go to the priests. This step was a step of faith: first obey, then see; first set out on the road, then be cleansed. And as they went, they were cleansed — in the original, katharizo [3], to be cleansed. The cleansing happened as they went, on the road, in that stretch when their feet had already stepped forward but their eyes had not yet seen any change. If they had stood where they were arguing, waiting until the disease was healed before going, they would never have reached that moment. Brothers and sisters, how many of our prayers are stuck right here: Lord, first let me see, and then I will obey. But the order in this passage is reversed: you go first, and cleansing waits for you on the road. Is there this year in your hand a command the Lord has already spoken, which you have been slow to walk out? Reconciliation, giving, forsaking a sin, accepting a post — take the first step first; grace is often revealed on the road.

Notice also that all ten of them obeyed, and all ten were cleansed. The Lord's mercy was not discounted: not one of those who cried out was left out. Common grace first comes to all; the sun first shines on the good and the evil, and healing was first given to ten. This is precisely the background of the next section: since all ten received it, why did only one come back? The problem is not whether grace is enough; the problem is where a person goes after receiving grace.

Think again about how those ten felt as they set out. They walked while still bearing the disease. Their clothes were still old, and there was not yet any sign of improvement on their bodies. What would the people around them say? The disease is not yet healed; why go to the priests — is that not asking for humiliation? But they did not wait for the talk to die down; the road they walked was a road watched by others. Obedience often costs this price: before the effect appears, you first bear the misunderstanding of others. Brothers and sisters, if for the step you have taken in obedience to the Lord you have not yet seen cleansing for a while, do not turn back. The text fixes the timing precisely: as they went, they were cleansed — not before setting out, not after arriving, but on the road as they went, step by step, until their skin was made new. Many graces are walked into, not waited into. This Thanksgiving, rather than sitting and taking stock of what is still lacking, take ten steps first for a command you have already heard. Perhaps at the tenth step, you will see that you have already been healed.

Do not overlook that stretch when they walked together either. Ten people of different backgrounds cried out together, walked together, and were healed together. Suffering had gathered them into one company, and grace also came to that company together. The church should be just such a place: people of different backgrounds, different peoples, and different accents standing together because of the same need, and being healed together because of the same Lord. Sadly, in the second half of the story, this company scattered. It scattered not because of quarrelling, but because each went home. After receiving grace, our most natural way of dispersing is for each to go home; the most unnatural — and yet the most important — is for someone among them to go against the flow of people and run back to the village entrance. Today's message is preached for that stretch of going against the flow.

## (17:00 - 30:00) Part Two: Seeing That He Was Healed, He Came Back — Thanksgiving Is Recognizing the One Who Gave the Grace (17:15-16)

One of them, when he saw that he was healed, came back. The hinge of this sentence is the word "saw." All ten were healed; all ten pairs of eyes saw their own skin. But only one person saw more than the fact that the disease was healed. He saw grace; he saw that this healing was not an accident, not luck, but something done by the Lord who stood at the village entrance. Seeing grace and seeing a result are two levels of seeing. Many people see only the result: the disease is healed, the deal is closed, the exam is passed, the child has come home. This one who came back saw one more level: who did it. The starting point of thanksgiving is precisely this extra level of seeing: if you regard grace as luck, you will only celebrate; if you see clearly who gave the grace, you will come back.

The text gives three actions for the way he came back. First, with a loud voice he glorified God. When they came, the ten of them cried aloud for mercy; when he returned, he alone glorified God with a loud voice. The voice was just as loud; the content was completely changed: the voice of crying out reaches upward to ask, the voice of thanksgiving reaches upward to offer. To glorify God is to send the credit back to heaven: not to credit your own good fortune, not to credit the priest's quick hands, but to credit God. Second, he fell down at the feet of Jesus. The man who had stood at a distance now fell down at His feet: the distance had completely changed. From standing afar off to his face pressed to the ground — this is the whole journey by which grace brings a person near. Third, he gave Him thanks. "To give thanks" is, in the original, eucharisteo [1], that is, to thank, to give thanks; this word later became the church's core vocabulary for remembering the Lord's grace. The object of his thanksgiving was very specific: he did not thank fate, and he did not thank himself for persevering well; fallen down, he thanked the person Jesus. The place where Thanksgiving most easily goes astray is turning thanksgiving into a feeling: I feel very grateful in my heart. Thanksgiving in the Bible is not a feeling; it is an action: coming back, falling down, crying out, giving glory. A feeling stays where it is; only an action can walk to the Lord's feet.

Only at this point does the text reveal his identity: this man was a Samaritan. Luke presses this sentence to the end, like lifting a lid. In the earlier part, the ten stood together without distinction of people; only here is it made plain: the one who came back was the outsider who was least thought of. In Jewish eyes Samaritans were mixed, unclean, and theologically astray, yet it was he who did the most right thing among the ten. For those who grew up sitting in the synagogue, this is a gentle warning: being near grace does not equal knowing how to be grateful; knowing the rules does not equal recognizing the Lord who gives grace. Sometimes an outsider, having received mercy only once, carries thanksgiving all the way through; insiders, having received too much, instead treat grace as routine. Brothers and sisters, what we have received this year is probably far more than this Samaritan received. Is our coming back enough to count?

Note also that his coming back had a cost. If he had continued on to the priests, he could have completed the formalities as soon as possible, returned to the community, and embraced his family. Coming back meant that he first set the formalities aside and first saw the One who had given the grace. He put the order right: first give glory, then handle the formalities; first see the Lord, then go home. We often say: when I have finished this busy stretch, I will properly give thanks, properly worship, and properly accompany my family in serving the Lord. But the formalities of grace are never finished; once thanksgiving is put last, it often gets put away altogether. This one man teaches us: thanksgiving must cut in line; it must rush ahead of celebration and ahead of reunion, and come first to the Lord's feet.

Look more closely at the road by which he came back. When he came, ten people shouted with loud voices; when he returned, one person sang loudly. When he came, he stood at a distance and did not even dare to draw near; when he returned, he fell down at His feet with his face pressed to the ground. When he came, he called Jesus Master — that was a term of respect; when he returned, he no longer called Him anything at all; he spoke with his whole body: glorifying, falling down, giving thanks. True thanksgiving in the end always turns from words into posture: the knees bend, the head bows, the hands open. In our churches, fewer and fewer people fall down in worship, and those who raise their hands are more and more reserved, but this Samaritan reminds us: for a person who has received great grace, the body reacts before reason does. He had never learned the liturgy of worship; he only knew too well where he had originally stood, and he knew too well who had brought him to these feet.

There is one more thing worth remembering: when he came back, the Lord was still there. The Lord had not hurried on down the road; He stood at the village entrance and waited for this one man who came back. We often worry that we have returned too late: a vow made in that year has dragged on for three years, the person we should have thanked is no longer there, the season for turning back has been missed. But the Lord still stands there. He asked where the nine were, which shows that He had been counting all along, and waiting all along. As long as you are willing to turn around, the village entrance is not far; as long as you are willing to come back, falling down is not too late. Today is the day of the journey back. Do not speak again of next year, next time, or when there is time. Grace has waited for you this long; do not make thanksgiving wait for it any longer.

## (30:00 - 39:00) Part Three: Where Are the Nine? — Between Being Cleansed and Receiving Salvation, There Is Still One Return (17:17-19)

Jesus' response is first a string of questions: Were not ten cleansed? Where are the nine? Was no one found who returned to give glory to God except this stranger? The Lord asked three questions in a row, each carrying regret. That word — "stranger" — is, in the original, allogenes [4], a foreigner, a man of another people. The Lord Himself pointed out this identity not to shame anyone, but so that all who heard would remember: the one who returned to give glory was, of all people, this foreigner. The Lord counted: ten, one, nine absent. The quietest sound in this passage is the silence of those nine. They had done no great evil: they had obeyed, they had gone to the priests, and no doubt they were glad and went home to be reunited with their families. But they were absent from thanksgiving. Absence is the lightest, yet most common, form of indebtedness assigned in this passage: not rejecting grace, but receiving grace and walking away, leaving the One who gave the grace at the village entrance.

Let us not rush to scold the nine. First, compare ourselves: when we asked, we cried aloud together as ten; after we were healed, are we one of the nine? The vow made at the bedside — do you still remember it after the disease is healed? The prayer made in the hard pass — do you still count it after the pass is crossed? The peace sought before a child was born — after the child is born healthy, a few years later, to whom have we given the credit? When the Lord asks where the nine are, He is in fact also asking today's congregation: this year you have received no small amount of cleansing, provision, and deliverance from danger. How many times have you come back? Thanksgiving is not a program for a festival; it is the return ticket of those who have received grace. Without the return journey, the story of grace is only half told.

Then the Lord said to this man who had returned the weightiest sentence in the whole passage — in the KJV words we have just read: Arise, go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole. Hear the verb underneath the rendering: "hath made thee whole" is, in the Greek, sesoken se, from sozo [2] — the New Testament's ordinary word for "to save," the very word the Chinese Union Version renders "has saved you." The point of this sermon stands on that Greek word, and it is right to say it plainly: your faith has saved you. All ten were cleansed; only this one heard, "has saved you." Cleansing belongs to the body; salvation is wholeness: sins forgiven, reconciliation with God, entry into His kingdom. What the nine received was the removal of disease; what this one received was, beyond the removal of disease, a Savior as well. His coming back to give thanks was originally only an act of thanksgiving, yet it became the door to receiving salvation: at whose feet he fell down, from Him he received the deeper grace. The Lord also deliberately made it plain: your faith has saved you. What faith? The faith that brought him back: the faith that recognized the Giver of grace, gave glory to God, and fell down in thanksgiving. If faith goes only as far as crying out, it obtains healing; if faith goes as far as thanksgiving, it obtains salvation. Please hear this sentence clearly this morning: thanksgiving is not politeness after receiving grace; thanksgiving is the last, and most important, step of faith. The nine did not lose by not believing; they lost by believing incompletely: they believed as far as the road out, and believed too little of the road back.

The story does not tell us the ending of the nine; Scripture leaves it blank. We can only say according to the text: they were cleansed, and each went his own way. And this one who came back rose and walked away carrying the words "has saved you." His road from then on separated from those nine roads: one was the road home to reunion, the other was the road of following the Giver of grace. I hope that everyone seated here today receives not only cleansing, but receives this sentence in full: your faith has saved you.

Connect this story also with the Thanksgiving table. Tonight many families will gather around a table to count their blessings: this year's work, house, children, and peace. Counting blessings is a good thing, but the order the Lord teaches in this passage is: first return to His feet, then return to the table. If the order is reversed, thanksgiving is left with only atmosphere; if the order is right, a whole table of food can become an altar. This year, before gathering around the table, first lead the whole family in one act of coming back: read this passage together, speak aloud together one thing the Lord has done this year, and pray aloud together for that thing. Children may not remember the taste of a table of dishes, but they will remember the sight of their father and mother falling down in thanksgiving. If thanksgiving is to be passed to the next generation, it depends not on the abundance of the festival, but on the footprints of the return journey.

Pastors and fellow workers, please also keep this passage in your hearts. Among the people we serve, the nine will always be more than the one. Do not be disheartened because of those who are absent, and do not boast because of those who come back. The Lord Himself experienced nine out of ten leaving; we simply proclaim the grace in full and state the call clearly, and then, like the Lord, stand at the village entrance and welcome everyone who turns back. Among those who come back, some will hear salvation; for those who are absent, the Lord is still waiting. We do not count the sins of the absent; we count the grace of those who return, and for those who have not yet come back, we keep a place at His feet.

Look at the nine from another angle. They were not unbelieving; they believed the command, and they received the effect. They were not without joy; no doubt they held their families and wept and laughed. The difference between them and the one who returned was only one step: they finished the story at the healing, while the one who returned finished the story at the Giver of grace. Seen across a lifetime, this one step is the dividing line in many people's years of believing in the Lord: some believe for a whole lifetime and stop at the level of receiving help, getting whatever they ask for, treating the Lord as help that is always on call; others take one more step forward and recognize that this Helper Himself is the greatest grace, and so they worship, fall down, and follow, being people of the return journey all their lives. This morning, when the Lord asks where the nine are, it is not for condemnation, but for recall. His voice still echoes at the village entrance today: I have given you so much — where are you? Blessed are those who hear this question, because the question itself is an invitation: it is not too late to come back, there is still a place at His feet, and the words "has saved you" are still waiting to be spoken to you.

Leave this passage also for those who are parents to hear. Children receive so much from us: food, school fees, shelter, opportunities. What we hope for is not a polite thank-you; we hope that when they grow up, they will recognize the love behind all of this and walk back. God's heart as a Father and the hearts of earthly parents meet here: He does not lack our glory; what He lacks is us, this person, coming back. So teaching children thanksgiving is not teaching politeness; it is teaching them to recognize the road: the road of recognizing the source, the road of recognizing the way home. If we ourselves first walk this return journey, our children will recognize this road. May our generation first be the one who comes back, so that the next generation will have a few fewer of the absent nine.

Hear the Lord's arithmetic once more at the end: ten, one, nine. The Lord remembers it clearly, not vaguely at all. The Lord also remembers clearly the grace we have received in this life: which mercy in a time of sickness, which preservation in a time of danger, which provision at the end of our road — He counts them all. He counts not to collect a debt, but so that when we come back, item by item can be matched. Today, if you are willing to come back and fall down, you do not need first to organize beautiful words. Just as you remember them, say them to Him one by one: for this one, thank You; for that one, thank You too. As you count, you will discover that this year He never left the village entrance, and that the ones who were absent all along were us. May today's worship of thanksgiving become the first step of the return journey for many, beginning at His feet and walking into salvation.

Therefore, this Thanksgiving, let us establish a new rule: after receiving grace, first make the return journey, then celebrate; first fall down, then raise the cup; first recognize the Lord who gave the grace, then enjoy the fruit of grace. Once this order is established, the festival is no longer just a lively occasion, but a worship in which the whole family comes back together. May the Lord remember everyone who is willing to turn around.

## (39:00 - 43:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: those who are still standing at a distance. You feel that your problem cannot be seen by others. You stand far away and dare only to shout one cry: have mercy on me. Today's story tells you: those who stand at a distance can shout into the Lord's ears. Do not give up crying out because of a sense of distance. However far you are from Him, His mercy will travel just as far.

The second kind: those who hold the command but have not yet set out on the road. You have understood all the Lord's words; you are just waiting for the effect to appear first. Today, please change the order: go first, obey first; cleansing is on the road. For that matter you have been dragging out, take the first step this week: make that phone call, send that message, make that decision.

The third kind: those who have received grace and are hurrying to handle the formalities. Your thanksgiving is always scheduled after you finish being busy, and the busyness never ends. Learn from this Samaritan: let thanksgiving cut in line. First return to the Lord's feet, then handle the formalities of life. Before you go home today, first do one concrete act of coming back: a period of prayer, a testimony, an offering presented to Him.

The fourth kind: the nine who regard grace as luck. You say this year your fortune was good, you caught the chance, it happened by coincidence. Luck does not listen to prayer, and luck does not ask where the nine are. Identify the right object of glory: if God did it, give it to God; give glory loudly, without ambiguity.

The fifth kind: those who feel they are foreigners. You feel your background is wrong, your accent is wrong, your past is wrong, and that it is not your turn to represent thanksgiving. The one the Lord Himself praised was precisely that foreigner. In His kingdom, those who come back are not outsiders; those who come back first receive first the words "has saved you."

The sixth kind: those who have received only cleansing and have not yet received salvation. This year your disease was healed, your matters succeeded, your passes were crossed, but the deepest layer of your heart has not yet been settled. Today, do not just give thanks and leave. Fall down, entrust your whole person to this Lord who gives grace, and receive His complete words: your faith has saved you.

## (43:00 - 45:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we counted an account: ten cried out, ten were cleansed, one came back, one heard salvation. The gap is not in grace; the gap is in the return journey. May we all be the one who comes back: see clearly who gave the grace, glorify God with a loud voice, fall down and thank Him, and then arise and walk the road ahead carrying salvation.

Let us pray together: Lord who shows mercy, thank You. When we stood at a distance and cried out, You heard; when we had not yet seen the effect, You caused us to be cleansed on the road. Lord, forgive us for the days when we were the nine: receiving grace and walking away, leaving You at the village entrance. Today we come back, fall down at Your feet, and give glory to You. Please also say to us: Arise, go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] eucharisteo: Greek εὐχαριστέω (G2168), to thank, to give thanks; Luke 17:16 records that the man who returned fell down at the feet of Jesus and gave Him thanks, using precisely this word; for the meaning, see BDAG: thank, give thanks, express gratitude. This manuscript's statement that "thanksgiving is not a feeling but an action" is developed from the juxtaposition of the three actions in that verse.

[2] sozo: Greek σῴζω (G4982), to save, to rescue, to make whole; in Luke 17:19 the Lord's words to the man who returned are σέσωκέν σε ἡ πίστις σου, which the KJV renders "thy faith hath made thee whole" and the Chinese Union Version "your faith has saved you" — the verb is the standard New Testament word for salvation (so throughout Luke–Acts). This forms a deliberate contrast with all ten being cleansed together (ἐκαθαρίσθησαν, 17:14, 17). For the meaning, see BDAG: save, rescue, deliver. This manuscript's teaching on "the difference between cleansing and salvation" is developed from this verb and this contrast, and the body states that basis openly.

[3] katharizo: Greek καθαρίζω (G2511), to make clean, to be cleansed; Luke 17:14, "as they went, they were cleansed," and 17:17, "Were not ten cleansed?" both use this word; for the meaning, see BDAG: make clean, purify, declare clean. This manuscript's statement that "cleansing happened on the road" is developed from the timing in 17:14.

[4] allogenes: Greek ἀλλογενής (G241), a foreigner, a man of another people; in Luke 17:18 the Lord calls the man who returned "this foreigner"; it appears only here in the New Testament; for the meaning, see BDAG: foreign, of another race. From this, this manuscript develops its teaching on the warning that the thanksgiving of an outsider gives to the congregation.

[5] On showing oneself to the priests: In Luke 17:14 the Lord commanded the ten to go and show themselves to the priests, according to the pattern in Leviticus 14 by which those healed were examined and declared clean; this manuscript's statement that "the command was premature" is developed from that background and the narrative timing of 17:14, and the source is noted here especially.

[6] On the Samaritan: Only in Luke 17:16 is it made plain that the man who returned was a Samaritan. This manuscript develops its teaching according to Luke's delayed revelation in the narrative; this is an observation at the level of reading the text, not a separate claim of historical fact, and it is explained here especially.

## Devotional Excerpt

Ten men with leprosy cried out for mercy, ten obeyed and set out on the road, and ten were cleansed along the way. Grace was given to every one of them without measure. Yet in the end, only one was commended by the Lord Himself: the man who saw that he was healed and came back. He praised God in a loud voice and fell at the feet of Jesus, giving Him thanks. He could have gone straight to complete the formalities and hurry home to his family, and no one would have blamed him. Instead, he placed thanksgiving ahead of reunion and went first to the One who had shown him mercy. Because he returned, he received more than healing. He heard the Lord's word over him — thy faith hath made thee whole: your faith has saved you (see note [2]). Count the mercies of your year today: sickness healed, hardships carried through, daily provision never lacking, your family kept in peace. Then ask yourself one question: how many times have I gone back? Many stories of grace are only half told. They stop at receiving, while the second half is the return journey: coming back to recognize who gave the gift, coming back to give Him the glory, coming back to fall down and say thank You. Today, be the one who comes back. Return first to His feet, and only then to the table of reunion.

The road back is never long. It is only one turn. The words are never many. They are only one honest thank You. Yet that single turn carried a healed man into salvation, and that single sentence turned an ordinary festival into worship. Let thanksgiving become more than a season in your home. Practice the return journey every day: name the mercy, find the Giver, give Him the glory out loud, and fall at His feet before you rush to the next errand. The Lord who waited at the edge of the village is still waiting for the ones who have not yet come back, and His question still carries love rather than anger. Come back today, and hear Him say it over you as well.

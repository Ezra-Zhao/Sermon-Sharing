## Summary (approx. 100 words)

Abram went up from Egypt and returned to Bethel, to the place where he had first built an altar, once again calling upon the name of the Lord. As the possessions of Abram and his nephew Lot increased, the land could not support them dwelling together, and strife arose between their herdsmen. Abram took the initiative to let Lot choose the whole land first; Lot lifted up his eyes and chose the well-watered plain of Jordan, yet drew near to Sodom. Abram was left with seemingly arid Canaan, yet after their separation God Himself told him to lift up his eyes and look north, south, east, and west, promising to give him all the land he saw and descendants as the dust of the earth. This sermon examines three things: those who return first go back to the altar; yielding in times of conflict is not weakness but faith that God gives enough; and what man leaves behind, God takes to make the entirety of His promise.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Introduction · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

At the end of the previous sermon, Abram's household was escorted out of Egypt by men commanded by Pharaoh. It was not dignified, but they came out.

Turning to Chapter 13 today, I first ask everyone to notice the route he took after returning: he did not go straight looking for pasture or trade; he first returned to Bethel, back to "the place where he had first built an altar," and called upon the name of the Lord there once again. For a man who had just lost face in Egypt, the first thing he did upon returning was not to make excuses or stage a turnaround, but to return to the altar. Brothers and sisters, the road of return always begins anew beside the altar.

And the real test in this chapter followed immediately: not a famine, not Pharaoh, but strife among his own kin. The herdsmen of Abram and Lot began to quarrel. A stranger's sword is easy to dodge, but conflict among one's own kin is the hardest to handle. Today's sermon examines how a called person handles a situation where "there is not enough to go around": Does he snatch? Does he strive? Or does he speak those astounding words—you choose first?

Let us first read several key passages of Scripture:

"Then Abram went up from Egypt to the South, he and his wife and all that he had, and Lot with him." (Gen. 13:1, excerpt)

"And he went on his journey from the South as far as Bethel, to the place where his tent had been at the beginning, between Bethel and Ai, to the place of the altar which he had made there at first. And there Abram called on the name of the LORD." (Gen. 13:3–4, summary excerpt)

"Now the land could not support them, that they might dwell together, for their possessions were great, so that they could not dwell together. And there was strife between the herdsmen of Abram’s livestock and the herdsmen of Lot’s livestock. The Canaanites and the Perizzites then dwelt in the land." (Gen. 13:6–7)

"So Abram said to Lot, 'Please let there be no strife between you and me, and between my herdsmen and your herdsmen; for we are brethren. Is not the whole land before you? Please separate from me. If you take the left, then I will go to the right; or, if you go to the right, then I will go to the left.'" (Gen. 13:8–9, excerpt)

"And the LORD said to Abram, after Lot had separated from him: 'Lift your eyes now and look from the place where you are—northward, southward, eastward, and westward; for all the land which you see I give to you and your descendants forever. And I will make your descendants as the dust of the earth; so that if a man could number the dust of the earth, then your descendants also could be numbered.'" (Gen. 13:14–16, excerpt)

"Then Abram moved his tent, and went and dwelt by the terebinth trees of Mamre, which are in Hebron, and built an altar there to the LORD." (Gen. 13:18)

Today we will look at this in three parts:

First, 13:1–5, Going up from Egypt, first returning to the altar—the one who turns back is in no hurry to stage a turnaround, but first restores a life of calling upon the name of the Lord;
Second, 13:6–13, You choose first—when conflict comes, the elder lets go and the junior picks the best; two ways of "lifting up one's eyes" lead to two different destinies;
Third, 13:14–18, Only after Lot departed did God speak—what man left behind, God took as an everlasting promise; Abram walked through it on foot and marked it with an altar.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. When Abram returned from Egypt, You did not hinder him from returning to the altar; when he let Lot choose first, You spoke to him personally after he let go. We pray that You teach us this lesson today: not to seize in the midst of conflict, and when there is not enough to go around, still to believe that You give enough; open our eyes as well, that we may learn how Abram lifted up his eyes, and not follow the way Lot lifted up his eyes. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: Going Up from Egypt, First Returning to the Altar (13:1–5)

### 1.1 "Went Up to the South": The directional word appears again, this time going up

Verse 1 (excerpt): "Abram went up from Egypt to the South." In the previous sermon, we noted the directional words in the Bible: going down to Egypt, going up to Canaan. "Down" refers to elevation; "up" also refers to elevation. Geographically, it was going from the Nile lowlands back to the Canaanite hill country; spiritually, it was a person turning from a life of relying on the world back to a life of relying on the promise. The Bible does not use lengthy discourses to describe Abram's repentance; with just the single word "up," it makes his direction abundantly clear.

Look carefully at the roster of those traveling together: his wife Sarai returned, "all that he had" was intact, and Lot went with him. What God preserved for him in Egypt lacked not a single thing. Brothers and sisters, when someone has stumbled in Egypt, the thought that most easily surfaces upon returning is, "Am I still worthy? Does God still want me?" The answer at the beginning of Chapter 13 is very quiet: setting aside whether you are worthy or not, the altar is still there, the road is still reconnectable, and there is still a place for you in God's company. The appearance of grace is often like this: not bringing up old debts, but first providing a way back.

### 1.2 "The Place Where He Had First Built an Altar": The coordinates of return are the altar, not achievements

Verses 3 and 4 are the gentlest two verses in the entire chapter: he went on his journey toward Bethel, to the place where his tent had been at the beginning, the place where he had first built an altar, "and there Abram called on the name of the LORD."

Notice that he did not return to the oak tree of Shechem, nor to the camp in the South, but to "the place where he had first built an altar." A person turning back needs coordinates, and those coordinates cannot be "how glorious I once was," but can only be "where I once met God." The altar at Bethel was the second altar he had built in Canaan (Gen. 12:8), the last place he called upon the name of the Lord before leaving Canaan, and now it became his first stop upon returning. Between these lay the whole episode of Egypt: lies, humiliation, being escorted out. The altar had not moved; it was the man who moved. Now that the man had returned, the altar was still waiting for him.

"Called," in Hebrew *qara* (H7121, to call, proclaim, invoke) [1], is the exact same word as in Genesis 12:8. In the previous sermon, we said that there was no altar beside Egypt, and Abram remained entirely silent throughout his time in Egypt; in this chapter, as soon as he opens his mouth, it is to call upon the name of the Lord. Restoration begins with opening one's mouth to pray. How many people, returning from Egypt, are busy explaining themselves to others and striving to prove themselves, yet refuse to first return to the side of the altar to pray quietly. When the order is reversed, the heart cannot be settled.

Warren W. Wiersbe, in his commentary on Genesis, when explaining Abram's return to Bethel, reminds readers that his return to the place where he had at the beginning built an altar and called on the Lord shows that the way of restoration after failure is to return to the place of initial fellowship with God, rather than relying on new achievements to cover old failures. (cf. Warren W. Wiersbe, *The Bible Exposition Commentary: Genesis*)

Psalm 51 is David's prayer after an even greater failure; he did not ask for his throne to be restored first, but asked to "Create in me a clean heart," and "Restore to me the joy of Your salvation" (Ps. 51:10, 12, paraphrased). First restore the joy, first return to the altar side; the matter of the throne could wait. Abram and David learned the very same lesson.

### 1.3 Very Rich in Possessions: A New Test After Being Blessed (13:2, 5–6 Preview)

Verse 2 adds a note on his family wealth: "Abram was very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold." Verse 5 says that Lot also had flocks and herds and tents. Do you remember? Part of these possessions was brought right out of Egypt. God preserved his entire company out of Egypt, even preserving their possessions, and now their possessions had become so great as to pose a new problem: the land was not able to support them.

Brothers and sisters, spiritual test questions change with the seasons: in poverty, you are tested on whether you believe; in wealth, you are tested on whether you strive. When you have nothing, you are tested on whether you wait; when you have plenty, you are tested on whether you yield. Abram had just passed the hurdle of fear, and immediately came the hurdle of strife. Each hurdle gets closer to home: Pharaoh was an outsider, but Lot was his own nephew; the danger of Egypt was glaringly obvious, but a family dispute comes wrapped in emotions, reasonings, and the pretext that "the herdsmen are only looking out for my good." In the next part, we will see how he passes this hurdle.

To sum up the first part in one sentence: **For those who return from Egypt, return to the side of the altar before talking about anything else; when the direction is right, it is "going up"; when the coordinates are right, it is the place where the altar was first built; restoration is not a dramatic reversal, but calling anew upon the name of the Lord.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: You Choose First (13:6–13)

### 2.1 "The Land Was Not Able to Support Them": The Beginning of Strife Is Often a Real Issue (13:6–7)

Verses 6 and 7 lay out the problem very realistically: "Now the land was not able to support them, that they might dwell together, for their possessions were great, so that they could not dwell together." When the herdsmen strove, they strove over pastures and water sources—real issues essential for the livestock's survival, not someone picking a fight out of sheer boredom. What is more, the Bible coldly adds a sentence: "The Canaanites and the Perizzites then dwelt in the land"—outsiders were watching right beside them. When two households of the called strive over pastureland in front of outsiders, they are not merely striving over grass; they are striving over their witness.

"Strife"—the Hebrew word *riv* (H7379, dispute, controversy, litigation)[2]—this is the very word describing what the herdsmen were engaged in. The Bible does not shy away from this word, nor does it lay the blame solely on the herdsmen: the strife of the herdsmen is a thermometer of the relationship between their masters. If there is already tension between the masters, the herdsmen will strive all the more fiercely; if the masters are willing to sit down and talk, the herdsmen will naturally quiet down. Therefore, Abram handled this matter not by dealing with the herdsmen, but by dealing with his relationship with Lot—this move showed the responsibility of a leader.

Proverbs 15:18 offers a contrast: "A wrathful man stirs up strife, But he who is slow to anger allays contention" (paraphrased). It also says: "A shepherd whose strife scatters the flock is not as good as a master who is willing to take a step back first"—this latter half is not Scripture, but my own summary of the principle for you; the biblical principle is clear: one who allays strife is more precious in God's eyes than one who wins the strife. Later, the Lord Jesus in the Sermon on the Mount explicitly named such people: "Blessed are the peacemakers, For they shall be called sons of God" (Matt. 5:9).

### 2.2 "For We Are Brethren": Abram's Calculus Is Different from the World's (13:8–9)

When Abram opened his mouth, his first sentence was not about dividing the land, but about defining the relationship: "Please let there be no strife between you and me... for we are brethren."

"Brethren"—the original Hebrew is *achim* (H251, brothers, relatives)[3], literally "brothers." Lot was his nephew, yet Abram addressed him as a brother: by seniority of generation, he was the elder; in terms of relationship, he acknowledged kinship first. The order is vital: acknowledge the relationship first, then talk about distribution; safeguard "who we are" first, then address "how things are divided." How many families and teams fall apart precisely because the order is reversed: they talk about distribution right off the bat, and as they talk, the relationship vanishes; the land is divided clearly, but the people are torn apart.

Then came that earth-shattering statement: "Is not the whole land before you? Please separate from me. If you take the left, then I will go to the right; or, if you go to the right, then I will go to the left."

Please note who said this: it was the one who was called, the one who was older, the one who had just received the promise from God, "To your descendants I will give this land" (Gen. 12:7). By promise, by seniority, by reason, in every way he should have had first pick. Yet he yielded the entire right of first choice to Lot. Brothers and sisters, this was not mere politeness; this was faith: because the land was promised to him by God, he was not afraid to yield; because the Giver is God, he had no need to grab. For someone who truly believes the promise, the hands are open; those whose fists are clenched tight may say they believe with their mouths, but in their hearts they are terrified that God will not provide.

The words of Philippians 2 are a New Testament echo of this scene: "in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself. Let each of you look out not only for his own interests, but also for the interests of others" (Phil. 2:3–4, paraphrased). Abram lived this out beforehand in Genesis: he looked out for Lot's choice, looked out for the peace of both households, and disregarded only his own prerogative of the first move.

### 2.3 Lot Lifted His Eyes: Choosing the Best, Drawing Near to the Worst (13:10–13)

For Lot's response, the Bible uses an action verb identical to the one God uses later when speaking to Abram: "lifted his eyes."

"And Lot lifted his eyes and saw all the plain of Jordan, that it was well watered everywhere (before the Lord destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah) like the garden of the Lord, like the land of Egypt as you go toward Zoar." (Gen. 13:10)

"Lifted his eyes"—the Hebrew word *nasa* (H5375, to lift up, raise), when used with eyes, means "to lift up one's eyes and look"[4]. When Lot lifted his eyes, what he saw was water: well-watered, like the Garden of Eden, like the land of Egypt. Please notice those words, "like the land of Egypt"—Lot had just come up from Egypt, and the affluence of Egypt was still imprinted on his eyes. His criteria for choosing land were half the garden, half Egypt. Whatever has fed one's eyes will incline one's choice. Abram's eyes had been nourished beside the altar; Lot's eyes had been nourished in Egypt; the same piece of land, two ways of seeing.

In verses 11 and 12, Lot's footsteps shifted step by step: he "chose for himself all the plain of Jordan, and Lot journeyed east"; he "dwelt in the cities of the plain and pitched his tent even as far as Sodom." First choosing the plain, then dwelling in the cities, and finally pitching his tent toward Sodom—by chapter 14, he had already moved into the city of Sodom (cf. Gen. 14:12); by chapter 19, he was sitting in the gate of Sodom, and when the city was destroyed, he fled in disgrace, his wife turned into a pillar of salt, and his possessions were entirely lost (cf. Gen. 19). Brothers and sisters, a downward slide never happens in a single leap; it shifts tent by tent. With every shift, one gets a little closer to danger and a little farther from the altar, all while thinking, "It's just more convenient to live a little closer."

In verse 13, the Bible renders a verdict on Sodom: "But the men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinful against the Lord." When Lot was choosing land, he saw only that it was well-watered; he did not see this verse. When a person chooses a place, if they look only at pastures and water but not at people's hearts, only at house prices but not at the moral atmosphere, only at income but not at the cost, the more accurate their choice appears, the deeper they will sink.

Here we need to ignite things a bit.

Brothers and sisters, who among us today does not hold a picture of the "plain of Jordan" in their hands? An opportunity with higher income that must be traded for your integrity, a social circle that looks respectable but shifts your tent day by day, a relationship where warnings are obvious, yet you only see that it is well watered. Lot was not an evil man; he was just someone who, when he lifted his eyes, saw only water and no altar. Today, not tomorrow, bring out that piece of land you are currently choosing, and ask one more question before God: This land is well watered, but what is it next to? Where is it going to move my tent? Before choosing land, return to the altar first; let your eyes be washed by God, and then lift your eyes. Furthermore, to those whose tents have already moved in the wrong direction, move one tent back today—moving back is not shameful; it is moving into the city of Sodom that leaves you with no way back.

Summing up Part 2 in one sentence: **When strife came, Abram first recognized their kinship and then yielded the whole land, because the promise was given by God and he was not afraid to yield; Lot lifted his eyes and looked only at what was well watered, moving his tent pitch by pitch toward Sodom—two ways of lifting the eyes, two different destinies.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part 3: God Spoke Only After Lot Had Left (13:14–18)

### 3.1 "After Lot Had Separated from Abram": God Expanded the Promise After Man Let Go (13:14–15)

The time indicator in verse 14 is the key to the entire chapter: "And the LORD said to Abram, after Lot had separated from him..."

God opened His mouth only after Lot had left. It was not that God was partial; rather, there are some words that can only be taken in after a person's hands have let go. Abram yielded the right of first choice, watching the lush plain being picked away, while what remained of Canaan was mostly mountainous terrain, looking dry and barren. At this very moment, God said: "Lift your eyes now and look from the place where you are—northward, southward, eastward, and westward; for all the land which you see I give to you and your descendants forever."

Both lifted their eyes, but the lifting of the eyes that God gave Abram and Lot's lifting of the eyes were completely different in direction: Lot lifted his eyes, looked only eastward, and saw one plain; Abram lifted his eyes, looked in all four directions—northward, southward, eastward, and westward—and saw the whole. Lot lifted his eyes, and his standard was being well watered; Abram lifted his eyes, and his standard was God's "I give to you." What Lot chose and took was visible benefit, while what Abram received was all that could be seen plus "forever." Brothers and sisters, man calculates how much is taken, but God calculates how much is given; man chooses surface area, but God gives duration—forever.

"Land," in Hebrew *eretz* (H776, land, earth)[5]; "forever," related expressions of *ad-olam* (H5704+H5769, forever, everlasting)[6]. The unit of time God gave here was not the season of Lot's tents, but eternity. Those who trade eternity for present well-watered pleasures will always calculate wrongly.

### 3.2 "As the Dust of the Earth": The Promise of Descendants Counted from the Dust (13:16)

God then changed the metaphor for his descendants: "And I will make your descendants as the dust of the earth; so that if a man could number the dust of the earth, then your descendants also could be numbered."

"Dust," in Hebrew *aphar* (H6083, dust, dirt)[7]. In Genesis 12, the promise of descendants was only "a great nation"; here, for the first time, an image was given: the dust of the earth. The very dirt Abram trod underfoot was the descendants God was counting for him. Man chose and took the water of the plain, but God left behind the soil of the hill country—water dries up, but dirt cannot be numbered. Later, God gave the metaphor of the stars (Gen. 15:5) and the metaphor of the sand on the seashore (Gen. 22:17), each greater than the last. Brothers and sisters, the portion left behind after others have picked is often the very portion God uses as His metaphor: the most inconspicuous dust, in God's hand, becomes an innumerable future.

Nor should we forget the reality: when this was spoken, Abram still did not have a single descendant, and the fact of Sarai's barrenness had not changed at all. God chose to stand precisely in front of "nothing" and speak of "too many to count." Faith is stretched wide in such a gap: the eyes see an empty womb, while the ears hear of the dust of the earth; listening to what the ears hear rather than what the eyes see—this was the way Abram walked, and this is the way we must walk.

### 3.3 "Walk in the Land Through Its Length and Its Width" and the Altar at Hebron: Measuring the Land with Feet, Acknowledging the Lord with an Altar (13:17–18)

God's final command came with an action: "Arise, walk in the land through its length and its width, for I give it to you." (Gen. 13:17)

"Length and width"—the original text speaks of the length and width of the land: length, *orekh* (H753)[8]; width, *rochav* (H7341)[9]. God told Abram to measure with his feet the land that was not yet in his possession. Walking is the exercise of faith: wherever the feet step, the heart believes. The promise is not waited for by lying in a tent; it is walked out. The land was not obtained by choosing; it was given by God, but the feet had to walk through it.

Verse 18 concludes with that familiar action again: "Then Abram moved his tent, and went and dwelt by the terebinth trees of Mamre, which are in Hebron, and built an altar there to the LORD."

Returning from Egypt, he went back to the altar at Bethel; after yielding the plain, he built another altar at Hebron. In Abram's life, what was marked most clearly on the map was not the inheritance he chose, but the altars he built: one at Shechem, one at Bethel, and now one at Hebron. Land chosen by human hands will change owners, but the altars built remain before God. Every time Abram received a new promise, he responded by building an altar; his view of inheritance was altar first, land second—the order was never reversed.

Here is today's second ignition point, and also the call of the entire sermon.

Brothers and sisters, someone today is standing on the piece of land left behind after being picked over: good opportunities were chosen by others, good positions were taken by others, and what remains in your hand is the portion that looks dry and barren. That silent cry of "It's unfair!" in your heart—God has heard it. Genesis 13 was written for you: Lot chose what was well watered, but God reserved what is forever for the one who let go; the land left behind by man, God took and made the entirety of the promise. Today, stop calculating over the plain that others have chosen! Do three things according to God's words to Abram: lift your eyes, look in all four directions, and see what God has still reserved for you; arise, walk the length and width, tread your allotted portion with your feet, and make it solid with your hands; build an altar right where you are, calling on the name of the Lord first before talking about gain and loss. The one who lets go suffers no loss—this statement is not mere comfort; it is an account verified by Abram with his entire life.

Summing up Part 3 in one sentence: **God spoke only after Lot had left: He told the one who let go to lift his eyes in all directions and walk through the whole land; the promise expanded from a single plain to forever, and descendants expanded from none at all to the countless dust—what was left behind by man, God took to be the whole.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

Today, this chapter speaks to six categories of people:

**To seekers:** You might feel that believers in God are just bound to suffer loss, and that Abram yielding first choice to Lot looks foolish. Please read this chapter to the end: what the one who yielded ultimately received was something the one who picked could not even dream of—land forever, and innumerable descendants. God does not call people to be fools; He has simply switched the ledger: calculated by the ledger of heaven, the one who lets go never loses. If you are willing to surrender just a bit of your life's right of choice to Him, He will gradually reveal His arithmetic to you.

**To new believers:** Having returned from an Egyptian-style life, learn Abram's priority first: return to the altar, prioritizing prayer and worship over explaining yourself to people or proving yourself. Furthermore, when facing strife with others, recognize the relationship first before discussing distribution; ask "Who are we?" before asking "How should things be divided?" Once you have learned these two things, your faith will begin to take root.

**To brothers and sisters who have been in the Lord for many years:** After many years, what is easiest to strive for is position, the right to speak, and the claim of "I was here first." Measure yourselves by Abram today: the one who had been called the longest and held the highest seniority was precisely the one who yielded first. If you are currently in a deadlock over an issue in the church, try being the first to say, "You choose first"—this is not conceding defeat; it is moving your security away from seized resources and back into God's promise.

**For parents:** When sharing things or setting rules at home, the first thing children notice is not whether the distribution is fair, but whether their parents are striving with each other. The separation between Abram and Lot had an impact on their descendants that takes several chapters to fully unfold. When parents are willing to yield, willing to prioritize family bonds, children learn: relationships are far more valuable than that piece of land. When parents fight to the bitter end in front of their children over minor gains, what children learn is to pitch their tents toward Sodom. Family upbringing is hidden precisely in these choices.

**For those striving in the workplace:** Competing for projects, credit, and budget within a team is a daily lesson. The workplace ethics of chapter 13 teach us: protect relationships first, discuss division later; place your security in your own integrity and God’s provision, not in snatching the upper hand first. We must also learn from Lot as a negative example: when changing jobs, taking contracts, or choosing partners, do not look only at how well-watered it seems—ask one more question: What are the hearts of the people like here? What is the atmosphere like? Where will my tent be shifted toward?

**For those currently in suffering:** You may find yourself in the position of being left with what others did not want: the good has been taken away, and what remains is dry and difficult; or perhaps you are watching someone close to you move their tent toward danger, pitch by pitch, unable to stop them. Listen to the words of verse 14: God spoke to Abram only after Lot had separated from him. When people depart, it does not mean God has departed; being left behind does not mean being cast off. First build an altar, first lift up your eyes; the words God desires to speak to you often come only after stillness and letting go.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Summary · Closing Prayer

Let us review today's three phrases:

First, coming up from Egypt, return first to the altar—the one who turns back is in no hurry to turn the tables, but returns to the place where he first built the altar, once again calling upon the name of the LORD;  
Second, you choose first—acknowledge kinship first, then yield the whole land; the promise is given by God, so do not fear yielding; Lot looked only at what was well-watered, yet his tent shifted toward Sodom;  
Third, only after Lot left did God speak—lift up your eyes in all four directions, walk through the length and breadth of the land, and build another altar; what man leaves behind, God turns into an everlasting promise.

Now let us bow our heads and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. When Abram let go, You did not let him suffer loss; when Lot chose the plain, You kept what is eternal for the one who stayed behind. Forgive our tight grasp when we strive, and purify our standards when we lift up our eyes, so that the riches of Egypt may not remain as a measuring rod in our sight. Teach us in our families, churches, and workplaces to prioritize relationships, to be willing to yield, and to place our security in Your promise. In the places where we are, lead us to lift up our eyes, arise, and build an altar. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

[3] achim: Hebrew אַחִים (plural of H251 ach), brothers, relatives; see Gen. 13:8 "for we are brethren"; the Chinese Union Version renders it as "flesh and blood," literally brothers; definitions according to BDB, HALOT.

[4] nasa: Hebrew נָשָׂא (H5375), to lift up, bear, carry; when used with "eyes," it means to lift up one's eyes and look; see Gen. 13:10 of Lot lifting his eyes, and 13:14 where God commands Abram to lift his eyes; definitions according to BDB, HALOT.

[5] eretz: Hebrew אֶרֶץ (H776), earth, land, country; see Gen. 13:6, 9, 14–17, etc.; definitions according to BDB, HALOT.

[6] ad-olam: related expressions of Hebrew עַד־עוֹלָם (ad H5704 until; olam H5769 forever, everlasting); see Gen. 13:15 "forever"; definitions according to BDB, HALOT.

[7] aphar: Hebrew עָפָר (H6083), dust, earth, powder; see Gen. 13:16 "the dust of the earth"; definitions according to BDB, HALOT.

[8] orekh: Hebrew אֹרֶךְ (H753), length; see Gen. 13:17 in the expression related to "length" in "walk through its length and its breadth"; definitions according to BDB, HALOT.

[9] rochav: Hebrew רֹחַב (H7341), breadth, width; see Gen. 13:17 used alongside orekh; definitions according to BDB, HALOT.

[10] parad: Hebrew פָּרַד (H6504), to divide, separate; see Gen. 13:9, 11, 14 in the narratives related to "separate, depart"; mizbeach: Hebrew מִזְבֵּחַ (H4196), altar, see Gen. 13:18; definitions of both words according to BDB, HALOT.

[11] Regarding Gen. 13:8–9 "If you take the left, then I will go to the right": Chinese Union Version rendering; English comparisons: KJV has "if thou depart to the left hand, then I will go to the right"; NIV and NRSV are similar in general meaning (English citations are recorded from publicly available standard texts). Lu Zhenzhong's translation of this verse is referenced, though not directly quoted in this manuscript.

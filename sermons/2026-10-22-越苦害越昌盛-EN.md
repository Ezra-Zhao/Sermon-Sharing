## Summary (about 100 words)

Joseph and his whole generation died, yet the Israelites were fruitful and multiplied and filled the land of Egypt. A new king who did not know Joseph arose and set taskmasters over them with heavy burdens, yet the more they were afflicted, the more they multiplied; Pharaoh turned to ordering the midwives to kill the male infants, but Shiphrah and Puah, because they feared God, preserved the children alive; in the end Pharaoh commanded his people to throw the boys into the Nile. Prosperity has never rested on the king's permission, and affliction cannot stop the multiplying of the promise.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

When the book of Genesis closes, its last scene is a coffin. Joseph said as he was dying: "I die: and God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land unto the land which he sware to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob." (Genesis 50:24, excerpt, KJV) He also made the children of Israel swear that in the future they would carry his bones up to that land. Then Joseph died, and his coffin rested in Egypt. A whole book stops at a waiting like this: the man died, but the promise had not yet been fulfilled; the coffin was in a foreign land, and the word about God's visitation still hung in the air.

Today we open the book of Exodus. Please note its very first words: "the children of Israel." The continuation in the original shows that this is not starting over from scratch, but carrying the preceding text forward. The Hebrew name of Exodus is "Names," taken precisely from the first sentence of this chapter, "the names of the children of Israel." A book that opens with "names" does not begin with the name of some great personage, but first recites, one by one, the names of one family. The previous scroll ended with a coffin; this scroll begins with a list. The coffin speaks of death; the list speaks of descendants. God's story, in just this way, passes over death and writes onward.

This chapter has no parting of the Red Sea, no giving of the law, and even Moses has not yet been born. It has only three things: a list of seventy persons, a system of forced labor that presses heavier and heavier, and two midwives standing before Pharaoh. Yet the whole chapter has one sentence that strikes us again and again, verse 12: "But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and grew." This is also the title of today's sermon: The More They Were Afflicted, the More They Multiplied.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And Joseph died, and all his brethren, and all that generation. And the children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and waxed exceeding mighty; and the land was filled with them." (Exodus 1:6-7, excerpt, KJV)

"Therefore they did set over them taskmasters to afflict them with their burdens. And they built for Pharaoh treasure cities, Pithom and Raamses. But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and grew. And they were grieved because of the children of Israel." (Exodus 1:11-12, excerpt, KJV)

"And the king of Egypt spake to the Hebrew midwives, of which the name of the one was Shiphrah, and the name of the other Puah. ... But the midwives feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the men children alive." (Exodus 1:15, 17, excerpt, KJV)

"And Pharaoh charged all his people, saying, Every son that is born ye shall cast into the river, and every daughter ye shall save alive." (Exodus 1:22, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, the last page of Genesis is a coffin, and the first page of Exodus is names. Teach us today to know this: human generations pass, dynasties change, benefactors are forgotten, and suffering escalates, but Your promise does not break. In affliction, let us not stake our hope on any king's permission, but stake it on Your faithfulness alone. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: From Seventy Names to Filling the Land (1:1-7)

First look at the list at the head of this chapter: "Now these are the names of the children of Israel, which came into Egypt; every man and his household came with Jacob. Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah, Issachar, Zebulun, and Benjamin, Dan, and Naphtali, Gad, and Asher. And all the souls that came out of the loins of Jacob were seventy souls: for Joseph was in Egypt already." (Exodus 1:1-5, excerpt, KJV)

We have read this list before. In Genesis chapter 46, when Jacob's whole household went down to Egypt, the Bible already counted them carefully once: all the souls of Jacob were seventy persons (Genesis 46:27). Why, at the opening of a new scroll, list them all over again? Because the list here is a bridge. The previous scroll told of a called family; this scroll tells of an oppressed nation; the list joins the two together and reminds the reader: the people who later were slaves in Egypt were not a crowd that appeared out of nowhere — they are precisely the descendants of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the heirs of the promise. Their going down to Egypt was not the end point of wandering; it was on the road toward the fulfillment of what God had already said to Abraham more than four hundred years earlier — "thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs, and shall serve them; and they shall afflict them four hundred years" (Genesis 15:13). The suffering had not yet begun, but God's word had already arrived first. This matters greatly: the Israelites did not blunder into suffering in Egypt, nor was God's promise a patch improvised after suffering arrived. God spoke first of the sojourning and the affliction, and He also spoke first of "afterward shall they come out with great substance" (Genesis 15:14). Exodus chapter 1 places these two sentences before us at the same time: one already being fulfilled, the other not yet — and the reader must learn to live between the two sentences.

There is one more detail in the list: "for Joseph was in Egypt already." The eleven brothers "came"; Joseph "was already there." He arrived first, he became the governor, he prepared the grain, he brought the whole family in and settled them in Goshen. Almost all the glory of the second half of Genesis is tied to the one man Joseph. Yet Exodus, with one sentence calm to the point of cruelty, turns that whole generation over: "And Joseph died, and all his brethren, and all that generation." All died. The one who saved the whole family died, and those who were saved died too; those who wept as they recognized one another died — the famine, the tears, the embraces, the forgiveness, all became matters of the previous generation. The Bible is not sentimental; it honestly tells us that even the greatest generation passes away entire. Human kindness expires with the person; human merit is diluted by the generations. If Israel's survival had rested on the merit of Joseph alone, then the day Joseph died, this nation should have ended.

But in verse 7 the pen turns abruptly: "And the children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and waxed exceeding mighty; and the land was filled with them." The original piles verb upon verb, like water rising beyond control. The first two words are our first word anchor today: "were fruitful and multiplied," in the original parah (פָּרָה, H6509, to bear fruit, to be fruitful) and ravah (רָבָה, H7235, to increase, to become many) [1]. As soon as these two words sound, anyone who has read Genesis should prick up their ears: in Genesis chapter 1, God blessed mankind and said, "Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth" (Genesis 1:28); after the flood, God repeated the same blessing to Noah and his sons; and when God appeared to Jacob at Bethel He said again: "And God said unto him, I am God Almighty: be fruitful and multiply; a nation and a company of nations shall be of thee, and kings shall come out of thy loins." (Genesis 35:11, KJV) Exodus 1:7 is precisely the echo, on Egyptian soil, of this chain of promises. Seventy persons went down to Egypt, and now they "filled the land" — and this word "filled" is exactly the language of blessing in Genesis: God willed that mankind fill the earth, and now Jacob's descendants first filled the land of Egypt.

One more word deserves a pause: in "waxed exceeding mighty," the word "mighty" is in the original 'atsum (עָצוּם, H6099, mighty, strong, powerful in number) [2]. This word appears twice in this chapter: in verse 7 the narrator uses it to describe the flourishing of the Israelites, and in verse 9 the new king uses the same word to describe the Israelites as a threat to Egypt. The same fact is called prosperity on God's side and called threat on the king's side. The multiplying of the promise, in the eyes of those who fear God, is a blessing; in the eyes of those who fear losing power, it is a danger. All the tension of Exodus chapter 1 is hidden in these two readings of one word.

So in Part One we must grasp this: the prosperity did not come from Joseph's office. While Joseph lived, what the Israelites received was settlement: a plot of Goshen, Pharaoh's reception, the preservation of a whole family's lives. That was already great grace. But the prosperity of verse 7 happened after Joseph's death, after a whole generation had passed away. It does not rest on any great man; it rests on that blessing in Genesis chapter 1, on God's promise to the patriarchs. Brothers and sisters, this is a great comfort to us: in your years of believing in the Lord, you may have received great help from some elder, some pastor, some benefactor; they are like Joseph, people God prepared ahead of you. But please remember: God's blessing was never held hostage by a human being. Benefactors pass away, generations change, and one day the person who helped you is no longer there — but God's promise does not stop together with anyone's coffin. Joseph died, and the promise lived; that generation passed, and the next generation "filled the land." The way God works has always been to pass over the coffin and, following the list of names, write onward.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The New King Who Did Not Know Joseph, and the Growth That Could Not Be Stopped (1:8-14)

Verse 8 is the hinge of the whole chapter: "Now there arose up a new king over Egypt, which knew not Joseph." What does it mean that he did not know Joseph? It does not mean this king had not studied his history textbook. The story of how Joseph prepared grain for Egypt, saved it from famine, and gained all the land for Pharaoh in the famine could not possibly have been unknown. "Not know" here is the breaking of a relationship: the new dynasty no longer acknowledged Joseph's merit, and no longer regarded the Israelites as the descendants of a man of merit. Gratitude has a shelf life, especially in the hands of power. The favor owed by the previous king can be cancelled in one stroke by the next. When Stephen retold this history in Acts, he put it even more directly: it was when another king arose that the people "grew and multiplied," just as God had promised Abraham (Acts 7:17-19). Please lay these two things on top of each other: at the very same time that God's promise was accelerating toward fulfillment, the earthly king was turning hostile. Prosperity and suffering did not arrive one after the other; they arrived at the same time. The Israelites were not first prosperous and then unlucky; they drew affliction because of their prosperity, and in the midst of affliction they went on prospering.

The new king said to the Egyptians: "Behold, the people of the children of Israel are more and mightier than we." (Exodus 1:9) In these words there is truth and falsehood. The truth is that the Israelites indeed had become many; the falsehood is the fearful inference: "Come on, let us deal wisely with them; lest they multiply, and it come to pass, that, when there falleth out any war, they join also unto our enemies, and fight against us, and so get them up out of the land." (Exodus 1:10) Note that the last thing Pharaoh feared was that they would "get them up out of the land" — yet in the whole book of Exodus that follows, what Pharaoh desperately blocks is precisely their leaving. Inferences made in fear are never logical: if the people are many, that is a threat; if the people leave, that is also a threat; staying is a calamity, leaving is a loss. Once power defines a group as a threat, anything can become a charge against them. This chapter does not develop political commentary; it simply lays out the facts: the multiplying of a nation was read by the king as a hidden danger of war; a group of peacefully settled sojourners was rewritten by the king's fear into an imagined enemy.

Then came the first level of affliction: forced labor as a system. "Therefore they did set over them taskmasters to afflict them with their burdens." "Taskmasters" in the original is sare mas (שָׂרֵי מַס, sar, H8269, chief, official, in the construct form, plus mas, H4522, forced labor, corvée) — "officers of the forced labor," officials charged specifically with compulsory labor [3]. "Afflict" is in the original 'innah (עִנָּה, H6031, in the intensive causative stem, to oppress, to make suffer, to bring low), used in succession in verses 11 and 12 of this chapter [4]. Notice the order: first the defining by fear, then the establishment of offices, then the stepping up of burdens. When suffering falls on a people, it has often already put on the clothing of a system, with a title, an establishment, and a construction schedule. The Israelites were assigned to build two treasure cities for Pharaoh: Pithom and Raamses. Treasure cities were store-cities for grain and military supplies — Joseph once stored grain for Egypt to save lives; now Joseph's descendants built storage as forced laborers. In the same world of words, kindness had flipped into bitter service. The empire's storehouses grew bigger and bigger, while the people who built them were treated as consumable material inside the storehouse. The Bible records the names of these two cities not to give us an archaeological curiosity, but to make us remember: behind many magnificent works of history are engraved the sweat and blood of nameless people, and God records the account of the sufferers, entry by entry, in His own book.

Verses 13 and 14 push the forced labor to the top: "And the Egyptians made the children of Israel to serve with rigour: and they made their lives bitter with hard bondage, in morter, and in brick, and in all manner of service in the field: all their service, wherein they made them serve, was with rigour." Mortar, brick, and every kind of work in the field — from building materials to farm work, nothing was left out; and "with rigour" appears twice in just two verses. When affliction reaches this point, the purpose is already very clear: it was not only to make them work more, but to grind away this nation's vitality with forced labor, to exhaust them until they could not bear children, so that the multiplying would stop by itself. This was Pharaoh's calculation: you are good at bearing children, are you? I will leave you no strength to bear them. You are many, are you? I will break you down before you become many.

Yet verse 12 is the first thunderclap of the whole chapter: "But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and grew. And they were grieved because of the children of Israel." The form of the original sentence is "just as ... so ...": just as the Egyptians afflicted them, so they multiplied and spread abroad. However much the affliction increased, the growth increased; the scale of oppression became the scale of growth. This does not say that suffering itself is a good thing; the Bible never whitewashes forced labor — mortar and brick were truly bitter, and bitter lives were truly bitter lives. What this verse says is something else: affliction does not possess the kind of deciding power it imagines itself to have. Pharaoh thought heavy burdens could terminate the multiplying; God let the multiplying continue under the heavy burdens. The Egyptians' reaction was to be "grieved," a word carrying disgust and dread: the oppressor discovered for the first time that the tool in his hand had failed. The harder he pushed, the more they grew; this sense of losing control made the king more uneasy than any resistance could.

Brothers and sisters, there is a measure here that must be stated clearly, lest we misuse this verse. God did not say that affliction deserves to be celebrated, nor did He tell sufferers to refuse deliverance and rest content in forced labor; the whole book of Exodus is precisely the story of God Himself coming down to dismantle this system of forced labor. What verse 12 gives us is not a slogan that "suffering is beneficial," but an assurance that "affliction has no right to terminate the promise": human oppression can change the circumstances of God's people, but it cannot change the destiny of God's people; it can increase their burdens, but it cannot stop God's hand of blessing. If prosperity were granted by the king, it should have been cut off the moment the king turned hostile; Exodus chapter 1 tells us that prosperity never rested on the king's permission, and therefore the king's affliction cannot take it back either.

Psalm 105, looking back on this period, states both sides in full: "He increased his people greatly; and made them stronger than their enemies. He turned their heart to hate his people." (Psalm 105:24-25, excerpt, KJV) The psalmist did not call the affliction an accident, nor the prosperity a stroke of luck: the people's strength came from God's making it so, and the enemies' hatred is also placed under God's remembrance. This is not asking us to whitewash affliction; it is telling those who are being afflicted: the hatred you encounter is not outside God's sight; the God who makes you prosper will also Himself remember the account of that hatred, and will Himself make a way and vindicate you.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Midwives' Fear of God, and the Third Level of Affliction (1:15-22)

When forced labor could not crush the multiplying, Pharaoh changed tactics: from a public system to the secret birthing room. The second level of affliction is killing infants by a borrowed knife. "And the king of Egypt spake to the Hebrew midwives, of which the name of the one was Shiphrah, and the name of the other Puah: When ye do the office of a midwife to the Hebrew women, and see them upon the stools; if it be a son, then ye shall kill him: but if it be a daughter, then she shall live." (Exodus 1:15-16, excerpt, KJV)

Please stop here and notice two names: Shiphrah and Puah. In this chapter Pharaoh has no name — the text calls him only "the king of Egypt" and "Pharaoh"; yet two midwives of lowly station have their names recorded clearly. The Bible's way of recording names is often the opposite of the world's way: the world records the names of kings and forgets the names of small people; the Bible leaves the almighty king anonymous in this chapter and lets the names of two midwives remain. They are among the few "nameless ones" in the Bible who are actually named — not queens, not prophets, only women who delivered babies in the birthing room. Yet the turning of history was precisely in the birthing room. Pharaoh thought the war was at the border and at the construction site; God placed the battlefield beside the birth stool: for a moment, the survival of a nation lay in the hands of whether or not two women feared God.

Pharaoh's command was extremely insidious: kill the male infants, strike at the very moment of birth, and make it look like a difficult labor, like an accident, so that the mothers would think it was fate and the midwives would not bear the name of public executioners. The daughters were to be kept, because in his eyes girls posed no "war" threat and could later be assimilated and married into Egyptian households — to exterminate a nation, one does not have to kill everyone; break off the male line, scatter the genealogy, and after a few generations there is no such nation anymore. This was a command with exterminating intent, clear and unmistakable.

"But the midwives feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the men children alive." (Exodus 1:17, KJV) This is the second great word anchor of this chapter: "feared," in the original yare' (יָרֵא, H3372, to fear, to revere); verse 17 says the midwives feared God, and verse 21 says again that "because the midwives feared God," God made them households [5]. The same "fear," two objects: Pharaoh wanted them to fear the king's command; God made them revere God. The fear of God is not an emotion; it is an ordering: when the king's command and God's will collide head-on, they placed God before the king. They "saved the men children alive" — "saved alive" is in the original chayah (חָיָה, H2421, in the causative stem, to make live, to preserve life) [6]. This word appears once more at the end of the chapter, but from Pharaoh's mouth: "every daughter ye shall save alive." The same phrase "save alive": Pharaoh used it to draw the line of who deserved to live; the midwives declared by their action that a child's life is not drawn by the king, but given by God. The king said which boys should die; they let the boys live. This is what the fear of God looks like when it lands on the ground — letting live those who should live.

Here the measure of the teaching must be guarded, because this passage is the easiest to over-preach. The facts of this chapter support only the following, and we speak only according to these facts: first, there was a command directly ordering the killing of innocent infants, and that command itself was evil; second, the midwives, because they feared God, did not carry out this murderous command; third, the point the Bible praises is their fear of God and their preserving of life, not the verbal detail of their answer to Pharaoh about the Hebrew women being vigorous — the text records that exchange, but it does not stop to discuss whether the answer was true or false; its lens stays on the four words "feared God"; fourth, for this, God caused the people to multiply and made the midwives households. We do not expand this chapter into a political manifesto, we do not apply it as a template to any regime or figure in reality, and we do not derive from it a general rule that "any command may be disobeyed." The boundary this chapter gives is narrow, and it is clear: when a command directly requires a person to take the life of the innocent, the one who fears God places God above the command, and would rather bear the risk herself than let a child die by her own hand. This is not a program of rebellion; this is the bottom line in the birthing room: hands are for delivering babies, not for killing infants.

Please also see the price they paid and the reward they received. They disobeyed at the risk of their lives — when Pharaoh summoned them for questioning, they were standing in a place where they could lose their heads at any moment. Yet the text says God made them "households": the women who, in the birthing room, brought to completion the children of countless other families had their own households brought to completion by God Himself. The world measures small people by whether they are useful; God measures small people by whether they revere Him. Shiphrah and Puah had no army, no pulpit, no office; they had only a pair of hands for delivering babies and a heart that feared God, and God used people like these to stand at the turning point of history. That Moses could later survive and be placed in an ark by the riverside had, as its background, the generation of boys whom the midwives first preserved: a nation was first protected in the birthing room by people who feared God, and only afterward were there the wilderness, Mount Sinai, and the promised land.

When forced labor failed and the borrowed knife failed, Pharaoh played his last card. The third level of affliction is a public command to the whole people: "And Pharaoh charged all his people, saying, Every son that is born ye shall cast into the river, and every daughter ye shall save alive." (Exodus 1:22) The first level was a system pressing on the whole people; the second level was a secret order falling into the hands of two midwives; the third level is the king distributing the task of killing infants to all his people — every Egyptian was pulled in as an executioner. The Nile was Egypt's lifeline, the river god they worshipped, the source of the land's fertility; Pharaoh would turn this river of life into a graveyard for Hebrew boys. Affliction here reaches its summit: public, total, no longer concealed. And the Bible stops this chapter here, stops at the riverside, without immediately giving a solution. At the very opening of the next chapter, a boy of the house of Levi is born, is hidden for three months, and when he can no longer be hidden, is placed in an ark of bulrushes daubed with slime and pitch and laid "in the flags by the river's brink" — the same river: the king wanted it to swallow life, but God would use it to lift up a deliverer. But that is the next chapter's story. What this chapter leaves us is an ending suspended at the riverside: the command has been issued, the river is flowing, and children are still being born. The reader is left by God inside the tension in order to learn one thing first — before the solution has appeared, recognize clearly whose hand is upholding this nation: not the king's permission, not human cleverness, but that God who makes people "the more afflicted, the more multiplied."

Gather this part together in three sentences for ourselves. First, prosperity does not rest on permission: do not misread God's blessing as the environment's goodwill toward you, and when the environment turns hostile, do not think the blessing has been cut off; the source is in God, not in the king. Second, affliction cannot stop the promise, but we do not beautify affliction: we cry out for deliverance for those who suffer, just as God later came down Himself to deliver; we only hold this certainty, that the oppressor's calculation does not hold the final stroke — God does. Third, small people who fear God stand at the turning point: you may have no position and no reputation, and in your hands only a humble piece of work, but the turning points of history are often not in the throne room, but in the birthing room, at the riverside, beside the desk where you work faithfully every day. The bottom line you hold because you fear God may be protecting a next generation you cannot yet see.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 1 and seen a list of names, three levels of affliction, and a growth that could not be stopped. The list tells us that this nation is the descendant of the promise: seventy persons went down to Egypt — that is the bridge from Genesis, not a break. The three levels of affliction tell us that oppression escalates — from the taskmasters' burdens, to the secret order in the birthing room, to the command to all the people at the riverside. The unstoppable growth tells us that God's word outlasts the king's command: "The more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and grew." And in the darkest place of the whole chapter stand two midwives with names: Shiphrah and Puah. They tell us that fearing God is not a matter of words; it is placing God before the king, and letting children who should live, live.

Let us pray together: Faithful Lord, thank You: Joseph's generation passed away, but Your promise did not pass away; the new king did not know Joseph, but You know every name of Your people. When we are oppressed, misunderstood, and afflicted level upon level, let us not stake our hope on any king's permission, nor take suffering as evidence of Your absence. Give us the reverence of Shiphrah and Puah: when Your word collides with human commands, to place You first; let our hands be used only to bring life to completion, never to harm the innocent. And please open a way Yourself for those in affliction, as You later came down Yourself to deliver Israel. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who saves all peoples, Amen.

The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[6] chayah: Hebrew חָיָה (H2421), to live, to survive; in the causative stem, to make live, to preserve life, to keep alive. Exodus 1:17, "saved the men children alive," 1:18, Pharaoh's rebuke, "why have ye saved the men children alive," and 1:22, Pharaoh's command to "save alive" the daughters, all use this root. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the same phrase 'save alive,' used by the king to draw a line and by the midwives to bring life to completion" is a homiletical exposition based on reading 1:17 and 1:22 together.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and grew." (Exodus 1:12, KJV) "But the midwives feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the men children alive." (Exodus 1:17, KJV)

Exodus chapter 1 is a chapter with no miracle in it: no Red Sea, no pillar of cloud, only the taskmasters' burdens growing heavier, and in the birthing room an order to kill infants. Yet not one word of God fell to the ground. Joseph died, but the promise lived on; the king turned hostile, but the people kept multiplying. Two midwives of no standing, Shiphrah and Puah, because they feared God, put God before the king and let the children live, and history turned at the edge of their hands. Brothers and sisters, if your prosperity is built on someone's permission, you are finished the day that person turns; if it is built on God's promise, affliction can only pile higher — it cannot stop the multiplying. Do not fear the heavy burden first; fear losing reverence first. Put God first, and hold the line beside your own hands that preserves life — you do not know at the turning point of which generation you are standing. Pray once today for the burden on your own shoulders: do not first ask some king to show favor; first ask the Lord to give you reverence; do not first ask to escape at once; first ask to keep your bottom line where you are. The burden can be handed to Him; the bottom line must be held by you. Prosperity will come, because the promise stands; you only stand faithfully, and wait for the God who makes people the more afflicted, the more multiplied.

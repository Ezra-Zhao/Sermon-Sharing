# Twelve Amens — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 27

> Status: First translation · Draft awaiting proofreading (首译·初译待校). English version of the Chinese final text dated 2026-10-24, which passed the ZH strong re-check (the source records the sāter word-form at two places as corrected in that re-check). Scripture quotations in this version are embedded verbatim from the KJV text actually retrieved for this translation (see the Batch 9 first-translation record at the end); they are not re-translated from the Chinese.

> Title verification: Three candidates were considered. (1) "Written Plainly upon the Stones," from the command to engrave the stones in v. 8 — the chapter's textual skeleton. (2) "Twelve Amens," from the twelve curses of vv. 15–26, after each of which the people answer "Amen" once — exactly twelve occurrences in the chapter, its vocal skeleton. (3) "This Day Thou Art Become the People of the LORD Thy God," from the declaration of identity in v. 9 — the chapter's covenant skeleton. Candidate (2) is adopted: stones weather and the stationing disperses, but these twelve "Amens" are the words the people signed with their own mouths — here the covenant is not heard and left behind; it is answered clause by clause and claimed clause by clause. The English title is fixed as "Twelve Amens."

## Introduction (00:00–06:00)

[Summary Layer] Chapter 26 consists of two liturgical texts to be spoken aloud with one's own mouth; chapter 27 is a rite to be made with one's own hands and taken in with one's own ears: set up stones, plaister them, write on them, build an altar, offer sacrifices, stand divided on two mountains, proclaim aloud, and answer clause by clause. The law has been spoken (chs. 12–26); what follows is not dismissal, but entry — the words are first written on stones, and then the people stand between the two mountains and sign the covenant with their own voices. [Fact Layer] The speakers who issue the commands in this chapter rotate three times: at v. 1, "Moses with the elders of Israel" issue the command together; at v. 9, "Moses and the priests the Levites" speak together; at v. 11, Moses charges alone — elders, priests, and Moses take turns at the speaker's place; by design this rite is not one man's monologue but the whole people's public act. [Theological Development Layer] Four things in this chapter deserve to be registered in advance: the words on the stones (the law made public and made to endure), the sacrifices on the altar (worship before entering the land), the people on the two mountains (blessing and curse each have their station), and the twelve Amens (the people's own-mouth claiming of every curse). The four compose one picture: before a nation enters its inheritance, it proclaims its identity, its boundaries, and its responsibility aloud before heaven and earth. This sermon's four sections unfold accordingly: Section 1, Written upon the Stones (vv. 1–8); Section 2, Standing between the Two Mountains (vv. 9–14); Section 3, the First Six Amens (vv. 15–20); Section 4, the Last Six Amens and the One Comprehensive Clause (vv. 21–26). [Fact Layer] One structural fact of the whole chapter is registered first: the chapter commands the people to stand on Mount Gerizim "to bless the people" (v. 12), yet it writes down not a single word of blessing — in this chapter the blessing has a station but no text; the only words written out verbatim are the twelve curses. Where the text of the blessing is to be found, ch. 28 of this book gives its own account (registered by section reference only); why this chapter weights the curses and leaves the blessing light is read differently by commentators, and this sermon sets this textual fact side by side only, with no ruling (Note 8).

## 1. Written upon the Stones (Deut 27:1–8; 06:00–19:00)

[Fact Layer] Verse 1 is the chapter's command frame; the discourse's quotation mark opens at this verse. The KJV in full: "And Moses with the elders of Israel commanded the people, saying, Keep all the commandments which I command you this day." Items registered one by one. First, the commanders are Moses and the elders (zəqēnîm, H2205) — the elders stand beside Moses at the speaker's place in this book's discourses, and the public character of this chapter's rite is set from here. Second, "this day" occurs four times in the chapter (vv. 1, 4, 9, 10; Note 2) — its first occurrence is here: every step of the rite is nailed to "this day"; it is not a future conception but the schedule for the day of crossing. Third, the governing line is a single sentence: keep all the commandments — the stones, the altar, the mountains, and the voices below all serve this one sentence. [Fact Layer] Verse 2 is the command to set up the stones, in full: "And it shall be on the day when ye shall pass over Jordan unto the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee, that thou shalt set thee up great stones, and plaister them with plaister:" Items registered. First, the time is "on the day" — crossing and stone-setting fall on the same day (the 2010 version reads "on that day" here; entered separately, Note 3). Second, the material is "great stones" — the base text does not fix their number; the weight of the clause is not in the count but in the scale: what is to be set up is a monument, not rubble. Third, the technique is "plaister them with plaister" (sîḏ, H7875) — the stones are first plaistered with a layer of plaister as a writing surface: the words are not cut into the bone of the stone but written on the plaistered face (vv. 3 and 8 twice say expressly "write"); the number of the verb form rendered "plaister" is undetermined and is registered as pending verification (Note 8). Fourth, "set up" stones occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 2, 4) — commanded here first, and given its place at v. 4. [Fact Layer] Verse 3 is the command to write, together with the promise of entering the land, in full: "And thou shalt write upon them all the words of this law, when thou art passed over, that thou mayest go in unto the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee, a land that floweth with milk and honey; as the LORD God of thy fathers hath promised thee." Items registered. First, "all the words of this law" — in the Chinese base ledger this phrase occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 3, 8); in the KJV it occurs three times, the third at v. 26 (see Note 2; the two ledgers are set side by side and not merged): what is written is "all the words," not a selection and not a summary — the public edition of the law must be the whole book. Second, the occasion of the writing is after "thou art passed over," inside the land — the monument stands within the inheritance, not in the wilderness: the law is the charter of those who enter the land; first the monument is set up, then the land is divided. Third, "a land that floweth with milk and honey" occurs only here in this chapter — the wording of the confession text of ch. 26 (26:9, 15; within-book cross-references registered only) reappears here: the same land is, in the confession, the object looked back upon, and in the stone-setting command, the reality about to be entered. [Fact Layer] Verse 4 names the mountain where the stones are to be set up, in full: "Therefore it shall be when ye be gone over Jordan, that ye shall set up these stones, which I command you this day, in mount Ebal, and thou shalt plaister them with plaister." Items registered. First, the base text, with the Masoretic text, reads "mount Ebal," and the KJV likewise reads "in mount Ebal." Second, this verse carries the chapter's most important variant: the 2010 Union Version (both the "God" and the "Shangdi" editions, simplified and traditional alike) reads "Mount Gerizim" — a reading deriving from the Samaritan Pentateuch tradition (set side by side in Note 5). Third, the difference between the two pictures is registered as it stands: read with the Masoretic text, the law-stones and the altar stand on the mountain of cursing (Ebal, where the six tribes of v. 13 announce the curse); read with the Samaritan text, the stones and the altar stand on the mountain of blessing (Gerizim, where the six tribes of v. 12 bless the people). Which reading is prior, this sermon does not rule (Discipline 2). [Fact Layer] Verses 5–6 are the command to build the altar, in full: "And there shalt thou build an altar unto the LORD thy God, an altar of stones: thou shalt not lift up any iron tool upon them. Thou shalt build the altar of the LORD thy God of whole stones: and thou shalt offer burnt offerings thereon unto the LORD thy God:" Items registered. First, at v. 5 the base text breaks the sentence with a semicolon (modern-punctuation editions use a comma; entered separately in Note 3). Second, "thou shalt not lift up any iron tool upon them" — the altar stones may not be worked with an iron tool; v. 6 restates it positively as "whole stones": the original is ʾăḇānîm šəlēmôt, and šālēm (H8003) means whole, undivided — the specification for the altar's stone is whole stone: God's altar does not take stone that human hands have dressed. Third, the same stones meet two fates: the stones for writing are to be plaistered (a writing surface prepared by human hands), and the stones for the altar may not be touched by iron (human working forbidden) — within one chapter, text and sacrifice, writing and not-writing, are kept clearly apart. Fourth, within v. 6 the two forms of the divine-name formula stand side by side in the Chinese base text (entered separately, Note 3) — for the chapter's ten occurrences of the sacred name in their forms, see Note 2. [Fact Layer] Verse 7 is the second offering on the altar and a feast, in full: "And thou shalt offer peace offerings, and shalt eat there, and rejoice before the LORD thy God." Items registered. First, the offerings are of two kinds: the burnt offerings belong wholly to the altar (v. 6), and the peace offerings may be eaten (v. 7) — first wholly offered, then eaten together, in the altar's usual order. Second, "rejoice" occurs only here in this chapter — the proclamation of the curses has not yet begun, and there is rejoicing before the altar first: the emotional ground-tone of this chapter is not fear but joy within worship (the rejoicing of the offering company at 26:11 is registered as a within-book cross-reference only). Third, the place of the eating and rejoicing is "before the LORD thy God" — the feast is set beside the altar, not at home. [Fact Layer] Verse 8 is the restatement of the writing command and the close of the first discourse, in full: "And thou shalt write upon the stones all the words of this law very plainly." Items registered. First, the quotation mark closes at the end of this verse in the base text (opened at v. 1, closed at v. 8). Second, "very plainly" is in the original a form of bāʾar (H874) with an adverbial "make it thoroughly clear" — the writing is to be written plainly, plainly: the law's publication is not only that it be set up, but that it be legible, recognizable, and unambiguous. Third, this verse and v. 3 form a head-and-tail frame: v. 3 says what is to be written (all the words of this law), and v. 8 says how it is to be written (very plainly) — all the commands of the first section are framed by these two "write" sentences. [Theological Development Layer] The order of this section may not be reversed: first the stones are set up, then the land is divided; first the words are written, then the proclamation is heard; first there is an altar, then there is stationing on the mountains. In Israel's schedule for entering the land, the law stands first — not settle the inheritance first and supply the faith afterwards, but set the words up on stones first, and then receive the land. There is a further layer: the written stones stand where everyone passes in and out, and the words are for all to see — the law is not the priestly class's secret store; it is a public text written on a plaistered face for anyone to read plainly. [Summary Layer] This section may be taken in four points: first, the stones are set up on that day (v. 2); second, the writing must be the whole text (v. 3); third, the altar is built of whole stones (vv. 5–6); fourth, written plainly, and with rejoicing (vv. 7–8). [New Testament Reading Layer] This section registers one structural echo against the New Testament only, with no reading backwards: the contrast between the law written on stones as an "outward letter" and the New Testament's "written on the fleshy tables of the heart" is a contrast commonly registered at the reading layer (the relevant passages are registered by index only; among them, the phrase "written on the fleshy tables of the heart" was verified directly, verse page by verse page, in leg-supply round 17, and is the base text's own wording at 2 Cor 3:3, "not written on tables of stone, but on fleshy tables of the heart," registered at phrase level) — the Old Testament's stone and plaister, the New Testament's heart and Spirit: the contrast is registered so, and the relation is not ruled upon. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two items are registered in this section. First, the altar rule "no iron tool / build of whole stones" stands close in wording to the altar law of Exod 20:24–25 (registered by section reference only; this sermon has not verified it word for word), and the relation between them (common origin, mutual annotation, or each a rule of its own) is read differently by commentators — set side by side only. Second, writing large characters on a plaistered face on a stone monument has its material background in the ancient world (Egypt and the Near East both have instances of writing on plaistered surfaces and setting up monuments to publish laws; registered generally at the discussion layer, with no unverified instance cited) — what is distinctive in this chapter is not the setting up of a monument itself, but that the monument's text is "all the words of this law": a nation's whole law, set up as one readable wall.


## 2. Standing between the Two Mountains (Deut 27:9–14; 19:00–30:00)

[Fact Layer] Verses 9–10 are the second round of speaking; a new set of discourse quotation marks opens at v. 9 and closes at the end of v. 10 in the base text. In full: "And Moses and the priests the Levites spake unto all Israel, saying, Take heed, and hearken, O Israel; this day thou art become the people of the LORD thy God. Thou shalt therefore obey the voice of the LORD thy God, and do his commandments and his statutes, which I command thee this day." Items registered. First, the speakers change shift: Moses and the priests the Levites — the rite's executive layer takes over here (the elders issued the command before; the priests speak after; the division of labour is registered as it stands). Second, the first sentence is "Take heed, and hearken" — before the proclamation, first be still: the liturgy of hearing begins in silence. Third, "this day thou art become the people of the LORD thy God" — the third occurrence of "this day" (Note 2); "art become" is a declaration in the completed form: not that they will become in the future, but that this day they have already become; the people's identity is declared in this verse first, and only then comes the proclamation on the two mountains — the order is registered as it stands: first the people, then the hearing of the curses. Fourth, the specification of response at v. 10 is "obey the voice … and do his commandments and his statutes" — in the base text "commandments and statutes" are written continuously, without a pause mark (entered separately, Note 3). Fifth, the 2010 version at v. 9 reads "Moses and the priests of the house of Levi charged," and renders "people" as "subjects" (entered separately, Note 3). [Fact Layer] Verses 11–13 are the stationing command: v. 11 is narrative, the quotation opens at v. 12 and continues at v. 13. In full: "And Moses charged the people the same day, saying, These shall stand upon mount Gerizim to bless the people, when ye are come over Jordan; Simeon, and Levi, and Judah, and Issachar, and Joseph, and Benjamin: And these shall stand upon mount Ebal to curse; Reuben, Gad, and Asher, and Zebulun, Dan, and Naphtali." Items registered. First, "the same day" is accounted separately from the chapter's four occurrences of "this day" (Note 2) — the narrator's time-word is "the same day"; the time-word inside the discourses is "this day." Second, the six tribes on Mount Gerizim, in the KJV name-forms: Simeon, and Levi, and Judah, and Issachar, and Joseph, and Benjamin — "Joseph" is registered here as the name of one tribe (the account in ch. 33 of this book, where at the blessing of the tribes Joseph is divided into the two names Ephraim and Manasseh, is registered by section reference only; the two accounts are set side by side, Note 8). Third, the six tribes on Mount Ebal, in the KJV name-forms: Reuben, Gad, and Asher, and Zebulun, Dan, and Naphtali. Fourth, the verb is "shall stand" (ʿāmaḏ, H5975, twice; Note 2) — not assemble, not pass by, but stand firm: the two companies are to stand on the two mountains as the two wings of the rite. Fifth, one textual observation is registered as it stands: the tribe of Levi stands on Gerizim (the blessing side, v. 12), and it is also the Levites who proclaim the curses at v. 14 — the same tribe both takes its station on the mountain of blessing and proclaims the curses aloud to all: office and station overlap here, and this sermon registers it only, without developing the reason. [Fact Layer] Verse 14 is the proclamation command, and the end of the Masoretic unit 11–14 (samekh at the end of this verse; Note 1), in full: "And the Levites shall speak, and say unto all the men of Israel with a loud voice," Items registered. First, the proclaimers are the Levites — a tribe with no inheritance, scattered among the cities, here serves as the proclaiming mouth of the whole people. Second, "with a loud voice" — in the original, qôl rām: the voice is to be loud, to carry far, to be heard by those standing over both mountains: the volume specification of this proclamation is written into the clause itself. Third, the verb in the original is ʿānâ (H6030) — its basic sense is "to answer, to respond": the Levites' proclamation is, in form, an "answering" first, and the people's "Amen" below is an "answering" after it (the people's answer at v. 15 uses the same word; Note 4) — the whole rite is one vast question-and-answer: one side proclaims aloud, the other answers clause by clause. [Theological Development Layer] The theological centre of this section is the order of identity and obligation. Verse 9 first says "this day thou art become the people," and only at v. 14 does the proclamation of the curses begin — the curses are not an entrance condition spoken to outsiders; they are the house rules spoken to those who have already become the people. A person does not become one of the people by passing the examination of the curses; it is because he has first been declared one of the people that he is qualified, and also obliged, to stand between these two mountains, hear every clause through, and answer every one. There is a further layer in the "standing": blessing and curse are not abstract true-or-false questions; they are geography a person must stand into in his own body — the mountain you stand on is the proclamation you belong to; and yet the whole people hear the voices of both sides at once: no one hears blessing only, and no one hears curse only. [Summary Layer] This section may be taken in four points: first, hearken in silence first (v. 9); second, identity goes first (v. 9); third, the two mountains divide the standing (vv. 12–13); fourth, proclamation with a loud voice (v. 14). [New Testament Reading Layer] This section registers one distant echo only, with no reading backwards: the memory of Mount Gerizim as a place of worship reappears in the New Testament in the Samaritan woman's conversation with Jesus about worship (John 4, registered by section reference only; this sermon has not verified it word for word and quotes nothing) — the afterwave of the two mountains' dispute continued for a thousand years; the echo is registered so, and the relation is not ruled upon. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items are set side by side in this section, per discipline. First, the received geographical identification of the two mountains: Mount Gerizim and Mount Ebal flank the valley of Shechem (in the region of modern Nablus) on either side, the received identification placing Gerizim to the south and Ebal to the north — this is the received geographical identification, registered only, not made an argument; the claim that the valley between the two mountains gathers sound and that voices can be heard across it is a traditional claim of long standing, and this sermon registers its existence only, without making an acoustical argument. Second, the relation between the advance notice of Deut 11:29 and this chapter — 11:29 has been verified word for word directly, and is set side by side here in full: "And it shall come to pass, when the LORD thy God hath brought thee in unto the land whither thou goest to possess it, that thou shalt put the blessing upon mount Gerizim, and the curse upon mount Ebal." — Chapter 11 is the advance notice (put the blessing upon Gerizim and the curse upon Ebal); chapter 27 is the detailed regulation (stones, altar, stationing, the text of the proclamation); whether the two texts stand as command and detailed regulation, or as traditions of different levels, is set side by side, with no ruling. Third, the correspondence between the execution account of Josh 8:30–35 and this chapter (building the altar, writing, proclaiming) is registered only in Note 6; of it, 8:34 has been verified word for word directly, and the quotation stands in Section 4's discussion layer; the relation between this chapter's "on the day" at v. 2 and the time of execution in Josh 8 (which in the narrative order of Joshua stands after the battle of Ai) is read differently by commentators — set side by side only.

## 3. The First Six Amens (Deut 27:15–20; 30:00–43:00)

[Fact Layer] The form of the proclamation is stated clearly first. Verse 15 is the first curse, and the format specimen for the whole list, in full: "Cursed be the man that maketh any graven or molten image, an abomination unto the LORD, the work of the hands of the craftsman, and putteth it in a secret place. And all the people shall answer and say, Amen." Items registered. First, each curse has three components: the charge (a participial clause describing the person and his act), the verdict "Cursed be" (ʾārûr, H779, twelve occurrences in the chapter; Note 2), and the answer "Amen" (ʾāmēn, H543). Second, v. 15 is the only verse in the chapter that reads "shall answer and say" — the other eleven (vv. 16–26) all read "shall say" (in the KJV likewise: v. 15 "shall answer and say," vv. 16–26 "shall say"; entered separately, Note 3): the wording of the first clause is the most complete, like the front page of a form contract. Third, the Masoretes cut each curse into its own small unit (samekh at the end of each verse; Note 1) — in the manuscript tradition the twelve curses are not one long string spoken in a single breath; they are twelve units, each closed in itself, corresponding one-to-one with the twelve "Amens." [Fact Layer] The first curse, the idol (v. 15): the charge in full stands above — "Cursed be the man that maketh any graven or molten image, an abomination unto the LORD, the work of the hands of the craftsman, and putteth it in a secret place." Items registered. First, the idol is named with two words together: pesel (H6459) is the graven image, massēkâ (H4541) the molten image. Second, "an abomination unto the LORD" (tôʿēḇâ, H8441) — the characterization of the charge is not determined by man; it is determined by God's abomination. Third, the actor is the "craftsman" (ḥārāš, H2796) — serving falsehood with his craft. Fourth, the key adverbial phrase is "in a secret place" (sāter, H5643, twice in the chapter, the other occurrence at v. 24; Note 4): this idol is not in a temple; it is in a secret place — no one sees it, no one can accuse it. The first curse opens by declaring its scope: the covenant's curses govern precisely where the human eye cannot reach. [Fact Layer] The second curse, parents (v. 16), in full: "Cursed be he that setteth light by his father or his mother. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, "setteth light by" — in the original, maqleh is a participle whose root sense is to treat as light, to make cheap: the clause locks not onto some outward act but onto an attitude of contempt toward one's parents: the attitude lies in secret, and the curse reaches it all the same (the root's number is registered as pending verification; Note 8). Second, this clause and the positive form of the fifth commandment (5:16 in this book, treated in its own sermon within the book; registered by section reference only) form a positive-and-negative pair: there, "Honour … that thy days may be long"; here, "setteth light by … Cursed be he" — the same commandment, its blessing side and its curse side, close together at this point. [Fact Layer] The third curse, the landmark (v. 17), in full: "Cursed be he that removeth his neighbour's landmark. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, the core of the charge is massîg gəḇûl — removing the landmark (gəḇûl, H1366; the root number of massîg is registered as pending verification; Note 8); moving a boundary is theft by inches: move it a foot, and no one knows; move it ten times, and there is no witness. Second, "neighbour" (rēaʿ, H7453) occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 17, 24; Note 4) — its first occurrence is here: the victim is one of your own, living beside you. Third, Deut 19:14 in this book has this same charge in command form; it has been verified word for word directly and is set side by side here: "Thou shalt not remove thy neighbour's landmark, which they of old time have set in thine inheritance, which thou shalt inherit in the land that the LORD thy God giveth thee to possess it." — Chapter 19 says "Thou shalt not"; chapter 27 says "Cursed be he": the one is statute, the other covenant oath; the same matter is, in the detailed regulations, a command, and in the covenant-making rite, an oath staked with a curse (the relation of the two texts is set side by side only). [Fact Layer] The fourth curse, the blind (v. 18), in full: "Cursed be he that maketh the blind to wander out of the way. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, "maketh … to wander out of the way" — in the original, mašgeh is the causative participle of šāgâ (H7686): not going astray oneself, but leading another astray. Second, the victim is "the blind" (ʿiwwēr, H5787) — a person who can walk only by relying on another's guidance: the charge locks precisely onto "exploiting dependence": he trusts your guidance, and you lead him off the right way. Third, Lev 19:14 has a clause on a neighbouring theme; it has been verified word for word directly and is set side by side here: "Thou shalt not curse the deaf, nor put a stumblingblock before the blind, but shalt fear thy God: I am the LORD." — The form in Leviticus is a prohibition (do not put a stumblingblock), the form in this chapter a curse (cursed be he that maketh to wander), and this chapter extends the act from "placing an obstacle" to "leading in the wrong direction": the two texts are set side by side only, with no ruling on their priority. [Fact Layer] The fifth curse, perverting judgment (v. 19), in full: "Cursed be he that perverteth the judgment of the stranger, fatherless, and widow. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, "perverteth the judgment" — in the original, maṭṭeh mišpāṭ: bending the judgment (mišpāṭ, H4941) aside (the causative form of nāṭâ, H5186). Second, the victims are a fixed trio: the stranger (gēr, H1616), the fatherless (yātôm, H3490), and the widow (ʾalmānâ, H490) — all persons with no protector; in the judgment hall no one speaks for them, and the bent scale tips toward them. Third, in the base text "fatherless, and widow" — the Chinese base writes "orphan-widow" continuously, without a conjunction (the 2010 version reads "orphan and widow"; entered separately, Note 3); the KJV likewise lists the three without a conjunction before "widow" beyond the commas as quoted. Fourth, Deut 24:17–22 in this book is the special section on this theme (treated in its own sermon within the book; registered by section reference only): there the form is command plus the memory of the Exodus; here the form is curse plus the whole people's Amen. [Fact Layer] The sixth curse, the father's wife (v. 20), in full: "Cursed be he that lieth with his father's wife; because he uncovereth his father's skirt. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, this clause is the only one of the twelve that carries a reason clause ("because …") — the reason is "he uncovereth his father's skirt" (kānāp, H3671): the skirt figures the father's bed and the father's dignity. Second, "lieth with" (šākaḇ, H7901) occurs here first, four times in the chapter (vv. 20–23; Note 4) — this clause opens the ethics group of four consecutive clauses, whose second half stands in Section 4. Third, in the base text the break between "Cursed be he!" and "because" is a full stop beginning a new sentence (modern-punctuation editions read it continuously with a comma; entered separately, Note 3) — with different punctuation, the reason clause is read with different weight; registered only. [Theological Development Layer] The map of the first six clauses — let the hearer draw it himself: God (the idol, v. 15), home (parents, v. 16), inheritance (the landmark, v. 17), road (the blind, v. 18), suit (the stranger, fatherless, and widow, v. 19), bed (the father's wife, v. 20). The six fields have one thing in common: none of them lies within a court's range — the idol in secret, the landmark with no witness, the contempt that cannot be pinned down, the bent scale: all cases that human judgment cannot reach. The logic of the twelve curses appears here: what human rule cannot reach, the covenant governs; what no one sees, God sees. And a further layer must be spoken through: to every clause the people must say "Amen" with their own mouths — Amen is not a vote of approval; it is a signature: this clause I acknowledge; if it falls on the one who violates it, I have no objection — even if it falls on myself, I have no objection. [Summary Layer] This section may be taken in three points: first, the form of the curses (v. 15 as the format specimen); second, the fields of the first six (worship, family, inheritance, road, justice, ethics); third, the common secret place (where the human eye cannot reach is precisely the covenant's jurisdiction). [New Testament Reading Layer] This section registers one echo only, verified word for word directly, with no reading backwards: 1 Cor 16:22, in full: "If any man love not the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be Anathema Maranatha." — A curse-form wording rare in the New Testament church literature (in the original, anathema); its object is not eleven specific classes of sin, but the one matter of "love not the Lord." Between the Old Testament's twelve clauses and this one New Testament sentence there is a formal contrast of "enumeration" and "gathering into one"; registered so, and the relation is not ruled upon. [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] One received scholarly account is registered in this section per discipline, and is not made an argument: commentators commonly observe that the sins selected in the curse list of 27:15–26 are mostly hidden acts, or acts where human testimony is hard to secure, or acts beyond a court's reach, comparable to the selection tendency of the curse sections in ancient Near Eastern covenant documents such as those of Egypt and the Hittites (registered generally at the discussion layer, with no unverified text cited) — this account explains why offences with definite penalties, such as adultery and theft in their ordinary forms, are not in this list: those sins are governed by the court; this list is governed by the covenant. This reading is registered only; this sermon's development stands on the distribution within the text itself (the two occurrences of "in secret," and the victims being mostly the unprotected), and does not depend on this account standing.


## 4. The Last Six Amens and the One Comprehensive Clause (Deut 27:21–26; 43:00–54:00)

[Fact Layer] The seventh to ninth clauses are the continuation of the ethics group, three clauses in a row, in full: "Cursed be he that lieth with any manner of beast. And all the people shall say, Amen." "Cursed be he that lieth with his sister, the daughter of his father, or the daughter of his mother. And all the people shall say, Amen." "Cursed be he that lieth with his mother in law. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, the seventh clause (v. 21): with a beast — the bottom-line clause of ethics. Second, the eighth clause (v. 22): the object is "his sister, the daughter of his father, or the daughter of his mother" (ʾāḥôt, H269) — in the base text the order puts "of a different mother, the same father" first (the original's order is "daughter of his father, daughter of his mother"; the 2010 version's word order is entered separately, Note 3); the KJV follows the original's order as quoted. Third, the ninth clause (v. 23): with his mother in law (ḥōtenet; its number is registered as pending verification; Note 8). Fourth, the four ethics curses (vv. 20, 21, 22, 23) form one group, all using the one word šākaḇ — within the boundary stand the father's bed, a sister, a mother in law, and a beast: the limits of the home, drawn clause by clause. There is no small matter in the ethics of the home, because the affairs of the home are likewise mostly in secret. [Fact Layer] The tenth clause, secret killing (v. 24), in full: "Cursed be he that smiteth his neighbour secretly. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, "secretly" (sāter) is its second occurrence — standing at a distance opposite the first clause (v. 15, setting up an idol in a secret place): the whole list opens with "in secret" and turns toward its close with "secretly"; the head-and-tail frame of sins in secret places is registered as it stands. Second, the charge in the original is makkê rēʿēhû bassāter — "he that smiteth his neighbour in secret" (the causative participle of nākāh, H5221): "neighbour" (rēaʿ) at its second occurrence answers the landmark of v. 17 at a distance — one neighbour, two secret harms: remove his landmark in silence, take his life in a secret place. Third, secret killing is the case a court finds hardest to handle: a killing with no eyewitness could, in ancient judicature, scarcely even be opened as a case — precisely for this reason it is written into the covenant's curses. [Fact Layer] The eleventh clause, bribery (v. 25), in full: "Cursed be he that taketh reward to slay an innocent person. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, the "reward" (šōḥaḏ, H7810) is the thing taken, and the verb is "taketh" (lāqaḥ, H3947) — a hand stretches out, and a life is gone. Second, the "innocent person" is in the original "innocent blood" (the imagery of dām nāqî) — the victim is named by his "blood": blood is evidence that speaks (the imagery of the Genesis stream within this book; registered by section reference only). Third, this clause and the fifth (v. 19, perverting judgment) form the head and tail of the justice group: there the scale is bent aside; here the scale is bought — the corruption of judgment, one from partiality and one from greed, and both enter the covenant's curses. [Fact Layer] The twelfth clause, the comprehensive curse (v. 26), in full: "Cursed be he that confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them. And all the people shall say, Amen." Items registered. First, the quotation marks finally close in full at the end of this verse in the base text — the discourse opened at v. 1, the proclamation continued at v. 12, and the curse texts clause by clause from v. 15, all gather at the chapter's last word. Second, the form of the charge enlarges abruptly: the first eleven each point to one matter; this clause points to the law as a whole — "he that confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them": in the original, yāqîm is the causative form of qûm (H6965), meaning "to raise up, to make stand firm, to confirm" — the object of the curse is not the person who commits some one clause, but the person who does not raise up the words of the law and do them. Third, the base text at this verse has no word for "all" — and the Masoretic original likewise has no quantifier "all, every" (entered separately, Notes 3 and 5): the words of the law are a whole; no "all" needs to be added for them to be indivisible. In the KJV, by contrast, this verse does read "all the words of this law" — the four forms are set side by side in Notes 3 and 5, unreconciled. Fourth, the paragraph marker at the end of this verse is the chapter's only petuhah — the preceding fourteen are all samekh; this one alone closes openly: the door of the twelve curses opens outward at the last word, closing this chapter of the whole book. [Fact Layer] This chapter's most direct meeting with the New Testament stands on this very clause. Gal 3:10 has been verified word for word directly, in full: "For as many as are of the works of the law are under the curse: for it is written, Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law to do them." Items registered. First, what Paul quotes is precisely 27:26 — one discrepancy between the quotation's form and the Masoretic text is registered as it stands: in Paul's Greek (seen directly on the page's NA28 line) there is pāsin, "all things," before the book of the law; the Masoretic text of 27:26 has no such quantifier; the KJV at both places (27:26 and Gal 3:10) renders "all"; and CUNPSS has no "all" at 27:26 but does have "all things" at Gal 3:10 — the four forms are set side by side in Notes 3 and 5, with no ruling. Second, Paul's use: he reads one "curse of response within a rite" as the situation of all who are of the works of the law — the echo of the twelve Amens is amplified in the New Testament to embrace all mankind. [Theological Development Layer] See clearly the relation of v. 26 to the eleven before it: each of the first eleven has its own person and its own sin, and the hearer can well think, "That is not I"; the twenty-sixth verse closes the door: he that confirmeth not all the words and does them not — everyone has a share, and in the sound of the Amens no one can stand outside the list. The logic of the twelve Amens is complete at this point: in the first eleven Amens, the people sign for their neighbours' sins; in the twelfth Amen, the people sign for themselves. [New Testament Reading Layer] At the reading layer this section has one quotation whose base-text position is fully secured — Gal 3:13, verified word for word directly, in full: "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree:" Items registered. First, Paul's reading of this chapter gathers here: the curse of 27:26 falls on "us," and the way out lies not in removing the curse but in One who was "made a curse" and bore it in man's place; in the Chinese base text a parenthesis at this verse itself notes that "made" is the original wording where its main line reads otherwise — a CUNPSS entry, registered in the source's Note 4 and carried over here as such. Second, the sentence quoted at 3:13, "Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree," is Deut 21:23 (ch. 21 of this book has its own sermon, within which the direct verification of 21:23 is complete; this sermon registers the relay-quotation relation only and does not repeat the correction). Third, between "was made" and "bore," the point is the form of bearing the curse: it is becoming the curse itself. This layer is Paul's reading, registered as it stands as a New Testament reading, and is not read backwards as the original meaning of ch. 27. [Summary Layer] This section may be taken in three points: first, the boundaries of the home (vv. 21–23); second, the head-and-tail of sins in secret places (the "in secret" frame of v. 24 with v. 15, and the justice frame of v. 25 with v. 19); third, the comprehensive curse and the redemption out of it (v. 26 and the two quotations in Galatians). [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two items are set side by side in this section. First, the textual-form question of 27:26 (the Masoretic text without "all"; the Septuagint and Paul's quotation with pāsin) — the four forms across versions and quotation are set side by side in Notes 3 and 5; which is prior belongs to the professional judgment of textual criticism — set side by side only, with no ruling. Second, the correspondence between the execution account of Josh 8 and this chapter's commands — Josh 8:34 has been verified word for word directly, in full here: "And afterward he read all the words of the law, the blessings and cursings, according to all that is written in the book of the law." — Command (Deut 27) and execution (Josh 8) answer each other in the structure "build the altar — write — proclaim" (8:30–33 and 35 are registered by section reference only); the priority and editorial relation of the two books' accounts is a topic of long scholarly discussion — set side by side only, with no ruling.

## Conclusion (54:00–60:00)

[Theological Development Layer] We close by returning to this chapter's four things. The words on the stones: the law is written out, written very plainly, for all to read — the foundation of faith is not a secret but a public text. The sacrifices on the altar: before the land is divided, worship; before settling down, offer up — the order itself is faith. The people on the two mountains: blessing and curse are not concepts; they are positions a person must stand into — where you stand is the proclamation you belong to. The twelve Amens: one after another, all signatures — the people have no auditor's seat anywhere; every clause must be claimed with their own mouths. And after the twelfth falls, the chapter closes openly with a petuhah: the door is not shut dead upon the curse. The sentence of v. 9 still waits ahead of us — "this day thou art become the people of the LORD thy God": becoming the people comes first, hearing the curses through comes after; identity is given by grace, and responsibility is signed by oneself. Next time, when we open ch. 28, the words of blessing will be written out sentence by sentence; but before that, this chapter first teaches us one thing: serious faith begins with being willing to say "Amen" with one's own mouth — not vague toward God's words, not absent from one's own responsibility, letting every answer count like something written on a stone: plainly, and for good.

## Devotional Extract

Moses commanded the people to write the law on the plaistered stones: "And thou shalt write upon the stones all the words of this law very plainly." (27:8) God's words are not hidden away; they are written plainly, made public, for anyone to come and read. But after reading them plainly there is one more step: the people stand between Mount Gerizim and Mount Ebal, hear the Levites proclaim clause by clause, and answer, sentence by sentence. When the proclamation reaches the twelfth clause — "Cursed be he that confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them. And all the people shall say, Amen." (27:26) — the Amen must still be said. Toward others it is easy; toward oneself it is hard; of the twelve Amens the hardest is the last, because it includes everyone. Paul says this is precisely our situation: by continuing in the law, no one can stand. Yet this chapter has another sentence standing ahead of it: "this day thou art become the people of the LORD thy God" (27:9). Becoming the people is not earned by examination; it is declared; and Christ has already taken that curse over to himself — "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us" (Gal 3:13). So our Amen today is no longer a signature made in fear; it is an answer made in gratitude: the words have been written plainly, the sin has been redeemed clean, and what remains is to live every day, earnestly, as one Amen that counts.

## Devotional Extract (KJV)

Moses commanded: "Cursed be he that confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them. And all the people shall say, Amen." (Deut 27:26, KJV). Twelve times the people answered Amen on Mount Ebal's side of the valley—once for the secret idol, once for the moved landmark, once for the bribe, and finally once for anyone who does not uphold all the words of the law. The last Amen left no one outside the list; every voice signed its own name. This is where the apostle Paul takes up the chapter: the curse of the law is real, and none of us can continue in all things written in the book of the law. But Paul does not end there: "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree" (Gal 3:13, KJV). The words were written plainly on plastered stones so that no one could plead ignorance; the curse was answered not by louder Amens but by One who bore it. Our Amen today is therefore not the signature of the afraid, but the answer of the redeemed: the law is plain, the curse is carried, and our part is to say Amen—and to mean it.

# Rise Up, LORD — A Sermon on Numbers 10

- Text: Numbers 10:1-36
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending cross-review — marked as a first translation awaiting proofreading (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Numbers 10; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible and cross-checked against the same edition as carried on Bible Gateway, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations; verses not checked word for word are given in summary only, never as quotations.
- Note on the title: The tentative directions for this chapter's Chinese title ranged over its own words — the silver trumpets, the setting forward, Hobab, the prayer at the ark. Checked against the New Punctuation text verse by verse, "silver trumpets" was rejected as a title drawn from the text itself: at verse 2 the Chinese reads "make two trumpets of silver," and the words for "silver" and "trumpet" do not stand together unbroken there; the familiar two-character heading is an editors' section heading, not a phrase of the text. Two genuine unbroken phrases were available — "blow an alarm" (vv. 5, 6, 9) and "be to us instead of eyes" (v. 31) — but the chapter's peak is Moses' saying when the ark set forward at verse 35, whose first four Chinese characters, "Rise up, we pray," stand unbroken in the base text and pair with verse 36's "Return, we pray," gathering the whole chapter into its two prayers, one for the setting forward and one for the resting. The Chinese title was therefore fixed on those four characters, and the English title renders them from the KJV of verse 35: "Rise up, LORD." See Endnote 2.
- A base-text correction carried into both manuscripts: briefing material for this chapter described verse 36 as speaking of Israel's "myriads of myriads." Checked against the base texts, that wording does not stand in either: the Chinese base text reads "the thousands of Israel" in its own phrasing (literally, "the thousand-times-ten-thousand men of Israel"), and the KJV reads "the many thousands of Israel." Both manuscripts record the verse as their base texts give it, and set out the Hebrew phrase in section IV and Endnote 5. The briefing figure was not adopted.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Through nine chapters Israel has camped at Sinai — numbered, arrayed, charged, and feasted — and has not yet marched a single step. In chapter 10 the book moves for the first time. First come the trumpets: the LORD commands two trumpets of silver, of a whole piece, to be blown by the priests, with a finely divided code of signals — both trumpets blown gather the whole congregation at the door of the tabernacle; one trumpet gathers the princes only; an alarm sets the camps forward, the first alarm for the camps on the east side, the second for those on the south; to gather the congregation they blow, but sound no alarm; in war the alarm brings remembrance before God and salvation from enemies; and on days of gladness, at solemn feasts and at the beginnings of months, the trumpets are blown over the offerings as a memorial before God. Then comes the march: on the twentieth day of the second month in the second year the cloud is taken up from off the tabernacle of the testimony, Israel leaves the wilderness of Sinai, and the cloud rests in the wilderness of Paran; the host moves in the order of chapter 2 — Judah first, the sons of Gershon and Merari bearing the dismantled tabernacle behind the first camp, Reuben next, the Kohathites bearing the sanctuary in the midst, Ephraim after them, and Dan as the rereward of all the camps. At the point of departure Moses pleads with Hobab, the son of Reuel the Midianite: refused once, he pleads again — "thou mayest be to us instead of eyes" — and promises that what goodness the LORD does to Israel, the same will Israel do to him. At the close, the ark goes before them three days' journey to search out a resting place, the cloud of the LORD upon them by day; when the ark sets forward Moses says, "Rise up, LORD," and when it rests, "Return, O LORD, unto the many thousands of Israel." This sermon reads the chapter in its own order: first, how the trumpet code turns a multitude into a host that can hear one command; then, how the host sets forward in its order; then, the plea to a wilderness guide, refused and renewed; and last, the two prayers that stand at either end of the ark's journey. The thread it traces is this: Israel's marching hangs at one end on a command that can be clearly heard, and at the other on the One who walks ahead — the trumpet keeps the host in step, the ark gives the journey its direction and its rest; and man's part is to move at the trumpet, follow the ark, and learn to open and close every stage between setting out and halting with prayer.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 Two trumpets of silver: a command clearly heard, before a host evenly marched

(Fact) The chapter opens not with a march but with the making of trumpets. Verse 2 stands in the KJV: "Make thee two trumpets of silver; of a whole piece shalt thou make them: that thou mayest use them for the calling of the assembly, and for the journeying of the camps." (Num 10:2, KJV) The verse settles material, workmanship, number, and use in one breath: the material is silver; the workmanship is of a whole piece — hammered from one piece, not cast in parts; the number is two; the uses are two — for the calling of the assembly, and for the journeying of the camps. The instrument's Hebrew name is ḥăṣōṣrâ (Strong's H2689), checked for this sermon against the lexicon: it is the priests' straight metal trumpet, and not the same instrument as the šôpār, the ram's horn used on other occasions; the trumpets of this whole chapter are these two hammered trumpets of silver. Two trumpets look a simple thing, yet they are the signal-box of every corporate act Israel will ever perform: without the trumpet sound, a camp of hundreds of thousands is a sea of people; with it, the sea becomes a host.

(Fact) The division of the signals is laid down item by item in verses 3-7; in preparing this sermon the items were counted verse by verse, and they are five. The first item, gathering the whole congregation (v. 3): "And when they shall blow with them, all the assembly shall assemble themselves to thee at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation." (Num 10:3, KJV) Both trumpets blown, the whole assembly is summoned — to Moses, and to the door of the tabernacle. The second item, gathering the princes only (v. 4, Summary): if they blow with one trumpet only, the princes, the heads of the thousands of Israel, gather to Moses — the number of trumpets blown fixes the breadth of the summons: one for the leaders, two for all. The third item, the first marching alarm (v. 5, Summary): when an alarm is blown, the camps that lie on the east parts go forward. The fourth item, the second marching alarm (v. 6, Summary): when an alarm is blown the second time, the camps on the south side take their journey, the alarm being the appointed signal for their journeys. The fifth item, the difference of blowing for gathering and for marching (v. 7): "But when the congregation is to be gathered together, ye shall blow, but ye shall not sound an alarm." (Num 10:7, KJV) The same trumpets are blown two ways — a plain blowing to gather, an alarm to move — and if the two blowings were confused, the command itself would be thrown into confusion. Two Hebrew words carry this distinction, and their pairings with their numbers were checked individually for this sermon: the plain blowing is tāqaʿ (Strong's H8628), the ordinary verb for blowing the trumpet; the "alarm" is the noun tərûʿâ (Strong's H8643), a shout, a clamour, an alarm-cry — rendered in the Chinese base text as "blowing out a great sound." It is not merely a louder note; it is a different signal: short, urgent, rousing. One further matter must be stated exactly as the text leaves it, and not completed on the text's behalf: verses 5-6 expressly assign marching alarms to the east and south camps only — the first alarm for the east, the second for the south. At which alarm the west and north camps set forward, this passage does not say. This sermon sets out the two alarms the text names, and leaves the other two camps' signals unassigned rather than filling them in from the marching order that follows.

(Fact) Who blows is settled at verse 8, in the KJV: "And the sons of Aaron, the priests, shall blow with the trumpets; and they shall be to you for an ordinance for ever throughout your generations." (Num 10:8, KJV) Three layers, one by one. First, blowing is the priests' charge — the sons of Aaron blow; not the princes, not Moses, not any volunteer. The trumpet is the instrument of command, and the hand of command rests with the priesthood; that in itself is order. Second, the ordinance's term is "for ever throughout your generations" — no wilderness improvisation, but a standing rule to be carried into the land and down the ages. Third — and here the English manuscript records its own independent check of the Chinese original's assertions — this chapter nowhere names Eleazar or Ithamar. In preparing this translation every name in verses 1-36 was checked against the KJV text: the chapter names Moses, Hobab, Raguel, and the twelve captains of the camps; of Aaron's sons it speaks only collectively, as "the sons of Aaron, the priests" (v. 8). The division of oversight between Eleazar and Ithamar belongs to chapter 4, and briefing material that assigned the two men duties in this chapter is not borne out by this chapter's text; this sermon does not read chapter 4's arrangement back into chapter 10.

(Fact) The trumpets have two last uses, at verses 9 and 10. Verse 9 stands in the KJV: "And if ye go to war in your land against the enemy that oppresseth you, then ye shall blow an alarm with the trumpets; and ye shall be remembered before the LORD your God, and ye shall be saved from your enemies." (Num 10:9, KJV) And verse 10: "Also in the day of your gladness, and in your solemn days, and in the beginnings of your months, ye shall blow with the trumpets over your burnt offerings, and over the sacrifices of your peace offerings; that they may be to you for a memorial before your God: I am the LORD your God." (Num 10:10, KJV) Gather the passage's uses together and count them: the calling of the assembly and the journeying of the camps (v. 2); war (v. 9); feasts and new moons with their offerings (v. 10) — the trumpet runs through three faces of Israel's life: the march, the battle, and the worship. Verses 9 and 10 further share one keyword: remembrance. In war, blown with the alarm, Israel is "remembered" before God, and saved; at the offerings, the trumpets are "a memorial" before God. In the Hebrew the two words are of one root, their pairings checked individually: "remembered" at verse 9 is the passive of zākar (Strong's H2142), and "memorial" at verse 10 is the noun zikkārôn (Strong's H2146). Here the trumpet is not only a signal heard by men; it is a sound presented before God: men blow, and God remembers; God remembers, and men are saved. One end of the silver trumpet gives orders to the camp; the other end presents the camp before heaven.

(Application) This passage's first lesson for today's hearers: the first preparation for moving together is not strength of leg but clearness of hearing. Israel had lain at Sinai the better part of a year; the law was given, the tabernacle built, the host arrayed — and still, before a step is taken, God commands the making of trumpets: the question how a whole people hears one command is settled before the question where the people is to go. With hundreds of thousands, if every man listens to his own voice, the order to move produces a drift east and a drift west — not a host but a crowd. The finer the trumpet code — how many trumpets summon whom, which blowing does what, who may blow — the steadier the host's step. We are apt to think the life of faith suffers from lack of direction; very often it suffers from too many trumpet calls, and too mixed: this voice says east, that voice says west, each blowing its own tune, and a man stands frozen where he is. This chapter's silver trumpets are two in number, two in manner of blowing, and one in the office that blows them — simple, clear, impossible to mishear. God's people march evenly when they have first grown used to the true trumpet's sound: which call gathers, which call moves, which call sends to war — only the ear that can tell them apart can move at the right one.

(Application) Mark also who blows. That priests blow the trumpets means that command, here, is not a display of power but a worshipper's charge: the same men who serve at the altar on ordinary days stand before the camp blowing on the march, and blow beside the offerings at the feasts — the same men, the same breath. The trumpet is therefore no cold barrack-room order; it carries the smell of the sanctuary with it. The sound that gathers men to the tabernacle door is the sound that sends them to battle, and the sound that rises over burnt offerings and peace offerings as a memorial. Israel, listening to the trumpet day by day, learned to hear march, war, and worship as one thing: life before God. Turn the question on ourselves: what sound have our ears grown used to? Urgency, anxiety, comparison, dread? A man moves in the direction of the sound he is used to hearing. May we give our ears back to that hammered trumpet of silver — made by men's hands, but tuned by God; limited in its range, and every note of it counted.


### II. 08:00-24:00 The cloud taken up: the twentieth day of the second month — out of Sinai, camp by camp

(Fact) The trumpets made, the day comes. Verse 11 stands in the KJV: "And it came to pass on the twentieth day of the second month, in the second year, that the cloud was taken up from off the tabernacle of the testimony." (Num 10:11, KJV) Verse 12 continues: "And the children of Israel took their journeys out of the wilderness of Sinai; and the cloud rested in the wilderness of Paran." (Num 10:12, KJV) Two coordinates of time must first be set straight (Summary): Israel reached Sinai in the first year after the exodus (Exod 19:1, Summary) and stayed at the mountain the better part of that year — covenant, law, tabernacle, census, and array all belong to that stay — until, on the twentieth day of the second month of the second year, the cloud was taken up from the tabernacle for the first time, and the whole people broke camp for the first time. This is no ordinary removal: it is the covenant people's first departure, in full array, from the place of the covenant, toward the land of promise. Verse 13 names the character of this first march (Summary): they first took their journey according to the commandment of the LORD by the hand of Moses. The word "first" carries weight in the narrative: the journeys before this were the journeys of a flight; the journeys after it are the journeys of an ordered host. From this day, Israel walks in formation.

(Fact) The order of the march is recorded camp by camp in verses 14-28; in preparing this sermon the verses were checked one by one, and the captains' names are given here in Summary, not as quotations. The first camp, Judah in the lead (vv. 14-16, Summary): in the first place went the standard of the camp of Judah according to their armies, over his host Nahshon the son of Amminadab; over Issachar, Nethaneel the son of Zuar; over Zebulun, Eliab the son of Helon. After the first camp, the tabernacle was taken down (v. 17, Summary): "And the tabernacle was taken down; and the sons of Gershon and the sons of Merari set forward, bearing the tabernacle." (Num 10:17, KJV) — the Gershonites bearing the line of curtains and fabrics, the Merarites the line of boards and frame (their charges are chapter 4's, Summary); they march ahead of the second camp so that, arriving first, they may set the tabernacle up. The second camp, Reuben (vv. 18-20, Summary): the standard of Reuben set forward, over his host Elizur the son of Shedeur; over Simeon, Shelumiel the son of Zurishaddai; over Gad, Eliasaph the son of Deuel. After the second camp come the Kohathites (v. 21, Summary): "And the Kohathites set forward, bearing the sanctuary: and the other did set up the tabernacle against they came." (Num 10:21, KJV) — the ark, the table, the candlestick, and the altars (their covering and bearing are chapter 4's, Summary) enter the column at this point, and the text pauses to add that the tabernacle was set up before they arrived: frame and coverings first, the holy things after, the joints of the order meshing exactly. The third camp, Ephraim (vv. 22-24, Summary): the standard of Ephraim set forward, over his host Elishama the son of Ammihud; over Manasseh, Gamaliel the son of Pedahzur; over Benjamin, Abidan the son of Gideoni. The fourth camp, Dan in the rear (vv. 25-27, Summary): the standard of Dan set forward last, as the rereward of all the camps, guarding the whole host's rear; over his host Ahiezer the son of Ammishaddai; over Asher, Pagiel the son of Ocran; over Naphtali, Ahira the son of Enan. The passage closes at verse 28, in the KJV: "Thus were the journeyings of the children of Israel according to their armies, when they set forward." (Num 10:28, KJV) A plain sentence, and the general acceptance of the whole order: what should be first is first, what should be last is last, the holy things in the midst, the tabernacle gone before, and troops on every side.

(Fact) This order answers, item by item, to the encampment of chapter 2 (Summary): there the stations were fixed — Judah to the east, Reuben to the south, Ephraim to the west, Dan to the north, with the tabernacle and the Levites' camp in the midst; here the marching order is fixed — the east camp first, the south next, the west after, the north as rereward. At rest the array is a cross laid flat; on the march it is the same array drawn out into a column; and the sanctuary's place among the people does not change between stillness and motion: dismantled, borne, and carried, it loses nothing of its standing in the host. The Gershonites' and Merarites' loads travel between the first and second camps; the Kohathites' holy things between the second and third; front, rear, left, and right are Israel's armies. "God in the midst of His people" is, in the wilderness, not a metaphor; it is a formation.

(Application) Notice the trigger of the march: not Moses' decision, not the princes' council — the cloud taken up. Verse 11 records only that the cloud moved; verse 12, that Israel moved. The sequence is one before the other, and it admits no reversal. In that year at Sinai Israel's first marching lesson was waiting: while the cloud does not rise, the camp does not break, however settled and familiar the mountain foot has become; the moment the cloud rises, the whole camp moves, however long the wilderness ahead. This is a reminder to the man in a hurry to be gone, and the same reminder to the man who will not leave: the day of departure is not man's to fix. Moses is the leader, and even he cannot appoint the date of breaking camp; what he can do is array the host, make the trumpets, secure the guide — and then wait for the cloud. Preparation is man's part; timing is God's. The man whose preparation is complete does not panic in the waiting; the man whose preparation is lacking cannot march in order even when the cloud moves. This book divides the two things with perfect clarity: chapters 1-9 are all preparation, and chapter 10 moves in a single verse. Many lives reverse the proportion: preparation scamped, departure hurried — and halfway out, the discovery that no trumpet was made, no host arrayed, and no one appointed to bear the holy things.

(Application) In the marching order there is also comfort for those who walk in the middle and at the rear. The Kohathites, bearing the sanctuary, walk in the very midst of the host — neither conspicuous at the front nor guarding at the back — silently, between two camps; and Dan's camp walks last, as the rereward of all: the rear guard is not a broken remnant but the camp that shields the families and the baggage and watches for pursuers; the man who walks last carries the whole host's way back. The text gives every camp its name and its captain; no camp is "and the rest." In God's host, positions have an order, but the recording has no omissions: Nahshon at the head has a name, and Ahira at the rear has a name; the bearer of the holy things has a charge, and the bearer of the curtains has a charge. The man who stares at his not being in the first camp will miss the text's arithmetic: the first camp marches first because east faces toward the land — the first to march is the first to face the wind; the rereward marches last because the host needs someone to guard the road behind. Before and after are the formation's need, not a ranking of worth. Appointed to a camp, a man lifts its standard high and keeps its ranks together in that camp — and that is this chapter's lesson to a church on the march.

### III. 24:00-42:00 Hobab: leave us not — thou mayest be to us instead of eyes

(Fact) At the very point of departure the text sets down a family matter. At verse 29 Moses addresses a kinsman, in the KJV: "And Moses said unto Hobab, the son of Raguel the Midianite, Moses' father in law, We are journeying unto the place of which the Lord said, I will give it you: come thou with us, and we will do thee good: for the Lord hath spoken good concerning Israel." (Num 10:29, KJV) The verse carries a great deal, layer by layer. First, the man invited: Hobab, the son of Reuel — Raguel in the KJV's spelling — the Midianite. The Chinese base text calls him Moses' father-in-law and adds its own parenthesis, "or, brother-in-law": the base text itself sets the two readings side by side, and the Chinese manuscript reproduces the parenthesis as it stands. Second, Moses' invitation falls into two halves: the first half is a declaration of fact — we are journeying to the place of which the LORD said, "I will give it you"; the second half is the sharing of a promise — come with us, and we will do thee good, for the LORD hath spoken good concerning Israel. Moses invites a companion not with sentiment but with the promise: the land has an Owner, and that Owner has spoken good; to come is not merely to walk with us, but to enter the circle of those among whom that good is shared.

(Fact) Hobab's answer is blunt, in the KJV: "And he said unto him, I will not go; but I will depart to mine own land, and to my kindred." (Num 10:30, KJV) Two clauses, no courtesies: I will not go; I will return to my own land and my kindred. His reasons stand written inside those words: he has a land of his own and a kindred of his own; and however large Israel's promise, it is the promise of Abraham's seed — why should a Midianite pull up his root from his own soil and follow a host fresh out of Egypt into a wilderness? By any human reckoning the refusal is wholly reasonable: no water ahead and no bread, the memory of pursuers behind, and the promised land lying beyond a whole wilderness and cities of giants. Moses does not let the matter drop; verses 31-32 stand in the KJV: "And he said, Leave us not, I pray thee; forasmuch as thou knowest how we are to encamp in the wilderness, and thou mayest be to us instead of eyes." (Num 10:31, KJV) "And it shall be, if thou go with us, yea, it shall be, that what goodness the Lord shall do unto us, the same will we do unto thee." (Num 10:32, KJV) The second invitation differs from the first in small and decisive ways. First, the promise is made exact: the first time, "we will do thee good," a general kindness; the second time, "what goodness the Lord shall do unto us, the same will we do unto thee" — goodness answering to goodness, the same portion the LORD gives us becoming the portion shared with him. Second, Moses states outright why Hobab in particular is asked: "forasmuch as thou knowest how we are to encamp in the wilderness, and thou mayest be to us instead of eyes." "Eyes" is the plural form of ʿayin (Strong's H5869) — the word's pairing with its number was checked for this sermon. A host of hundreds of thousands lacks nothing in men, in the pillar of cloud, in the ark — except one pair of eyes that knows the wilderness: where water is, where to camp, where the old caravan roads run, where the dead places lie. That knowledge is not in the book of the law; it is in the feet of a man like Hobab, born and bred in Midian.

(Fact) This passage ends, in this chapter, at that point: whether Hobab went, the chapter does not record. Verse 33 turns directly to Israel's departure from the mount of the LORD, and Hobab's answer is not given. That must be stated exactly, and no later Scripture used to supply this chapter with an ending. Hobab's web of relations has threads outside the chapter (Summary): in Exodus, Moses' father-in-law is the priest of Midian, Jethro, and the names Reuel and Jethro stand in a relation the several passages do not state in one uniform way (Exod 2:18; 3:1; ch. 18, Summary); and Judges records that Heber the Kenite, of the children of Hobab, Moses' kinsman, afterwards dwelt within Israel's borders (Judg 4:11, Summary). These materials show that Hobab's house and Israel did have dealings afterwards; the immediate outcome of this scene the text leaves blank. A blank is also part of the text: the preacher acknowledges a blank as a blank, and does not invent Hobab's final nod or shake of the head.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) Hobab's exact relation to Moses has long been discussed by commentators. The Chinese base text at verse 29 calls him Moses' father-in-law and itself notes "or, brother-in-law"; the underlying Hebrew term for the male in-law covers a wider range of wife-side male relations than the single English word "father-in-law"; and with the layering of the names Reuel and Jethro in Exodus, and the Judges passage that speaks of Hobab as Moses' kinsman, scholarship has framed the relation in more than one way: Hobab as brother-in-law, the son of Reuel — that is, of Jethro; Hobab as the father-in-law himself, with Reuel as his father or clan name; or the terms as varying with different strands of tradition. This sermon sets out the existence of these discussions and the base text's own side-by-side note, and decides for none of them; its argument does not rest on the genealogy. What the chapter's own text makes clear, verse by verse, is enough: he is Moses' Midianite kinsman; he is a man who knows the wilderness; and he is pleaded with twice to come. On those three points this sermon builds.

(Application) What pierces in Moses' plea to Hobab is Moses' candour. A leader fresh from receiving the law, with the pillar of cloud above him and the ark behind him, opens his mouth to say, "Leave us not, I pray thee" — not "we have no need of you," nor "we shall manage without you," but lays the host's deficiency openly on the table: we do not know the wilderness; you do; you can be our eyes. A leader's maturity is not omnicompetence; it is knowing what eyes his host lacks, and bowing his head to ask for them. The cloud gives direction; it does not choose a campsite between two wadis. The ark goes before; it does not find the water. God's guidance of Israel never abolished men's experience and knowledge; it enlisted them into the host. Hobab's wilderness knowledge, in Moses' eyes, is not secular wisdom competing with the cloud; it is a gift that can serve the whole camp as eyes. The church today wavers on this same point: at one extreme, trusting the cloud only and despising experience, it will not even ask the man who knows the road; at the other, trusting the guide only and forgetting the cloud, it hands the host over to whoever knows the road best. Moses holds both: with one hand he asks Hobab to be eyes; with the other he lifts the ark to the front. However skilled the eyes, the direction remains the ark's; however manifest the ark, the camping still asks the eyes.

(Application) Hobab's refusal also deserves a pause before any blame. "I will depart to mine own land, and to my kindred" — one's land and one's kindred are a man's root; and what Moses asked him to pull up was precisely that root: to leave the place of his birth and follow a host of another blood, another law, and another God into a land that existed, so far, only in a promise. The demand was not smaller than the one Abraham once heard — leave thy country, and thy kindred. Hobab's first answer is to fall back into his root; that is not coldness, it is honesty. He does not pretend to be moved, nor put Moses off with a "let me think it over"; he sets his difficulty plainly on the table. And precisely because the refusal is real, Moses' second invitation finds its purchase: the promise stated exactly, goodness for goodness; the office stated honourably — you are not a follower in our train; you are our eyes. In inviting a man to travel with us, Moses leaves us a pattern: do not dodge the other man's difficulty; do not promise good in the air; do not press with sentiment. State the promise clearly; state the need plainly; state the other man's place with honour. And as for how he finally answers — Moses leaves the result in the text's blank: plead, and plead with all one's strength; but the going or staying is not to be seized by force. That measure is one that everyone who invites another to come along will have use for.


### IV. 42:00-56:00 The ark before them: three days' journey to a resting place, and two prayers, for the moving and for the rest

(Fact) The last passage begins from the mount of the LORD. Verse 33 stands in the KJV: "And they departed from the mount of the Lord three days' journey: and the ark of the covenant of the Lord went before them in the three days' journey, to search out a resting place for them." (Num 10:33, KJV) Verse 34 continues: "And the cloud of the Lord was upon them by day, when they went out of the camp." (Num 10:34, KJV) Read together, the guidance ahead of them is twofold: the ark went before them, to search out a resting place for them; the cloud was upon them by day, spread over the whole host — one before, one above: the ark in charge of direction and of the halting place, the cloud in charge of covering and of presence. The phrase "to search out a resting place" must be set steady in the original: "resting place" is mənûḥâ, a place of rest and settling — the ark's going before is no ceremonial vanguard; it goes to find, beforehand, the place where a wearied people will halt. The host's timetable is therefore plain: they walk, led by the ark; they halt, where the ark has first found the place. And the measure the chapter records for this first stage is three days: out from the mountain of the covenant, for the first three days' journey, the ark was before them the whole way.

(Fact) One layering inside the chapter itself must be set out side by side here, exactly, and not smoothed past. In the marching order of section II, the Kohathites bear the sanctuary between the second and third camps (v. 21, Summary) — by column position, the ark travels with the sanctuary in the host's middle. But verse 33 says in so many words that "the ark of the covenant of the Lord went before them in the three days' journey." Both sentences are this chapter's own text: one writes the column's position, the other writes the guidance in front. This sermon simply sets the two out, each in its own words: the column narrative read as column narrative, the closing narrative as closing narrative; neither is altered into the other, and neither is struck out. How the two writings are to be understood belongs to the discussion layer below; the facts are owned first: this chapter records both the ark borne in the midst of the column, and the ark going before them to search out a resting place.

(Fact) The crown of the chapter is Moses' two sayings at verses 35 and 36. The saying when the ark set forward stands in the KJV: "And it came to pass, when the ark set forward, that Moses said, Rise up, Lord, and let thine enemies be scattered; and let them that hate thee flee before thee." (Num 10:35, KJV) The saying when it rested: "And when it rested, he said, Return, O Lord, unto the many thousands of Israel." (Num 10:36, KJV) Read the two against each other, item by item. First, both are addressed to the LORD, and both are pleas: at the setting forward, "Rise up"; at the resting, "Return." Second, their contents face opposite ways — one outward, one inward: the setting-forward saying faces out, enemies scattered and haters fleeing, the hostility of the road ahead asked away by God's own rising; the resting saying faces in, God asked to return and to be among His people. Third, the numbers of verse 36 must be recorded as the base texts give them. The KJV reads "the many thousands of Israel"; the Chinese base text reads, in its own words, "the thousand-times-ten-thousand men of Israel"; and the Hebrew phrase joins two number-words in construction — rəḇāḇâ (Strong's H7233, a myriad, ten thousand) and ʾelep̄ (Strong's H505, a thousand) — whose literal skeleton is "the myriads of the thousands of Israel." The versions phrase it variously — many thousands, myriads of thousands, countless thousands — and this sermon sets the three layers side by side: as the base text writes, so each manuscript quotes; the Hebrew's two number-words are a piling-up of quantity to declare multitude, and are not converted into a modern census figure, nor adjudicated into one "exact" number. The English reviewer notes here, from his own independent check of the KJV and the Hebrew phrase, that the Chinese manuscript is correct on this point against the briefing material: neither base text contains the "myriads of myriads" wording that the briefing had relayed, and the correction stands in both manuscripts (see Endnote 5). The Chinese title of this sermon, and its English rendering, are taken from the first four words of this verse 35 saying.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) The two writings about the ark's place, at verse 21 and verse 33, have long drawn commentators' attention, and more than one reading has been offered: some take verse 33 to describe this special three days' journey, on which the ark left the regular column and went ahead alone to spy out the rest; some take "before them" as the language of guidance rather than of the column's geometry — the ark, though borne in the midst, is still what leads the whole host's direction; some take the two passages as different narrative layers set side by side, deliberately kept together by the text. This sermon sets out the drift of these discussions and decides for none. A second textual phenomenon, much discussed, belongs to the same layer: verses 35 and 36 stand in the Hebrew manuscript tradition bracketed, before and after, by an inverted letter-mark (the so-called inverted nuns), marking these two verses off almost as if by parentheses; commentators have taken the mark variously — as signalling that the two verses form a small independent unit of their own, or as connected with ancient scribal marks of transposition. The readings stand side by side; this sermon records the phenomenon's existence and the drift of the discussion, and builds nothing on any one of them. The weight of these two verses in Israel's later worship has, by contrast, a checkable textual fact: the opening of Psalm 68, "Let God arise, let his enemies be scattered" (Ps 68:1, Summary), answers in wording to verse 35 — an echo within the Old Testament itself, which this sermon states as a correspondence of texts, and does not present as Numbers' own statement of its purpose.

(Application) A prayer for the setting forward, and a prayer for the resting: this is the marching liturgy Moses established for all Israel, and the most portable thing this chapter leaves us. Moses did not wait until enemies appeared to pray: the ark moves, and his mouth opens first — Rise up. No enemy is yet in sight, and prayer has already handed the road ahead over. Nor did he wait until the camp was pitched and all was settled to give thanks: the ark rests, and his mouth opens at once — Return unto us. The two besetting faults of marching men are stopped by these two prayers: at the start, we think only of the road and forget who leads it; at the halt, we think only of resting and forget to ask who abides with us. Moses' prayers are astonishingly short — one sentence for the moving, one for the resting, no long discourse — and every word is on the joint: walking, ask God to go before and scatter the enemies; halting, ask God to be in the midst and abide with His own. The length of a prayer was never its weight; its weight lies in where it is set. A host whose every rising and halting is edged with prayer will not drift apart on the road.

(Application) Look again at the heart inside "to search out a resting place for them." The ark goes before not only to open the road but to search out the rest — God, walking ahead, is not only driving enemies from the front of the camp; He is finding tonight's halting place for it. The One at the host's head carries in His heart the man at the host's tail who can walk no further: where this stage ends, where the water is, where the camp shall be pitched — He goes and sees to it first. Many imagine a God who only issues orders and never minds the lodgings: the trumpet blows, and you must walk, and if you cannot keep up, that is your affair. This chapter's God is not so: for three days' journey His ark is continually before them, "to search out a resting place" — command and compassion marching together, guidance and providing together. We may follow with confidence: the One who calls us to set forward is responsible also for preparing the place where we shall stop; He will not lead a host down a road that has departures and no rests. Between "Rise up" at the setting out and "Return" at the halting lies the whole rhythm of a believer's walking life.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: move at the trumpet, follow the ark, and let prayer edge the risings and the rests

(Fact) The chapter's thirty-six verses can now be gathered into three lines. First, the line of command (vv. 1-10): two trumpets of silver, of a whole piece, blown by the priests — a plain blowing to gather, an alarm for marching and for war, trumpets over the offerings at feasts and new moons as a memorial; the items counted verse by verse stand at verses 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, and 10, one item each, with verse 8 fixing the blowers and the everlasting ordinance, and verse 2 the twofold use in general. Second, the line of march (vv. 11-28): on the twentieth day of the second month in the second year the cloud is taken up; out of Sinai, and the cloud rests in the wilderness of Paran; the four camps set forward in chapter 2's array — Judah first, Reuben next, Ephraim after, Dan as rereward — with Gershon and Merari bearing the tabernacle ahead of the second camp, and the Kohathites bearing the sanctuary between the second camp and the third, the tabernacle arriving first and set up first. Third, the line of guidance (vv. 29-36): Moses twice pleads with Hobab to be their eyes; Hobab at first refuses, and the chapter leaves the outcome blank; the ark goes before them three days to search out a resting place, the cloud above them by day; and at the setting forward stands the prayer "Rise up," and at the resting the prayer "Return, O LORD, unto the many thousands of Israel." The three lines twist into one cord: the trumpet keeps the host obedient and in order; the formation keeps the sanctuary lodged in the host's midst; the eyes supply men's experience; and the ark fixes the direction and the rest.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, before departing, get the trumpets made: without a clear command there is no orderly host — preparation done to the full is what the one moment of departure stands on. Second, in the host, be willing to ask for eyes: even Moses opens his mouth to say "Leave us not," confessing that his host needs a pair of eyes that knows the wilderness — to own a lack and ask a man to fill it is not a leader's weakness but a host's blessing. Third, between rising and resting, edge everything with prayer: the ark moves, and we ask Him to rise up; the ark rests, and we ask Him to return — if a life's thousands of departures and halts each had such a sentence before it and behind it, a man would not lose God on the road. May we, a generation of travellers, hear the trumpet clearly, keep step with the ark, and keep these two short prayers always on our lips: Lord, as we set forward, rise up; as we rest, return and be among us.

(New Testament reading) One New Testament passage is traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter's trumpets; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. Paul, writing of speech in the church's assembly, asks: if the trumpet give an uncertain sound, who shall prepare himself to the battle? (1 Cor 14:8, Summary) — the silver trumpet's signals had to be distinct, or the congregation would not know whether it was gathering or marching; the New Testament moves the same picture onto words and teaching: an indistinct sound leaves everyone at a loss. This sermon states the correspondence as it stands in the New Testament text, and does not read Paul's sense back as the original meaning of Numbers. (Application) But with the layers kept straight, the correspondence has its weight: Numbers 10 is first of all itself — hammered trumpets of silver, a cloud on the twentieth day of the second month, the rolls of four camps, and two sayings at the ark's going and resting. With all that standing solid, we read the New Testament's "uncertain sound" knowing what certainty and uncertainty of sound are: this chapter's trumpet has, for every note, a name, an addressee, and a consequence. May our words and our witness learn from that silver trumpet — not many trumpets, not mixed tunes: gathering when it is time to gather, marching when it is time to march, and every note counted.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's own hands. You may be standing at your own Sinai: everything in readiness — a believer for years, the doctrines familiar, your place in the array assigned — and still not broken camp, because the cloud has not yet moved. This chapter says to you: while the cloud does not move, quietly make the trumpets, array the host, and secure the guide; preparation is not waste, and waiting, too, is obedience. Or you may already be on the road: marching in one of the camps, bearing the portion assigned you, the water ahead invisible and tonight's campsite unknown. This chapter says to you also: the ark is before you, even now searching out a resting place for you; the cloud is above you by day; you are not walking the wilderness alone. And there is a third hearer — the Hobab who is asked to be eyes: you know some stretch of road well, and your experience is exactly the eyes others lack. Moses' "Leave us not, I pray thee" is spoken to you today as well. In whichever place we stand, may we, before the next setting forward, first hear that silver trumpet clearly; and may every departure and every halt of ours begin with Moses' two sayings — and end in them.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 10 stays with me most in the two sayings of Moses when the ark set forward and when it rested. When it set forward, he said, "Rise up, LORD, and let thine enemies be scattered; and let them that hate thee flee before thee." And when it rested, he said, "Return, O LORD, unto the many thousands of Israel." One saying for the moving, one for the resting: as we set out, before anything else, we ask the Lord Himself to rise up and go before us; as we halt, we ask for nothing more than that He return and dwell among us. How many of my own departures have been hurried ones — bags packed, plans listed, and prayer forgotten; and how many of my halts have been collapsed ones — the work done, the man emptied, and no thought of asking God to abide. Moses teaches me to set prayer at both ends of the journey: when the trumpet sounds, pray first; when the feet stop, pray again. And there is that one word about the ark: it went before them "to search out a resting place for them" — the One who walks ahead of me not only opens the road; He also finds the place where I shall rest tonight. So I pray today: Lord, tomorrow, whether I am to set forward or to rest, hear these two sayings from me first: when I set forward, rise up and go before me; when I rest, return unto me and abide with me. The road is in Thy hand, and the resting place is in Thy hand — and I will follow with a quiet heart.

2. Title: the Chinese title is drawn, under this series' rule, from an unbroken phrase of the Chinese base text. Three candidate phrases were checked verse by verse in Numbers 10: "blow an alarm" (vv. 5, 6, 9), "be to us as eyes" within the sentence "thou mayest be to us instead of eyes" (v. 31), and "rise up, we pray" with its pair "return, we pray" (vv. 35, 36). "Silver trumpets" was rejected: at verse 2 the base text reads "make two trumpets of silver," in which the words for silver and trumpet do not stand unbroken together; titles using it are editors' section headings, not the text's own sequence. The title was fixed on the first four characters of the ark's setting-forward prayer at verse 35, paired with verse 36's prayer for the resting, because those two sayings gather the whole chapter — command, march, and guidance alike — into its close. The KJV of verse 35 begins the saying "Rise up, Lord"; the English title renders the Chinese title from that wording.
3. The counting of the signal items, checked verse by verse: the trumpet items of this chapter are set out by verse — verse 3 (both trumpets blown, the whole congregation gathers to the door of the tabernacle), verse 4 (one trumpet, the princes), verse 5 (the first alarm, the east camps set forward), verse 6 (the second alarm, the south camps; with the express note that their journeys are by alarm), verse 7 (for gathering, blow, but sound no alarm), verse 9 (in war, blow an alarm; remembered, and saved), verse 10 (days of gladness, solemn days, and beginnings of months, blow over the offerings, as a memorial) — seven verses carrying one item each. Verse 2 states the general twofold use (calling the assembly; journeying of the camps), and verse 8 the blowers (the sons of Aaron, the priests) and the ordinance's term (for ever, throughout the generations). Section I's "five signals" counts the marching and gathering signals of verses 3-7; verses 9 and 10 are set out separately under war and worship. The two statements of the count are marked in the text, and together neither double-count nor omit any item of the passage. Verses 5-6 expressly name the east and south camps' alarms only; the west and north camps have no express marching alarm in this passage, a gap the sermon states as it stands and does not fill.
4. The marching order cross-checked, verse by verse: verses 14-28, fifteen verses, are structured as — Judah's three tribes (vv. 14-16); the tabernacle taken down, with Gershon and Merari setting forward (v. 17); Reuben's three tribes (vv. 18-20); the Kohathites bearing the sanctuary (v. 21); Ephraim's three tribes (vv. 22-24); Dan's three tribes as rereward (vv. 25-27); and the general close (v. 28). Four camps of three tribes each make the twelve tribes, agreeing in number with the array of Numbers 2 (Summary); the captains, twelve in number, are each named in his own verse, and the sermon gives them in Summary. The Levites stand outside the four camps in this arrangement: Gershon, Merari, and Kohath enter the marching sequence by their bearing charges (vv. 17, 21), in continuity with the records of Numbers 2 and 4, the levels of which the sermon marks separately.
5. The wording of verse 36 in three layers, checked in preparing both manuscripts, and re-checked independently for this English manuscript against the KJV rather than taken from the Chinese manuscript's statement: (a) the KJV reads "Return, O Lord, unto the many thousands of Israel"; (b) the Chinese base text reads, in Chinese, "Return, O LORD, unto the men of Israel, thousand times ten thousand" — a "thousand-myriad" phrasing, in which the characters for myriad-upon-myriad in the doubled form relayed by the briefing material do not occur; (c) the Hebrew phrase joins rəḇāḇâ (H7233, a myriad) with the construct plural of ʾelep̄ (H505, a thousand), its literal skeleton being "the myriads of the thousands of Israel." The three layers stand side by side in section IV; this sermon states the multitude and converts it into no modern exact figure, nor adjudicates between the versions' phrasings. That later Chinese versions phrase the verse yet otherwise (one, for instance, makes it a vocative: "O LORD of Israel's thousands upon thousands, return") only confirms that quotations of this verse must return to the base text used and be checked there word by word.
6. Hobab's identity and the levels of the names: the Chinese base text at verse 29 calls Hobab Moses' father-in-law, with its own parenthetical alternative "or, brother-in-law" — both readings stand inside the base text itself, and the Chinese manuscript reproduces them. His full designation in the Chinese base text is "Hobab, the son of Reuel the Midianite"; the KJV reads "Hobab, the son of Raguel the Midianite, Moses' father in law." "Reuel" and "Raguel" are two spellings of the one name — the KJV itself uses Reuel in Exodus — a difference of the name's rendering level, which this English manuscript leaves exactly as the KJV of this verse gives it. The chapter records no outcome for Hobab: after verse 32 the text turns directly to the departure at verse 33, and whether Hobab consented to go is not stated in this chapter; the Chinese manuscript and this one both mark the blank as a blank. Judges 4:11 records that Heber the Kenite, of Hobab's children, afterwards dwelt within Israel's borders (Summary): evidence that his house later had dealings with Israel, not a filling-in of this chapter's blank.
7. The check for Eleazar and Ithamar, recorded by the English reviewer as an independent verification of the Chinese original: briefing material had suggested that this chapter assigns duties to Eleazar and Ithamar. In preparing this translation, every name and office in Numbers 10:1-36 was checked in the KJV text: the only priestly body named is "the sons of Aaron, the priests," collectively, at verse 8; the named persons are Moses, Hobab, Raguel (in Hobab's designation only), and the twelve captains of the camps. Eleazar and Ithamar do not occur in this chapter. Their division of oversight in Numbers 4 (Eleazar with the oversight of the whole, Ithamar over Gershon and Merari) belongs to that chapter's record, and neither manuscript reads it into this one; this note is filed so that no later citation repeats the briefing's error.
8. ḥăṣōṣrâ (Strong's H2689), "a (metal) trumpet, a clarion": the pairing was checked and is correct; in this chapter the word is the noun for the trumpets made and blown at verses 2, 8, 9, and 10, and this sermon argues from those checked verses only, making no claim about the word's distribution across the whole Bible. This trumpet and the šôpār, the ram's horn, are two instruments; the chapter's signals are throughout the silver trumpets, and the horn does not enter this chapter. tāqaʿ (H8628), "to blow (a trumpet)," and tərûʿâ (H8643), "a shout, an alarm-cry": both pairings were checked individually and are correct. The chapter's distinction between a plain blowing for gathering and an alarm for marching rests, in the Hebrew, precisely on this pair — the plain blowing of tāqaʿ (vv. 3, 4, 7, 8, 10) and the alarm of tərûʿâ (expressly at vv. 5, 6, 9); the verses were checked one by one for this sermon.
9. zākar (Strong's H2142), "to remember," and zikkārôn (Strong's H2146), "a memorial, a remembrance": a root pair, both pairings checked individually and correct. Verse 9's "ye shall be remembered" is the passive of zākar — remembered before God; verse 10's "a memorial" is zikkārôn; the two verses answer each other from the one root, and section I argues from them on that basis. ʿayin (H5869), "an eye": the pairing was checked and is correct; verse 31's "instead of eyes" is this word's plural construct form ("our eyes"). rəḇāḇâ (H7233), "a myriad, ten thousand," and ʾelep̄ (H505), "a thousand": both pairings checked and correct; the phrase of verse 36 joins the two words, and this sermon states its piled-up sense of multitude only, converting it into no exact number (see Endnote 5). The word mənûḥâ, "a resting place," at verse 33 is given in this sermon by its form and use in that verse, without a Strong's number: where a number has not been individually verified, no number is attached — that is this sermon's word-anchor rule.
10. The levels of the two discussions: the inverted letter-marks (inverted nuns) before and after verses 35-36 are a phenomenon actually present in the Hebrew scribal tradition, discussed in the commentaries of Milgrom and Ashley among others (see Bibliography); this sermon keeps it at the (Scholarly and historical discussion) level, recording its existence and the drift of the opinions, and adopts none of them as a conclusion. The two writings of the ark's place, at verse 21 and verse 33, are kept at the same level, the opinions set side by side and undecided. The correspondence of wording between Psalm 68:1 and Numbers 10:35 is a checkable echo between Old Testament texts; this sermon marks its level in the discussion paragraph of section IV and in this note, and does not present it as Numbers' own statement. The correspondence of 1 Corinthians 14:8 with this chapter's trumpets is placed at the (New Testament reading) level, and is not read back as this chapter's original sense.

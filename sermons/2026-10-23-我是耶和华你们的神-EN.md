# I Am the LORD Your God — A Sermon on Numbers 15

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Draft — awaiting cross-review (Sermon Production Batch 3, 23 October 2026, unpublished); first translation awaiting proofreading
Text: Numbers chapter 15 (41 verses in full)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV); the companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (和合本新标点本), verse by verse. The English text is not a back-translation of the Chinese: every quotation below is KJV wording, and where the two base texts differ in wording, the difference is registered, not harmonized.
Title verification: The title is taken from the closing self-declaration of the chapter, read as it stands in the base text: "I am the LORD your God, which brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God: I am the LORD your God" (15:41). The sentence occurs twice within the single verse, and it closes the whole chapter. Candidates examined verse by verse and set aside: "One law and one manner" (15:16) covers only the native-and-stranger section; "that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes" (15:39) is the purpose of the fringe alone; "the soul that doeth ought presumptuously" (15:30) names only one side of the ignorance/presumption boundary. Only 15:41 gathers offerings, the one law, atonement, judgement and the fringe under one root: whose this people is. Full record in Endnote 2.
Base-text notes (where the two base texts or common paraphrase differ, the base text governs): KJV 15:30 reads "doeth ought presumptuously" and "reproacheth the LORD"; the Chinese base text reads 擅敢行事 ("acts presumptuously/with a high hand") and 亵渎了耶和华 — neither "deliberately" (the 2010 Union Version's word) nor "despises lightly" appears in the Chinese base text. KJV 15:19 reads "the bread of the land," where the Chinese base text reads 吃那地的粮食 ("when you eat the grain of the land"); KJV 15:20-21 reads "the first of your dough," where the Chinese reads 初熟的麦子磨面 ("the first ripe grain, ground into flour"). KJV 15:15 calls the rule "One ordinance... an ordinance for ever," the Chinese 都归一例...永远的定例. Each manuscript follows its own base text throughout; details in Endnote 3.

## Summary

The sentence of the previous chapter has just fallen: this generation shall not see the land, and the camp must turn back into the wilderness (Numbers 14). Chapter 15 does not resume the itinerary. It opens with the liturgy of entry: "When ye be come into the land of your habitations, which I give unto you" (15:2) — spoken to the very generation sentenced to die outside, written for life inside. First the general rule of fire offerings: from herd or flock, a burnt offering, a sacrifice in performing a vow, a freewill offering, or the solemn feasts, all "to make a sweet savour unto the LORD" (15:1-3); the meal offering and drink offering rise in three grades with the size of the animal — lamb, ram, bullock — flour, oil and wine measured grade by grade (15:4-12). Then one law: as the native does, so shall the stranger do; "One law and one manner shall be for you, and for the stranger that sojourneth with you" (15:13-16). Then the firstfruits: when you eat the bread of the land, offer up a cake of the first of your dough as a heave offering, throughout your generations (15:17-21). Then the chapter turns to the boundary of sin: what is committed by ignorance, for congregation or for one soul, the priest makes atonement for, "and it shall be forgiven him" (15:22-29); but the soul that acts presumptuously, native or stranger, "shall utterly be cut off; his iniquity shall be upon him" (15:30-31). The boundary is not left abstract: a man gathering sticks on the sabbath is found, brought to Moses, Aaron and all the congregation, put in ward until it is declared, and stoned without the camp (15:32-36). The chapter ends at the border of the garment: fringes throughout their generations, a ribband of blue upon the fringe, so that looking on it they remember and do all the commandments and seek not after their own heart and their own eyes (15:37-40); and the closing word is one sentence spoken twice — "I am the LORD your God" (15:41). Offerings, one law, atonement, judgement, fringe: five things gathered into one self-declaration. There is this God first; then there are these rites and these boundaries.

## Main Text

### 1. "When Ye Be Come into the Land": The Fire-Offering Rule and the Three Grades (Numbers 15:1-12) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) Between the end of the last chapter and the beginning of this one there is a seam that must be pointed out exactly as it stands. The last chapter closed with the presumptuous assault beaten back to Hormah (14:45) and the sentence already pronounced. This chapter's first verse mentions no journey and no camp; it records speech: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying," (15:1). The second verse pushes the hearers into the future: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, When ye be come into the land of your habitations, which I give unto you," (15:2). In the KJV the land is "the land of your habitations, which I give unto you" — the giving precedes the dwelling; entry is written as a settled matter. When these words were spoken, the generation hearing them had just been sentenced not to enter (14:23); yet the liturgy's tense stands after entry. This manuscript simply sets the juxtaposition out: the chapter does not explain the displacement, and how commentators handle it belongs to the discussion layer (see the last paragraph of this section). The sermon first says what the text says: the liturgy is written according to the promise, not according to this generation's outcome.

(Fact) The general rule stands in verse 3, quoted verbatim: "And will make an offering by fire unto the LORD, a burnt offering, or a sacrifice in performing a vow, or in a freewill offering, or in your solemn feasts, to make a sweet savour unto the LORD, of the herd or of the flock:" (15:3). The verse has three layers. First, the source of the animals: "of the herd or of the flock" — cattle and sheep; the three grades below are drawn from these two herds by size. Second, the occasions, counted one by one, four in number: a burnt offering; a sacrifice in performing a vow; a freewill offering; the solemn feasts. Third, the general name and the general verdict: all are an "offering by fire," and the verdict is "to make a sweet savour unto the LORD." "Sweet savour" is this chapter's echo-word, occurring six times (15:3, 7, 10, 13, 14, 24), registered in the endnotes. The general name "offering by fire" is set up first; everything below — meal offering, drink offering, sin offering — is to be understood under it.

(Fact) Verses 4-12 are the table of measures. The text gives no general principle, only grade-by-grade numbers, transcribed and checked as follows. First grade, the lamb (15:4-5): "Then shall he that offereth his offering unto the LORD bring a meat offering of a tenth deal of flour mingled with the fourth part of an hin of oil." (15:4). The drink offering: "And the fourth part of an hin of wine for a drink offering shalt thou prepare with the burnt offering or sacrifice, for one lamb." (15:5). Three items in the grade: flour a tenth deal, oil the fourth part of an hin, wine the fourth part of an hin. Second grade, the ram (15:6-7): "Or for a ram, thou shalt prepare for a meat offering two tenth deals of flour mingled with the third part of an hin of oil." (15:6). "And for a drink offering thou shalt offer the third part of an hin of wine, for a sweet savour unto the LORD." (15:7). Three items: flour two tenth deals, oil the third part of an hin, wine the third part of an hin. Third grade, the bullock (15:8-10): verse 8 sets the occasion (in summary: preparing a bullock for a burnt offering, or for a sacrifice in performing a vow, or peace offerings unto the LORD), verse 9 gives the meal offering: "Then shall he bring with a bullock a meat offering of three tenth deals of flour mingled with half an hin of oil." (15:9). Verse 10 gives the drink offering: "And thou shalt bring for a drink offering half an hin of wine, for an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD." (15:10). Three items: flour three tenth deals, oil half an hin, wine half an hin. Set side by side, the rule is plain: the larger the animal, the heavier the accompaniment — flour rises from one tenth deal to two to three; oil and wine from a fourth part of an hin to a third part to half an hin — step by step in equal steps, no grade skipped, no grade merged. This manuscript records only the base text's own numbers: ephah-fractions and hin are the base text's own measures, and no metric conversion is asserted in the main text; conversions are kept in the discussion layer as reference only.

(Fact) After the table come two verses of general rule that gather the grades into standing law. Verse 11: "Thus shall it be done for one bullock, or for one ram, or for a lamb, or a kid." (15:11). Four animals are named in one sentence: bullock, ram, lamb, kid. The kid enters the list here for the first time; the three grades above were framed on lamb, ram and bullock, and the kid is handled on the lamb's grade — the text gives it no grade of its own, and this manuscript supplies none. Verse 12: "According to the number that ye shall prepare, so shall ye do to every one according to their number." (15:12). The general rule is a rule of multiplication: for however many animals are prepared, the accompaniment is multiplied per animal; the measures are not discounted for quantity, and no simplified rite is created for quantity either. Verses 4-12 are nine verses, one number per verse, one grade per animal; this passage cannot be read fast. Every time Israel offered after entering the land, this table lay on the altar-side; every fraction on it is part of "a sweet savour unto the LORD," not a reference figure to be adjusted at will.

(Application) This section gives today's hearers its first lesson in two words: "When ye be come." Every line of the liturgy is written in the future: when ye be come into the land, when ye eat of the bread of the land — nothing has happened yet. To a camp that has just failed and just been sentenced, God speaks first not of review but of the order of worship after entry: how you will offer, how you will remember, how you will be holy unto your God, written down line by line in advance. This is not a lack of compassion; it is another kind of compassion. He has not sentenced this people's future away. What was sentenced away was this generation's qualification to enter — not the covenant's future. After our own falls we habitually confuse the two: we take one failure as the cancellation of the whole future. This chapter separates them on the spot: the consequences run as sentenced (see chapter 14); the liturgy is written as promised. A man bearing consequences must still prepare for the future according to the liturgy. To set rules for tomorrow is itself evidence that tomorrow has not been given up.

(Application) The second lesson lies in the equal steps of the grades. The accompaniment rises with the animal, which tells us that giving before God was never one flat price: the bullock has the bullock's grade, the lamb the lamb's grade, each according to its measure, each by its numbers. The text does not let the man with a lamb measure himself against the bullock's grade, nor let the man with a bullock shrink to the lamb's. It requires only that each grade be carried out in full. Today's people fail in two opposite directions here: measuring themselves by another's grade, and producing self-pity or self-righteousness; or using "the heart is what matters" to cut their own numbers down until the accompaniment is gone and only an empty animal is left. This chapter's table blocks both roads. Your grade is set by what you have; your duty is to fulfil that grade in full — flour, oil and wine, nothing missing, nothing short. A sweet savour is sweet not because the animal is expensive, but because it is done according to the number.

(Summary) Words in this section, as philological statements only. The meal offering is minḥâ (H4503), here the grain accompaniment offered with the animal sacrifice, rendered "meat offering" in the KJV; the drink offering is nesek (H5262), the poured-out wine; the fine flour is sōlet (H5560); the unit of the ephah scale, rendered "tenth deal" in the KJV, is ʿiśśārôn (H6241), literally "a tenth (of an ephah)"; the sweet savour is the fixed pair rêaḥ nîḥôaḥ (rêaḥ H7381, nîḥôaḥ H5207), rendered in this chapter "a sweet savour." The "vow" is neder (H5088); the peace offerings rest on the plural form of šelem (H8002); the burnt offering is ʿōlâ (H5930). Every number above was checked individually before writing; the register is Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The placement of this chapter has long been a point of discussion among commentators; the positions are stated, not adjudicated. After the sentence of chapter 14 and before the rebellion of Korah in chapter 16, a whole chapter of entry liturgy is inserted. Two readings of the arrangement exist: either it is a deliberate insertion — between narratives of judgement, liturgical evidence is set down first that the covenant's future is not thereby abolished; or liturgical materials of differing origin have been placed here by theme, their original setting not being this narrative point. Which reading to adopt belongs to the discussion layer. The sermon registers only what the finished text itself shows: in the received order, chapter 15 does stand between sentence and rebellion, and the reader first hears "shall not see the land" and then "When ye be come into the land of your habitations, which I give unto you." The tension between the two sentences is left by the book to the reader; the sermon does not smooth it away. Likewise, modern capacity conversions of the measures (what an ephah or a hin amounts to in today's units) differ among authorities; the main text adopts none of them, keeping conversions in this layer as reference only: the measure-names and fractions are the text's; the conversions are later men's.

### 2. One Law for Native and Stranger; A Cake of the First of the Dough (Numbers 15:13-21) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) The table is barely finished before the text widens the circle of offerers. Verse 13 fixes the native first: "All that are born of the country shall do these things after this manner, in offering an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD." (15:13). Verse 14 turns to the stranger, quoted verbatim: "And if a stranger sojourn with you, or whosoever be among you in your generations, and will offer an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD; as ye do, so he shall do." (15:14). Two cases of stranger are named in the verse, counted one by one: the stranger who sojourns with you now, and whosoever is among you in your generations — the one settled for generations. For both cases there is one rule of offering: "as ye do, so he shall do" — not a reduced grade created for strangers, and not exclusion from the grade; the same grade. Verse 15 raises the rule to a standing ordinance: "One ordinance shall be both for you of the congregation, and also for the stranger that sojourneth with you, an ordinance for ever in your generations: as ye are, so shall the stranger be before the LORD." (15:15). The KJV's wording is "One ordinance": not two sides each stepping back to a compromise, but both gathered under one ordinance. Its term is "for ever in your generations"; its location is "before the LORD" — before men there may be a distinction between native and sojourner; before the LORD, that distinction does not enter the grade of the offering.

(Fact) Verse 16 is the section's total, quoted in full: "One law and one manner shall be for you, and for the stranger that sojourneth with you." (15:16). Two "one"s side by side: one law, one manner. Read verses 13-16 together and the "one" sayings form a chain of four verses, registered in sequence: the native does "after this manner" (13); the stranger does "as ye do" (14); "One ordinance shall be both for you... and also for the stranger" (15); "One law and one manner shall be for you, and for the stranger" (16). From "this manner" to "as ye do," from "as ye do" to "one ordinance," from "one ordinance" to "one law and one manner" — the phrasing grows more statutory with each verse, until verse 16 is fully a law's sentence. The reach of this oneness is used twice more later in the chapter: once in the oneness of atonement (15:29) and once in the oneness of the presumptuous (15:30); this manuscript registers them as they come. Here the account of this section is set down first: one rule for offerings, one rule for atonement, one rule for judgement — oneness runs through mercy and severity alike; it is not mercy only, and not severity only.

(Fact) Verse 17 opens a new speech: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying," (15:17). Verse 18's entry-sentence is to be read against verse 2: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, When ye come into the land whither I bring you," (15:18). Verse 2 called the land "the land of your habitations, which I give unto you"; here it is "the land whither I bring you." The same land: one saying speaks of giving, the other of bringing. Giving is the title; bringing is the journey; set side by side, entry is neither earned by men nor walked by men alone. Verse 19 is the heave offering's general command: "Then it shall be, that, when ye eat of the bread of the land, ye shall offer up an heave offering unto the LORD." (15:19). In the KJV it is "the bread of the land"; the Chinese base text here reads 粮食, the grain/produce of the land (the difference is registered in the base-text notes and Endnote 3; each manuscript follows its own base text). On the KJV's wording: when the land's produce has become the people's daily bread, at that moment the heave offering falls due — not at a bumper year, not at a feast; when you eat, you offer. The heave offering and the meal stand in one sentence: eating is the people's routine, and offering is that routine's first procedure.

(Fact) The substance of the heave offering is verse 20, quoted verbatim: "Ye shall offer up a cake of the first of your dough for an heave offering: as ye do the heave offering of the threshingfloor, so shall ye heave it." (15:20). The KJV's picture is the kitchen's: of the first of your dough, offer up a cake. (The Chinese base text pictures the field: 初熟的麦子磨面 — the first ripe grain ground into flour, then made into a cake; the two base texts picture the substance differently, registered in Endnote 3, each followed in its own manuscript.) The standard of comparison is stated: "as ye do the heave offering of the threshingfloor, so shall ye heave it" — the threshingfloor's heave offering was lifted out of the threshed heap; this heave offering is lifted out of the first dough. The venue moves from threshingfloor to kitchen; the specification does not change. Verse 21 closes the rule: "Of the first of your dough ye shall give unto the LORD an heave offering in your generations." (15:21). The subject is still "ye"; the term is "in your generations"; the substance is still the first of the dough. The command of verse 20 has become by verse 21 the standing custom of the generations: every generation eats the bread of the land, and every generation first offers up the first cake. "In your generations / throughout your generations" occurs three times within this sermon's second section (15:14, 15, 21) and five times in the chapter; the register is Endnote 4.

(Application) The one-law passage holds up a mirror to today's congregation first. Men naturally split the law into inside and outside: our own people by favour, outsiders by the letter; our own people's fault is carelessness, the outsider's fault is lawlessness. This chapter's oneness blocks that road to the face: before the LORD, as ye are, so shall the stranger be. The weight of the sentence is not only in how generously it treats the stranger — it also establishes the other side at once: the stranger's forgiveness is of the same grade as the native's (in verse 26 the sojourning stranger is forgiven together with all the congregation), and the stranger's boundary is of the same grade as the native's too. Grace of one grade, boundaries of one grade — that is what one law means. If today's church keeps a double standard of "old members" and "newcomers," "locals" and "outsiders," this chapter's order is the correction: do not ask first which side this person belongs to; ask first under which ordinance this matter falls. There is one ordinance, and all who stand under it are the congregation.

(Application) The firstfruits heave offering gives the daily routine its lesson: the heave offering does not begin on the temple's high platform; it begins at the table. When ye eat of the bread of the land, ye shall offer — God has inserted "his first" into the most routine act in which men most easily forget him: eating. A hungry man thinks of being filled; the land's first lesson is: before the first mouthful, lift up a portion. The portion is not large — one cake; but its position is heavy, for it stands before the whole family's meal. Order is the shape of faith: not giving him what is left after eating one's fill, but, before eating, offering up the first. Today's people put giving last in the ledger: rent, utilities, food, entertainment all paid, and only then is he considered. This chapter reverses the order: the first is his first; what remains is our bread. One cake will not change a household's fortunes; it changes whom the household owns as master.

(Summary) Three word-notes only. First, "All that are born of the country" renders ʾezrāḥ (H249), the native-born; "stranger" renders gēr (H1616), the sojourner. The pair runs through the chapter (15:13-16, 29, 30) and is the skeleton of its identity vocabulary. Second, "law" and "manner" in verse 16 are the pair tôrâ (H8451) and mišpāṭ (H4941); the verse's sentence in the original is "one tôrâ and one mišpāṭ," and the KJV renders it "One law and one manner"; the Chinese base text renders it 一样的条例，一样的典章 ("the same ordinance, the same statute"). The pair's domain is the whole of life, not the offering grade only. Third, the heave offering is tərûmâ (H8641), a portion lifted up and set apart from the whole; "the first" is rēʾšîṯ (H7225), "first, beginning"; the substance rendered "dough" in the KJV is ʿăriśâ (H6182), which the Chinese base text renders 麦子磨面, the first grain ground to flour. The two base texts picture the substance differently: the English text sees the kneaded dough, the Chinese text sees flour ground from the first ripe grain. Each manuscript follows its own base text — the Chinese manuscript writes the flour, the English writes the dough — and the difference is registered here rather than edited away (details in Endnote 3).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The later practice of this heave-offering rule and its correspondence with this chapter are registered here, not developed: Nehemiah 10:37 records the returned community covenanting to bring the firstfruits, including the first of the dough, to the chambers of the house of God (in summary); Ezekiel 44:30 assigns the first of the dough among the priests' portions (in summary). Both treat the offering of the first as a written standing custom, consonant with this chapter's generational term. How scholarship orders this section (15:17-21) relative to the corresponding provisions in Deuteronomy and Ezekiel — which precedes, which derives — is not agreed, and belongs to this layer. The sermon states only the fact: this chapter's heave offering starts from eating, runs for generations, and consists of one cake; all three points stand in the base text itself and do not need later practice to hold up.

### 3. Forgiven in Ignorance; Cut Off in Presumption (Numbers 15:22-31) (24:00-42:00)

(Fact) From verse 22 the speech turns to sin, and its first two verses are one long conditional sentence, quoted across the verse break exactly as the KJV reads: "And if ye have erred, and not observed all these commandments, which the LORD hath spoken unto Moses, Even all that the LORD hath commanded you by the hand of Moses, from the day that the LORD commanded Moses, and henceforward among your generations;" (15:22-23). The scope of the condition must be counted first: what is not observed is "all these commandments," whose extent is all that the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses, and whose term runs "from the day that the LORD commanded Moses, and henceforward among your generations" — from the day of the giving, generation after generation stands inside this scope. The KJV names the condition "erred": verse 22, "if ye have erred." That word is the head of the whole passage: the atonement and the forgiveness below all hang on this classification — "for it is ignorance" (15:25). The way of atonement first receives its classification, then its procedure. The text does not let men offer at random for unclassified sin; it states plainly: what this passage treats is error, ignorance — not presumption. Presumption is treated separately at verse 30, with a whole atonement procedure standing between the two passages; the two must not be read merged.

(Fact) Ignorance at the congregation's level stands in verse 24, quoted verbatim: "Then it shall be, if ought be committed by ignorance without the knowledge of the congregation, that all the congregation shall offer one young bullock for a burnt offering, for a sweet savour unto the LORD, with his meat offering, and his drink offering, according to the manner, and one kid of the goats for a sin offering." (15:24). The verse's elements, counted one by one. First, the classification: "committed by ignorance without the knowledge of the congregation" — the mark of the offence is that the congregation did not know; the sin was accomplished unawares. Second, the offerings, two animals: one young bullock for a burnt offering, and one kid of the goats for a sin offering. Third, the accompaniment: "with his meat offering, and his drink offering, according to the manner" — the phrase "according to the manner" sends the reader back to the table of the first section: the bullock's grade stands unchanged; the offering for ignorance is not discounted. Fourth, the verdict: "for a sweet savour unto the LORD" — even on the occasion of atonement for sin, what is offered is still called a sweet savour. Verse 25 is the priest's office and the declaration of forgiveness, quoted verbatim: "And the priest shall make an atonement for all the congregation of the children of Israel, and it shall be forgiven them; for it is ignorance: and they shall bring their offering, a sacrifice made by fire unto the LORD, and their sin offering before the LORD, for their ignorance:" (15:25). The sentence's order must be read exactly: the priest makes atonement first; "it shall be forgiven them" follows; and the ground is written in "for it is ignorance" — the forgiveness rests not on the offering's costliness but on the sin's classification as ignorance. Verse 26 completes the circle of the forgiven: "And it shall be forgiven all the congregation of the children of Israel, and the stranger that sojourneth among them; seeing all the people were in ignorance." (15:26). The sojourning stranger is not absent from forgiveness: the one law of the second section is here fulfilled upon forgiveness itself — for ignorance, native and sojourner are forgiven together.

(Fact) Ignorance at the individual's level stands in verses 27-28. Verse 27 fixes the offering: "And if any soul sin through ignorance, then he shall bring a she goat of the first year for a sin offering." (15:27). Set against verse 24: the congregation's sin offering is a kid of the goats; the individual's is a she goat of the first year — the animal's specification follows the size of the offender's circle. Congregation and individual each keep their own grade; the individual may not borrow the congregation's scale to fill his form, nor the congregation settle its account at the individual's scale. Verse 28 is the formula, quoted verbatim: "And the priest shall make an atonement for the soul that sinneth ignorantly, when he sinneth by ignorance before the LORD, to make an atonement for him; and it shall be forgiven him." (15:28). The verbs stand in the same order as verse 25: the priest makes an atonement for him — "and it shall be forgiven him." This formula of forgiveness occurs three times in the chapter (15:25, 26, 28), twice at the congregation's level and once at the individual's, registered in Endnote 4: whatever the size of the circle, the sentence of forgiveness is the same sentence. Verse 29 closes the ignorance passage, and it is still the one law: "Ye shall have one law for him that sinneth through ignorance, both for him that is born among the children of Israel, and for the stranger that sojourneth among them." (15:29). For sinning through ignorance there is one law; native-born and sojourner fall under the same one — the oneness of verse 16 here lands a second time, landing on atonement.

(Fact) At verse 30 the pen turns sharply; the whole verse verbatim: "But the soul that doeth ought presumptuously, whether he be born in the land, or a stranger, the same reproacheth the LORD; and that soul shall be cut off from among his people." (15:30). Where paraphrase diverges from the base text it is registered here on the spot: the KJV reads "doeth ought presumptuously" and the Chinese base text 擅敢行事; the charge's verb is "reproacheth" in the KJV, 亵渎 in the Chinese — no softer word stands in either base text. "Presumptuously" renders a phrase in the original whose picture is a hand raised high — acting openly, knowingly, with the hand lifted (the philology is registered in the Summary paragraph below). The verse's elements are three. First, the object: "the soul that doeth ought presumptuously," one singular soul, set far opposite verse 27's "any soul": the same one soul — in ignorance, forgiven; in presumption, cut off. Second, the identity: "whether he be born in the land, or a stranger" — the one law here lands a third time, landing on judgement. Third, the classification: "the same reproacheth the LORD" — the problem with presumptuous action is not located in the rule broken but in its posture toward the LORD: not not-knowing, but reproach. Verse 31 gives the reason, quoted in full: "Because he hath despised the word of the LORD, and hath broken his commandment, that soul shall utterly be cut off; his iniquity shall be upon him." (15:31). Two verbs carry the charge: he "hath despised the word of the LORD," he "hath broken his commandment." The sentence is "shall utterly be cut off," more absolute than verse 30's "shall be cut off." The last clause is the passage's full stop: "his iniquity shall be upon him." For the soul in ignorance, sin is atoned by the priest and forgiveness reaches him; for the presumptuous soul, the iniquity rests on himself — no one atones in his place, no offering is listed for him. Through verses 22-29 the offerings are listed full; in verses 30-31 there is not one lamb. That blank is itself part of the sentence.

(Application) The distinction between ignorance and presumption is this chapter's sharpest lesson for the conscience. The two passages differ not in the sin's size but in the sin's posture: ignorance is "without the knowledge of the congregation," is "error" — men did not know they had crossed the line. Presumption is the hand raised high — a man knows where the line is, crosses it deliberately, and treats the crossing as contempt for the one who drew it. For the former God prepared a complete way of atonement: offering, priest, forgiveness, nothing missing. For the latter he prepared only cutting off. This lesson corrects two common self-deceptions. The first pushes every sin onto ignorance: "I didn't know," said long enough, dresses even the known as unknown. But ignorance in this chapter has a strict mark: not-knowing is a judgement of fact, not a performance of attitude; feigned not-knowing is not classified by the text under ignorance — it falls under presumption. The second treats every sin as a dead end: having erred, a man thinks God is finished with him. But this chapter wrote the way of atonement out in such detail and fullness precisely for such a man to hear clearly: error has a road, the priest has a part, forgiveness has its sentence — "and it shall be forgiven him" is the settled end written for the one who erred. What is to be feared is not erring, but dragging error into presumption: to know, and still not turn; and as the days of not turning lengthen, the posture itself changes.

(Application) Notice also the formula of forgiveness: "the priest shall make an atonement for him; and it shall be forgiven him." Forgiveness is not announced by the offender himself, nor passed by a congregational vote; it is declared by the text after the priest has made atonement before the LORD. The forgiveness men manufacture for themselves is mostly explanation: how hard it was, how everyone does it, never again. The text's forgiveness does not travel the road of explanation; it travels the road of atonement: someone bears for you before God, and only then are you forgiven. How the Christian reader takes this up is registered in the New Testament layer below; as to this passage's own facts, the lesson Israel learned is: sin is forgiven never because the sin grew lighter, but because someone made atonement for you before God. Hold that sentence fast, and when your turn comes to bear for another, you will not pass off a weightless "never mind" as forgiveness. "Never mind" is not counting it; forgiveness is not counting it at a price paid by another. The two are as far apart as heaven and earth.

(Summary) This section's words, as philological statements, registered one by one. The KJV's "erred / by ignorance / through ignorance" group renders the šəgāgâ word-field (H7684), sin committed unwittingly, through oversight; verse 22's "erred," verse 24's "by ignorance," verse 26's "in ignorance," verse 27's "sin through ignorance," verse 28's "sinneth ignorantly," verse 29's "sinneth through ignorance" — the same root recurs in every verse of the passage: in chapter 15, verses 22, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 and 29, seven verses, the most densely distributed word in the chapter. "Make an atonement" is kāpar (H3722); the priestly atonement formula occurs twice (15:25, 28). "It shall be forgiven" is the passive of sālaḥ (H5545), used in this chapter only in the ignorance passage (15:25, 26, 28) and not once in the presumption passage — the distribution of the word matches the distribution of the sentences exactly. "Presumptuously" renders bəyād rāmâ, "with a raised hand": yād (H3027) is hand; rāmâ is the feminine form of rûm (H7311), to lift up, be high; the KJV elsewhere renders the phrase "with a high hand," and the Chinese base text renders it 擅敢行事. "Reproacheth" is the participle of gādap̄ (H1442), to revile; "despised" is bāzâ (H959); "hath broken" is pārar (H6565), to break apart, annul a commandment; "cut off" is kāraṯ (H3772), twice in the chapter (15:30, 31); "iniquity" is ʿāwōn (H5771). Every number above was checked individually in lexica and interlinear concordances before writing.

(New Testament Reading) The New Testament's closest counterpart to this passage stands in Hebrews 10 (in summary only): the author treats the one who sins wilfully after receiving the knowledge of the truth — for such a one there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, but a certain fearful looking for of judgement (summary of Heb 10:26-27) — and sets "despised Moses' law" against "trodden under foot the Son of God" as lesser against greater (summary of Heb 10:28-29). The New Testament reading is structured like this chapter's boundary: sacrifice for ignorance, none for presumption; but the New Testament carries the boundary one layer deeper — its warning is aimed not at outward breaches of rule but at treating what one has come to know as something to be trodden on. The reading of Paul's own case (summary of 1 Tim 1:13: "who was before a blasphemer... but I obtained mercy, because I did it ignorantly in unbelief") falls precisely on this chapter's ignorance side: what was done in not-knowing, and what is done presumptuously after knowing, the New Testament distinguishes as clearly as this chapter does. This layer registers readings only: in its own setting this chapter establishes the boundary of congregational rite and penalty; the New Testament application alters not one word of it.

### 4. The Man Who Gathered Sticks: Put in Ward, Awaiting Declaration (Numbers 15:32-36) (42:00-56:00)

(Fact) The sentence on presumption has barely been given when the narrative supplies a case. Verse 32 verbatim: "And while the children of Israel were in the wilderness, they found a man that gathered sticks upon the sabbath day." (15:32). The placement of the text is itself interpretation: this case is set nowhere else than immediately after "that soul shall be cut off from among his people." The facts of the case are one sentence: the time, the sabbath day; the place, the wilderness; the person, a nameless man; the act, gathering sticks. The text records no motive, no number of sticks, no reason for gathering — the motive column is empty, and this manuscript treats it as empty: the handling below does not stand on motive but on the collision of the act with the day. How heavy an act gathering sticks is, the text does not weigh; it states only that this was done on the sabbath. The reader must acknowledge the blank as blank, neither inventing for this man a plea of cold and hunger nor inventing a posture of open provocation — both inventions speak where the text has not spoken.

(Fact) The procedure then unfolds; the steps are counted one by one and registered from the base text, five in all. Step one, the finding (15:32): "they found a man that gathered sticks" — someone found him; the case opens not by confession but by discovery. Step two, the bringing (15:33): "And they that found him gathering sticks brought him unto Moses and Aaron, and unto all the congregation." (15:33). Three destinations, none omitted: Moses, Aaron, and all the congregation. Those who found him did not deal with him privately, did not carry out a sentence on the spot; they handed the man over before the whole congregation. Step three, custody and waiting (15:34): "And they put him in ward, because it was not declared what should be done to him." (15:34). This is the case's most important step: the man is already seized, the offence already established, yet the handling pauses — because "it was not declared what should be done to him." No one stretched the previous passage's sentence by analogy on his own authority; no one laid the two words "cut off" directly on this man's head. The whole congregation would rather put the man in ward and wait than fix the penalty ahead of God's declaration. Step four, the declaration (15:35): "And the LORD said unto Moses, The man shall be surely put to death: all the congregation shall stone him with stones without the camp." (15:35). The sentence comes from God, delivered through Moses: the penalty is death; the executors, all the congregation; the place, without the camp; the instrument, stones — four items, all present. Step five, the execution (15:36): "And all the congregation brought him without the camp, and stoned him with stones, and he died; as the LORD commanded Moses." (15:36). The execution matches the declaration item for item — brought without the camp, stoned with stones, done as commanded — and the verse's tail, "as the LORD commanded Moses," is the case's certificate of completion: from finding to execution, not one of the five steps came from private will.

(Fact) Set against the sentence passage before it, two things in this case must be stated exactly. First: the sentence passage's word is "cut off" (15:30-31); this case's execution words are "put to death" and "stoned" (15:35-36). The text does not explain how "cut off" and "put to death" correspond in this case — whether they are two sayings of one thing, or whether "cut off" points elsewhere and this case received its own specific sentence. The chapter does not say, and this manuscript sets the two groups of words side by side without stitching them together. Second: before this case was handled there was the waiting of "it was not declared," which shows that at least at the moment this case occurred, the specific penalty for sabbath work had not yet been promulgated as a read-out statute; the sentence was declared ad hoc in the case, not looked up in an existing code. This is to be read in the text's own order: first the act, then the custody, then the declaration, last the execution. The later statutory order (the sabbath statute is stated in other books; see the discussion layer) is not to be filled back into this case's point in time.

(Application) This case gives today's congregation two lessons, both lying in the procedure. The first is the lesson of waiting. The congregation held in its hand the heavy word of the passage just given — "shall be cut off from among his people" — and facing a man caught in the act, the first thing they did was not to execute but to put him in ward and wait: "it was not declared what should be done to him." At just the moment when anger and public pressure most easily push men to act at once, waiting for the declaration is reverence. How much damage in communities comes from translating "in principle this deserves the heavy end" directly into "this time we do the heaviest," skipping the inquiry, skipping the waiting, skipping the step where the judgement comes from above. This chapter's five-step procedure is slow, but no step is missing: finding, bringing, custody, declaration, execution. The slowness is not delay; it is the refusal to let men's hands run ahead of God's word. The second lesson is the whole congregation's: all the congregation execute; all the congregation brought him and brought him out. This case has no place for bystanders from beginning to end: the law is the whole camp's law, and the end of the law-breaker is a testimony the whole camp bears together. Today's people like to treat discipline as the leaders' business and boundaries as somebody else's business; this chapter puts the stones in the whole congregation's hands, and the meaning is plain: whether a community can hold its boundaries has never been a matter for a few leaders; it is the whole body's matter.

(Summary) Two word-notes only. "Ward" in "they put him in ward" renders mišmār (H4929), a place of guarding or custody; the base text's term denotes pre-sentence custody, not a prison after sentencing — in procedure the two stand at opposite ends, and this chapter uses the former. The stick-gatherer is named in the original by a participle, "the gatherer of sticks" (root qāšăš — form recorded without a number, not individually verified for this manuscript, per the stated convention); "sabbath" is šabbāṯ (form and rendering correspondence recorded likewise without a number, for the same reason). Further: the gathering of sticks has nothing to do with the manna-gathering narratives elsewhere in Numbers; this case's sticks are fuel-wood as the act implies, but the text assigns no use, and this manuscript does not specify cooking or heating on its behalf.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) This case's correspondence with statutes in other chapters is stated as fact and kept in its layer: Exodus 31:14-15 and 35:2 state in so many words that sabbath work shall surely be put to death (in summary); had those statutes been applied, this case's penalty would not have lacked a text to cite. Yet this chapter's narrative says plainly "it was not declared." The order and relation between the two — whether this case occurred before those statutes were promulgated, or whether the narrative deliberately foregrounds the procedure of case-by-case declaration — is handled differently by commentators and belongs to this layer; this manuscript sets them side by side only: the statutes are in other books, the case is in this chapter, the waiting for declaration is recorded in this chapter. Again, the blasphemy case of Leviticus 24 runs an almost parallel procedure: the man was first put in ward (Lev 24:12, in summary: held in custody awaiting the LORD's direction, wording varying slightly among versions), and afterwards brought forth without the camp and stoned (Lev 24:14, 23, in summary) — both cases are custody first, declaration second, execution without the camp; they are the only two cases of this procedure in the Pentateuch (this count of "two" is registered as counted from these two cases as examined). The relation between the two cases — a common procedural tradition, or mutual reference — belongs to the discussion layer; this manuscript registers the procedural parallel itself and no more. The later discussion of the stick-gathering case in Jewish tradition (the rabbinic debate whether "gathering sticks" is itself a case of presumption) is likewise entered here in one stroke, not developed and not adjudicated.

### 5. The Fringe on the Border, and the Sentence Spoken Twice (Numbers 15:37-41) (58:00-60:00)

(Fact) The chapter's last speech begins at verse 37: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying," (15:37). Its content is verse 38, quoted verbatim: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and bid them that they make them fringes in the borders of their garments throughout their generations, and that they put upon the fringe of the borders a ribband of blue:" (15:38). The making, counted item by item, four in all. First, the subjects: the children of Israel — every one has a share; no distinction of priest and layman. Second, the term: "throughout their generations" — this command belongs to the same class as the offering ordinance and the one law; all are generational. Third, the place and the object: fringes made "in the borders of their garments." Fourth, the mark within the mark: upon the fringe of the borders, "a ribband of blue." Where paraphrase diverges it is registered here: the KJV reads "a ribband of blue" — a ribband, with its colour; the Chinese base text reads 蓝细带子 with the verb 钉, "fasten," in a clause without the comma some paraphrases insert. And the addressee of this command deserves restating: the judgement case has just ended, and the next command is issued not to offenders but to the daily clothing of all Israel. After the boundary is drawn, God puts the lesson back on every man's border.

(Fact) The fringe's purpose stands in verse 39, a complete chain of purpose, quoted verbatim: "And it shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them; and that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes, after which ye use to go a whoring:" (15:39). The chain, counted link by link. First, wearing: the fringe is on the garment, not kept in a chest. Second, looking: the mechanism is the eye; looking is the first act. Third, remembering and doing: looking issues in remembering, remembering in doing, and the object is "all the commandments of the LORD" — not a remembered selection. Fourth, the negative: "that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes" — the reverse of doing is stated: seeking after one's own heart and one's own eyes; heart and eyes are two sources, one within, one without, and the state of seeking after them is named "go a whoring." Fifth, "after which ye use to go a whoring" — the KJV's "ye use to" says this is their habit: seeking after heart and eyes is not this people's future risk but their standing practice; the fringe is not a defence against a hypothetical enemy but a prescription aimed at an old, recurring ailment. Verse 40 gathers the chain into belonging: "That ye may remember, and do all my commandments, and be holy unto your God." (15:40). The end of remembering and doing is not moral self-sufficiency; it is "be holy unto your God" — in this chapter's syntax, holiness is a predicate of belonging: to whom you are holy, his you are.

(Fact) Verse 41 is the chapter's conclusion and the source of this sermon's title, quoted in full: "I am the LORD your God, which brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God: I am the LORD your God." (15:41). The verse's structure must be counted as it stands: one self-declaration spoken twice, with the Exodus fact set between — "which brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God." The first utterance is the declaration; the second is the seal; the clause between is the ground: he is their God not by empty self-naming but on the strength of the already-accomplished fact that he brought them out of the land of Egypt. Set against paraphrase: the KJV reads "to be your God," the Chinese base text 要作你们的神 with 作 (not 做); and the opening "I am the LORD your God" joins hands with verse 40's "be holy unto your God": the people are his because he is their God; he is their God because he brought them out. Widen the view to the whole chapter: this self-declaration occurs in this chapter only in this verse, yet twice within the verse. Every other section of the chapter — the offerings ("unto the LORD," 15:3), the one law ("before the LORD," 15:15), the atonement ("the priest shall make an atonement... before the LORD," 15:28), the judgement ("the LORD said unto Moses," 15:35) — turns on the axis of the LORD and this people; the conclusion only names that axis aloud. Of the chapter's five sections, four are the rules and one is the root; when the rules are finished, what is heard last is the root's own voice.

(Application) The fringe's lesson is addressed to the eyes. The path of human sin is drawn here with precision: the eyes seek first, the heart follows, the feet arrive last. Verse 39 sets heart and eyes side by side, but the fringe's mechanism works on the eye's road alone: let the eyes, before they seek, first collide with remembering. This is not suppression; it is getting there first. In the same downward glance, what is met can be the temptation or the blue ribband on the border; whichever arrives first, the other retreats. God's method for this people is plain to the point of stubbornness: he does not begin by demanding a new heart; he begins by posting a guard for their eyes. Today's people have dismantled most of the guards posted for the eyes: the phone at the pillow, the screen in the palm; the eyes' first sight on waking and last sight before sleep meet queues arranged by the world. Translated into today's speech, the fringe's lesson is: you must arrange with your own hands what your eyes see first — set the commandment on the eyes' necessary road, as the blue ribband was fastened on the border, fastened at the readiest, most-glanced-at places of your days. Remembering is not a mood; it is an arrangement.

(Application) The closing sentence, spoken twice, is the chapter's gift to those who bear consequences. This generation has just heard the forty-year sentence (chapter 14); the chapter has gone the long way round through rites and boundaries, and it lands at last on "I am the LORD your God" — spoken twice. The first utterance tells them: your identity has not been rewritten by the sentence; the one who brought you out of Egypt still names himself your God. The second tells them: this does not need your confirming once more; God himself speaks it the second time and sets the seal. In the midst of consequences, the first thing a man loses is the answer to "am I still his?" This chapter's conclusion answers that question head-on — not settling accounts first, but speaking the name first. The fringe was fastened on the border for this too: every time a man looks down and doubts his belonging, his eyes meet the ribband first, and the ribband carries him back to the conclusion — "which brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God." Spoken twice in the wilderness, that sentence is enough for this generation to walk the rest of its road, and enough for us to walk our stretch of it.

(Summary) This section's words, as philological statements. The "fringe" is ṣîṣîṯ (H6734), the tassel or fringe hanging at a garment's edge, rendered "fringe" in the KJV; the "borders" of the garments rest on kānāp̄ (H3671), whose base sense is wing, extremity, corner — a garment's corner is so named; the Chinese base text renders it 衣服边上 and 底边. The "ribband" is pəṯîl (H6616), a twisted cord or band; "blue" is təḵēleṯ (H8504), the blue-violet dye and its colour-name. "Remember" is zākar (H2142), the same root as the trumpet's "memorial" in chapter 10. The "seek" of "seek not after" is tûr (H8446) — the same verb as "searching" the land in chapter 13: there it searched out the land; here it searches out a man's own heart and eyes; the verb is the same, the direction reversed. "Go a whoring" is zānâ (H2181). All numbers above, except where expressly noted, were checked individually before writing.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The archaeology and traditional detail of the fringe are kept in this layer as reference only, never asserted in the main text: the source of the blue dye təḵēleṯ (the Mediterranean murex-shellfish secretion theory being the most widely circulated), its broad use in the tabernacle fabrics and the priestly garments (many places in Exodus, in summary), and the later regulations of the tallit and of ṣîṣîṯ — knot-counts, tying methods and the like — all belong to the layer of historical and traditional discussion. This chapter's text gives four facts only: on the borders of the garments, fringes, a ribband of blue, throughout their generations; the regulatory detail is not in this chapter at all. Again, Deuteronomy 22:12 has another garment-border rule (in summary: a sentence of the form "thou shalt make thee fringes upon the four quarters of thy vesture"), whose similarities and differences with this chapter (that passage uses a different original word for the fringe, and has no ribband of blue) are facts of the texts; the harmonizing of the tradition's similarities and differences belongs to the discussion layer. The practice of garment fringes in the days of the Lord Jesus is registered among the cross-references (Matt 9:20; 14:36; 23:5, reading layer) and is not read back into this chapter.

## Devotional Excerpt

“And it shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them; and that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes, after which ye use to go a whoring” (Num 15:39, KJV). The fringe put the commandments where the eyes could not miss them, for what we follow most easily is not another's voice but our own heart and our own eyes. This chapter first marks out the way of pardon — for the soul that sinneth ignorantly the priest maketh atonement, “and it shall be forgiven him” (15:28) — and then marks the boundary: the soul that doeth ought presumptuously reproacheth the LORD. The fringe cometh after both, not as an ornament but as a reminder. Thou knowest where pardon is, and thou knowest where the boundary is; now carry the commandments on the border of thy garment, so that whenever thine eyes drop, they meet the commandment before they meet the desire. And over the fringe standeth the sentence spoken twice: “I am the LORD your God, which brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God: I am the LORD your God” (15:41). Remember first, and then act — that is how a people becometh holy unto its God.

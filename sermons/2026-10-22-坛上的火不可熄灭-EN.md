Taking the fire that must be kept burning on the altar of burnt offering as its main thread, this sermon treats the priests' morning wood and clearing of ashes, their eating of the most holy things, the appointed time for eating the thanksgiving offering, and the priest's wave breast and right thigh, closing with the summary formula at the end of chapter 7 that gathers up the ordinances of the five offerings.

## I. The Same Sacrifices, Seen from the Other Side of the Altar 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, when we read Leviticus as far as chapter 6, if we are not careful we easily fall into a misconception: why does it speak again of the burnt offering, again of the meat offering, again of the sin offering, the trespass offering, and the peace offering? Were they not all covered in chapters 1 to 5? Is this repetition? May we skip it? Do not skip it. The opening of chapter 6 is a structural hinge of the whole book; skip it, and you will not understand why Leviticus tells of the same sacrifices twice.

Notice first the change of addressee. This is a fact at the level of structural observation, not a pattern the preacher has forced upon the text. In chapters 1 to 5, God's word is spoken mainly to the Israelites, to the people who bring offerings: if any man of you bring an offering, how he is to bring it, how to lay his hand on it, how to kill it; if a man sins through ignorance in some matter, how he is to offer a sin offering and a trespass offering. Those are ordinances from the standpoint of the door of the tabernacle, from the offerer's position. At chapter 6 verse 8 the text says: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Command Aaron and his sons" (Lev 6:8-9, KJV). The addressee has changed. From here to the end of chapter 7, God's word is spoken mainly to the priests, to the sons of Aaron: once the offering has reached the altar, how it is to be burned, how the ashes are to be cleared, how the priest's portion is to be reserved, where and by whom the most holy flesh is to be eaten, which part of the peace offering belongs to the priest. The same sacrifices: in the first half spoken from the offerer's side, in the second half from the priest's side; the first half teaches the people how to bring, the second half teaches the priests how to handle.

This change of viewpoint matters greatly. Sacrifice is not the people dropping an offering at the door and having nothing more to do with it, nor is it the priests performing at the altar a set of movements the people cannot understand. Sacrifice is one complete act of worship, with a side that brings and a side that handles; with the offerer's participation and with the priest's office. If the people read only chapters 1 to 5, they will think sacrifice ends with handing the offering over; if the priests keep only chapters 6 to 7, they will think sacrifice is merely altar procedure. Put the two halves together and the picture is complete: God accepts man's offering, and God solemnly instructs those who handle the offering how to complete every step in holiness.

Notice also a word that runs through these two chapters: ordinance. Almost every section opens with a sentence of this kind: "This is the law of the burnt offering"; "This is the law of the meat offering"; "This is the law of the sin offering"; "This is the law of the trespass offering"; "This is the law of the peace offering." The Hebrew word is torah. Ordinarily we translate torah as "the law," referring to the whole teaching God gave through Moses; but here it does not refer in general to the whole law. It refers specifically to the regulation, the ordinance, of one particular offering — the complete set of instructions for how that offering is to be handled. When the text says "This is the law of the burnt offering," it does not mean one isolated rule, but the name for the whole manner of handling the burnt offering. The notes will give the original form, transliteration, number, and dictionary references for this word; in the body of the sermon, hold fast to one thing: chapters 6 to 7 are the torah of each of the five offerings, the priests' own book of procedure.

For this reason, at the end of chapter 7 there is a summary formula that gathers up the whole passage: "This is the law of the burnt offering, of the meat offering, and of the sin offering, and of the trespass offering, and of the consecrations, and of the sacrifice of the peace offerings" (Lev 7:37, KJV), and the text takes care to record the place and the source: it is what the LORD commanded Moses on Mount Sinai, to be passed on to the children of Israel. As we read this long passage today, we read backwards from that summary: in the end, the ordinances of the five offerings gather into one sentence — the priest's office is not a programme man invented, but a procedure God Himself appointed on the mountain and in the tabernacle.

This sermon will not spread out every detail of these two chapters item by item; that could not be finished in two hours, and the main thread would be drowned. We take one main thread, the one in the title: "The fire shall ever be burning upon the altar; it shall never go out" (Lev 6:13, KJV). Fire is the centre of the ordinance of the burnt offering, and the centre of the priest's office. Every morning the priest lays wood on the altar and clears away the ashes, keeping watch over this fire day and night, because if there is no fire on the altar, sacrifice cannot even be spoken of. That the fire does not go out means that worship is not interrupted; it means that the place where Israel meets with God is prepared day and night; it means that every morning and every night an offering can be presented, sin can be atoned for, and thanks can be declared. All the priest's other work can be understood as unfolding around this fire: laying wood is for the fire, clearing ashes is for the fire, and eating the most holy things and dividing the wave breast and the right thigh are likewise bound up with this altar, this fire, and the order of this sanctuary.

So do not read chapter 6 as a heap of petty kitchen and furnace regulations. Read in it the solemnity of the priestly office: God entrusted the fire on the altar to a company of men, and commanded them not to let it go out. Within that entrustment there is repetitive labour, morning wood, night watch, soiling ashes, and holy garments. A great office is often hidden in day-after-day faithfulness. Next we shall follow this fire downward: first the law of the burnt offering and the fire kept ever burning, then the meat offering and the eating of the most holy things, then the appointed time for eating the peace offering and the priest's portions, and finally back to the summary formula, with a careful word on how the New Testament reads this passage.

## II. The Law of the Burnt Offering: The Fire on the Altar Shall Be Kept Burning 08:00-24:00

The law of the burnt offering is recorded in chapter 6, verses 8 to 13. The text says: "The priest shall put on his linen garment, and his linen breeches shall he put upon his flesh, and take up the ashes which the fire hath consumed with the burnt offering on the altar, and he shall put them beside the altar. And he shall put off his garments, and put on other garments, and carry forth the ashes without the camp unto a clean place" (Lev 6:10-11, KJV). In this passage three actions deserve slow attention: the burning through the night, the laying of wood in the morning, and the clearing of the ashes.

First, the rhythm of night and morning. The text commands that the burnt offering remain upon the hearth on the altar all night unto the morning, and in the morning the priest shall burn wood on it, lay the burnt offering in order upon it, and burn thereon the fat of the peace offerings. In other words, the work of the altar is not a bustle by day and an emptiness by night. By night an offering lies slowly burning on the fire; in the morning the priest comes, not to kindle a strange new fire, but to continue the night's fire — adding fresh wood, laying on a fresh offering. A day is thus marked out by the rhythm of sacrifice: the night has its burning, the morning its offering. When Israel wakes in the morning, the fire at the tabernacle is already burning; when Israel lies down at night, the fire on the altar has not yet gone out. This is not ritual waste; it is a declaration: the place where God meets His people does not close, does not go cold; the way of atonement and of nearness stands open day and night.

Next, the summit of the whole passage, and the source of this sermon's title: "…and the priest shall burn wood on it every morning, and lay the burnt offering in order upon it… The fire shall ever be burning upon the altar; it shall never go out" (Lev 6:12-13, KJV). The text uses the word "ever" — in Hebrew, tamid, meaning continually, constantly, without interruption. The notes will treat this word. In Scripture, tamid is used not only of the altar fire, but also of the lamp kept always burning in the tabernacle, of the shewbread always set forth, and of the daily burnt offering of morning and evening. It depicts a continuance maintained not by a moment's fervour, but by institution and faithfulness. That the fire is to be ever burning does not mean the fire will miraculously never go out by itself, needing no man's care; exactly the opposite — it is because the priest must add wood every morning and prepare every night that the fire can be kept ever burning. The continuance of the miracle is carried out in man's day-after-day faithful actions. If the priest is lazy one morning, the fire weakens; if he neglects it for several days, the altar goes cold. "It shall never go out" is both God's command and the priest's trust and warning.

The origin of this fire is told elsewhere in Scripture; we treat it only as intertextual background, not to be confused with the altar fire itself. Leviticus chapter 9 records the day the priestly office formally began: "And there came a fire out from before the LORD, and consumed upon the altar the burnt offering and the fat" (Lev 9:24, KJV), and all the people shouted and fell on their faces. That is the narrative of the altar fire's beginning: the first fire to consume an offering came from God Himself. Numbers chapter 16 records that when Aaron took a censer and stood between the dead and the living to stay the plague, the fire in the censer was taken from the altar — the altar fire at work in atonement and intercession. The flame in the bush in Exodus chapter 3 is the sign of God's appearing when Moses was called; it is not the same thing as the altar fire, and can serve only as a distant intertext of flame-theophany, never as an account of the altar fire's origin. Keeping the three distinct actually brings out the altar fire's uniqueness: it begins with God's coming, yet it must be maintained by the priest's daily wood; it is a fire God kindled, and a fire man must keep. Grace comes first, faithfulness follows close behind — this is the theological skeleton of the priestly office.

Next, the regulation for clearing the ashes — the passage most easily skipped, and the one richest in lessons. What has burned a night and a day at last becomes ashes; in Hebrew, deshen, the fatty ashes left from the burnt offering. If the ashes are not cleared, the altar is blocked: fresh wood cannot be laid, a fresh offering cannot be set in order. So the priest takes up the ashes, puts them beside the altar, and then carries them out without the camp to a clean place. Notice the clothing for this dirty work: the priest is to put on his linen garment and his linen breeches to take up the ashes. Clearing ashes is not rough work handed to a casual labourer; it is holy office, performed in holy garments and according to regulation. The man who carries ashes must first dress in the vestments, and only then take the ashes in hand; and when the ashes are carried without the camp, he must change into other garments. The holy garments are not carried without the camp, and the garments from without are not brought into the sanctuary — the distinction of clothing draws the boundary between holy and common, and teaches one thing: the most inconspicuous work of clearing is still part of worship in God's house; nothing at the altar is so lowly that it may be done carelessly.

The homiletical exposition for today is direct, but we mark the level: the facts of the text are the regulations of wood, ashes, and changing garments; the exposition is that those who serve God must not love only the moments when the flame leaps up on the altar, but must also be willing to do the work of morning wood and of carrying ashes. What tests faithfulness most in the church is often not the few conspicuous minutes on the platform, but the day-after-day preparation, tidying, and watching below it. The fire does not go out not because someone occasionally burns with great zeal, but because someone rises early every day to add wood and bends down every day to clear ashes. The priest's glory lies not in how splendid his garments are, but in this: the fire entrusted to his hand did not go out in his hand.

One more layer must not be missed: the continual burnt offering was offered for all Israel; it was not a private offering offered when someone happened to feel moved. In keeping the fire, the priest kept the whole congregation's way of access to God. An individual could offer his own burnt offering and peace offering, but the fire ever burning on the altar and the offerings of morning and evening maintained the meeting of the whole nation with God. The priest's personal faithfulness therefore carried public weight: his slackness on one morning would cool not merely one altar, but the people's way of coming before God. That is why this passage uses words like "command" and "law" again and again — the priest's office is not a talent performed for men's viewing, but a trust on which the worship of the whole people depends.

## III. The Meat Offering and the Most Holy Things: The Priest Eats in the Holy Place and Bears for the People 24:00-42:00

With the fire kept, we come next to how the priest eats. From chapter 6 verse 14 comes the law of the meat offering; from verse 24 the text turns to the sin offering; and from the end of chapter 6 to chapter 7 verse 7, to the trespass offering. Through this long passage one phrase recurs: most holy. The remainder of the meat offering is most holy; the sin offering is most holy; the trespass offering is most holy. In Hebrew, qodesh qodashim, literally "holiness of holinesses," most holy; the notes will give the original form and number. In the tabernacle context this phrase carries the greatest weight: the most holy place bears it, the offerings of the Day of Atonement bear it, and now the flesh of the meat offering, the sin offering, and the trespass offering bears it too. It does not mean especially delicious, nor especially precious; it means that this thing has been wholly brought within God's sanctuary, and to take it outside that sanctuary is profanation.

Precisely because it is most holy, the manner of eating is utterly solemn. The text commands that Aaron and his sons shall eat the remainder of the meat offering with unleavened bread in the holy place, in the court of the tabernacle of the congregation; that the priest shall eat the flesh of the sin offering in the holy place; and that if the blood of a sin offering is brought into the tabernacle to make atonement in the holy place, its flesh shall not be eaten but burned with fire. Who may eat is also restricted: the males among the sons of Aaron may eat it; women and outsiders may not. The place of eating is also restricted: it must be in the holy place, within the court of the tabernacle; it may not be taken home as an ordinary meal. Eating here is not the enjoyment of a benefit; it is part of the office: the priest represents God in accepting the offering, and represents the people in receiving this most holy thing. That the most holy thing is given to the priest to eat shows precisely that it first belonged to God — God gives a part of His own portion to those who serve Him, as their portion and inheritance.

Here there is a very weighty sentence, which must be read together with Leviticus chapter 10 verse 17. Chapter 7 verse 7 says that the trespass offering is as the sin offering, and its flesh belongs to the priest who makes atonement with it; around chapter 6 verse 29 the text likewise says the priest shall eat the sin offering. In chapter 10, Moses rebukes Aaron's sons for not eating the goat of the sin offering in the holy place, and gives the reason: it is most holy, and God has given it to them "to bear the iniquity of the congregation, to make atonement for them before the LORD" (Lev 10:17, KJV paraphrase in line with the text). The priest's eating of the sacrificial flesh is bound together with bearing iniquity and making atonement for the people before God. The priest's eating is not turning the sinner's cost into his own delicacy; by the ritual act of eating, he takes the people's sin upon himself, carries it into the sanctuary, and deals with it before God. This is a connection at the factual level of the text, not a later embellishment: Leviticus 10:17 locks together in one place the four words — eating, most holy, bearing, atonement. If, when we preach on the priest eating the most holy things, we preach only of the priest's privileges, we lose the weightiest link. The priest's eating is intercessory eating, official eating: the man who stands between the people and God receives this trust with his own body.

The law of the meat offering carries another reminder: apart from the small memorial portion of the meat offering that is wholly burned on the altar to God, the remainder belongs to the priests — but a meat offering offered by a priest himself must be wholly burned and shall not be eaten. Near the end of chapter 6 the text makes this distinction in particular: the priest's own meat offering is to be entirely burned and does not return to the priest himself to eat. The regulation binds the one who administers it just as much: the priest cannot administer for the people with one hand and reserve a portion for himself from his own offering with the other. Those who administer holy things are first governed by the regulations of holy things; office is not a pass granting unbounded privilege, but a stricter boundary.

This passage also contains a very concrete detail: the distinction of vessels, recorded in chapter 6 verse 28: "But the earthen vessel wherein it is sodden shall be broken: and if it be sodden in a brasen pot, it shall be both scoured, and rinsed in water" (Lev 6:28, KJV). This is a factual statement of ritual cleanness, and we state it as fact without drawing it out further. The reasoning is clear within the ritual logic: once the flesh of the most holy offering has been cooked in it, the vessel has contracted holiness. An earthen vessel is porous and absorbs the broth; it cannot be washed clean, and to keep it for later ordinary use would confuse holy and common — so it must be broken: better to destroy the vessel than to let a holy thing fall into common use. A bronze vessel does not absorb water; it can be scoured and rinsed clean and used again — so it must be scoured and rinsed. Two kinds of vessel, two treatments, and behind them one principle only: a vessel that has touched a most holy thing may not casually return to everyday use. Holiness leaves a trace, and those who handle holy things must answer for that trace to the end — not sparing even a pot or a jar.

Taken together, the priest's eating in the holy place and the fire on the altar in the morning are two sides of one office. The side of the fire speaks of continuance: wood added daily, never going out. The side of eating speaks of bearing: receiving the most holy things in the holy place, bearing the iniquity of the people. The priest is not a man who stands at a distance directing others' worship; he is a man who keeps the fire with his time and receives the offering with his body. The weight of the office falls on this continuance and this bearing. In homiletical use today we must likewise keep the boundary: we do not simply equate ourselves with the hereditary priestly office of Aaron's sons, which was a specific appointment within the Old Testament sanctuary system; we learn from the shape of that office the character of service — keeping continuance, willing to bear, accepting restraint, and taking no advantage from holy things.

## IV. The Appointed Time for Eating the Peace Offering and the Priest's Portions: Thanksgiving Must Not Be Delayed to the Third Day 42:00-56:00

From chapter 7 verse 11 comes the law of the peace offering. This is the only one of the five offerings whose flesh the offerer himself may eat, so the emphasis of the ordinance falls on the appointed time for eating and on the division of portions. The peace offering itself falls into three cases: offered for thanksgiving, called the thanksgiving offering; offered for a vow; and offered as a freewill offering. The appointed time for eating differs among the three, and the ordinance records it with great precision. We shall state the facts clearly first, and then speak of the exposition.

What is offered for thanksgiving must be eaten the same day it is offered; none of it may be left until the morning. Offered together with the thanksgiving offering is a set of cakes: unleavened cakes mingled with oil, unleavened wafers anointed with oil, and cakes of fine flour fried with oil. Why is the thanksgiving offering in such haste? The ritual timetable itself speaks: thanksgiving is an expression with a time limit; the matter of that day is thanked for on that day, and not left overnight. Man's gratitude is the thing most easily delayed: when grace is received we say we will give thanks; a few days later, in the press of business, we forget; a few days more, and even what we were to give thanks for has grown dim. The ordinance nails thanksgiving to the same day and permits nothing to be left until the morning, teaching by timetable that gratitude must not be delayed — the response to grace received has its own present moment, and if the moment is missed, the offering is no longer that offering. What is offered for a vow or as a freewill offering is granted one day more: it may be eaten on the same day and on the morrow, and what remains on the third day must be burned with fire; its eating time is a full day longer. A vow is pledged before God, and a freewill offering is an additional offering given voluntarily; such offerings carry the character of a feast and of sharing, and eating together with family and friends takes time, so the ordinance, measuring human circumstance, grants two days. But the grant has an edge, and the edge is the third day.

The flesh of the third day is characterised by the text with a very heavy word, treated in the notes: if the flesh of the peace offering is eaten on the third day, "it shall not be accepted, neither shall it be imputed unto him that offereth it: it shall be an abomination, and the soul that eateth of it shall bear his iniquity" (Lev 7:18, KJV). The Hebrew word is piggul, denoting what is corrupt and abominable, ritually discarded. The third day is not a question of the meat being slightly less fresh; it is that the eating time God appointed has been transgressed, and the offering turns over from acceptance to abomination. So stands the boundary: on the second day, still within grace; on the third day, fallen into abomination — with no room for negotiation in between. This fact also corrects a confused notion: that so long as the heart is sincere, when one eats and how one proceeds do not matter. Leviticus answers firmly: the day God appoints is the day; past that day, however sincere the heart, the offering does not count. This is not God being unfeeling; it is that the honesty of worship itself includes acting according to God's word, within the boundaries God has set. Thanksgiving and vows delayed to the third day have, ritually, already changed in nature.

The division of the peace offering is recorded from chapter 7 verse 28 onward. The offerer brings the offering; the priest burns the fat on the altar to God; the breast is to be a wave offering and the right thigh a heave offering, given to the priest who handles the offering. Specifically: the wave breast belongs to Aaron and his sons, the portion of the whole priestly family; the right thigh belongs to the individual priest who with his own hand sprinkles the blood and offers the fat, his portion for the labour of handling this offering. Wave and heave are the names of two ritual motions: the wave offering is waved back and forth in presentation before the LORD; the heave offering is lifted up in presentation; the notes will give the original forms of both words. The meaning of the motions lies in the posture of presentation: first waved and first lifted before God, showing that this portion is first presented as God's, and only then given back by God to the priest as his portion. The priest's portion is not snatched from the people's hands; it passes through God's hand: first presented before God, then received back — and the order cannot be reversed. The offerer also has his own portion: the remaining flesh of the peace offering, eaten together with family and neighbours, in joy, within the appointed time. So in one peace-offering animal, the fat goes to the fire on the altar, the breast and the thigh go to the priest, and the remaining flesh goes to the offerer's household to share — God, priest, and people each receive their portion, and the three sit at one table within one offering. That is the concrete shape of the word "peace": peace is not an abstract feeling, but a relationship restored in shared eating.

Within this section there are also two prohibitions that must not be overlooked: the fat and the blood of the sacrificial animal shall not be eaten. The fat is to be burned on the altar to God; the blood, in which is the life, is to be poured out for atonement, and man may not take life itself as food. These two are declared again and again, in chapter 3 and from chapter 7 verse 22 onward, and they are the boundary of holiness within the feast of the peace offering: eating and rejoicing together must not eat away the boundary; the joy of shared eating must live within being set apart as holy. The richer the feast, the clearer the boundary must be — this too is Leviticus's constant teaching.

Finally we return to the summary formula of chapter 7 verses 37 to 38, which gathers up the whole passage. "This is the law of the burnt offering, of the meat offering, and of the sin offering, and of the trespass offering, and of the consecrations, and of the sacrifice of the peace offerings; which the LORD commanded Moses in mount Sinai, in the day that he commanded the children of Israel to offer their oblations unto the LORD, in the wilderness of Sinai" (Lev 7:37-38, KJV). The five offerings were spoken of from the people's side in chapters 1 to 5 and completed from the priests' side in chapters 6 to 7; here they are combined into one complete torah list. The summary also points out in particular the source and the occasion: what the LORD commanded Moses, on Mount Sinai, when Israel was to offer sacrifices to the LORD in the wilderness. The wilderness is a place, and it is also a reminder: this set of ordinances was not a luxury to be used only after settling in Canaan, but a daily procedure given while still on the road in the wilderness. The fire burned day and night in the camp in the wilderness; offerings were presented daily on the altar in the wilderness; in the place least like home, God's people first learned how to worship. The summary formula is therefore not merely a table of contents; it is the fixing of a journey in place: God established the order of worship in the wilderness, and the people, carrying this fire, walked on toward the promised land.

## V. The Fire Is Still Burning: The Summary and the New Testament Reading 58:00-60:00

Brothers and sisters, having seen the five offerings from the priests' side, we return once more to that fire. What is spoken in this final short section of the sermon is marked as the level of New Testament reading and homiletical use; it is not read back as the direct meaning of the Leviticus text itself. Hebrews chapter 13 says: "We have an altar, whereof they have no right to eat which serve the tabernacle" (Heb 13:10, KJV); the author then exhorts us, by Jesus, to "offer the sacrifice of praise to God continually," and not to forget to do good and to communicate, "for with such sacrifices God is well pleased" (Heb 13:15-16, KJV). First Peter chapter 2 says that believers, as lively stones, are built up a spiritual house, an holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God by Jesus Christ; and that believers are "a chosen generation, a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:5, 9, KJV). The New Testament reads Leviticus in this way not to summon us to rebuild a bronze altar and add wood to it day and night, but to join the shape of the priestly office to the work Christ has already accomplished: the nearness that the altar fire maintained is now opened to believers in Christ, and the shadow of the priest bearing in the holy place finds its substance in the great High Priest who truly bore the sins of many.

The homiletical use falls on two things. The first is "continually." The fire on the altar ever burning answers to what Hebrews calls offering the sacrifice of praise continually: worship is not a burst of festal excitement, but continuance within ordinary days — wood added in the morning, and a response of thanksgiving not delayed to the third day but given on the day itself. The second is bearing. That the priest ate the most holy things and bore for the people reminds the believing community, itself a priesthood, that worship is never only receiving; it includes interceding for others, weeping with those who weep, and carrying other people's burdens before God. Both uses arise from the factual shape of this passage and do not alter the meaning of the original text: the original text commands the sanctuary office of Aaron's sons, and the New Testament and the sermon are the echo and application of that office in Christ — the levels are distinct, each in its place.

As we leave, may we remember the priest beside the altar, adding wood in the morning and carrying ashes in his linen garment. The fire must not go out, not because fire itself is stubborn, but because God established the ordinance and men were faithful day by day. God still accepts the worship His people offer according to His word; He still delights in thanksgiving offered on the day itself; He still entrusts to His people the office of bearing for others. May we keep that "continually," offer that "same day," and not let the fire in our hands go out in our generation.

## Notes

1. Difference of chapter division: in the Masoretic Text (MT) of the Hebrew Bible this passage is Leviticus 6:1–7:38; that is, MT 6:1 corresponds to 6:8 in English and Chinese versions, MT chapter 6 has twenty-three verses, seven fewer than in the Chinese and English versions (whose chapter 6 has thirty verses), and chapter 7 is the same in both. The Scripture range of this sermon is therefore given as 6:8–7:38 following the Chinese and English versions, verse citations follow those versions, and where division is at issue the MT is noted as the basis. See Milgrom, *Leviticus 1–16*, text note on 6:1, and Wenham's introduction on the division problem.

2. torah: Hebrew תּוֹרָה, transliteration torah, noun, teaching, instruction, law, ordinance, Strong H8451. BDB: direction, instruction, law; HALOT: instruction, law, regulation. For the specialised usage in this passage see 6:9 זאת תּוֹרַת הָעֹלָה (zot torat ha'olah, "This is the law of the burnt offering"), and the identical opening formulas for the meat offering, sin offering, trespass offering, and peace offering at 6:14; 6:25; 7:1; 7:7; 7:11; 7:37: here torah denotes the complete set of regulations for a single offering, not the whole Mosaic law in general. See Hartley's discussion of the introductory formula of each section.

3. esh tamid: Hebrew אֵשׁ תָּמִיד, transliteration esh tamid, the fire kept ever burning, the fire always burning on the altar (6:12–13). Of this, tamid תָּמִיד, transliteration tamid, continually, constantly, without interruption, Strong H8548. BDB: continuance, perpetual, constantly; HALOT: continuity, regularly, continually. The same word is used for the continual offering (Exod 29:38–42), the lamp always burning (Exod 27:20), and the shewbread (Lev 24:8), forming the group of continual (tamid) institutions of the tabernacle. Milgrom treats the tamid of the altar fire together with the priest's duty of morning and evening wood.

4. deshen: Hebrew דֶּשֶׁן, transliteration deshen, noun, fatty ashes, sacrificial ashes, Strong H1880. BDB: fat ashes, ashes of fat, i.e. remains of burnt sacrifice; HALOT: ashes (of burnt offering). See 6:10–11: the priest, wearing linen garments (bigde-vad), takes up the ashes and puts them beside the altar, then changes his garments and carries them out without the camp to a clean place. The linen garment and breeches are priestly holy garments (cf. Exod 28:39–43); that the clearing of ashes is done in holy garments shows that clearing too belongs to the holy office. See Wenham's note on 6:10–11.

5. qodesh qodashim: Hebrew קֹדֶשׁ קָדָשִׁים, transliteration qodesh qodashim, most holy, holiness of holinesses, Strong H6944 (qodesh, holy, set apart). BDB: most holy thing, most holy; HALOT: most holy. See 6:17; 6:25; 6:29; 7:6: the remainder of the meat offering, the sin offering, and the trespass offering are all most holy, and must therefore be eaten by the males among Aaron's sons in the holy place (within the court of the tabernacle), and may not be taken outside the sanctuary. For the connection between the priest's eating of the most holy flesh and the bearing of iniquity, see Lev 10:17; Milgrom treats this at length: eating is the ritual act by which the priest takes up and removes the congregation's iniquity.

6. The distinction of vessels, 6:28: an earthen vessel (keli-cheres, a pottery vessel) in which the flesh of the sin offering is cooked must be broken; a bronze vessel (keli-nechoshet) must be scoured (maraq, to scour, rub) and rinsed in water. The reason lies in the logic of ritual cleanness: earthenware is porous, absorbs the broth, and cannot be fully cleansed; bronze can be washed and reused. This is a factual statement of ritual cleanness; see Wenham and Hartley on 6:28, both of whom explain the difference of treatment by the contagion of holiness.

7. todah: Hebrew תּוֹדָה, transliteration todah, noun, thanksgiving, praise, thank offering, Strong H8426. BDB: confession, praise, thanksgiving, thank-offering; HALOT: thanksgiving, thank-offering. See 7:12–15: the flesh of the thanksgiving offering (zevach todah) is to be eaten the same day, leaving none until the morning; the votive offering (neder) and the freewill offering (nedavah) may be eaten until the morrow (7:16–17). In the same passage, piggul: Hebrew פִּגּוּל, transliteration piggul, noun, abominable thing, corrupt and discarded sacrificial flesh, Strong H6292. BDB: foul thing, refuse; HALOT: spoiled, abominable (sacrificial meat). See 7:18: what is eaten on the third day is piggul, is not accepted, and the one who eats it bears his iniquity.

8. tenufah: Hebrew תְּנוּפָה, transliteration tenufah, noun, wave offering, waving in presentation, Strong H8573. BDB: swinging, waving, wave-offering; HALOT: wave offering, brandishing. See 7:30–31: the breast of the peace offering (chazeh hatenufah, the breast of the wave offering), having been waved in presentation before God by the priest, belongs to Aaron and his sons as their portion. The verbal root nuf has the sense of waving, swinging. See Milgrom's discussion of the wave rite and of ownership.

9. terumah: Hebrew תְּרוּמָה, transliteration terumah, noun, heave offering, contribution, lifted portion, Strong H8641. BDB: contribution, offering, heave-offering; HALOT: contribution, levy, sacred donation. For the context in this passage see 7:32–34: the right thigh of the peace offering (shok hayamin, the right thigh) is called shok hatrumah, the thigh of the heave offering, and belongs to the individual priest who handles that offering, in contrast to the wave breast, which belongs to the priestly family as a whole. Elsewhere terumah also denotes firstfruits and sanctuary contributions; here it denotes specifically the lifted and presented right thigh portion of the peace offering, and is not to be generalised.

10. The intertextual layering of the altar fire's origin: Lev 9:24, fire coming out from before the LORD and consuming the burnt offering, is the narrative text of the altar fire's beginning; Num 16:46, Aaron taking fire from the altar in a censer and staying the plague, is the altar fire at work in intercessory atonement; Exod 3:2, the flame in the bush, is the fire of God's appearing, different in nature from the altar fire, and serves only as background intertext for flame-theophany, not as an account of the altar fire's origin. For this layering see Wenham's note on 9:24 and Milgrom's intertextual discussion on 6:13; the body of this sermon follows this layering and does not confuse the three.

11. The summary formula, 7:37–38: zot hatorah ("This is the law of…") gathers up the burnt offering, the meat offering, the sin offering, the trespass offering, the peace offering, and the offerings of consecration (millu'im), and notes the source as what the LORD commanded through Moses on Mount Sinai, on the day Israel was to offer sacrifices in the wilderness. Structurally, this formula closes the whole sacrificial legislation of chapters 1–7 (chapters 1–5 to the people, chapters 6–7 to the priests); this is a structural observation. See Hartley's closing analysis of 7:37–38.

12. Sources at the level of New Testament reading: Heb 13:10–15, on the altar of which those who serve the tabernacle have no right to eat, and on praise, doing good, and sharing as sacrifices with which God is well pleased; 1 Pet 2:5, 9, on believers as a royal priesthood offering spiritual sacrifices. Both passages are the New Testament's interpretation of the Old Testament priestly office and its homiletical use; when cited, the level is to be marked, and they are not to be read back as the direct meaning of Leviticus chapters 6–7 themselves.

## Devotional Excerpt

The fire on the altar shall be kept burning; it shall not go out. The priest kept that fire not by a moment's zeal, but by adding wood every morning and clearing away the ashes every day. What I ask of the Lord today is not a passing stirring, but this constancy: in the morning, add the wood of prayer; clear out the ashes that have settled in the heart; do not let busyness and weariness smother the flame. If the ashes are not cleared, the altar is blocked; unconfessed debts, unforgiven bitterness, and long-postponed duties, if not cleared out in the morning, leave no room for a new offering. Nor let thanksgiving wait until the third day — grace received today is thanked for today; a debt remembered today is confessed today; a promise made to the Lord is not left until the fervour has cooled. The priest ate the most holy things in the holy place as a bearing, not an indulgence; today I will likewise bring my family's hardships and my friends' burdens before the Lord as my own concern, keeping the fire awhile for others and not only for myself. The breast and the thigh were first presented before God before they became the priest's portion; may all that is in my hands — time, strength, and income — likewise be presented before the Lord first, and then received with joy, lest I clutch grace as private property. Lord, keep the altar of my home and the altar of my heart burning — thanks not delayed, portions not hoarded — a small, unbroken fire before You every day, until the day You come, and may the fire never go out in my hands.

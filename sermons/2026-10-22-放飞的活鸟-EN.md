# The Living Bird Set Free

Preacher: Zhao Guangyi, composed from Scripture and the commentaries

Scripture: Leviticus 14:1-57 (KJV, aligned verse by verse with the Chinese original)

Translator's note: Translated from the Chinese original manuscript of the same date. This is a first translation awaiting proofreading (初译待校).

Summary: Leviticus 14 records the rite by which one healed of a diseased affliction is pronounced clean: the priest goes out of the camp to examine him; outside the camp the rite is performed with two living birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn and hyssop over living water, and the living bird is set free; the man enters the camp but lodges outside his tent seven days, and on the eighth day offers a trespass offering, a sin offering, a burnt offering and a grain offering. The second half treats the affliction of a house. This sermon distinguishes the facts of the rite from homiletic use and promises no healing efficacy.

---

## I. The Priest Goes Out of the Camp: After Healing, a Pronouncement Is Still Needed 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, chapter 13 ends with a man who rents his clothes, shaves his head bare, covers his upper lip and cries, "Unclean, unclean," dwelling alone outside the camp. Chapter 14 opens in the opposite direction: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, This shall be the law of the leper in the day of his cleansing: He shall be brought unto the priest: And the priest shall go forth out of the camp; and the priest shall look" (Lev 14:1-3, KJV). One is outside the camp; the other comes out from within; they meet at the boundary of the camp. Chapter 13 fixes a status; chapter 14 restores a normal standing. Chapter 13's pronouncement separates a man out; chapter 14's pronouncement welcomes him back. The two chapters form a pair: without the second, the severity of the first has no exit; without the first, the welcome of the second has no weight. Today we read chapter 14 with care, and watch how God prepares, for a man already healed, a road step by step back among His people.

First, the most important premise of the chapter, at the factual level, without ambiguity. The rite of chapter 14 is not a rite of healing. The order in the text is clear: the priest goes out of the camp and looks, and if the plague is healed in the leper, then the rite of cleansing is performed for him (cf. 14:3). Healing comes first; the rite follows. The rite does not produce the healing; it pronounces and confirms a healing that has already taken place, and restores the man's place in the worshipping community. This sermon therefore keeps one boundary throughout: this chapter is the rite by which one already healed is declared clean; it promises and implies no efficacy in real life. The priest is not a physician in chapter 13, and he is not a physician in chapter 14 either; his office is always examination and pronouncement — in chapter 13 mostly pronouncing unclean, in chapter 14 at last pronouncing clean. Once this level is distinguished, we will neither use chapter 14 to treat anyone's illness, nor despise the rite as superfluous once a man is healed. The recovery of the body is a man's own affair; returning into the camp, into his home and into tabernacle worship is the community's affair, requiring public examination, public pronouncement and public witness. God did not let the healed man slip quietly back among the crowd, nor let the crowd receive him on rumour; He appointed a procedure, so that the one who had been apart returns by procedure, and those who receive him do so in peace upon the pronouncement. The procedure itself protects the dignity of the returning one: his cleanness is not charity begged for, but formally pronounced according to God's ordinance.

Note also a detail of the verb, full of meaning. In chapter 13 the procedure brings the afflicted man to the priest — the sick man walks toward the priest. At the opening of chapter 14, it is the priest who goes forth out of the camp to look. Why the change? Because the afflicted man is still outside the camp and, by the ordinance, cannot come into the camp to see the priest; if he is to be examined, the priest must go out. The ordinance does not tell the man outside to find his own way in, nor does it postpone examination indefinitely; it commands the one who holds office to walk out, beyond the boundary of the camp, to the place where that man is. The first action of chapter 14 is not the sick man's but the priest's; not the running of the returning one, but the coming-out of the one who pronounces. This belongs to factual observation of the text: the subject has changed, the direction is reversed. One step further, as homiletic exposition, with its level marked: God's grace is often the party that walks out first; man waits outside the camp, and God sends His servant out to examine and to pronounce. This exposition accords with the direction of the action in the text, and this sermon uses it, without presenting it as a doctrinal slogan written out in chapter 14.

The structure of the chapter, surveyed first and then treated in detail. Chapter 14 has two great parts: the first treats the rite of a person's cleansing, verses 1-32; the second, the ordinance for the affliction of a house and the rite of a house's cleansing, verses 33-57. Within the first part there are two stages. The first stage takes place on the day of cleansing — the day the healing is examined — outside the camp: with two living birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn and hyssop, one bird is killed over living water, the living bird is dipped in the blood and the blood is sprinkled on the one to be cleansed seven times, he is pronounced clean, and the living bird is let loose into the open field; then the man washes his clothes, shaves off his hair, bathes in water, and is clean and may come into the camp. Yet note: he comes into the camp, but he may not yet go into his own tent; he shall abide outside his tent seven days. The second stage takes place on the eighth day, at the door of the tabernacle: a trespass offering first, one lamb, whose blood the priest puts on the tip of the man's right ear, the thumb of his right hand and the great toe of his right foot, and oil upon the same places; then a sin offering, a burnt offering and a grain offering; the priest makes atonement for him, and he is clean, fully restored. Between the two stages lie seven days of transition: already in the camp, not yet at home; already pronounced in a first measure, not yet having offered the final sacrifices. This intermediate state is one of the most thought-provoking designs of the chapter: restoration is not accomplished in a leap; God gives the community time, and gives the returning one time, so that the pronouncement of cleanness is worked out layer by layer, and the man long apart re-enters people and worship step by step. To take the main line and not spread out verse by verse is this sermon's method for chapter 14; the two stages, the seven days of transition and the eighth-day offerings are the skeleton of the section on the person. In the second half, on the house, the skeleton is astonishingly similar: examination, shutting up, treatment, re-examination, judgment, and a rite of cleansing — one procedure moved from a person onto a house. Seeing this isomorphism, we understand that chapter 14 tells not only how one man comes back, but also how a home, a house and a community's space are, after affliction, pronounced habitable again.

## II. Two Birds and Living Water: The Rite of Cleansing Outside the Camp 08:00-24:00

Now follow the priest out of the camp and watch the rite of the first stage. The priest shall command that there be taken for him that is to be cleansed "two birds alive and clean, and cedar wood, and scarlet, and hyssop" (Lev 14:4, KJV). The materials are prepared before the rite is performed; let us look at each at the factual level, and then at their combination. The two birds must be clean and alive. "Clean" means birds usable under the ordinance; "alive" means taken alive. Below, one is killed and one is finally set free; the fates of the two birds diverge from here. Cedar wood, a piece of the timber: the cedar is a tall, durable tree of the time, its wood strong and resistant to decay, used again and again in the building of sanctuary and palace; in this rite it is bound together with the hyssop as the instrument for sprinkling. Scarlet yarn — scarlet thread or wool, dyed deep red, of the same colour family as blood. Hyssop, a many-branched small shrub whose twigs can be bound into a small bunch to dip liquid and sprinkle it, exactly suited to this rite. None of the implements of the rite is a regular vessel of the altar, and none comes from the tabernacle; they are taken from field and daily life: the birds from the open country, the wood from the mountain, the yarn dyed wool, the herb gathered by a wall, and the water held in an earthen vessel. The first stage of the cleansing rite is not performed in the sanctuary but outside the camp, and its implements accordingly carry the air of the wild and the everyday. This is not a lowering of the rite but its location: this step deals with the transition of a man outside the camp before he returns within it; the place is beyond the boundary, the implements are from the fields, yet the procedure is just as much by God's command and just as solemn.

The central vessel and medium of the rite must first be made clear. The priest shall command one of the birds to be killed "in an earthen vessel over running water" (14:5, KJV). Running water — in the Chinese, living water — is not dead water or stored water, but flowing water, spring water, stream water; the original means, precisely, living water. The bird is killed above the earthen vessel holding the living water; the blood drips into the water, and blood and living water are joined in the vessel. Why living water? At the factual level: this chapter, and the ordinance of the red heifer's ashes mixed with living water in Numbers 19, both appoint living water as the water of the cleansing rite, taking its nature as continually flowing, never stale or corrupt, as against standing water. Discussions of the symbolic level in the commentaries are set side by side in the notes; the text of the sermon holds one steady main line: living water is here the medium appointed for the cleansing rite, and its flowing character is, in Israel's ritual language, always joined with cleanness and life.

The action of the rite falls into three acts. First, one bird is killed over the living water, as above. Second, the priest takes the living bird, the cedar wood, the scarlet and the hyssop, dips them in the blood of the bird that was killed and in the living water, and sprinkles it on the one to be cleansed seven times, and pronounces him clean (14:6-7). See the picture clearly: the living bird does not fly away spotless; it is first dipped into the liquid in which its fellow's blood is mixed with living water; the cedar wood and hyssop are bound as the sprinkling instrument and the scarlet joins the things together; the priest sprinkles this mixed liquid on the one to be cleansed seven times. Seven times is, in this book, the recurring number of completeness in rites of cleansing and consecration; sprinkled seven times, the action is complete. After the sprinkling, the priest pronounces him clean — a pronouncement again; note that in chapter 14, as in chapter 13, the priest's mouth still makes the pronouncement that decides a man's place in the community. Third, and the most moving act of the whole rite: the priest lets the living bird loose into the open field (14:7). One bird dies above the earthen vessel; one bird, bearing the traces of blood and water, flies toward the open field and does not return.

How are this pair of birds to be understood? By rule, this sermon sets the views side by side and draws no conclusion. The first view, the carrying-away of uncleanness: the living bird symbolically bears the uncleanness now removed and flies to an uninhabited place, carrying the uncleanness away from camp and people, as uncleanness exiled far away, never to return. The second view, the restoration of life: attending to the fact that the living bird is alive and is finally released to fly, it sees in it a figure of the cleansed man's restored life and freedom: death belongs to the past, the living flies toward the future, and the man, like the bird, is released from the loneliness outside the camp into open space. The third view, the paired-rite view: attending to the structure itself — one dies, one lives; one remains, one departs — it holds that the two birds form a pair whose meaning lies in contrast and completion: without the bird that is killed there is no blood, and the rite is no rite; without the bird that is released, the cleansing has no visible exit and witness. Lack either bird, and the ritual language is incomplete. This view names no single symbolic object and argues only from the ritual structure. Each view has its support: the first, in the act of release in the rite; the second, in the imagery of life and flight; the third, in the structure of the whole rite. Milgrom, Wenham and Hartley each give the symbolism of this rite a different centre of gravity; this sermon sets the three views side by side without declaring a conclusion, and in homiletic use takes only their common floor: cleansing has both a side of cost and a side of release; both an act of blood and an act of flight.

A guardrail must be added here, distinguishing the levels from the two goats of the Day of Atonement in Leviticus 16. In chapter 16 one goat is killed for the LORD and one is sent away for Azazel into the wilderness; the pair of goats and the pair of birds of chapter 14 are indeed structurally similar: one dies, one departs; one remains, one is released. This sermon acknowledges this as a structural analogy: both places use the ritual language of a pair of living creatures, one dying and one released, to treat the departure of sin and uncleanness from the community. But the analogy stops at structure and must not be turned into a direct equation. The differences are at least three. First, the occasion differs: chapter 14 is the individual rite of a healed person's cleansing; chapter 16 is the great annual rite of atonement for all the people. Second, the object differs: chapter 14 treats the removal and pronouncement of ritual uncleanness; chapter 16 treats the sins of all Israel laid upon the goat and carried away. Third, the action differs: chapter 16 has the express act of laying on of hands and confession, putting all the sins upon the goat; before the release of the living bird in chapter 14 there is no record of laying on of hands or of confession, and the text does not state, as chapter 16 does, what if anything the bird bears. Precisely because it does not state it, the carrying-away view can be listed only as one view among others and cannot borrow chapter 16's express words to settle chapter 14's symbolism. Keeping this level distinct makes the analogy an honest analogy, instead of pressing two passages into one lump.

When the rite outside the camp is finished, the one cleansed must do three things: wash his clothes, shave off all his hair, and bathe in water; then he is clean and may come into the camp (14:8). The extent of the shaving, the text says, is all his hair — his beard, his eyebrows, all his hair shall he shave off, thoroughly and without remainder. Bathing, washing clothes and shaving are all acts of putting off the old: the traces of the days outside the camp are removed together with water and razor. Having done these, he crosses the boundary of the camp and comes in. Yet the text immediately adds: he "shall tarry abroad out of his tent seven days" (14:8, KJV). Into the camp, but not into his home: this is the transition zone chapter 14 sets for return. Why not let him go home at once? At the factual level we can only state that the ordinance so arranges it; the text gives no reason. The inferences of the commentaries may be set side by side: one holds it a need of observation — seven days is this book's customary cycle of re-examination, and though the returning one has been pronounced, a period of observation remains, so that the state of cleanness stands firm before everyone's eyes; another holds it a reality of transition — a man long apart, returning to family and people, needs a buffer; the seven days inside the camp but outside his tent let him return first to the community's space and then to his own home's space, returning in two steps. The two views stand side by side; this sermon decides neither. What is certain is that these seven days are not punishment: he is already clean, already inside the camp, no longer crying "Unclean," no longer dwelling alone outside. These seven days are transition, the second stretch of the road of return. On the seventh day he shaves all his hair again, washes his clothes and bathes in water, and he is clean (14:9). Twice shaving, twice bathing, with seven days of waiting between: the first stage is concluded, and the door of the eighth day stands ahead.

## III. The Eighth Day: Blood and Oil on Ear, Hand and Foot 24:00-42:00

On the eighth day the return reaches its last station. "And in the eighth day he shall take two he lambs without blemish, and one ewe lamb of the first year without blemish, and three tenth deals of fine flour for a meat offering, mingled with oil, and one log of oil. And the priest that maketh him clean shall present the man that is to be made clean, and those things, before the LORD, at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation" (Lev 14:10-11, KJV). Note the movement of place: the first stage outside the camp, the second at the door of the tabernacle. The one cleansed walks nearer step by step: from outside the camp, to inside the camp outside his tent, to the door of the tabernacle. The advance in space is the progress of the return. At the door, the order of the offerings unfolds, and this order has one conspicuous peculiarity that must be stated honestly: the trespass offering comes first. In the usual impression, the sin offering treats sin and the trespass offering treats liability and reparation; the two are promulgated in that order in chapters 4-5, and occasions of offering also mostly put the sin offering first. But in the eighth-day order of chapter 14 the priest first kills one lamb for a trespass offering, and only then offers the sin offering, followed by the burnt offering and the grain offering. This peculiarity is, in this sermon, only stated as a fact and discussed in the notes; no system of reasons will be forced together to round the order off. The fact that can be stated is this: the trespass offering leads here, then follows the anointing with blood and oil, then the sin offering, burnt offering and grain offering follow in turn, the priest makes atonement for him before the LORD, and he is clean (cf. 14:19-20). The commentaries' discussion of the trespass offering's precedence runs in several directions: one attends to the trespass offering's character of both return and reparation, holding that the cleansed man, long absent from sanctuary and community, in his return first treats by a trespass offering a liability-like uncleanness and absence, as making an account for the worship and consecration of the days he owed; another attends to the fact that the blood used in the anointing in this rite is taken precisely from the trespass offering's lamb, so that its precedence is a procedural necessity, since the anointing must use its blood; another reminds the reader that the order of offerings in Leviticus varies by occasion, and that the cleansing rite is a class of its own, not to be trimmed by the usual order elsewhere. The several directions stand side by side; this sermon draws no conclusion. The fact itself is enough for reverence: the order God appoints for the returning one is not arranged by our habitual taxonomy, but by the need of this one occasion.

The anointing is the central action of the eighth day, and the place where this chapter answers chapter 8 across the book. The priest takes some of the blood of the trespass offering and puts it upon the tip of the right ear of him that is to be cleansed, upon the thumb of his right hand and upon the great toe of his right foot; he takes some of the log of oil, pours it into the palm of his own left hand, dips his right finger in it and sprinkles it seven times before the LORD, and puts of the oil that is in his hand upon the tip of the right ear, the thumb of the right hand and the great toe of the right foot, upon the blood of the trespass offering; and the rest of the oil in his hand he puts upon the head of him that is to be cleansed (14:14-18). Record the details one by one: the parts are on the right side — earlobe, thumb and great toe, three places; the order is blood first, oil after, the oil put upon the blood, the two layers laid together; the oil is first sprinkled seven times, as the blood was sprinkled seven times outside the camp, with seven as the number of completion; the remaining oil is put on his head, extending the anointing from the three places to the head as the representative of the whole body. The ear is the organ of hearing, the hand of working, the foot of walking; this reading of the symbolism of the parts of the body is of long standing and belongs to the level of homiletic exposition, which this sermon marks and then uses: a man cleansed and restored — his hearing, his doing and his walking are all to be consecrated anew and to re-enter the life of God's people. The ear that heard the loneliness outside the camp now hears God's word and the people's song; the hand idle outside the community's labour now works again, provides again, serves again; the foot that halted outside the camp now walks into the camp again, draws near the tabernacle again, and walks the road home again. This exposition grows naturally from the fact of the parts and is not an allegory imposed from outside; this sermon uses it, while still distinguishing: the plain sense of the original is the ritual action; the symbolism of the parts is a homiletic reading.

The parallel with Leviticus 8 is the firmest line at the level of textual observation, and must be stated. Chapter 8 records the rite of consecration of Aaron and his sons: Moses took the anointing oil and the blood upon the altar and sprinkled it on Aaron and his sons; and he took the blood of the offerings and put it upon the tip of Aaron's right ear, the thumb of his right hand and the great toe of his right foot, and likewise upon the same parts of his sons, and sprinkled and poured the oil and the blood (cf. Lev 8:22-30). The parts are the same: tip of the right ear, thumb of the right hand, great toe of the right foot; the elements are the same: blood and oil; the order is close: blood comes first, oil follows. The two occasions: one is a priest's consecration, the other a diseased man's cleansing and return; one stands at the starting point of holy office, the other at the end point of return. This parallel is a fact of the text: the same anointing language is used upon two persons seemingly at the farthest remove — the bearer of the most holy office, and the cleansed man long outside the camp. Its theological meaning is set out in several views, with no conclusion drawn. The honour view: the anointing raises the cleansed man to a dignity almost priestly, showing that restoration is not a second-class reception but a complete restoration, complete to the point of being described in the language of consecration to office. The consecration view: attending to the common function of the anointing — whoever is touched by blood and oil is consecrated and set apart for God's use; the priest is consecrated to serve, the cleansed man is consecrated to return; the point is not sameness of office but sameness of consecration. The structural view: arguing only from the editorial structure of Leviticus, it holds the parallel between chapters 8 and 14 to be deliberate: chapter 8 sets up the priest who can pronounce cleansing; chapter 14 has the priest use almost the rite that set himself up to pronounce a man's cleansing and return; the book's beginning and latter part are thus clasped together. The three views stand side by side; this sermon's position is: the fact of the parallel is certain beyond doubt; the use of its meaning may be wide; a conclusion may not be pronounced. Preaching should be most restrained here: we point out the correspondence the text itself sets out, and let the congregation see that upon a man returning from outside the camp there is the shadow of the rite of consecration to office — this is already striking enough, without adding a sentence the text has not spoken.

The eighth-day order completes itself after the anointing: the priest offers the sin offering and makes atonement for him that is to be cleansed; then he kills the burnt offering, and offers the burnt offering and the grain offering upon the altar, and he is clean (14:19-20). Note that the grain offering appears here again: in the returning man's list there are flour, oil, livestock and birds — a complete set of worship. The man long away from the tabernacle offers, on his return, the people's whole daily set of worship; he is not assigned a reduced, probationary worship; what he offers is the same as everyone's. The completeness of the return lies precisely here: not a return to the edge of the crowd, but to the centre of worship; not tolerated to remain, but pronounced clean, offering with all, eating with all, singing with all.

Then the text does something that shows the system's compassion: it opens a scaled-down road for the poor. If he is poor and cannot get so much, he shall take one lamb for a trespass offering — the very lamb of the anointing, which may not be scaled down — while the animals for the sin and burnt offerings may be scaled down to a turtledove or young pigeon for a sin offering and one for a burnt offering, and the fine flour of the grain offering is reduced to a tenth deal, the oil still one log (14:21-22). The priest likewise takes the blood of the trespass offering and puts it on his ear, hand and foot, likewise puts on the oil, likewise makes atonement for him, and he is clean (cf. 14:24-29). See the boundary of the scaling clearly: what may be scaled is the grade of the sacrificial animals; what may not be scaled is that one lamb of the trespass offering, the anointing with blood and oil, and the priest's pronouncement. In the poor man's rite of return, not one of the core actions is lacking; the rich man offers two lambs and a ewe, the poor man one lamb and two birds, and the pronouncement is the same — he is clean; the entry into the camp is equally complete. This is of one line with the grading of the burnt offering in chapter 1 and of the sin offering in chapter 5: God's ordinance always measures each man's strength, and does not let poverty become an obstacle to drawing near to God. Poverty is not a second-class citizenship of cleanness; the ordinance writes this sentence with its own hand into the procedure of return. The section on the one cleansed closes at verse 32, and the text expressly marks it: this is the law of him in whom is the plague, including him who cannot get so much as pertains to his cleansing (cf. 14:32). Not one may be left out: that is the close of the first half of chapter 14.

## IV. The Affliction of a House: The Same Procedure, from Person to House 42:00-56:00

The second half of the text suddenly changes scene and time. The LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron: when you come into the land of Canaan, which I give you for a possession, and I put the plague in a house of the land of your possession, the owner shall come and tell the priest (cf. 14:33-35). Note: the stage of the house ordinance is not the wilderness but Canaan; Israel is still camping in the wilderness, and God has already made an ordinance for the days when they will live in houses of stone. This is the same arrangement as the grain offering's writing farming life in advance into the wilderness ordinances: God sets His people's future days in advance within the order of worship and cleanness. If the owner of a house sees a sign of the plague in his house, he must first empty the house of everything in it, lest, after the priest has looked, everything in the house be pronounced unclean, and then go and tell the priest and ask him to come and look (cf. 14:35-36). Emptying before examination is both consideration in procedure and rigour in procedure: before judgment, the things in the house are not implicated in advance; after judgment, they are treated according to the judgment.

What does the priest look for in the house? The text says: he looks on the plague, and if the plague is in the walls of the house with hollow strakes, greenish or reddish, whose sight is lower than the wall, he goes out and shuts up the house seven days (cf. 14:37-38). Greenish and reddish hollow strakes are the characteristic signs of the house plague; the colour words in the original are treated in a separate note; hollow and lower than the wall surface shows that the spot is not a stain floating on the surface but a lesion eaten into the body of the wall — the very same judgment as "deeper than the skin" in the case of the person. On the seventh day the priest comes again and looks, and if the plague has spread in the walls, he commands the stones in which the plague is to be taken out and cast into an unclean place outside the city; the house is scraped within round about, and the mortar poured out outside the city; other stones and new mortar are taken, put in and plastered (cf. 14:39-42). This is the middle treatment: dig out, scrape, replace, replaster; remove the diseased part, repair the wall, and observe again. If, after the scraping, the replacing of stones and the replastering, the plague comes again and breaks out in the house, the priest looks again, and if the plague has spread in the house, it is a fretting plague; the house is unclean, and the whole house shall be broken down, its stones, its timber and all its mortar carried to an unclean place outside the city (cf. 14:43-45). This is the heaviest treatment: not repair, but demolition. If the plague does not return, the priest pronounces the house clean, because the plague is healed (cf. 14:48). The treatments are in three grades: shutting up for observation, digging and scraping for repair, and breaking down and carrying out; with the three grades for the plague of garments in chapter 13 — washing, tearing out, burning — the logic of order from light to heavy is exactly the same: what can be kept is not destroyed, what can be repaired is not demolished, and only what breaks out again and frets without ceasing is treated to the end. The aim of the ordinance is never the pleasure of destruction, but the stopping of loss and the preserving of what can be preserved.

The rite of the cleansing of a house is especially worth noting, for it is almost a repetition of the rite of the two birds outside the camp. For the cleansing of the house the priest takes two birds, cedar wood, scarlet and hyssop, kills one bird in an earthen vessel over running water, dips the living bird with the wood, scarlet and hyssop in the blood and the running water, sprinkles the house seven times, and lets the living bird loose into the open field outside the city; so he makes an atonement for the house, and it is clean (cf. 14:49-53). The implements are the same, the actions the same, and even the destination of the release is the same: the open field. Chapter 14 opens its section on the person with the rite of the two birds and closes its section on the house with the rite of the two birds; beginning and end are clasped by the same ritual language. A house is not a person and has no flesh and blood, yet the priest speaks of making atonement for the house — an extension of ritual language: the house, as the space in which men dwell and live, is also taken into the order of clean and unclean, and also needs to be pronounced and restored. Verses 54-57 close the whole chapter, listing together every kind of plague — of the person, of garments, of houses — and marking this as the general law for examination and for pronouncing clean or unclean. Chapters 14 and 13 together are one complete code of examination and cleansing: chapter 13 is chiefly examination, chapter 14 chiefly cleansing; the hinge of chapter 13 is pronouncing, the hinge of chapter 14 is atonement and pronouncement.

The structural observation of this section must have its level expressly marked. The isomorphism of procedure between the section on the person and the section on the house is a structural observation: both sections run in five steps — examination, shutting up seven days, treatment of the lesion, re-examination and judgment, and a rite of cleansing; both begin with the priest's looking and end with the priest's pronouncement; in the middle of both there is a seven days' waiting and graded treatments. This isomorphism shows that in the cleansing order of Leviticus, person, garments and house are not three unrelated sets of rules but one order unfolded in three domains: wherever the life of God's people reaches — body, clothing, dwelling — it lies within the range of examination and cleansing. The observation stops here; to go further requires caution. The ancient context of the treatment of houses is stated honestly in this sermon: this is a religious ordinance by which ancient Israel, in its settled life in Canaan, treated mould-like lesions of walls within its ritual categories; its object is the part of the wall, or the whole house, judged to have a fretting plague, and its ground lies within the framework of ritual cleanness. The statement stops here: this sermon draws from this chapter no advice for the treatment of houses today, and teaches no one how to deal with today's mould, walls or property; the hygiene and repair of dwellings today should follow the professional routes of modern building and public health — a different domain from this chapter's ordinance — and Scripture must not be mixed into use as real-life guidance. Keeping this line is what makes a reading of the house section honest: we admire its procedural beauty, its compassionate heart and its structural wholeness, without mistaking a ritual ordinance of three thousand years ago for a handbook for life today.

## V. Go, Show Yourselves to the Priests: The New Testament Reading and the Road of Return 58:00-60:00

Finally, by this sermon's rule, the New Testament reading is marked as its own level and not read back as the plain sense of chapter 14. Mark 1:44 records that Jesus, having cleansed a man with the plague, charged him to say nothing to any man, but to go and show himself to the priest, and offer for his cleansing the things which Moses commanded, for a testimony unto them. Luke 17:14 records that ten men with the plague stood afar off and cried out, and Jesus said, "Go shew yourselves unto the priests. And it came to pass, that, as they went, they were cleansed." Both places are the most direct echoes at the level of New Testament reading: Jesus healed the men, yet still sent them back into the procedure of chapter 14 — to show themselves to the priests and offer what Moses commanded. The healing is from Jesus; the pronouncement still follows the procedure; for return is not only a matter of the body, but also of being formally received by the community and witnessed by all. The homiletic use is here: the Lord Himself fulfilled the law's road of return; His cleansing reaches the body and also sends the man back among people and worship. The cleansing theme of Hebrews 9 belongs to a reading at the same level: that chapter treats how the blood of the old covenant, sprinkled, sanctified to the purifying of the flesh so that men could worship, and how the blood of Christ purges the conscience so that men serve the living God — the New Testament author himself joins Leviticus' language of cleansing to Christ; we cite this joining and mark it as the New Testament's reading, without saying that chapter 14 itself already speaks of conscience and of Christ.

We close with chapter 14 itself. Two birds, one killed and one set free; blood sprinkled seven times; seven days of waiting; on the eighth day, blood and oil on ear, hand and foot — and a man who once cried "Unclean" outside the camp stands again at the door of the tabernacle, offering with all the people. There is a road of return, the road has an order, and every step of the order was commanded by God in advance. May everyone among us who feels he is still outside the camp hear this chapter today: the priest has come out of the camp, the living bird has flown with the blood into the open field, and the door of the eighth day is still open. Let us pray: Lord, we thank You that You do not let the healed be stranded outside the camp, and that You have prepared for return a road of pronouncement. Make us willing to return in Your order, and willing to receive, upon Your pronouncement, everyone who returns, so that in Your camp there is no forgotten corner. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Notes

1. metzora: Hebrew מְצֹרָע, transliteration metzora, noun, one afflicted with tzara'at, a person with the diseased affliction, Strong number H6879. Meaning according to BDB: leprous, leprous man; HALOT: one afflicted with tzara'at. It is a participial noun from tzara'at (צָרַעַת, H6883); the one to be cleansed in Lev 14:1-3 is so named. Following the caution of the note in chapter 13: tzara'at is not an equivalent term for modern Hansen's disease, and metzora must not be directly translated or understood as a "leper" in the modern medical sense; the sermon text calls him the afflicted one, the one to be cleansed.

2. mayim chayyim: Hebrew phrase מַיִם חַיִּים, transliteration mayim chayyim, living water. mayim, water, H4325; chayyim, living, life, the plural form of chay, Strong number H2416. Meaning according to BDB: living waters, running water; HALOT: running, living water. It denotes flowing spring or stream water, as against dead or stored water. The earthen vessel of living water in Lev 14:5-6, 50-52, and the red heifer's ashes mixed with living water in Num 19:17, both appoint this as the water of the cleansing rite.

3. 'erez: Hebrew אֶרֶז, transliteration 'erez, noun, cedar wood, cedar, Strong number H730. Meaning according to BDB: cedar; HALOT: cedar. It denotes a tall tree of the Lebanon cedar kind, its wood strong and resistant to decay, used in the building of temple and palace. The cleansing rite of Lev 14:4, 6, 49, 51 binds the cedar wood with the hyssop as the sprinkling instrument; the same combination appears in Numbers 19.

4. sheni tola'at: Hebrew phrase שְׁנִי תוֹלַעַת, transliteration sheni tola'at, scarlet, scarlet yarn. sheni, scarlet, deep red, Strong number H8144; tola'at, worm, the kermes insect, Strong number H8438, its name arising from the deep red dye made from that insect. Meaning according to BDB: sheni, scarlet; tola'at, scarlet material; HALOT likewise. Leviticus 14:4 in the Chinese Union Version is "scarlet yarn"; the original is the scarlet material, connected with the use of scarlet yarn in the sanctuary in Exodus 25-28.

5. ezov: Hebrew אֵזוֹב, transliteration ezov, noun, hyssop, Strong number H231. Meaning according to BDB: hyssop; HALOT: hyssop. A many-branched small shrub that can be bound as an instrument for sprinkling. At the Passover in Exod 12:22 hyssop dips the blood and strikes the lintel; the cleansing rites of Numbers 19 and Leviticus 14 both use it; Psalm 51:7, "Purge me with hyssop," is the same plant. The exact botanical species is discussed by scholars; the sermon text takes only the fact of its use, bound for sprinkling.

6. tzippor hachayyah: Hebrew צִפֹּר הַחַיָּה, transliteration tzippor hachayyah, the living bird that is set free. tzippor, bird, H6833; hachayyah, the living (feminine), H2416. Its symbolic interpretations are set side by side: the carrying-away of uncleanness view, argued from the act of release; the restoration-of-life view, argued from the imagery of life and flight; and the paired-rite view, argued from the structure of one dying and one living. Milgrom gives weight to the rite's function of removing defilement, Wenham cautiously sets the symbolic views side by side, and Hartley stresses the declarative character of the ritual act itself. This sermon draws no conclusion, and expressly marks: it must not be directly equated with the goat for Azazel in Leviticus 16, but used only as a structural analogy — the occasion, the object, and the presence or absence of laying on of hands and confession differing at three levels, as distinguished in the sermon text.

7. 'asham and the order of offerings: trespass offering, Hebrew אָשָׁם, transliteration 'asham, Strong number H817. Meaning according to BDB: guilt, guilt-offering; HALOT: reparation offering. Leviticus 14:12-18 performs the anointing with the blood of the lamb of the trespass offering, and its precedence over the sin offering is a peculiarity of this chapter. Milgrom explains it by the trespass offering's inclusion of return for what is owed to the sanctuary, Wenham by the procedural reason that the anointing must use its blood, and Hartley by the reminder that the cleansing rite is a class of sacrificial order of its own. The three directions stand side by side; the sermon text draws no conclusion.

8. log: Hebrew לֹג, transliteration log, a log, a measure of capacity, Strong number H3849. Meaning according to BDB: log, a liquid measure; HALOT likewise. By the common scholarly reckoning it is about a third to half a litre; the exact figure is disputed. In Lev 14:10-18 the returning one brings one log of oil for the sprinkling and anointing; in the scaled-down version for the poor its measure is not reduced (14:21-24), showing that the oil in this rite may not be scaled down.

9. The parts of the anointing and the parallel with Lev 8: Lev 14:14-17 and Lev 8:22-30 both put blood on the tip of the right ear (tenukh 'ozen, the earlobe; 'ozen, ear, H241), the thumb of the right hand (bohen yad; bohen, thumb, H931) and the great toe of the right foot, followed by oil. The textual parallel belongs to the factual level: the parts, the elements, and the order of blood first and oil after are the same in the three respects. Its theological meaning is set out in several views: the honour view, the consecration view, and the structural-correspondence view; Milgrom, Wenham and Hartley differ in emphasis, and this sermon draws no conclusion, as distinguished in the text.

10. nega' tzara'at in the house section: the affliction of a house is still named in the original with nega' (נֶגַע, H5061, plague, lesion) plus tzara'at (14:34). The priest's examination, the shutting up seven days (hesgir, הִסְגִּיר, the causative form of H5462), the taking out of stones, the scraping, the replacing of stones and replastering, and demolition upon relapse — the procedure is isomorphic with the section on the person. The house is treated here within ritual categories; its ancient context is the cleansing ordinance of settled life in Canaan, and this sermon draws no advice for the treatment of houses today.

11. yerakraqot / adamdamot: the colour words for the hollow strakes in the wall of the house at Lev 14:37. yerakraqot, original יְרַקְרַקֹּת, yellowish-green, greenish, the feminine plural of yaraqraq (יְרַקְרַק, H3422); meaning according to BDB: yellowish green; HALOT: greenish. adamdamot, original אֲדַמְדָּמֹת, reddish, the feminine plural of adamdam (אֲדַמְדָּם, H125); BDB: reddish; HALOT likewise. The hollow strakes in the original are sheqa'arurot, depressions, whose sight is lower than the wall, parallel to the judgment "deeper than the skin" in the section on the person. The Chinese Union Version renders them greenish and reddish hollow strakes.

12. Sources at the level of New Testament reading: Mark 1:44 and Luke 17:14, where Jesus charges those cleansed to show themselves to the priests and offer what Moses commanded, refer precisely to the examination and cleansing rite of Lev 14; Hebrews 9:13-14, 22 treats the sprinkling of blood and cleansing under the old covenant and develops, by contrast, the cleansing of the conscience by the blood of Christ. All three belong to the level of New Testament reading and homiletic use: they show how the New Testament takes up chapter 14's procedure and cleansing theme, without being read back as the plain sense of chapter 14 within its Old Testament framework; nor does this chapter's rite thereby acquire any promise of efficacy in real life.

## Devotional Excerpt

I have stood at a distance for too long. What a person long kept apart fears is not a seat, but the eyes of others when he returns: Is he really well? Will it come back? Should we avoid where he sat? Leviticus 14 shows me that God thought of the returning one long ago: the priest himself comes out of the camp to see me; I do not have to force my way back. The living bird, dipped in blood, flies into the open field and carries those days outside the camp far away, never to return and entangle me. And there are seven days in which I may come slowly — first standing within the camp, then walking back to my own home. On the eighth day, blood is put on my ear, my hand, and my foot: what I hear, what I do, and where I walk can all belong to the Lord again. I will also learn to receive others in the same way: not to define a person for life by one sickness or one season of distance, but to walk with him through the seven days of waiting, to keep a seat for him when he returns, and to pull out a chair at the table. As the Lord sent the priest out of the camp to find me, so I will step out of my own comfort to visit those still far away, and tell them the way back is not cut off, the door is still open, and the voice of pronouncement still sounds. Today I will no longer hide at a distance and cry out; I will walk back step by step, in His order. When He pronounces me clean, I will sit at peace among His people — no longer hiding, no longer ashamed — as one whom He Himself has welcomed home.

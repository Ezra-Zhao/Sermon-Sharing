## Summary

Chapter 15 has just set out the liturgy of entry and the boundary of sin when chapter 16 opens with the largest company of the wilderness narrative. Korah, great-grandson of Levi, with Dathan, Abiram and On of the Reubenites, and two hundred and fifty princes of the children of Israel, rose up before Moses and gathered themselves together against Moses and against Aaron, saying that all the congregation are holy, every one of them, and the LORD is among them (16:1-3). Moses heard it and fell upon his face; he did not defend himself but fixed the appointment: "Even to morrow the Lord will shew who are his, and who is holy" (16:5). The instrument of the trial is the censer — tomorrow, fire and incense before the LORD, and the man whom the LORD chooses shall be holy (16:6-7). To the sons of Levi Moses puts the charge plainly: separated to come near and to do the service of the tabernacle — is that a small thing, and do you seek the priesthood also? You are gathered together against the LORD (16:8-11). Dathan and Abiram refuse the summons, twice: "We will not come up," and they call Egypt itself a land that floweth with milk and honey (16:12-14). Moses, very wroth, protests to the LORD that he has not taken one ass from them (16:15). The next day the two hundred and fifty censers stand at the door of the tabernacle; the glory of the LORD appears; God speaks of consuming the congregation in a moment, and Moses and Aaron fall upon their faces: "shall one man sin, and wilt thou be wroth with all the congregation?" (16:16-22). The congregation is commanded to get up from about the tabernacles of the three households, and Moses stakes the sign beforehand: if the LORD make a new thing, and the earth open her mouth, then you shall understand that these men have provoked the LORD (16:23-30). He had made an end of speaking, and the ground clave asunder; the earth swallowed the households and their goods, and fire from the LORD consumed the two hundred and fifty men that offered incense (16:31-35). The brasen censers of the burned are not discarded: they are made broad plates for a covering of the altar, a memorial unto the children of Israel (16:36-40). But on the morrow the whole congregation murmurs: "Ye have killed the people of the LORD." The plague begins; fourteen thousand and seven hundred die; Aaron runs with his censer into the midst of the congregation and makes atonement for the people — "And he stood between the dead and the living; and the plague was stayed" (16:41-50). Read the chapter's arithmetic honestly: the company is counted in hundreds, the intercessors are two; judgement falls twice, and twice the two men fall upon their faces; and what finally stays the plague is not a slogan but a man's standing place.

## Main Text

### 1. "Ye Take Too Much upon You": The Company, the Fallen Face, and the Trial of the Censers (Numbers 16:1-11) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) The chapter opens with a genealogy, quoted from the base text: "Now Korah, the son of Izhar, the son of Kohath, the son of Levi" (16:1) — three generations read in a chain: Levi, Kohath, Izhar, Korah. He is no stranger from outside the camp; he is of Levi, of the house of Kohath, of the same tribe and the same house as Moses and Aaron themselves. Named with him, registered one by one from the base text: "and Dathan and Abiram, the sons of Eliab, and On, the son of Peleth, sons of Reuben" (16:1). Four names stand in the first verse: Korah first, Dathan and Abiram next, On last. A blank must be registered here at the outset: the name of On occurs this once in the whole chapter, and never again — not in the trial, not in the refusal, not in the opening earth, not in the fire, not in the sign, not in the plague. Where he went and what became of him the chapter does not say, and this sermon registers the blank without filling it (see Endnote 8). Verse 2 gives the weight of the company: "And they rose up before Moses, with certain of the children of Israel, two hundred and fifty princes of the assembly, famous in the congregation, men of renown:" (16:2). The number two hundred and fifty occurs three times in the chapter (16:2, 17, 35); here it counts men — princes of the assembly, famous, men of renown. These are not a rabble; they are men with standing and place in the congregation, and with the four leaders they "rose up before Moses" together. That rising is the chapter's first great movement: later God twice commands Moses and Aaron to separate themselves (16:21, 45), and the congregation is commanded to depart from about the three tabernacles (16:24, 26) — the dividing line of the whole chapter is drawn from this one rising.

(Fact) Verse 3 is the charge, and its words fall in three layers, read from the base text. First, the verdict: "Ye take too much upon you." Second, the ground: "seeing all the congregation are holy, every one of them, and the Lord is among them:" — both clauses are, in themselves, true: the congregation is indeed a people set apart to the LORD (compare ch. 15, cross-reference layer), and the LORD has indeed promised to dwell among the children of Israel. Third, the conclusion: "wherefore then lift ye up yourselves above the congregation of the Lord?" (16:3). From "every one holy" they infer "no man set above." The point of dispute in this chapter is never whether the congregation is holy; it is whether holiness abolishes separation. Korah's company marches under a true sentence — and draws from it a conclusion the sentence does not contain. Moses' answer below (16:9-10) does not deny the congregation's holiness; he denies the inference: it was God himself who separated the Levites to come near and do the service, and to seek the priesthood also is to reach for a further separation beyond the one given. Read verse 3 slowly: the rebellion opens not with a lie but with a truth misused — "all holy" is true; the step from "all holy" to "no distinction of office" is the company's own addition.

(Fact) Moses' first response is not an answer but a falling: "And when Moses heard it, he fell upon his face:" (16:4). The falling occurs three times in the chapter (16:4, 22, 45) — once at hearing the charge, once at the word of consuming, once before the plague. Register the positions exactly: Moses never stands tall for himself before men; he falls down before God for the people. In verse 5 he speaks to Korah and all his company, quoted verbatim: "Even to morrow the Lord will shew who are his, and who is holy; and will cause him to come near unto him: even him whom he hath chosen will he cause to come near unto him." (16:5). Three items. First, the appointment: "even to morrow" — no sentence on the spot; the verdict is deferred to the morning, and a night is left to them. Second, the judge and the standard: it is the LORD who will shew, and the standard is double — who are his, and who is holy. The company has talked of nothing but holiness; Moses hands the question "who is holy" back to the LORD to shew. Third, "come near" occurs twice in the verse: "will cause him to come near unto him... will he cause to come near unto him" — nearness to God is not seized; it is caused. The chosen man is caused to come near. Verse 5 is the chapter's order of proceedings: everything that follows — the fire of the censers, the mouth of the earth, the copper on the altar, the stayed plague — is the execution, item by item, of this one shewing.

(Fact) The manner of the trial stands in vv. 6-7, quoted verbatim: "This do; Take you censers, Korah, and all his company; And put fire therein, and put incense in them before the Lord to morrow: and it shall be that the man whom the Lord doth choose, he shall be holy: ye take too much upon you, ye sons of Levi." (16:6-7). The procedure has three steps — take censers, put fire therein, put incense in them — the place is before the LORD, the day is tomorrow. The rule of judgement is one sentence: "the man whom the Lord doth choose, he shall be holy" — holiness is not carried by vote or proved by renown; it is shewn by choosing. The last clause is one of the chapter's hinges and must be read exactly: Moses returns the charge's first words upon the chargers. "Ye take too much upon you" in v. 3 is what they said of Moses and Aaron; in v. 7 it is what Moses says of the sons of Levi. The phrase occurs twice in the chapter, once as accusation, once as rejoinder, and between the two occurrences stands the trial of the censers: who is taking too much upon himself will be settled not by the charge but by tomorrow's fire. Nor is the censer an arbitrary instrument: it is the very vessel of the office in dispute, the office of coming near and burning incense (compare Aaron's censer at 16:46-47, fact-layer cross-reference within the chapter). The men who claim the office are summoned to stand before the office's own vessel.

(Fact) In vv. 8-11 Moses' address narrows from Korah and his company to the sons of Levi. Verse 8 opens: "Hear, I pray you, ye sons of Levi:" (16:8). Verse 9 first counts the separation already given, quoted verbatim: "Seemeth it but a small thing unto you, that the God of Israel hath separated you from the congregation of Israel, to bring you near to himself to do the service of the tabernacle of the Lord, and to stand before the congregation to minister unto them?" (16:9). The separation has three works: to bring you near to himself, to do the service of the tabernacle, and to stand before the congregation to minister unto them. Register that standing here: the Levites' appointed place is standing before the congregation in service. In v. 48 Aaron's standing is between the dead and the living in atonement — the same posture, two different stations; one is the standing office, the other is a standing in disaster, and the chapter does not confuse them. In the KJV the question "seemeth it but a small thing" stands within v. 9; in the Chinese base text it stands in v. 10 — the verse division differs, registered in the head notes. Verse 10 presses the second question, quoted verbatim: "And he hath brought thee near to him, and all thy brethren the sons of Levi with thee: and seek ye the priesthood also?" (16:10). The weight is in the "also": not from nothing to something, but beyond nearness already given and brethren already brought near, a seeking of the priesthood in addition. Verse 11 delivers the verdict, quoted verbatim: "For which cause both thou and all thy company are gathered together against the Lord: and what is Aaron, that ye murmur against him?" (16:11). The point lands not on Moses' wounded feelings but on the true object of the gathering, renamed: you think you are gathered against Aaron; you are gathered together against the LORD. "What is Aaron?" — he is only a man separated out; murmur against him, and the murmur's terminus is not in him.

(Application) This section gives today's hearer a first lesson: discern the misuse of a true sentence. Not one word of the company's banner is invented: all the congregation are holy — true; the LORD is among them — true. The fault is not in the banner but in the inference: from "all holy" to "no distinctions," from "God among us" to "any man may come near." The hardest disputes in a church are seldom raised by falsehood; they are raised by a true sentence drawing a false conclusion — "we are all priests," therefore office may be self-taken; "God loves all," therefore every boundary is man-made. This chapter's method is worth learning: Moses does not debate the banner; he appoints a trial — he hands the dispute back to God's shewing and lets choosing make the separation plain. In a like dispute today the first question is not "is the sentence true?" but "what does the sentence conclude?" A true sentence used to abolish a separation God himself has made has become a banner, not the truth. And let the hearer examine himself: what does my loudest spiritual sentence conclude — that I submit the more to the separation given me, or that I may exalt and take for myself?

(Application) The second lesson is in Moses' falling. Before a company of some hundreds of men in public accusation, his first movement is not to organize a defence, not to issue a statement, but to fall upon his face. Falling is not weakness; it is the transfer of the case. The defendant is nominally Moses, but the matter in dispute is the LORD's choosing; had he defended himself on the spot, the case would have shrunk into a contest for power. He falls, and the case remains in God's court. What he then says is only two things: an appointment — even to morrow; and a trial — take you censers. A night's time, a public proof, and not one word of personal attack. In a leader under attack the first reaction tells everything: the man in haste to clear his own name litigates a smaller and smaller case; the man who falls first, appoints, and sets a public proof leaves the judgement with the one who shews. Even Moses' wrath is kept for v. 15, and is spoken to the LORD, not vented on the crowd — reverse that order, and you have our common mistake.

(Summary) The words of this section, as philological statements only. Two words for the congregation alternate in the chapter: ʿēdâ (H5712), the constituted congregation or company — Korah's "company" is called by this word — and qāhāl (H6951), the assembled whole; "the congregation of the Lord" at the end of v. 3 uses the latter. "Princes" is nāśîʾ (H5387). "Rose up" is qûm (H6965); "gathered themselves together" is the causative of qāhal (H6950), from the same root as qāhāl. "Holy" is qādôš (H6918). "Censer" is maḥtâ (H4289), properly a fire-pan or vessel for coals. "Fell upon his face" is nāpal (H5307), literally "fell, lay prostrate"; the Chinese renders it 俯伏在地 ("fell prostrate on the ground"). "Chosen" is bāḥar (H977); "to come near" is qārab (H7126), in v. 5 twice in the causative: "will cause him to come near." "Separated" is the causative of bādal (H914). "Tabernacle" is miškān (H4908), "to minister" is šārēṯ (H8334), "the priesthood" is kəhunnâ (H3550), "ye murmur" is lûn (H3885). All numbers were verified one by one before writing; the register is in Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The composition of the company has long been read two ways; both are set out, neither adjudicated. Read as the chapter now stands, Korah the Levite, Dathan, Abiram and On the Reubenites, and the two hundred and fifty princes form one company, and the charge shifts with its addressee — to the Levites, the priesthood; to the whole company, the exalted place of Moses and Aaron. A second, scholarly opinion holds that the chapter as we have it weaves together originally distinct traditions of rebellion — one of Levites claiming the priesthood, one of Reubenites claiming leadership — and points to such features as the disappearance of On and the differing accounts of the households' ends as material for discussion. Which view is taken belongs to the discussion layer. This sermon registers only the facts of the text as it stands: four names in v. 1, two hundred and fifty princes in v. 2, and addresses spoken to distinct hearers; the sermon reads the chapter in that arrangement, and does not take it apart before preaching it.

### 2. "We Will Not Come Up": Refusal, Wrath, and Intercession at the Door (Numbers 16:12-22) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 12 opens a second front: Moses sent to call Dathan and Abiram. The trial was appointed for Korah, the rebuke spoken to the Levites; these two are of neither company, and Moses summons them separately — giving them the chance to speak face to face. The answer is one sentence: "We will not come up:" (16:12). The sentence occurs twice in the chapter (16:12, 14), once before their long charge and once after it, like two nails pinning the speech between them: the verdict is fixed before the words are heard — we will not come up. Their charge in v. 13, read in layers from the base text: they describe the exodus as "thou hast brought us up out of a land that floweth with milk and honey, to kill us in the wilderness" (16:13). The phrase "a land that floweth with milk and honey" occurs twice in the chapter (16:13, 14): in v. 13 it names Egypt; in v. 14, the land not yet entered — "Moreover thou hast not brought us into a land that floweth with milk and honey, or given us inheritance of fields and vineyards: wilt thou put out the eyes of these men? we will not come up." (16:14). The same phrase, set once on Egypt's head and once on the promise's: the land of bondage is called flowing with milk and honey, and Moses is blamed for not yet bringing them into a land flowing with milk and honey. The words are unchanged; the valuation has been turned upside down. Register also the direction word: the exodus verb in this chapter is "brought us up" (16:13), the standing idiom of the exodus narrative (cross-reference layer) — up out of Egypt was "up"; yet to Moses, who calls them, they say "we will not come up." The irony of direction is set out by the text itself and needs no help from the preacher. The two charges of v. 14 are each half of a fact — they had indeed not yet been brought in, nor given the inheritance; the conclusion of deceit is, again, their own addition.

(Fact) Verse 15 records Moses' wrath, quoted verbatim: "And Moses was very wroth, and said unto the Lord, Respect not thou their offering: I have not taken one ass from them, neither have I hurt one of them." (16:15). Three items, read one by one. First, the wrath and its addressee are kept apart: he was very wroth, but what he said, he said unto the LORD — not in an outburst at Dathan and Abiram. Second, the petition is a single thing: "Respect not thou their offering" — the offering is the presentation they are about to bring (for the word, see the Summary below); he asks God not to accept this company's offering, and leaves the judgement itself with God. Third, his own account has exactly two entries: not one ass taken, not one man hurt. The men who charge him with making himself altogether a prince over them can produce no instance of goods seized or persons harmed, and his self-vindication stops at those two entries — no catalogue of his merits, no inventory of his labours. Wrath, here, never becomes an instrument of revenge: he does not ask for punishment to fall; he asks only that the offering not be respected. Punishment, when it comes (16:20-21, 44-45), is initiated by God himself, not drawn down by Moses' prayer.

(Fact) In vv. 16-17 Moses fixes tomorrow's assembly, quoted verbatim across the two verses: "And Moses said unto Korah, Be thou and all thy company before the Lord, thou, and they, and Aaron, to morrow: And take every man his censer, and put incense in them, and bring ye before the Lord every man his censer, two hundred and fifty censers; thou also, and Aaron, each of you his censer." (16:16-17). The count of the censers must be registered exactly as the base text gives it, without harmonizing: v. 2 counts two hundred and fifty princes; v. 17 counts "two hundred and fifty censers," and immediately adds Korah and Aaron, "each of you his censer." Read by the letter, the censers at the door are the two hundred and fifty, with Korah's and Aaron's besides; the text nowhere gives a merged total, and this sermon sets the numbers side by side without adding them up (counting convention in Endnote 4). Verse 18 records the execution: "And they took every man his censer, and put fire in them, and laid incense thereon, and stood in the door of the tabernacle of the congregation with Moses and Aaron." (16:18). Verse 19 records the peak of the movement, quoted verbatim: "And Korah gathered all the congregation against them unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation: and the glory of the Lord appeared unto all the congregation." (16:19). Korah gathered the whole congregation as witnesses and as weight; the court of the trial is thus filled with onlookers — and for that very reason the shewing that follows must be made before all the congregation. The appearing of the glory occurs twice in the chapter (16:19, 42), both times when the congregation is gathered against: men assembled to the full, the glory appears; judgement and intercession alike unfold under that glory.

(Fact) Verses 20-22 give the first word of consuming and the first intercession. God's word in v. 21, quoted verbatim: "Separate yourselves from among this congregation, that I may consume them in a moment." (16:21). The sentence returns almost word for word in v. 45 (two occurrences; see Section 5), and between the two occurrences stand the opening earth and the brasen plates: after the first sentence, judgement falls on the three households and the two hundred and fifty only, and the congregation is separated out and preserved. How that is possible, v. 22 answers — Moses and Aaron fall upon their faces, and their words are quoted verbatim: "O God, the God of the spirits of all flesh, shall one man sin, and wilt thou be wroth with all the congregation?" (16:22). The argument of the intercession is a single question: one man sinning, and wrath upon all the congregation, do not match. The address — "the God of the spirits of all flesh" — is the question's foundation: every spirit of all flesh is in his hand; he can count exactly who has sinned and who has not, and needs no indiscriminate reckoning of the whole congregation as one sum. Read "one man" against the announcement of v. 30 and the scope of the swallowing in v. 32 (fact-layer cross-reference within the chapter): the intercession does not ask God not to judge; it asks that judgement distinguish — that sin be reckoned to the sinning, and those who took no part not be consumed together with them. The command of v. 24 falls exactly along the line of that question — "Speak unto the congregation, saying, Get you up from about the tabernacle of Korah, Dathan, and Abiram" (16:24): the range of judgement is marked out, the congregation departs first, and only then does the earth open.

(Application) This section sets a mirror before the hearer, in the words of Dathan and Abiram. To call Egypt a land flowing with milk and honey is the wilderness's characteristic falsifying of memory: the place of suffering, after a stretch of road and a few missed meals, is remembered as sweetness, and the leader who has not yet brought the people into the land is renamed a deceiver. Their words contain facts — they had not arrived, they had not received — but the facts are set inside an inverted valuation: bondage become sweetness, delay become fraud. Anyone who longs for "the old days" in a low season must pass this examination: was what I remember really good, or only less hard than today? Calling Egypt flowing with milk and honey does not shorten the wilderness; it stops the feet — these men refuse even to be called into a face-to-face hearing, and say "we will not come up" twice. Refusal of conversation is this company's mortal sickness: Korah at least stands before the trial; Dathan and Abiram will not even meet. A man who will not appear, will not hear the proof, and will only shout from his own tent door, can only stand at his own tent door when judgement comes (16:27).

(Application) The second lesson lies between Moses' wrath and Moses' intercession. Moses is not a man without wrath: "Moses was very wroth" is what the text plainly records. But his wrath keeps two rules. First, it is spoken to God, not acted out on men — the anger is laid before God, not converted into treatment of the offenders. Second, in wrath he still keeps exact accounts — one ass, one man, two entries, no enlargement into character and motive. And more than that: in v. 22 the very man publicly accused by the congregation's princes and vilely refused by Dathan's party turns and falls on his face for that whole congregation, asking God, "shall one man sin, and wilt thou be wroth with all the congregation?" Intercession is not the absence of a view of people; it is the refusal to let one's own wrath decide other men's lives and deaths. For anyone wronged today — in church, at home — the hard lesson is here: you may be very wroth; you must still fall down and intercede. Lay the anger before God, state the accounts exactly as they are, and then stand between God and the very people who have just attacked you. This is not softening — the judgement below falls all the same, and falls where it should. Intercession never bargains for the abolition of judgement; it contends that judgement distinguish, and that grace be preserved.

(Summary) The words of this section, as philological statements only. In "that floweth with milk and honey," "floweth" is zûb (H2100), "milk" is ḥālāb (H2461), "honey" is dəbaš (H1706); the phrase is the exodus narrative's fixed description of the promised land, here reversed onto Egypt. "Fields" is śādeh (H7704), "vineyards" is kerem (H3754), "inheritance" is naḥălâ (H5159). "Offering" in v. 15 is minḥâ (H4503), here the presentation they were about to bring. "Gathered" in v. 19 is from the same root qāhal (H6950) as the "gathered themselves together" of v. 3. "Spirit" in v. 22 is rûaḥ, recorded by form only; "of all flesh" renders the phrase "of the spirits of all flesh," where "flesh" is bāśār, likewise recorded by form only — the numbers of these two words were not individually verified before writing, and they are recorded by form, expressly stated as such. All other numbers above were verified one by one; see Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) That Dathan and Abiram call Egypt a land flowing with milk and honey is widely read by commentators as deliberate reversal: the phrase describes the promised land in Exodus 3:8 and elsewhere (summary-layer cross-reference), and here, from the rebels' mouths, is set upon Egypt — most read it as irony or embittered exaggeration, a few as the sincere expression of their valuation; which it is belongs to the discussion layer. This sermon registers only the textual facts: the phrase occurs twice, once of Egypt and once of the land, and the speakers are Dathan's party. Again, the question of v. 22 resembles in form and argument Abraham's intercession for Sodom in Genesis 18:25 — "shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (summary-layer cross-reference): whether the two constitute a deliberate echo is a discussion-layer question. This sermon sets them side by side and adjudicates no dependence.

### 3. "If the LORD Make a New Thing": Departure from the Tents, the Opening Earth, and the Fire (Numbers 16:23-35) (24:00-42:00)

(Fact) From v. 23 the word of God is spoken to Moses alone, and its content is quoted verbatim: "Speak unto the congregation, saying, Get you up from about the tabernacle of Korah, Dathan, and Abiram." (16:24). Note that the three names stand together in the command: Korah, Dathan, and Abiram. The trial was held at the door of the tabernacle, but the first line of judgement is drawn around the tabernacles of the three households; Korah's tabernacle is named in the same sentence as Dathan's and Abiram's. Verse 25 records Moses' execution: "And Moses rose up and went unto Dathan and Abiram; and the elders of Israel followed him." (16:25). To the men who had refused to come to him, Moses goes himself; the elders follow, as witnesses of this last announcement. Verse 26 is the warning, quoted in its latter half verbatim: "and touch nothing of theirs, lest ye be consumed in all their sins." (16:26). The warning has two parts — depart from the tents, touch nothing of theirs — and the reason is stated: the danger is not contamination by things but belonging: "lest ye be consumed in all their sins." Whoever handles their goods has drawn his own position inside their range. Verse 27 records the two standings, quoted verbatim: "So they gat up from the tabernacle of Korah, Dathan, and Abiram, on every side: and Dathan and Abiram came out, and stood in the door of their tents, and their wives, and their sons, and their little children." (16:27). The congregation withdraws; the men of the two households come out and stand — wives, sons, little children, all at the tent doors. The narrative writes this standing very quietly: no shouting, no action, only standing. This standing faces, across the chapter, Aaron's standing in v. 48 (fact-layer cross-reference within the chapter): on one side, standing at one's own tent door awaiting judgement; on the other, standing between the dead and the living staying the plague. From beginning to end this chapter is about positions, and the place of one's standing decides its outcome.

(Fact) Verses 28-30 are Moses' announcement of the sign beforehand — the key to the judgement that follows — and must be read layer by layer from the base text. Verse 28 states the theme: "Hereby ye shall know that the Lord hath sent me to do all these works; for I have not done them of mine own mind." (16:28). And the announcement carries its own test clause: "hereby ye shall know." Verse 29 is the negative arm of the sign, quoted verbatim: "If these men die the common death of all men, or if they be visited after the visitation of all men; then the Lord hath not sent me." (16:29). If they die the common death — an ordinary death, an ordinary visitation — then Moses is proved not sent; he stakes his own commission on the negative arm first. Verse 30 is the positive arm, quoted verbatim: "But if the Lord make a new thing, and the earth open her mouth, and swallow them up, with all that appertain unto them, and they go down quick into the pit; then ye shall understand that these men have provoked the Lord." (16:30). Four items, counted one by one. First, the subject is the LORD: "if the Lord make a new thing" — a thing never before made, made as a sign. Second, the manner: the earth opens her mouth and swallows them up. Third, the manner of death: "they go down quick into the pit" — down alive, not dead and buried. Fourth, the conclusion: "then ye shall understand that these men have provoked the Lord" — the verdict the judgement is to shew is stated in advance: their sin is not a difference of policy; it is provocation of the LORD. The full shape of the sign's logic is this: the conditions are published first (a common death, and Moses is false; the new thing accomplished, and they have provoked the LORD), the fulfilment follows on the spot, and no room is left between for the preacher to manoeuvre. This chapter's judgement is no sentence first and reasons after, no outburst of temper; it is a shewing executed step by step in the open — appointment at v. 5, procedure at vv. 6-7, conditions at vv. 28-30.

(Fact) The fulfilment begins at v. 31, quoted verbatim across two verses: "And it came to pass, as he had made an end of speaking all these words, that the ground clave asunder that was under them: And the earth opened her mouth, and swallowed them up, and their houses, and all the men that appertained unto Korah, and all their goods." (16:31-32). The exactness of the timing is written into "as he had made an end of speaking": the words scarcely finished, the ground clave asunder. The scope of the swallowing is registered item by item from v. 32: the men themselves, their houses, all the men that appertained unto Korah, and all their goods. In the KJV "their houses" names the households; the Chinese base text reads 他们的家眷, "their families" — the two base texts' wording differs at this point in the breadth of the term, and each manuscript follows its own (registered in Endnote 3). The item "all the men that appertained unto Korah" must be left exactly as it stands, neither enlarged nor reduced: the text says that the men and goods appertaining to Korah were swallowed; it nowhere states in so many words where Korah himself was, or by what manner of death he died (for the tension with Num 26:11, see the discussion paragraph below and Endnote 8). Verse 33 records the result, quoted verbatim: "They, and all that appertained to them, went down alive into the pit, and the earth closed upon them: and they perished from among the congregation." (16:33). "Went down alive into the pit" answers v. 30 word for word; and "the earth closed upon them" is the narrative's cold composure — the opening was the new thing; once closed, the earth is earth again, and the congregation is short of these households. Verse 34 records the onlookers: all Israel round about fled at the cry of them, "for they said, Lest the earth swallow us up also" (16:34). Verse 35 is the second stroke of judgement, quoted verbatim: "And there came out a fire from the Lord, and consumed the two hundred and fifty men that offered incense." (16:35). The two strokes keep their objects distinct: the earth swallows the persons and goods of the three households' tents; the fire consumes the two hundred and fifty men who offered incense at the door — the answer to the trial of the censers is here: fire came out from the LORD, and it consumed the very men who bore the censers. The two hundred and fifty of v. 2, the two hundred and fifty censers of v. 17, and the two hundred and fifty men of v. 35 meet at this verse: the counts of men and of consumed men agree; the count of censers carries Korah's and Aaron's besides, registered side by side (see Section 2), and this sermon does not merge or recalculate them.

(Application) The first lesson of this section is the courage and honesty of Moses' published sign. He dares to state the conditions beforehand: if nothing extraordinary happens, I am false; if the new thing is done, they have provoked the LORD. This is the precise opposite of all vague spiritual rhetoric — vague words can never be falsified, and can never be verified either. Moses' sign bears examination on the spot, because his ground is not his own feeling but the one who sent him. Whoever bears witness or carries a message today should learn this verifiable honesty: state the conditions plainly, stake the conclusion, and do not use "God moved me" as an inspection-free badge. The hearers have their lesson too: the conditions of vv. 28-30 were spoken openly to the whole congregation, and having heard them, a man could still choose to depart from the tents or to stand in the doorway. A sign, however plainly set out, cannot move another man's feet for him: what saved men when judgement fell was not having heard the sign, but the feet that moved after hearing it. The congregation departed; the households stood; the outcome divides exactly there.

(Application) The second lesson is in the "touch nothing." Moses warns the congregation: touch nothing of theirs, lest ye be consumed in all their sins. Sin has a contagious shape, and it commonly begins with taking a small advantage: that household's goods are forfeit anyway — what is one piece? That company's words have a point anyway — what is one echo? This chapter treats belonging with the utmost seriousness: position and possessions are both declarations — where you stand, and whose goods are in your hand, are the drawing of yourself into a range. Men today rarely choose sides at a tent door, but they choose positions daily in approvals, forwards and alignments: to keep a distance from evil is not squeamishness; it is self-preservation. The command "depart" occurs repeatedly in this chapter — God speaks it to Moses and Aaron, the congregation performs it toward the three households — and departure, at times, is not cowardice but obedience; the nearness that refuses to depart ends, often, as a nearness consumed together.

(Summary) The words of this section, as philological statements only. The "new thing" of v. 30 is a noun meaning a created thing, a new creation, from the same root (bārāʾ) as its verb "make/create"; neither word's number was individually verified before writing, and they are recorded by form only, expressly stated as such. The verb of the earth "opening" her mouth means to open, to gape; "swallow up" is bālaʿ; "the pit" renders šəʾôl — all three recorded by form only. "Houses" in v. 32 renders the Hebrew word for house/household, where the Chinese base text reads 家眷, "families"; the two renderings differ in breadth, and each manuscript follows its own base text (Endnote 3). "Sign" and "memorial" are treated together in Section 4's Summary. No count or quotation in this section's fact layer depends on the unverified words above; verified numbers are registered in Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two discussions attach to this section; both are set out, neither adjudicated. First, the nature of the opening earth: geological readings (faulting, collapse, and the like as natural-event accounts) and literary readings ("the mouth of the earth" as personifying narrative language, the "new thing" as theological declaration rather than geological report) stand side by side, and the earth-swallowing motif in ancient Near Eastern literature is also adduced for comparison. The chapter's own text establishes only three things: the announcement beforehand, the fulfilment immediately upon it, and the scope limited to the three households; this sermon's main text stands on those three, and the mechanism is left to the discussion layer. Second, the end of Korah and the differing accounts of the two households: v. 32 states expressly that the swallowing included "all the men that appertained unto Korah," while Numbers 26:11 states expressly, "Notwithstanding the children of Korah died not" (cross-reference, summary layer). The tension between the two — whether Korah himself died by the earth or by the fire, and how his children survived — has been read in several ways: some distribute Korah between the two strokes, some understand "the men that appertained" as excluding sons who were not present; the readings are set out together here, and this sermon adjudicates none of them. Deuteronomy 11:6, recalling the event, names Dathan and Abiram only, and not Korah (summary-layer cross-reference); that stroke too is registered, to be read with ch. 26 — the tension stands plain, and the sermon does not smooth it away.

### 4. Broad Plates for a Covering of the Altar: The Brasen Censers as a Sign (Numbers 16:36-40) (42:00-56:00)

(Fact) The first command after the judgement is not about the dead; it is about the censers. In v. 36 the LORD speaks unto Moses, and v. 37 is quoted verbatim: "Speak unto Eleazar the son of Aaron the priest, that he take up the censers out of the burning, and scatter thou the fire yonder; for they are hallowed." (16:37). The executor is Eleazar, the son of Aaron, the priest — the clearing of the trial's remains belongs to the priestly house. Two things come out of the same fire with opposite dispositions: the fire is to be scattered yonder; the censers are to be taken up. The reason is one clause: "for they are hallowed." Verse 38 explains both the hallowing and the destination, quoted in two sentences verbatim: "The censers of these sinners against their own souls, let them make them broad plates for a covering of the altar: for they offered them before the Lord, therefore they are hallowed: and they shall be a sign unto the children of Israel." (16:38). The censers' record is stated plainly: their owners are "these sinners against their own souls" — the verdict-word is self-destruction; they died not by accident but at the cost of their own souls — yet the vessels, because they were offered before the LORD, are set apart, beaten into broad plates, and laid as a covering of the altar, a sign unto the children of Israel. The vessels' shape is changed for good: a censer stands upright to hold fire; a plate lies flat to cover an altar. The same brass, from an instrument of office seized, becomes a sign of office guarded.

(Fact) Verse 39 records the execution, quoted verbatim: "And Eleazar the priest took the brasen censers, wherewith they that were burnt had offered; and they were made broad plates for a covering of the altar:" (16:39). "Brasen" names the material here for the first time — before, they were simply censers; the metal is registered only as the remains are cleared: the censers of the burned were brass. Brass endures fire: the fire consumed the men; the censers remained. Verse 40 is the section's conclusion, quoted verbatim in full: "To be a memorial unto the children of Israel, that no stranger, which is not of the seed of Aaron, come near to offer incense before the Lord; that he be not as Korah, and as his company: as the Lord said to him by the hand of Moses." (16:40). Three items, read one by one. First, the sign's purpose-word is "memorial": in v. 38 they are "a sign"; in v. 40, "a memorial" — one object, two names: a sign to everyone who comes after, a memorial to all Israel. Second, the content of the boundary: no stranger, not of the seed of Aaron, shall come near to offer incense — the principle of v. 5, that the chosen man is caused to come near, is here cast into a written rule with an uncrossable line around the burning of incense. Third, the form of the warning: "that he be not as Korah, and as his company" — Korah's name stays on the altar not in a roll of honour but in a register of warning; every man who afterwards approaches the altar and sees the altar's covering reads this chapter over again. A special registration belongs to this section on the matter of chapter division: from v. 36 to v. 50, this passage is 17:1-15 in the Hebrew canon (see the head notes and Endnote 1) — verse references in preaching follow the Chinese and English numbering throughout, and anyone consulting the Hebrew canon or versions that follow it must convert, and must not mix the two numberings.

(Application) This section gives today's congregation a lesson in how to handle the remains of a failure. God did not command the censers to be destroyed, buried, or thrown into the wilderness and done with; he commanded that the vessels of sinners be beaten into plates and laid on the altar that all Israel saw every day. A memorial's purpose is not to shame the dead but to protect the living: a boundary written only in a book will be forgotten; a boundary cast upon the altar is read again at every offering. Churches and families handle past failures at two extremes: destroy every trace and pretend nothing happened — and the next generation falls in the same place; or keep the failure as a curiosity, told and retold until nothing is left but the telling. This chapter gives a third way: make the lesson into a sign, and set it at the very centre of worship — neither avoided nor consumed, only remembered: men crossed this line; the cost is here; let those who come after not cross it. And there is comfort in the altar's brass: even the vessels of sinners can, in God's hand, be made a sign that guards the boundary. A man's failure need not be mere refuse — provided it has been beaten, and its shape changed, and it no longer struts about in a censer's form.

(Application) The second lesson is for everyone who comes near to God "near enough." The boundary of v. 40 is drawn without softening: not of the seed of Aaron — not even to come near to offer incense. This is not God begrudging nearness: v. 5 has said it plainly — him whom he hath chosen will he cause to come near unto him. Nearness is grace; and precisely because nearness is grace, nearness cannot be self-taken. The root fault of Korah's company was to treat grace as entitlement: God had caused the Levites to come near in service, and they reached out their hands for the priesthood; grace, received, is a gift — grace, grasped at, becomes the charge. Everyone in service should ask himself today: is the place where I stand a place I was caused to come to, or a place I squeezed myself into? The man who was brought there stands at rest, and can give account even in withdrawing; the man who squeezed in must guard daily against other men and against the shewing, and stands like a startled bird. The boundary is not a wall to block nearness; it is the rail that protects it — the rail standing, those who come near are safe.

(Summary) The words of this section, as philological statements only. "Sign" is ʾôṯ, recorded by form only; "memorial" is zikkārôn, recorded by form only; "seed" is zeraʿ, recorded by form only — these three words' numbers were not individually verified before writing, and they are recorded by form, expressly stated as such. "Hallowed / holy" at vv. 37-38 is from the same word qādôš (H6918) as the "holy" spoken of men at vv. 3, 5, 7: the vessels are hallowed because they were offered; the men are destroyed for reproach — the contrast of the one word in its two applications is the text's own. In "sinners against their own souls," the word rendered "souls" is of the nepeš family; it is stated here as a summary-level observation only, without a number (the number was not individually verified). For the beating into "broad plates," this sermon records the base text's words only and makes no claim about the craft.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The relation of this brass covering to the altar's form belongs to this layer alone, as material for reference: Exodus 27:2 and 38:2 record the altar itself with a brass covering and horns (summary-layer cross-reference); whether this chapter's plates were laid over an existing brass altar or themselves constitute the covering is read differently by different commentators, and the covering's survival through later sanctuaries has no express textual record — all this belongs to the historical discussion layer. This sermon stands only on what the chapter itself makes plain: the brass came from the censers of the burned, was made broad plates, was used for a covering of the altar, and its purpose was memorial and boundary — three facts, all in the base text. Again, the number of the censers is often raised in the discussion layer: v. 17's "two hundred and fifty censers," read together with Korah's and Aaron's one each, yields what total, and v. 39 does not restate the number of censers beaten — the text gives no merged total, and this sermon adopts none of the discussion layer's additions, setting the three passages side by side exactly as written (see Endnote 4).

### 5. Between the Dead and the Living: The Next Day's Murmuring and the Stayed Plague (Numbers 16:41-50) (58:00-60:00)

(Fact) The time word of v. 41 is "on the morrow" — the morrow after the earth opened and the fire consumed. Quoted verbatim: "But on the morrow all the congregation of the children of Israel murmured against Moses and against Aaron, saying, Ye have killed the people of the Lord." (16:41). The shape of the charge must be looked at exactly: the men who died by the opening earth and the fire are renamed "the people of the LORD"; Moses and Aaron, who executed the shewing, are renamed their killers. The sign had been fulfilled on the spot only yesterday (16:28-33); the congregation did not go back and examine the sign — they reversed the conclusion: not "those men provoked the LORD," but "ye have killed the people of the LORD." Verse 42 records the confrontation, quoted verbatim: "And it came to pass, when the congregation was gathered against Moses and against Aaron, that they looked toward the tabernacle of the congregation: and, behold, the cloud covered it, and the glory of the Lord appeared." (16:42). "Gathered against" is the same stroke as vv. 3 and 19: this is the chapter's third gathering against (16:3, 19, 42; count in Endnote 4). Verse 43 has a single sentence: "And Moses and Aaron came before the tabernacle of the congregation." (16:43). The two men under attack do not withdraw to their own tents; they move toward the tabernacle — toward the God who is about to speak.

(Fact) In vv. 44-45 the second word of consuming arrives. God's words are quoted verbatim: "Get you up from among this congregation, that I may consume them as in a moment. And they fell upon their faces." (16:45). The sentence is nearly word for word the sentence of v. 21 (two occurrences) — the same declaration, once before the judgement and once after the sign: yesterday they had seen the earth open with their own eyes and touched the altar's brass with their own hands; today they murmur again, and the declaration comes again. This time there is no intercessory question like v. 22's; the base text records only: "And they fell upon their faces" (16:45). Then the situation turns sharply: Moses' words in v. 46 are no longer a waiting for instruction but a race against time, quoted verbatim: "Take a censer, and put fire therein from off the altar, and put on incense, and go quickly unto the congregation, and make an atonement for them: for there is wrath gone out from the Lord; the plague is begun." (16:46). Five items, counted one by one. First, the instrument is again a censer — the vessel that consumed two hundred and fifty men yesterday is today the vessel of rescue. Second, the fire is from off the altar — not a fire of his own providing, but the altar's fire. Third, incense is put on. Fourth, the speed is "quickly" — the chapter's one word of haste: intercession tolerates no delay, because "the plague is begun." Fifth, the purpose is "make an atonement for them" — and "them" is the whole congregation that has just said, "Ye have killed the people of the LORD." Verse 47 records Aaron's execution, quoted verbatim: "And Aaron took as Moses commanded, and ran into the midst of the congregation; and, behold, the plague was begun among the people: and he put on incense, and made an atonement for the people." (16:47). Read the word "ran" exactly as it stands: the high priest runs through the camp, into the midst of a congregation among whom the plague is already begun. "Made an atonement" occurs twice in this passage (16:46, 47), once in Moses' command and once in Aaron's performance, with no discount between the command and the performance.

(Fact) Verse 48 is the chapter's climax and the title's source, quoted verbatim: "And he stood between the dead and the living; and the plague was stayed." (16:48). The sentence falls in two halves: the first is position — between the dead and the living, the already fallen on one side, the not yet fallen on the other, Aaron standing on the dividing line; the second is result — the plague was stayed. The text records nothing that he said and no further thing that he did; it records only the place where he stood and the staying of the plague: the narrative's evidence for the staying is this one standing place. Verse 49 gives the number, quoted verbatim: "Now they that died in the plague were fourteen thousand and seven hundred, beside them that died about the matter of Korah." (16:49). The number is transcribed as the base text gives it: fourteen thousand and seven hundred — and the text itself states "beside them that died about the matter of Korah": the plague's dead do not include those who died earlier by the earth and the fire, and the two numbers are not to be merged or recalculated; this sermon sets them side by side. Verse 50 closes the chapter, quoted verbatim: "And Aaron returned unto Moses unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation: and the plague was stayed." (16:50). "The plague was stayed" occurs twice at the chapter's close (16:48, "was stayed"; 16:50, "was stayed" again): once when he stood, and once when he returned. And Aaron returns to the place he set out from — the door of the tabernacle, the very place where the censers had gathered and the glory appeared yesterday. The chapter's narrative arc closes here: from a company rising up together in attack to one man standing and staying a plague; from two hundred and fifty censers bearing fire in offering to one censer bearing fire in atonement.

(Application) This section brings a heavy stroke down first, on v. 41's "on the morrow." The earth had scarcely closed its mouth, the brass had scarcely covered the altar, and by the next day the congregation can say, "Ye have killed the people of the LORD." A plainly shewn sign cannot stop an obstinate heart: men did see — they saw, and still explained it after their own narrative, renaming judgement as murder and the men who provoked the LORD as the people of the LORD. Men today are equally skilled at rewriting yesterday's lesson on the morrow: yesterday they trembled before the sign; today one emotional sentence overturns the whole boundary. The sermon does not fret on God's behalf here; it only reminds, exactly: a memorial is prepared for those willing to remember. The altar's brass can stop a later comer who is willing to look; it cannot stop a man bent on reversing the verdict the next day. And God's answer to the man bent on reversal is not another round of debate; it is another falling on the face and another atonement — Moses and Aaron did not say to the congregation, "You are beyond saving"; they fell down, took the censer, and ran. To make atonement for a people who reverse the verdict the next day is this chapter's heaviest lesson for those who lead: your work's fruit will often be overturned on the morrow, and your answer is still intercession, not a turned face.

(Application) The second lesson is the title itself: standing between the dead and the living. Aaron did not watch the plague from a safe distance, and he did not throw himself pointlessly among the dead; he stood on the dividing line, and the plague went no further. The intercessor's place is always so: one foot in the reality of judgement, one foot in the possibility of grace — neither denying that death has come, nor accepting that death must overrun everyone. Whoever intercedes today — in a family, a church, among friends — should learn this standing place: not on the living's side with cold commentary, not on the dead's side with despairing words, but in between, with fire from off the altar — that is, with the atonement God himself has provided — pleading for those beside him. "Stood" is the chapter's last verb of posture: the princes rose up against, the congregation gathered against, Dathan stood in his doorway, the Levites stood before the congregation to minister, and at last Aaron stood between the dead and the living. Everyone in this chapter is standing somewhere; the place of the standing decides whether a man is destroyed, serves, or stays a plague. The question to ask ourselves is not whether we are standing, but where.

(Summary) The words of this section, as philological statements only. "Wrath" is qeṣep (H7110). "Plague" is rendered by two different words in this passage: in Moses' words at v. 46 and in the narrative at v. 47 it is negep (H5063), a stroke or plague; in the narrative at vv. 48-50 it is maggēpâ (H4046), a slaughtering plague — one disaster, one word in the command and the opening narrative and another in the narrative that follows, and this sermon registers the distribution of the two words as it stands. "Was stayed" is ʿāṣar (H6113), properly to restrain, to stop. "Make an atonement / made an atonement" is kāpar (H3722), the same word as the atonement formula of chapter 15 (cross-reference layer). "Is begun" is the causative of ḥālal (H2490), "has begun." All numbers above were verified one by one; the register is in Endnote 5. "Cloud" and "glory" are recorded in the base text's words only, without numbers.

(New Testament Reading) The shape of this chapter's intercession has a clear line in the New Testament's reading of it, registered at the reading layer only, altering no fact of this chapter. Aaron, censer in hand, standing between the dead and the living and staying the plague — the New Testament speaks of a priest who ever liveth to make intercession (Heb 7:25, in summary), whose intercession is not one run but an everlasting standing. And Moses and Aaron, twice falling on their faces for a congregation that had attacked them, stand in the same shape as the New Testament's teaching to pray for those who persecute you (Matt 5:44, in summary): the object of intercession is first an enemy, then a people. Again, Korah's company is set down in Jude 11 as a warning — "the gainsaying of Core" (in summary): the New Testament's author uses this chapter's event as the standing specimen of rebellion, with the same function as the brass on the altar — a sign is for those who come after to see. This layer registers only how the New Testament reads the chapter; it does not read the completion back into this chapter's facts: the Aaron of this chapter can stay one plague, and cannot stay the next chapter's heart — and that limit too is left standing in the text exactly as it is.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two discussions attach to this passage; both are set out, neither adjudicated. First, the nature of the plague: v. 46 says "the plague is begun," and v. 49's dead number fourteen thousand and seven hundred; of the plague's agent and manner of spread the text records nothing, and any modern medical diagnosis by hindsight is speculation outside the discussion layer, adopted nowhere in this sermon's main text. The main text stands on the four things the text gives: wrath gone out from the LORD, the plague begun, the plague stayed after Aaron's atonement, and the dead at fourteen thousand and seven hundred (not including those who died about the matter of Korah). Second, the sequence of intercession and judgement: the declaration of v. 45 is nearly identical with that of v. 21; after the first declaration come the intercessory question and the command of demarcation, after the second, only the falling on the face and the running — whether the difference is the urgency of the moment abbreviating the question, or the narrative deliberately compressing, is discussed among commentators. This sermon registers only the textual facts: the question stands in v. 22 only, the running in v. 47 only, and both sequences begin with falling on the face and end with the congregation preserved; the similarities and differences are set out exactly, and no second question is supplied to the narrative by the sermon.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based):

“And he stood between the dead and the living; and the plague was stayed” (Num 16:48, KJV). The people had just murmured, “Ye have killed the people of the LORD” (16:41); the man who made atonement for them was the very man they had attacked. First Moses and Aaron fell upon their faces and asked, “shall one man sin, and wilt thou be wroth with all the congregation?” (16:22). Then Aaron took fire from off the altar and incense, and ran into the midst of the congregation where the plague was already begun. He stood with the fallen on one side and the still-living on the other. Read this chapter honestly before thou appliest it: we stand more often among the murmurers, and among those who need another to make atonement for them, than in Aaron's place. Only then comes Aaron's lesson: between wrath and the people, do not step aside to comment; take what God hath given, stand between the dead and the living, and intercede for those beside thee. The plague was stayed because the intercessor stood — and did not run away.

(iv) "if the Lord make a new thing" (16:30): only one clause of the sign announced beforehand, and it does not cover the next day's murmuring and Aaron's atonement. Candidate (i) is therefore the title. The Chinese title is established from the Chinese base text's own words at 16:48, 站在活人死人中间; the word order of the two base texts is reversed (Chinese: the living, the dead; KJV: the dead and the living), and each manuscript follows its own base text; neither title is a literal translation of the other, and this is registered here expressly.
3. Where the assignment's orientation differed from the base text, the base text is recorded as it stands: (i) The orientation named 16:5 "tomorrow morning the LORD will shew" — the Chinese base text reads 到了早晨，耶和华必指示 ("when morning comes, the LORD will shew"), and has no such two-word phrase; the KJV reads "Even to morrow the Lord will shew." (ii) The orientation spoke of the sign of 16:28-30 as the LORD "doing a new thing" — the Chinese reads 创作一件新事 ("create a new thing"), the KJV "make a new thing"; neither reads "perform." (iii) The orientation had the censers gathered at the door at 16:19 — the base text records the standing at the door at v. 18, and at v. 19 Korah's gathering of all the congregation (Chinese 招聚, "summoned and assembled," not 召聚). (iv) The orientation left the plague number's inclusion of the earlier dead to be settled from the base text — v. 49 settles it expressly: "beside them that died about the matter of Korah"; the number expressly excludes them, and the two numbers are not merged in either manuscript. (v) The orientation noted that 16:36-40 is 17:1-5 in the Hebrew canon — verified as correct, with this completion: not vv. 36-40 only, but vv. 41-50 as well continue as 17:6-15 in the Hebrew canon (see Endnote 1). (vi) The orientation named Dathan's party's reversal by the phrase "a land flowing with milk and honey" — verified at both occurrences (16:13, 14), with this precision added in the main text: at v. 13 the phrase names Egypt, which the orientation did not mark. (vii) The orientation's list of the company omitted On — the base text names "On, the son of Peleth" at 16:1, his only occurrence in the chapter, and his blank is registered in Endnote 8. (viii) Small differences between the two base texts, recorded together: at 16:32 the Chinese reads 他们的家眷 ("their families"), the KJV "their houses"; at 16:45 the Chinese reads 俯伏于地, at 16:4 and 22 俯伏在地 ("fell prostrate on the ground" — the preposition differs by one character within the Chinese text itself); and the division between vv. 9 and 10 differs between the base texts (see the head notes). At every point above, each manuscript follows its own base text.
4. Counts, verified item by item: The chapter has 50 verses; the five sections run to 11 (16:1-11), 11 (16:12-22), 13 (16:23-35), 5 (16:36-40) and 10 (16:41-50) verses respectively, 50 in all. "Two hundred and fifty" occurs three times (16:2, of the princes; 16:17, of the censers; 16:35, of the men consumed by fire); and the censer count carries a side-by-side registration: beyond the "two hundred and fifty censers" of v. 17, Korah and Aaron are each recorded with his own censer, and the text gives no merged total, which this sermon therefore does not compute. "Ye take too much upon you" occurs twice (16:3, the charge; 16:7, the rejoinder). "A land that floweth with milk and honey" occurs twice (16:13, of Egypt; 16:14, of the land). "We will not come up" occurs twice (16:12, 14). "But a small thing" occurs twice (KJV 16:9 and 13; Chinese 16:10 and 13 — the verse division differs, see the head notes). The falling upon the face occurs three times (16:4, 22, 45). The word of consuming occurs twice (16:21, 45, in nearly identical wording). The appearing of the glory of the LORD occurs twice (16:19, "appeared unto all the congregation"; 16:42, "appeared"). The gathering against occurs three times (16:3, 19, 42). "Come near" occurs four times (16:5 twice; 16:9; 16:10; the "come near" of 16:40 is a different construction — "come near to offer incense" — and is counted separately). "Holy" spoken of persons occurs three times (16:3, 5, 7); "hallowed" spoken of the censers, twice (16:37, 38). "Make / made an atonement" in the plague passage occurs twice (16:46, the command; 16:47, the performance). "The plague was stayed" occurs twice (16:48; 16:50). The dead in the plague are fourteen thousand and seven hundred (16:49), the text itself stating that this is beside them that died about the matter of Korah. God's speeches are four (16:21, 24, 37-38, 45, with the speaking introductions at 16:20, 23, 36, 44).

Cross-references (each counted individually and marked by layer; eight in all): (i) Leviticus 10:1-7 — Nadab and Abihu, consumed by fire for strange fire; summary layer; read against this chapter's trial of censers and fire. (ii) Numbers 14:13-23 — Moses' intercession for the whole camp, pardon granted and consequence not remitted; summary layer; set beside this chapter's two intercessions (16:22, 45). (iii) Numbers 26:9-11 and 26:10 — the retrospect on Dathan, Abiram and Korah, and "the children of Korah died not"; summary layer; its tension with 16:32 is set out, not adjudicated (see the discussion paragraph of Section 3). (iv) Deuteronomy 11:6 — the retrospect of the earth's opening and swallowing, naming Dathan and Abiram and not Korah; summary layer. (v) Psalm 106:16-18 — the earth opened and swallowed Dathan and covered the company of Abiram, and fire burned the wicked; summary layer; its distribution of the strokes does not wholly match this chapter's, and the difference is registered. (vi) Numbers 17 (in the Chinese and English numbering) — Aaron's rod budding; summary layer; the second shewing of the choosing, immediately following the trial of the censers. (vii) Jude 11 — "the gainsaying of Core" set down as a warning; reading layer. (viii) Hebrews 7:25 — ever living to make intercession; reading layer, in summary only, altering no fact of this chapter.
7. Counting convention for the censers, supplementary note: Endnote 4 counts "two hundred and fifty" by the letter of the base text as three occurrences — v. 2 of men (princes), v. 17 of censers, v. 35 of the men consumed — the numbers being the same and the things counted different; the main text draws no inference that sameness of number makes them in every respect the same set, and registers the three occurrences only. Korah's and Aaron's censers (16:17 end; 46) are registered separately, outside the two hundred and fifty; the convention is identical in both manuscripts.
8. The chapter's blanks, registered: On (16:1) occurs once in the whole chapter, and his end is not recorded in the text; Korah's own manner of death (by the earth or by the fire) has no express statement in this chapter, v. 32 recording only that "all the men that appertained unto Korah" were swallowed; the exact positions of the two households' tents, the names and numbers of the households swallowed, and the plague's agent and manner of spread are none of them recorded in this chapter. Later tradition's supplements on all of these belong to the tradition layer; neither the main text nor these notes adopt them; the blanks themselves are what is registered.
9. Independent-review note (English stage; the Chinese manuscript re-checked independently against the base text at this stage): At the English stage the Chinese manuscript was re-read verse by verse against the base text a second time, with particular attention to function words and near-synonyms, pronoun reference, and graphic variants of the 做/作 type. Results: (a) The orientation's phrase "a new thing performed" was confirmed against the base text as 创作 ("create"), not 施行 ("perform"), and the Chinese title section and §3 were written accordingly from the start; the KJV's "make a new thing" was likewise confirmed, and no correction to the Chinese fact layer was needed on this point. (b) One wording in the Chinese §4 Summary — which had spoken of the "souls" word of 16:38 as being "of the nepeš family" in a form that could be read as a numbered claim — was confirmed as deliberately unnumbered in both manuscripts and is expressly registered as unverified in Endnote 5; no numbered claim stands on it in either manuscript. (c) The verse-division difference between the base texts at 16:9-10 (the "small thing" question in Chinese v. 10, in KJV v. 9) was caught at this stage and registered in both manuscripts' head notes and in Endnote 3, so that neither manuscript cites the question under the other text's verse number. (d) In the final count-check after the English draft, three non-quotation items in the Chinese manuscript were corrected and the corrections carried into both manuscripts where applicable: the count of God's speeches in Endnote 4, first stated as five, was corrected to four (16:21, 24, 37-38, 45); the Chinese §5 wording on the three "gatherings against" was refined to register each verse's own wording (16:3, 19, 42) rather than a single compound phrase; a computed total of "two hundred and fifty-four men" in §1 of both manuscripts was replaced by "some hundreds," the text giving no merged total of leaders and princes; and the Chinese devotional paragraph, first measured at 276 Chinese characters, was expanded with two sentences drawn from 16:46-47 to within the prescribed 300-500 range (final measurement in the delivery record). (e) All other verse quotations in the Chinese manuscript — including 招聚 (not 召聚) at 16:19, 捡起 at 16:37, 创作 at 16:30, the semicolon inside 16:33, and the absence of any "and" within 活人死人 at 16:48 — were confirmed against the base text as quoted. No other discrepancy was found.

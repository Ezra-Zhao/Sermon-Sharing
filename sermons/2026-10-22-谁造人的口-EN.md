## Summary (about 100 words)

Moses asked a third time: they will not believe me. God gave three signs as credentials — the staff turned into a serpent, the hand made leprous and restored, and water turned to blood. Moses still pleaded that he was slow of speech, and God answered: Who made man's mouth? Is it not I, the LORD? In the end he begged God to send someone else; God was angry, yet still prepared Aaron to be his mouth. On the night journey home, Zipporah resolved the danger by circumcision; the brothers met on the mountain, the elders believed, and the people bowed their heads and worshipped. At the end of excuses, what Moses met was everything God had already prepared.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter we stopped at the flame of the bush. Moses took off his shoes, stood on holy ground, heard the name of God, and received the mission: go, and bring My people out of Egypt. He asked, who am I, and God answered, I will be with you; he asked, what is Thy name, and God answered, I AM THAT I AM. Both questions were answered, and by rights it was time to set out. But as this chapter opens, Moses speaks again, and this question cuts closer to every one of us than the first two.

Hear what he says: "But, behold, they will not believe me, nor hearken unto my voice: for they will say, The LORD hath not appeared unto thee." (Exodus 4:1, KJV)

In this sentence there is a voice all of us know well. It is not unbelief in God; it is fear that people will not believe us. It is not doubting that God exists; it is doubting that, when we stand before people, we will be taken seriously. Moses was eighty years old and had kept sheep in Midian for forty years. What Egypt remembered of him was a prince who had killed a man and fled; what Israel remembered of him was probably nothing at all. A man gone for forty years suddenly returns and says, God appeared to me, and I have come to lead you out of Egypt — why should anyone believe you? Moses' fear was not groundless. What he feared was standing empty-handed before people, with not one sentence that could convince them.

Today we will walk through this whole chapter together: how God dismantled Moses' excuses one by one, and how, after the excuses were exhausted, He still did not discard him, but placed Aaron, his wife, and a whole household into this mission. The peak sentence of the chapter is only one, hidden in God's answer to Moses: "Who hath made man's mouth? or who maketh the dumb, or deaf, or the seeing, or the blind? have not I the LORD?" (Exodus 4:11, KJV) This sentence is spoken to everyone who thinks he is not good enough.

Let us read several key passages:

"And the LORD said unto him, What is that in thine hand? And he said, A rod. And he said, Cast it on the ground. And he cast it on the ground, and it became a serpent; and Moses fled from before it. And the LORD said unto Moses, Put forth thine hand, and take it by the tail. And he put forth his hand, and caught it, and it became a rod in his hand." (Exodus 4:2-4, excerpt, KJV)

"Now therefore go, and I will be with thy mouth, and teach thee what thou shalt say." (Exodus 4:12, excerpt, KJV)

"And he said, O my Lord, send, I pray thee, by the hand of him whom thou wilt send." (Exodus 4:13, KJV)

"And the people believed: and when they heard that the LORD had visited the children of Israel, and that he had looked upon their affliction, then they bowed their heads and worshipped." (Exodus 4:31, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, every one of us keeps a list of excuses in his heart: I can't, I can't speak, I have no credentials, my past is stained. Speak to us today, item by item, through this chapter of Moses. Give credentials to the one who fears people will not believe; let the one who fears his own clumsy mouth hear "Who made man's mouth"; and let the one who has said "send someone else" know that You are angry yet You do not discard, that You persist to the end and provide to the end. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: They Will Not Believe Me — Three Signs, Credentials and Not a Performance (4:1-9)

First, look carefully at this question of Moses. He said, "they will not believe me," and this "they" means the elders and people of Israel, not Pharaoh. That is, Moses' first obstacle was not the enemy but his own people; not Pharaoh's hard heart but the people's suspicion. This question was in fact honest and practical: a leader who stands up to speak needs some credential; he cannot rest everything on one sentence, "God spoke to me." In the Old Testament, when God sent someone, He often gave a sign: Gideon asked for the sign of the fleece, Hezekiah for the sign of the shadow. God did not rebuke Moses for asking this question. The way He answered is worth watching slowly: He delivered no great lecture; He asked a most ordinary question — "What is that in thine hand?"

In Moses' hand was a staff. A shepherd's staff, wooden, old, the whole property of his forty wilderness years, and also the memorial of his failed life: once his hand had held the authority of Egypt's palace; now only a stick for driving sheep remained. God said, cast it on the ground. He cast it down, and it became a serpent, and Moses fled — note this detail: Moses himself ran away first. The first lesson of the sign was not taught to the people; it was taught to Moses himself: the thing you fear, you first pass through with your own hand. God said again, put forth your hand and take it by the tail. To grab a snake, the tail is the most dangerous place to grab; common sense says grab the head. God insisted that he reach out and grab the tail — obedience is sometimes doing a thing at which common sense trembles, but which God has said to do. He reached out and caught it, and it became a staff in his hand again. Later the text calls this staff "the rod of God" (4:20). The same staff, laid down once in obedience, had its name changed. That old thing in your hand, that failed record, that trade by which you make a living — placed in God's hand and taken up again, it is still itself, but its use has wholly changed. The starting point of a miracle is never God conjuring up something new; it is the staff already in Moses' hand.

The second sign was closer to the body: God told Moses to put his hand into his bosom; when he took it out, his hand was leprous, as snow; he put it in again and took it out again, and his hand was restored like the rest of his flesh. Leprosy among the Israelites was a disease of isolation, reckoned unclean. God pressed this sign onto Moses' own body, letting him first experience in his own bosom a passage "from unclean to clean, from a hopeless disease to restoration." In days to come he would tell the people of a God who makes people clean; his own hand was the evidence. This sign also carries a quiet meaning: God's power is not only on the staff outside; it also reaches the part of a person that is inside, hidden, and unfit to be seen. In his bosom Moses carried forty years of shame: he had killed a man, he had fled for his life, he had been rejected by his own people. When God's hand reached into that bosom and brought that hand out, it was not only healing leprosy; it was telling Moses: that hidden stretch of your life — I have touched it, and I can restore it.

The third sign was a provision held in reserve: if they still would not believe the first two signs, take some water from the Nile, pour it on the dry ground, and the water would become blood on the dry ground. The Nile was an object of Egyptian worship, the lifeline of Egypt. Water turned to blood was not only a credential for Israel to see; it was also the first declaration against Egypt's power — the first of the ten plagues would be exactly this, water turned to blood, and its seed is buried here. The scope of the signs was widening: from the staff in the hand, to the hand in the bosom, to the river of Egypt. God's order of working is often like this: first in small places, in your hand, on your body, He proves Himself trustworthy; the credentials for great things are accumulated one small proof at a time.

The first sign also carries a layer of meaning that Moses may not have fully understood at the time and that only later became clear: the serpent was no ordinary animal in Egypt. Pharaoh's crown bore the cobra emblem; the serpent was a symbol of Egyptian kingship and of its guardian gods. A shepherd's staff becoming a serpent in Moses' hand, and then being seized back into a staff, was like a silent sentence: the power Egypt deemed supreme is, in God's hand, a thing to be picked up and put down. In chapter 7, Aaron's rod became a serpent, the rods of Pharaoh's magicians also became serpents, and Aaron's rod swallowed up their rods — the seed of that scene is buried in this rod, cast down and taken up today. A sign was never only for emboldening Moses; from the first day it was declaring to the adversary to come: the power you are about to face, God has already measured it in the wilderness. So when Moses returned to Egypt, the text is careful to record that in his hand was "the rod of God." Brothers and sisters, the oldest, least impressive thing in your hand today — a job done for many years, a trade you live by, an unglamorous stretch of the past — if God will take it over, its weight in your hand becomes entirely different. The key is not whether the thing is precious, but whether it has once been laid down in obedience and taken up again by an outstretched hand.

Here we must fix the place of "signs" clearly, lest we learn it crooked. These three signs are called in Hebrew 'oth (אוֹת, H226), meaning sign, miracle, credential, token [1]. The essence of a sign is "pointing": it is not itself the destination; it points to the God behind it who sent the man. So God said it plainly in 4:8: if they will not hearken to your voice nor believe the first sign, they will believe the second sign. The sign serves faith; it is a crutch for faith, not a performance, and still less a stage for Moses to show his ability. Note also the order of the three signs: God took the initiative in giving them, and Moses passively learned; Moses did not request signs to satisfy curiosity — he received tokens in order to carry out the mission. In the church today there are two deviations about miracles: one treats the miraculous as a performance, chasing spectacle, until the sign becomes the star and God retreats behind the curtain; the other is simply ashamed to speak of God's works, until nothing is left but doctrine. Chapter 4 gives us the straight road: a sign is a credential, so that people will believe the Sender and hearken to the God who speaks. Signs may be examined; the mission is the main text.

There is also a small matter not to miss: God did not, because of this question of Moses, use up all three signs at once, but kept an order and a reserve: if they believed the first, the third need not be used. God gives credentials generously, but He does not waste them. Moses, now you have in your hand a staff, a hand, and the river water to come; when you go back, you have words to speak and credentials to show. The first line on the list of excuses, "no credentials," God has struck through. But Moses' list has a next line, and a harder one to strike — a line that names not a difficulty outside, but the man himself.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Man Slow of Speech, and "Who Made Man's Mouth" (4:10-17)

Moses' second sentence found its reason in himself: "O my Lord, I am not eloquent, neither heretofore, nor since thou hast spoken unto thy servant: but I am slow of speech, and of a slow tongue." (Exodus 4:10, KJV) Note the two layers in his words. The first layer is "heretofore": this was no momentary modesty, but his long-standing verdict on himself — I have never been a man who can speak. The second layer cuts deeper: even since Thou hast spoken unto Thy servant, it is still so. In other words: You appeared, You spoke, but I am still I, and this mouth is still this mouth, not one bit improved. Moses had equated "God is with me" with "I am immediately turned into another person," and finding himself unchanged, he assumed the mission could not land. How many people are stuck right here: I have believed, I have also heard the call, but why am I still this clumsy, this slow, this unpresentable? Has the call perhaps picked the wrong person?

First, a factual question: was Moses truly afflicted in speech, or was this self-deprecation? The wording of the text is kaved peh (כְּבַד־פֶּה, around root H3515), literally "heavy of mouth" [2]. He also said he was heavy of mouth and heavy of tongue; later, in 6:12 and 6:30, he changed to a heavier expression, calling himself a man of uncircumcised lips, in Hebrew 'aral sephatayim, meaning lips like something uncircumcised — unclean and unfit for use [2]. Yet Stephen in Acts chapter 7 says Moses "was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians, and was mighty in words and in deeds." With the two records set side by side, scholars explain differently: some say Moses did have an impediment of speech; some say this is the customary self-deprecation of a leader; some say that after forty years away from Egypt his language had grown rusty; some say his so-called inability concerned not speaking in public in his own defense and pleading. This manuscript will not hand down a verdict for everyone; it only points out the emphasis of the text itself: from beginning to end God never denied Moses' self-assessment, and never said, "Actually you speak very well." God did not argue with him about his eloquence score. God lifted the whole question one level higher.

God's answer is among the weightiest speeches in the Bible: "Who hath made man's mouth? or who maketh the dumb, or deaf, or the seeing, or the blind? have not I the LORD? Now therefore go, and I will be with thy mouth, and teach thee what thou shalt say." (Exodus 4:11-12, KJV) This word has three layers. First, the sovereignty of the Creator: the mouth was made by God, the mouth's functions are in God's hand, and dumbness and deafness, sight and blindness, are none of them outside God's rule. God was not belittling human defect; He was declaring: how your mouth came from the factory does not decide what it can be used for. Second, the logic of the mission was reversed: Moses' formula was, "I can speak, therefore I can go"; God's formula is, "You go, and I will be with your mouth" — eloquence is not the ticket for departure, but the supply after setting out. "I will be with thy mouth" is closer to the original than the idea of a technique: what God gives is not a set of speaking skills, but His own presence resting on Moses' mouth. Third, the promise of teaching: "and teach thee what thou shalt say" — the content did not need to be composed by Moses either. What to say, God teaches; how it comes out of the mouth, God accompanies. Only one thing was left to Moses: go, and open his mouth. The Lord Jesus later said almost the same to His disciples: when they deliver you up, take no thought beforehand what you shall speak; whatever is given you in that hour, speak that (Matthew 10:19-20). Jeremiah in his youth also said, "I cannot speak: for I am a child," and God likewise would not let him describe himself so, but touched his mouth and put His words in his mouth (Jeremiah 1:6-9). There is a clear line through the Bible: when God calls a person, He never inspects eloquence first; when God gives His words, He only asks the person to open his mouth in obedience. Insufficient eloquence has never been the threshold.

But Moses' reply is the sentence in this whole passage that sinks the heart most: "O my Lord, send, I pray thee, by the hand of him whom thou wilt send." (Exodus 4:13, KJV) He no longer named any specific difficulty; this was a total withdrawal: as long as it is not me, anyone will do. At this point the problem was very clear: it was not "I cannot," but "I will not." The first three questions could still be reasoned about; this sentence was a refusal of the will. The text continues: "And the anger of the LORD was kindled against Moses." Please do not skip this sentence. God was angry, which means obedience is no small matter, and pleading carried to its end becomes resistance. A God who appeared to you in the flame of the bush, told you His name, put credentials into your hand, and promised you His presence — to say to Him "send someone else" is to slight Him personally. God's anger was real, and preaching must not soften this verse.

Yet what follows is one of the most moving touches of humanity in the whole Bible: God was angry, but He did not change the man; God was angry, but on the spot He brought out His provision. "Is not Aaron the Levite thy brother? I know that he can speak well. ... And he shall be thy spokesman unto the people: and he shall be, even he shall be to thee instead of a mouth, and thou shalt be to him instead of God." (Exodus 4:14-16, excerpt, KJV) Note the tone of "Is not...?": Aaron was not a man God found at the last moment; he was already placed there — while Moses was tangled in the wilderness, his brother was already on the road to meet him. God's compassion and God's insistence stand side by side in the same speech: the mission does not change, and the man is not replaced; but at the hurdle you cannot cross, I will assign you a person to help you cross it. Aaron was to be Moses' mouth, and Moses to Aaron as God — later 7:1 states this relation more completely: "See, I have made thee a god to Pharaoh: and Aaron thy brother shall be thy prophet." God did not cancel Moses' office; Aaron was the outlet, not the substitute; the words still came from God to Moses, and Moses was still the man who stood before Pharaoh.

We are to learn two things from this passage. One is about ourselves: do not imitate Moses' fourth sentence. The first three may all be spoken: fearing that people will not believe may be spoken; feeling that one is not up to it may be spoken; laying difficulties before God is no disgrace, and God fields them one by one. But the sentence "send someone else" must not be spoken, because it closes the door of the conversation. You may be weak; you may not turn away. You may ask for help; you may not hand in your resignation. The other is about others: when a co-worker shrinks back in weakness, God's way is to be angry yet not discard, to persist while providing. When we lead people and walk with them as they grow, we should do the same: neither indulge escape nor kick the person away; state the standard clearly, assign the helper, and keep the person inside the mission to grow slowly. In his later life Moses truly became the man who spoke to Pharaoh — in the first plagues Aaron still relayed his words, but the further it went, the more Moses spoke himself. The mouth God gives is trained in act after act of obedience. The one who opens his mouth first only later becomes like a man who can open his mouth.

We must also add honestly: this provision, Aaron, later caused a great disaster himself. This same eloquent elder brother, at the foot of Mount Sinai, could not withstand the pressure of the people and with his own hands fashioned a golden calf. The helper God assigned Moses was not a perfect helper; helpers grow weak too, and can be pushed along by the crowd. This reminds us: receiving God's provision does not mean outsourcing responsibility. Moses could not say, "Aaron said it; it has nothing to do with me," just as we cannot push faith, the teaching of the next generation, and the truth that must be spoken entirely onto pastors, onto a spouse, onto the one who "speaks better than I do." Aaron was a mouth, not a conscience; he could voice for Moses, but he could not obey in Moses' place. God has compassion on our shortcomings and gives us partners, so that in partnership we still stand personally — not so that we can hide behind others. When Moses later interceded for the people on the mountain and spoke with God face to face, he relied not on Aaron's eloquence but on his own willingness, again and again, to come back before God. That road, no one can walk for us.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Blood on the Journey, the Meeting on the Mountain, and the People's Worship (4:18-31)

At last Moses set out. He first went back to his father-in-law Jethro and took leave properly; in Midian God added a word to settle his heart: "all the men are dead which sought thy life." He took his wife and his two sons, set them on a donkey, took the rod of God in his hand, and started back to Egypt. At this point the mission seemed to be running smoothly. And precisely on the road, there occurred one of the most difficult matters in the Bible.

"And it came to pass by the way in the inn, that the LORD met him, and sought to kill him." (Exodus 4:24, KJV) No warning, no explanation: God came upon him in the night, and a life hung by a thread. "Then Zipporah took a sharp stone, and cut off the foreskin of her son, and cast it at his feet, and said, Surely a bloody husband art thou to me. So he let him go: then she said, A bloody husband thou art, because of the circumcision." (Exodus 4:25-26, excerpt, KJV)

This passage is only three verses, yet it is one of the famous difficult texts of the Old Testament, and this manuscript must speak of it honestly: scholarship to this day has no generally accepted conclusion, and no pulpit should present one interpretation as the only answer. We set the main directions side by side. First, the context of the text ties the crisis directly to circumcision: the act that resolved the danger was circumcising the son, and Zipporah's words say expressly that it was "because of the circumcision." Circumcision was the sign of the covenant God made with Abraham (Genesis chapter 17), yet Moses, the head of the people, had a son who was uncircumcised — the man who would lead a nation into the covenant first owed the sign of the covenant in his own house; most commentators regard this as the core of the problem: Moses' negligence lay in his own family. Second, why was the son uncircumcised? The text does not say. Some conjecture that it had to do with Midianite family custom or the mother's reservations; some conjecture that Moses kept delaying; but all of these are conjectures, and where the text is silent, we mark it as conjecture and do not present it as fact. Third, whom "sought to kill him" refers to, at whose feet the blood was cast, and whom the touching concerned — the pronouns of the original do not name them, and through the ages there have been different readings, Moses himself or his son among them. And the chatan damim (חֲתַן דָּמִים, H2860) that Zipporah spoke, rendered "a bloody husband" in the King James Version and literally close to "a bridegroom of blood," has a precise meaning and referent that scholars dispute: some explain it as a relationship re-established, like a marriage covenant, by blood — the blood of circumcision; others explain it as the blood obtaining the preservation of her husband [3][4]. These disputes this manuscript records honestly, without handing down a verdict.

Silence is silence, but the lesson this scene teaches us is not blurred. First, the mission is not only on the platform; it begins at home. Moses was going to lead a nation into the covenant; his own house first had to stand in the covenant. The circle people in ministry most easily miss is precisely the nearest one: the work outside all in order, while the order at home, the faith of the children, and the covenants a husband and wife should keep are endlessly postponed. God stopped Moses on the road to settle the debt in his own house first, and only then released him to face Pharaoh. Second, in the moment of crisis it was Zipporah's decisive act that saved this household. She may not have understood all the theology, but she recognized the crux of the problem, acted quickly, and did it accurately. God upholds the mission with the whole household: Aaron ahead as the mouth, the wife on the road saving a life — Moses was never a man who carried the mission alone. Third, the name "bloody husband" carries weight: this mission would cost something, and it drew blood from the very beginning. The story of the exodus has blood all along its way: the blood on the doorposts at Passover, the Red Sea, the blood before the ark of the covenant. Zipporah's one cut was like a small advance notice: deliverance is never cheap.

After that night, the camera moves to the mountain. "And the LORD said to Aaron, Go into the wilderness to meet Moses." Aaron went, met Moses in the mount of God, and kissed him. After forty years the two brothers met again, and Moses told Aaron all the words of the LORD who had sent him, and all the signs He had commanded. Then the two went into Egypt, gathered all the elders of Israel, and Aaron spoke all the words and did the signs in the sight of the people. The thing Moses feared before the bush — "they will not believe me" — here received its answer: "And the people believed."

Note carefully the ground of the people's believing; the text writes it with complete clarity: "and when they heard that the LORD had visited the children of Israel, and that he had looked upon their affliction, then they bowed their heads and worshipped." (Exodus 4:31, KJV) They believed not only because they saw the signs, but even more because they heard a piece of news: God has visited, God has looked upon — so our suffering in Egypt, God has seen it, and God remembers it. "Visited" in the original is paqad (פָּקַד), the same word as in chapter 3, where God said, "I have surely visited you" [5]. A man who had endured generations in the brick kilns, hearing "God has visited you, God has looked upon your affliction," wept first, and then his head bowed. Worship is the end of this chapter, and also the end of this whole stretch of Moses' struggle: he feared people would not believe, and the people worshipped; he complained that his mouth was clumsy, and the words were spread abroad; he begged God to change the man, and God used him, and gave him a full company of helpers besides.

Set the application down here, spoken to four kinds of people. The first: those who tremble before their own people — you fear your family will not believe, your co-workers will not accept you, that if you open your mouth you will be mocked. The road God gave Moses was not first to train up self-confidence, but first to hold the credentials steady, speak the words clearly, and then stand out in obedience. Whether people believe is God's work; whether you open your mouth is yours. The second: those who reckon themselves inarticulate — in meetings, at home, when sharing the gospel, the moment you open your mouth you feel your words fail their meaning. Today God speaks the same sentence to you: Who made man's mouth? The words you should say, He will teach you. Do not use eloquence as a reason for resignation; let the inarticulate speak slowly and truthfully — God's word carries its own weight. The third: those who owe a debt at home — however busy the mission, first make up that lesson at home: the covenant that should be established, the duty that should be done, the people who should be accompanied; do not wait for God to stop you on the road. The fourth: those who have already said "send someone else" — Moses said this sentence; God was angry, but He did not change the man, and today it is the same. The head you turned away, God can let you turn back; the resignation you handed in, God may decline to approve. Turning back is no disgrace; the disgrace is knowing God is waiting and still stiffening your neck and not walking.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today, we have heard Moses' three excuses, and beyond them his fourth word — the wholesale shrinking back — and we have also heard God's provision answering them. He said he had no credentials, and God placed signs into his hand; he said he was slow of speech and of a slow tongue, and God said, Who made man's mouth? I will be with your mouth; he said, send someone else, and God was angry yet did not change the man, but brought Aaron before him instead. On the night of the journey home, God, through Zipporah's hand, completed the covenant in Moses' household; and when he reached Egypt, the elders heard and the people bowed their heads and worshipped. The end of excuses is not God's abandonment, but God's provision. Insufficient eloquence is not the threshold; disobedience is. God uses the willing, and He also uses a whole household to uphold His mission.

Let us pray together: Lord who made man's mouth, and who also made man's ear and eye, we place this unworthy mouth of ours into Your hand. Forgive our shrinking back — our "send someone else" — and heal our hearts that tremble before people. You gave Moses a mouth; teach us also the words we should say. You prepared Aaron and Zipporah for Moses; let us also uphold one another in the mission. Make us willing people, people who open their mouths, people who first establish the covenant at home. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

Both word meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Whether Moses truly had an impediment of speech or was speaking in self-deprecation, scholarly views differ; the main text of this manuscript sets them side by side without a verdict. "God did not argue with him about his eloquence score, but declared the sovereignty of the Maker of the mouth" is a homiletical exposition based on the text of 4:11.

[3] chatan damim: Hebrew חֲתַן דָּמִים (chatan, חָתָן, H2860, bridegroom, son-in-law), Zipporah's words in Exodus 4:25-26, rendered "a bloody husband" in the King James Version. The precise meaning of this expression and its referent (Moses, his son, a relationship established by blood, and so on) are disputed among scholars, and to this day there is no settled conclusion; the main text of this manuscript only sets the main directions of interpretation side by side and makes no assertion. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[4] mul: Hebrew מוּל (H4135), to circumcise, to cut off the foreskin; Exodus 4:26 "A bloody husband thou art, because of the circumcision (the setting of mul)"; Genesis chapter 17, where the covenant was established, is the basis. In Exodus 4:24-26 the object of "the LORD sought to kill him" and the object touched by the blood are not named by the pronouns of the text, and interpretations have differed through the ages; this manuscript has noted the narrative silence in the main text and does not fabricate a completion. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[5] paqad: Hebrew פָּקַד (H6485, to visit, look upon, care for, number); "the LORD had visited the children of Israel" in Exodus 4:31 answers to the same word in Exodus 3:16, "I have surely visited you"; Joseph's dying words in Genesis 50:24-25, "God will surely visit you," also use this word. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's paragraph "the ground of the people's believing was that they heard God had visited them" is argued from the sentence order of the text of 4:31 and is a homiletical exposition.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Who hath made man's mouth? or who maketh the dumb, or deaf, or the seeing, or the blind? have not I the LORD? Now therefore go, and I will be with thy mouth, and teach thee what thou shalt say." (Exodus 4:11-12, KJV)

We all carry Moses' list of excuses. He said, they will not believe me, and God put signs into his hand. He said, I am slow of speech, and God answered with a question: who made man's mouth? He said, send someone else, and God was angry — yet He sent Aaron, his brother, to stand beside him as his helper. Moses was already eighty when he started; the best years seemed to be behind him, but God simply said: now go. God never waits until we are capable in everything before He uses us. He is not looking for an impressive record, but for a willing and obedient heart. If today you dread opening your mouth, are ashamed of your past, or owe a debt of responsibility at home, do not keep postponing the list. Give your mouth to the Lord who made it, bring your household into His covenant, and set your foot on the road. The signs He gives, the words He supplies, the helpers He prepares; your part is only one thing: go, and speak. Remember Moses' staff: thrown down, it was a serpent; taken up, it was a staff again — yet its name had changed, and it was called the staff of God. You are the same: you do not first have to become someone else; first give yourself, shortcomings and all, into God's hand. Your "too late" and "too clumsy" are not the verdict with God. The verdict is only this: I will be with your mouth.

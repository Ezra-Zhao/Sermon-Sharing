# Ye Are the Children of the LORD Your God — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 14

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: First translation — pending proofreading (初译待校), unpublished (translated in full, paragraph by paragraph, from the finalized Chinese manuscript "Ye Are the Children of the LORD Your God" on Deuteronomy 14, which has passed strong re-verification in Chinese; this English version has not yet had native-speaker proofreading)
Text: Deuteronomy chapter 14 (29 verses in full)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), taken from the anchor text of this chapter held for this batch (KJV-申14.txt, 29 lines, fetched in full from the BibleGateway KJV whole-chapter page) and checked against it verse by verse; no quotation is back-translated from the Chinese. The cross-references quoted verbatim — Exodus 23:19, Exodus 34:26, and Leviticus 11:21 — were likewise fetched from BibleGateway KJV on the same date and are embedded from the KJV letter. The companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (CUNPSS) as its base text, checked verse by verse against the verse-by-verse pages at wd.bible (deu.14.N.cunp, the CUNPSS simplified line, the pinyin line, the BHS, and the KJV read together, line by line), with the parallel texts Exodus 23:19, 34:26 and Leviticus 11:13, 11:21, 11:40 likewise checked directly on their verse-by-verse pages. Numbering is registered first, so as not to confuse this chapter with the preceding one: the numbering of Deuteronomy 13 is the most complex in the whole book — the CUNPSS has an empty number and a fused number there and stands offset from the Masoretic text; this chapter is exactly the reverse: Deuteronomy 14 in the CUNPSS has 29 verses, aligned verse for verse with the Masoretic 29 verses and the KJV 29 verses, with no empty number and no fusion, so that the verse numbers of quotations in this chapter are the same number in the other versions. Points where the two base texts differ are registered separately, ten items: (i) at 14:1, "baldness between your eyes" — the original bên ʿênêkem is literally "between your eyes"; the Chinese base text renders it freely as "on the forehead," while the KJV renders it directly, "between your eyes"; registered separately. (ii) At the end of 14:2 the BHS carries the paragraph mark samekh; the declaration of election in this verse recurs at 7:6 in nearly identical words, with small differences of wording (registered separately in the manuscript on chapter 7). (iii) On the renderings of the seven wild beasts at 14:5: the Chinese base text reads deer, gazelle, roe deer, wild goat, elk, yellow sheep, and green sheep; the KJV reads hart, roebuck, fallow deer, wild goat, pygarg, wild ox, and chamois — the two versions diverge markedly. (iv) At 14:9, "fins" — the original sənappîr means a fin; the Chinese base text reads "wings," the Revised Chinese Union Version of 2010 reads "fins," and the KJV reads "fins"; registered separately. (v) At 14:16, tinšemet in the Chinese base text is "the horned owl" (角鸱), while the KJV reads "swan" — the two point to very different things; registered only. (vi) The list at 14:18 closes with the bat — the list calls itself a list of "birds," yet its last name is not a bird; this is the classification usage of the original text itself, registered only and not explained. (vii) On the quotation structure at 14:21: the Chinese base text opens quotation marks twice in this verse — "of anything that dieth of itself … an holy people unto the LORD thy God" is one closed unit, and "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in his mother's milk" is opened and closed separately; at the end of the verse the BHS carries the paragraph mark petuhah. (viii) At 14:23, "to place his name there" — the original is ləšakkēn šəmô; at 14:24 it is lāśûm šəmô; the two forms stand side by side and belong to the same group as the name-placing variants registered in the manuscript on chapter 12; registered separately. (ix) At the end of 14:27 and at the end of 14:29 the BHS carries samekh in both places; verse 29 is the last verse of the chapter. (x) Person: verses 1–21 address the congregation in the plural ("ye/you"), verses 22–29 turn to the individual in the singular ("thou") — the food section is promulgated to the whole people, the tithe section to the head of a household; registered separately.
Title verification: Five candidates were considered — (i) "Ye Are the Children of the LORD Your God," taken from the opening sentence of verse 1: "Ye are the children of the LORD your God"; (ii) "A Peculiar People unto Himself," taken from verse 2 — but this name has already been used for the sermon on Deuteronomy 7 and is not repeated; (iii) "Whatsoever Thy Soul Desireth," taken from verse 26; (iv) "Thou Shalt Not Seethe a Kid in His Mother's Milk," taken from the closing sentence of verse 21; (v) "Eat and Be Satisfied," taken from verse 29. Candidate (i) is adopted: the whole chapter opens with identity — children (v. 1), an holy people (vv. 2, 21), and those with no part or inheritance provided for (vv. 27, 29); the food lists and the tithe feast in between are this identity practised at the table. Taking the title from the sentence of identity gives the chapter's eating and not eating their footing. The English title is fixed from the chapter's own words in the KJV at 14:1: Ye Are the Children of the LORD Your God.

## Main Text

### Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Fact] Deuteronomy 13 guards the source of revelation; chapter 14 turns to the daily table. The chapter has 29 verses in two large sections: verses 1–21 treat eating — first identity is established (vv. 1–2), then the lists of what may and may not be eaten are given (vv. 3–20), and the section is closed with what dieth of itself and the prohibition on seething a kid (v. 21); verses 22–29 treat offering — the yearly tithe becomes a feast at the sanctuary (vv. 22–27), and the tithe of every third year becomes relief in the locality (vv. 28–29). Each section has its own closing sentence: the food section sounds the reason-sentence of the holy people at both ends (at v. 2, "For thou art an holy people unto the LORD thy God," and at v. 21, "for thou art an holy people unto the LORD thy God"), and the tithe section closes by saying, "that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all the work of thine hand which thou doest" (v. 29). This chapter stands third in the legal portion of chapters 12–26: chapter 12 fixes the place of worship, chapter 13 fixes the truth or falsehood of revelation, and chapter 14 brings separation into the three meals of the day and the harvest of the year — holiness has walked from the sanctuary to the kitchen stove. One further structural fact is registered: the divine name occurs eleven times in the whole chapter, in eight verses, while in verses 3–20, where the lists are densest, the divine name does not occur once — the lists are silent, the identity speaks; before and after the lists an identity sentence encloses them (v. 2 before, v. 21 after), and this is the binding thread of the chapter.

[Argument] This is an inference: the order of this chapter is the sermon of the whole chapter — first it says who you are, then it says what you eat. Identity is not the reward for the lists; the lists are the daily lesson of the identity. If the order were reversed — memorize the lists first and ask about identity afterwards — a man would think that holiness is something eaten into being; this chapter deliberately puts "children" before "menu": first they were chosen and became a people, and only then did this people's table acquire its boundaries. This is an inference.

[Summary] The outline of the chapter: identity first (vv. 1–2), the lists in the middle (vv. 3–20), mercy at the side (v. 21), the feast after (vv. 22–27), and the poor to close (vv. 28–29) — one table, five marks.

### Part One: Ye Are the Children of the LORD Your God (14:1–2; 05:00–14:00)

[Fact] Verse 1, identity and the boundary of mourning: "Ye are the children of the LORD your God: ye shall not cut yourselves, nor make any baldness between your eyes for the dead." Three items are registered: first, the identity sentence opens with "children" (bānîm, H1121) — the first name this chapter gives the people is not "people" but "children," and the Father's name follows at once: "the LORD your God." Second, both prohibitions concern the body and the dead: cutting yourselves for the dead (gāḏaḏ, H1413, in the hitpael — to cut oneself) and making baldness between your eyes (qorḥâ, H7144 — a bald place made by shaving; the original is literally "between your eyes," see the separate register in item (i) of the header). Third, neither prohibition carries a reason-sentence within the verse — the reason is not inside the verse but at its head: because you are children. The treatment of the body is fixed first by identity, not by grief.

[Fact] Verse 2, the unfolding of the identity: "For thou art an holy people unto the LORD thy God, and the LORD hath chosen thee to be a peculiar people unto himself, above all the nations that are upon the earth." Three items are registered: first, "an holy people" (ʿam qāḏôš; qāḏôš, H6918) — holiness here does not describe the people's conduct; it states the people's belonging: belonging to the LORD as an holy people. Second, election (bāḥar, H977) and "a peculiar people unto himself" (səgullâ, H5459 — an exclusive treasure): the range of the election is "above all the nations that are upon the earth," and its landing point is "unto himself." Third, this verse recurs at 7:6 in nearly identical words (see the separate register in item (ii) of the header): one and the same declaration of election was promulgated in chapter 7 for warfare and is promulgated in this chapter for eating — the field of the declaration's application is here extended to the table. The person in this verse turns from the plural to the singular ("thou"): the identity is spoken to all, and it is also spoken to each one.

[Argument] This is an inference: this part puts the expression of grief inside the frame of identity. To cut oneself and shave the forehead for the dead is to express grief by damaging the body; this chapter does not forbid grief — it forbids only treating one's own body by the rites with which the neighbouring peoples serve the dead, because this body has a prior belonging: it is the body of a child of God. A child's grief may be as great as weeping, but it may not be as great as self-destruction; the boundary lies not in the strength of the feeling but in the belonging of the body. This is an inference. Again: the landing point of the declaration of election in this chapter deserves attention — it is not after a battle report, not at a moment of triumph; it falls at the head of a food list: the most daily testing ground of election turns out to be the kitchen. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] Two echoes are registered (registered as reports, not verified word for word): Galatians 3:26 treats all becoming children of God through faith in Christ Jesus — the name "children" is reopened in the New Testament with faith as its gate; Romans 14 treats eating and not eating, with the criterion falling on love and conscience, not on the list itself. Registered only, not read back: what this part promulgates is the boundary of the body and the table for the people of the old covenant, and it does not itself state its continuance or abrogation in the New Testament.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] On the background of the two prohibitions at 14:1, two readings are set out side by side without adjudication: (a) the mourning-rite reading — cutting oneself and shaving the forehead were ritual acts of mourning for the dead in the Canaanite sphere, and this chapter forbids them in order to draw a boundary against the mourning rites of the neighbouring peoples; Leviticus 19:28, "ye shall not make any cuttings in your flesh for the dead," is parallel to this verse (registered), and 1 Kings 18:28 records the prophets of Baal cutting themselves after their manner with knives and lancets, the same kind of act appearing in a prophetic narrative (registered as a report). (b) The restraint-of-grief reading — the two prohibitions limit not the source from which the act is learned but the act itself: wherever it was learned from, a child's body may not be treated in this way. The two readings are set out side by side: the former stresses the source, the latter the belonging; this verse puts the identity sentence before the prohibitions, which conflicts with neither reading, and this manuscript does not adjudicate.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part One: first there is the standing of children, then there are the boundaries of the body; the declaration of election falls at the head of the list, and holiness flows from identity into daily life.


### Part Two: Only What Parteth the Hoof and Cheweth the Cud May Be Eaten (14:3–10; 14:00–26:00)

[Fact] Verse 3, the general rule of the lists: "Thou shalt not eat any abominable thing." Two items are registered: first, "abominable" (tôʿēḇâ, H8441) is the same word as "an abominable thing" at the end of chapter 13 (13:14 in the Chinese base text) — the abomination of the preceding chapter lay in idolatry, the abomination of this chapter lies at the table; one and the same word joins the altar and the table into a single line. Second, the general rule is a single sentence, and the list unfolds after it: first the class is established, then the names are listed.

[Fact] Verses 4–5, the positive list of land animals that may be eaten: "These are the beasts which ye shall eat: the ox, the sheep, and the goat, The hart, and the roebuck, and the fallow deer, and the wild goat, and the pygarg, and the wild ox, and the chamois." Three items are registered: first, the list has ten names — the first three are domestic animals (the ox, the sheep, and the goat — the original śeh kəśāḇîm and śeh ʿizzîm, that is, the sheep kind and the goat kind), and the last seven are wild beasts (ʾayyāl, ṣəḇî, yaḥmûr, ʾaqqô, dîšôn, təʾô, zāmer). Second, the positive list comes first — this chapter first says what may be eaten and only then what may not; in Leviticus 11 the standard is established first and those that may not be eaten are listed first, the order being the reverse; the two places are set out side by side in Endnote 4. Third, the correspondence of the seven wild beasts to modern species diverges markedly between the versions (see the separate register in item (iii) of the header); only the renderings of the base text are registered here. On the KJV letter the seven names are, in order, hart, roebuck, fallow deer, wild goat, pygarg, wild ox, and chamois, counted by hand as seven, with the three domestic animals making ten in all — the same total as the Chinese base text, the names at several places differing (see Endnote 3).

[Fact] Verse 6, the standard for land animals: "And every beast that parteth the hoof, and cleaveth the cleft into two claws, and cheweth the cud among the beasts, that ye shall eat." Two items are registered: first, the standard has two conditions, and both must be met in full: parting the hoof into two (parsâ, H6541; the word-form šesaʿ is registered, its number pending verification) and chewing the cud (gērâ, H1625). Second, the standard is open — "every beast that … that ye shall eat": the list is an example, the standard is the general rule; whatever falls outside the ten names but meets the standard may likewise be eaten.

[Fact] Verses 7–8, the prohibition of those that meet the standard only in part: "Nevertheless these ye shall not eat of them that chew the cud, or of them that divide the cloven hoof; as the camel, and the hare, and the coney: for they chew the cud, but divide not the hoof; therefore they are unclean unto you. And the swine, because it divideth the hoof, yet cheweth not the cud, it is unclean unto you: ye shall not eat of their flesh, nor touch their dead carcase." Four items are registered: first, four kinds are named (gāmāl, H1581; ʾarneḇeṯ, H768; šāpān, H8227; ḥăzîr, H2386), in two groups: the camel, the hare, and the coney chew the cud but do not divide the hoof, and the swine divides the hoof but does not chew the cud — each meets one condition and lacks the other. Second, the verdict is in the same form: "unclean unto you" (ṭāmēʾ, H2931) — unclean is here a relational word: it is unclean "unto you," that is, it may not enter the mouth as far as this people is concerned. Third, verse 8 adds a stroke: "nor touch their dead carcase" — the carcase (niḇlâ, H5038) may not be touched (nāgaʿ, H5060): the prohibition extends from eating to touching, and the boundary is drawn at contact. Fourth, at the end of verse 8 the BHS carries samekh: the land-animal list pauses here.

[Fact] Verses 9–10, the standard and verdict for water animals: "These ye shall eat of all that are in the waters: all that have fins and scales shall ye eat: And whatsoever hath not fins and scales ye may not eat; it is unclean unto you." Three items are registered: first, the water standard likewise has two conditions: having fins (sənappîr, H5579; the Chinese base text reads "wings"; see the separate register in item (iv) of the header) and scales (qaśqeśeṯ, H7193); whatever meets both may be eaten. Second, for water animals no list is given, only the standard — the names of the things in the water are too many, and here the standard wholly replaces the list. Third, at the end of verse 10 the BHS carries samekh: the water section and the land section are each closed by a samekh, and the punctuation of the lists is supplied by the text itself. The water list, it is registered on the KJV letter, has no names to count: the standard alone is given, fins and scales being its two items.

[Argument] This is an inference: the teaching of separation in this part turns on the one word "wholly": the two conditions must be met in full, and meeting one does not count. The camel can chew the cud, but its hoof is wrong; the swine's hoof is right, but it does not chew the cud — those that meet the standard in part and those that do not meet it at all stand in the same verdict, "unclean." Separation was never a matter of picking one agreeable mark and wearing it: the standard is a whole set, and to meet one and lack the other is not to be in the class of what may be eaten. This is an inference. Again: the positive list coming first is a gracious stroke of the pen: the first sentence of the law is not "thou shalt not," but "these are the beasts which ye shall eat" — the ten that may be eaten come before the four that may not; within the boundary, what comes first is the abundance of permission. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] This part has no direct New Testament quotation to register. One echo is registered (registered as a report, not verified word for word): Mark 7:19 records that when the Lord treated of food, the narrator notes, in effect, that he made all foods clean — the subject of the cleanness of food is raised again in the New Testament, and its verdict's relation to the lists in this part belongs to the reading layer; the text of this part stands on its own.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] On the ground of the food laws, four readings are set out side by side without adjudication: (a) the hygienic reading — the lists avoid things quick to spoil and quick to carry disease, and are an arrangement of ancient public health; (b) the symbolic-order reading — Mary Douglas, in Purity and Danger (1966), proposed that cleanness is the completeness of the created order, each thing after its kind, and that what meets the standard only in part is an anomaly of classification and therefore unclean; this reading had the greatest influence in the twentieth century, and its author herself later revised it in part — set out side by side with it; (c) the identity-mark reading — the lists need no other meaning; their function is precisely to make this people's table different from the tables of the neighbouring peoples, and separation itself is the purpose, agreeing with the arrangement of the identity sentence at verse 2 of this chapter; (d) the ecological-symbiotic reading — the lists protect certain species and the order of pasturage. The four readings are set out side by side: the text of this chapter gives only the lists and the identity sentence and gives no ground, and this manuscript does not adjudicate.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part Two: first the ten names that may be eaten are listed, then the two conditions that must both be met are established; those that meet the standard in part stand together as unclean, and within the boundary what comes first is the abundance of permission.

### Part Three: Birds, Flying Things, and What Dieth of Itself (14:11–21; 26:00–38:00)

[Fact] Verse 11, the opening sentence of the bird section: "Of all clean birds ye shall eat." Two items are registered: first, for birds no standard is set, only a counter-list is given — what follows lists what may not be eaten, and the clean birds (ṣippôr, H6833; ṭāhôr, H2889) outside the list may all be eaten; unlike the land animals, where there is "standard plus examples," here there is "prohibition list plus all the rest permitted": the legislative technique changes with its object. Second, this verse and verse 20 are the same sentence at head and tail (at v. 20 the KJV reads, "But of all clean fowls ye may eat" — on the KJV letter the closing sentence reads "fowls" where the opening sentence reads "birds," while the Chinese base text has the same phrase, "clean birds," in both places; registered separately), enclosing the whole section of birds and flying things between two sentences of permission.

[Fact] Verses 12–18, the list of birds that may not be eaten, twenty-one names in all, registered verse by verse: verse 12, three names: "But these are they of which ye shall not eat: the eagle, and the ossifrage, and the ospray," (nešer, H5404; peres, H6538; ʿozniyyâ, H5797). Verse 13, three names and a class: "And the glede, and the kite, and the vulture after his kind," (rāʾâ, H7201; ʾayyâ, H344; dayyâ, H1778; "after his kind," ləmînâ, mîn, H4327 — from this verse on, "after his kind" enters the phrasing of the list). Verse 14, one name and a class: "And every raven after his kind," (ʿōrēḇ, H6158). Verse 15, four names: "And the owl, and the night hawk, and the cuckow, and the hawk after his kind," (baṯ-hayyaʿănâ, H3284; taḥmās, H8464; šāḥap, H7828; nēṣ, H5322). Verse 16, three names: "The little owl, and the great owl, and the swan," (kôs, H3563; yanšûp, H3244; tinšemeṯ, H8580). Verse 17, three names: "And the pelican, and the gier eagle, and the cormorant," (qāʾaṯ, H6893; rāḥām, H7360; šālāḵ, H7994). Verse 18, four names: "And the stork, and the heron after her kind, and the lapwing, and the bat." (ḥăsîḏâ, H2624; ʾănāpâ, H601; dûḵîpaṯ, H1744; ʿăṭallēp, H5847). In all, twenty-one names, counted by hand on the KJV letter verse by verse — three, three, one, four, three, three, four — agreeing in total with the count of the Chinese base text, the names at several places differing (see Endnote 3); "after his kind" / "after her kind" occurs four times (vv. 13, 14, 15, 18). Two items are registered: first, the backbone of the list is carrion-eaters, birds of prey, night birds, and water birds, and its last name, the bat, is not a bird (see the separate register in item (vi) of the header) — the classification usage of the list is the original text's own, and this manuscript does not smooth it out for it. Second, the ancient sense of many bird names cannot be fixed with certainty, and the versions diverge widely (at v. 16 tinšemeṯ, which the KJV reads as "swan"); only the renderings of the base text are registered, and no modern species is asserted.

[Fact] Verses 19–20, flying creeping things and the close: "And every creeping thing that flieth is unclean unto you: they shall not be eaten. But of all clean fowls ye may eat." Three items are registered: first, flying creeping things (šereṣ hāʿôp — creeping things that fly, šereṣ, H8318) are unclean as a whole class, with no exception clause. Second, here there is one express difference from Leviticus 11: Leviticus 11:21 opens an exception — in the KJV letter, "Yet these may ye eat of every flying creeping thing that goeth upon all four, which have legs above their feet, to leap withal upon the earth;" (checked directly on the verse-by-verse page for Lev 11:21 in the Chinese companion manuscript's verification) — and the following verse lists the leaping insects that may be eaten; verse 19 of this chapter has no such exception and closes only with the "clean fowls" of verse 20. The difference is set out side by side only, and the relation between the two places is set out side by side at the Discussion layer. Third, the "fowls" of verse 20, ʿôp (H5775) in the original, properly denotes winged things in general: whether the scope of the permission sentence extends to clean flying creeping things is a question of reading, set out side by side at the Discussion layer, and the text itself does not adjudicate it.

[Fact] Verse 21, the close of the food section, two matters: "Ye shall not eat of anything that dieth of itself: thou shalt give it unto the stranger that is in thy gates, that he may eat it; or thou mayest sell it unto an alien: for thou art an holy people unto the LORD thy God. Thou shalt not seethe a kid in his mother's milk." Five items are registered: first, what dieth of itself (niḇlâ, the same word as at v. 8) may not be eaten — the object of the prohibition is the manner of death, not the species: a clean beast that dieth of itself likewise may not enter the mouth. Second, a way out is left for its disposal: it is to be given to the stranger sojourning in your town (gēr, H1616) to eat, or sold to an alien (nôḵrî, H5237) — the prohibition binds this people only and does not bind the neighbour; what may not be eaten is not poison; it is separation. Third, the reason-sentence sounds a second time: "for thou art an holy people unto the LORD thy God" — in the same form as verse 2: the food section begins with identity and ends with identity. Fourth, the prohibition on seething a kid: "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in his mother's milk" (lōʾ-ṯəḇaššēl gəḏî baḥălēḇ ʾimmô; ḇāšal, H1310; gəḏî, H1423; ḥālāḇ, H2461) — this sentence occurs three times in the Pentateuch: at Exodus 23:19 and 34:26 it follows immediately upon the command to bring the first of the firstfruits into the house of the LORD (both places checked directly on their verse-by-verse pages), and in this chapter it is placed at the end of the food section; the three contexts differ, the echo is registered only, and its sense is set out side by side at the Discussion layer. Fifth, at the end of this verse the BHS carries petuhah: the food section is formally closed here.

[Argument] This is an inference: verse 21 is the verse of the whole chapter that most shows its heart. A beast that dieth of itself is a forbidden thing for this people and food for the sojourner: the law does not command a man to throw the forbidden thing away, nor to hide it and enjoy or destroy it alone; it commands him to send it out — separation is not a licence for waste, and holiness does not take the poor man's rice bowl as its sacrifice. The sojourner is in your own town (at v. 21, "in thy gates") — he is not within the covenant, but he is within the town; his table is not governed by this law, but his stomach is remembered by this law. This is an inference. Again: the legislative technique of the bird list deserves recording — for what can be fully learned, give a standard; for what cannot be fully learned, give a list; for what is too numerous (the water animals), give the standard alone. Within a single chapter three methods are used together, and the teaching of separation does not rely on a single device. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] Two echoes are registered (registered as reports, not verified word for word): Acts 10–11, where Peter at Joppa sees a vision, a great sheet holding all manner of beasts and creeping things, and a voice saying that what God hath cleansed must not be called common — the picture of birds and creeping things in one sheet is on the same subject as the lists in this part; and 1 Timothy 4:3–5, treating of foods that are forbidden, whose verdict is that every creature of God is good, received with thanksgiving. Registered only, not read back: the two New Testament texts each have their own scene of argument and do not serve as notes on this part.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] First, on the relation between the two lists, three readings are set out side by side without adjudication: (a) the restatement reading — Deuteronomy 14 is a restatement and adaptation of Leviticus 11 on the plains of Moab: for land animals a positive list of ten names is added, for water animals the matter is cut down, the bird names are twenty-one against Leviticus 11's twenty (Leviticus has more in a different order; Deuteronomy has one more name, rāʾâ; the joining and dividing of the names in the two places is detailed in Endnote 4 — on the KJV letter the extra name is the "glede" at 14:13), for flying creeping things the locust exception is deleted, and for what dieth of itself the disposal is changed to giving it to the sojourner — the direction of the adaptation is to turn a priestly code into a list that the people can carry out at home; (b) the parallel-tradition reading — the two lists come from different traditions, each a piece in its own right, and the differences are differences between traditions, not traces of redaction; (c) the occasion reading — Leviticus 11 was promulgated to the tabernacle, Deuteronomy 14 to the households after entering the land, and the fullness or brevity follows the occasion. The three readings are set out side by side: the facts of the differences are at the Fact layer, and the judgment of their relation is not adjudicated in this manuscript. Second, on the origin of the prohibition on seething a kid, three readings are set out side by side without adjudication: (a) the ritual reading — it has been held to be directed against a Canaanite fertility rite of seething a kid in milk; in the twentieth century some scholars proposed, on the basis of the Ugaritic texts, a reading of a roughly similar sentence, but on re-examination it has mostly been held to be a misreading, and the documentary basis of this reading is weak — set out side by side with that doubt recorded; (b) the humane reading — Maimonides, in The Guide for the Perplexed, Part III, chapter 48, upholds this reading: a mother's milk is properly for nourishing the kid, and to seethe the kid in it is a cruel misplacing, forbidden as a teaching of a merciful heart; (c) the boundary reading — this prohibition belongs to the same group as the prohibitions on offerings of blood and leaven, a boundary law of separation unto holiness, whose force lies not in explanation but in observance; the later Jewish tradition developed from this sentence the whole body of dietary rules separating meat and milk (registered), whose scale goes far beyond the letter, the heaviest growth in tradition of a thrice-repeated short sentence. The three readings are set out side by side: this chapter registers only the echo among the three contexts (the firstfruits twice, a food list once) and chooses no reading.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part Three: birds are forbidden by list, creeping things as a whole class, and what dieth of itself by its manner of death; the forbidden thing is not thrown away but given to the sojourner in the town — separation and mercy stand together in the same verse.


### Part Four: Tithe a Tenth, and Eat It Before Him (14:22–27; 38:00–49:00)

[Fact] Verse 22, the command of the tithe: "Thou shalt truly tithe all the increase of thy seed, that the field bringeth forth year by year." Three items are registered: first, the person turns to the singular (see the separate register in item (x) of the header) — the tithe is promulgated to the head of a household. Second, "thou shalt truly tithe" in the original is ʿaśśēr təʿaśśēr (ʿāśar, H6237, the infinitive absolute strengthening the verb) — "thou shalt surely tithe a tenth": the tone of the command allows no bargaining. On the KJV letter the strengthening is carried by the adverb "truly," and the word "tithe" occurs once in the verse (see the count register in Endnote 2). Third, the base of the reckoning is "the increase of thy seed," "that the field bringeth forth year by year" (təḇûʾâ, H8393; zeraʿ, H2233) — the tithe is reckoned on the produce and settled by the year.

[Fact] Verse 23, the destination and purpose of the tithe: "And thou shalt eat before the LORD thy God, in the place which he shall choose to place his name there, the tithe of thy corn, of thy wine, and of thine oil, and the firstlings of thy herds and of thy flocks; that thou mayest learn to fear the LORD thy God always." Four items are registered: first, the contents are three kinds and one more: the tithe of thy corn (dāgān, H1715), of thy wine (tîrôš, H8492), and of thine oil (yiṣhār, H3323), and the firstlings of thy herds and of thy flocks (bəḵôr, H1060). Second, the destination is "before the LORD thy God" — the place of eating is the sanctuary, not the home: the first consumer of this tithe is the one who offers it himself, eating it before God. Third, the purpose sentence is express: "that thou mayest learn to fear the LORD thy God always" (ləmaʿan tilmad ləyirʾâ — lāmaḏ, H3925; yārēʾ, H3372) — reverence is learned, and the place where it is learned is the feast: the law sets a lesson in reverence at a yearly table. Fourth, "the firstlings" are set beside the tithe and sent with it to the sanctuary: how their usage agrees with the rule in Numbers 18 by which the firstlings belong to the Levites is set out side by side only (Endnote 6), and this chapter does not adjudicate it.

[Fact] Verses 24–25, the accommodation for a long road: "And if the way be too long for thee, so that thou art not able to carry it; or if the place be too far from thee, which the LORD thy God shall choose to set his name there, when the LORD thy God hath blessed thee: Then shalt thou turn it into money, and bind up the money in thine hand, and shalt go unto the place which the LORD thy God shall choose:" Four items are registered: first, the premise of the accommodation is written considerately: "when the LORD thy God hath blessed thee" — the road is too long to carry it precisely because the produce is much: the difficulty is the shape of being blessed. Second, the procedure has three steps: turn it into money (kesef, H3701), bind up the money in thine hand (ṣārar, H6887 — bind it up, tie it fast), and go to the chosen place: the produce is exchanged for money, and the money does not change the destination. Third, "it" in "able to carry it" in the original is śəʾētô (nāśāʾ, H5375 — to bear, to carry): the tithe is a burden carried on the way, and the law first acknowledges its weight. Fourth, the accommodation does not reduce the amount: exchanging it for money gives no discount, and the sanctuary makes no concession — the man from far away walks the long road, and the law lightens his luggage for him without changing his destination for him.

[Fact] Verse 26, the freedom of the feast: "And thou shalt bestow that money for whatsoever thy soul lusteth after, for oxen, or for sheep, or for wine, or for strong drink, or for whatsoever thy soul desireth: and thou shalt eat there before the LORD thy God, and thou shalt rejoice, thou, and thine household," Three items are registered: first, "whatsoever thy soul lusteth after" (bəḵol ʾăšer-təʾawweh nap̄šeḵā — whatsoever thy soul desireth; nep̄eš, H5315): at the feast before God, the menu is ordered by the offerer himself — oxen or sheep, wine or strong drink (yayin, H3196; šēḵār, H7941), whatsoever thy soul desireth may be bought. Second, those at the table with him are "thou, and thine household": the unit of the feast is the household. Third, the atmosphere of the feast is joy as a command: "thou shalt rejoice" (śāmaḥ, H8055) — joy here is not a permission but a duty: the lesson in learning reverence (v. 23) uses joyful teaching material.

[Fact] Verse 27, the reminder at the feast's boundary: "And the Levite that is within thy gates; thou shalt not forsake him; for he hath no part nor inheritance with thee." Three items are registered: first, the reminder is inserted between the clauses of the feast: however lively the feast at the sanctuary, the Levite in the locality must not be forgotten (ʿāzaḇ, H5800 — thou shalt not forsake, shalt not leave him). Second, the reason is that "he hath no part nor inheritance" (ḥēleq, H2506; naḥălâ, H5159) — he has no field, and if his name is left off the guest list of the tithe feast, his absence is no accident but structural: the man without produce can only rely on the man with produce remembering him. Third, at the end of this verse the BHS carries samekh; verses 27 and 29 are two reminders on the same subject in the chapter, one inserted in the festival tithe and one falling in the third-year tithe: the Levite's meal is named in both places.

[Argument] This is an inference: the most unexpected thing in this part is that the first use of the tithe turns out to be eating it oneself. The offerer thought a tenth was something handed over; this chapter says it is something carried out to be eaten — carried before God, eaten with the household, and eaten whatsoever the soul desireth. Reverence is not learned in austerity but in abundance: once a year, to set a tenth of the harvest before God and eat it is to acknowledge once a year that the other nine tenths were also given by Him. Eating is this chapter's method of teaching reverence. This is an inference. Again: the exchange for money on account of a long road is a sample of the law's considerateness — it does not lower the standard, it only lessens the weight; it does not allow a man to do as he pleases at home, but it does allow him to exchange his burden for money — the principle does not move, and the method can be accommodated. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] Two echoes are registered (registered as reports, not verified word for word): Matthew 23:23, treating of the tithe, where the Lord's words affirm, besides "the weightier matters," that the tithe "ought ye to have done" and not to leave undone — the subject of the tithe is raised again in the New Testament, with the order of weight arranged otherwise; and Hebrews 7, which unfolds its argument on the theme of the Levites receiving tithes. Registered only, not read back: the festival tithe of this part is the festival system of the people of the old covenant, and the two New Testament places each have their own object of argument; the rule of the one is not used to alter the rule of the other.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] On the usage of the tithe in its several places, two readings are set out side by side without adjudication: (a) the one-tithe-many-uses reading — Leviticus 27 fixes its belonging, Numbers 18 fixes it for the Levites, and Deuteronomy 14 fixes the festival feast and the relief of every third year: these are three disbursements of one and the same tithe, and the clauses complement one another; (b) the several-tithes reading — the places are different items: one for the Levites, one for the offerer's own festival use, and one of every third year for the poor, amounting in total to more than one tenth; the later Jewish tradition indeed arranged them under three names, "the first tithe, the second tithe, and the poor tithe" (registered). The two readings are set out side by side: the text of this chapter establishes only its own two entries (the yearly festival entry and the relief entry of the third year), this chapter does not answer how they are to be reckoned together with the other places, and this manuscript does not adjudicate; the relation of the cross-references has been registered in the notes to the manuscript on Numbers 18 and is followed here.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part Four: the tithe first becomes a feast, and reverence is learned in eating and drinking; a long road may be exchanged for money, and the standard takes no discount; between the clauses of the feast, one sentence is inserted — thou shalt not forsake the Levite.

### Part Five: In the Third Year, Lay It Up Within Thy Gates (14:28–29; 49:00–57:00)

[Fact] Verse 28, the arrangement of the third year: "At the end of three years thou shalt bring forth all the tithe of thine increase the same year, and shalt lay it up within thy gates:" Three items are registered: first, the cycle is three years: "at the end of three years" — not every year, but at every third year. Second, the action is "bring forth … and lay it up within thy gates" (bišʿārêkā — within your gates): this tithe does not travel far and does not go up to the sanctuary; it stays in the locality. Third, the "all" in "all the tithe" (kol-maʿśar — the whole tithe): the tenth of the third year is transferred in its entirety to local use, with not a fraction left for another purpose. The third-year account, recounted on the KJV letter, is the same in its facts as in the Chinese register: one whole tithe of that year, brought forth in full and laid up within the gates, for the four groups of the next verse.

[Fact] Verse 29, the list of beneficiaries and the promise: "And the Levite, (because he hath no part nor inheritance with thee,) and the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow, which are within thy gates, shall come, and shall eat and be satisfied; that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all the work of thine hand which thou doest." Four items are registered: first, the beneficiary list has four groups: the Levite (still marked by "no part nor inheritance"), the stranger, the fatherless (yāṯôm, H3490), and the widow (ʾalmānâ, H490) — the common point of the four groups is one and the same: none of them has a field of his own. Second, the verbs are "shall come, and shall eat and be satisfied" (śāḇaʿ, H7646): not to receive a ration of relief grain and leave, but to come and eat until satisfied — the dignity of relief is written in the verbs. Third, the promise sentence closes the chapter: "that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all the work of thine hand which thou doest" (bāraḵ, H1288) — all the work of thine hand: the range of the blessing extends from the field to every work of the hands. Fourth, at the end of this verse the BHS carries samekh, and the chapter is closed here; the last sentence of the chapter is not a prohibition but a blessing — the food section begins with identity, and the tithe section ends with blessing.

[Argument] This is an inference: the tithe in this chapter has two directions of flow — upward, carried before God and eaten by the whole household; and sideways, left in the town and eaten by all. The two directions use the same money, the same proportion, and the same reason — remember the One who gave you produce, and remember those who have no produce. The tenth of the third year does not leave the town, and the considerateness extends to geography: the poor cannot eat the feast of the sanctuary, so the law moves the feast into their town. This is an inference. Again: "shall eat and be satisfied" is the weightiest sentence of the chapter to the poor: the law does not say, "give them a little, lest they starve"; it says satisfied — read against the offerer's own "whatsoever thy soul desireth … thou shalt rejoice" at verse 26: the standard for giving to others is the standard of one's own feast. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] Two echoes are registered (registered as reports, not verified word for word): Acts 4:34–35 records that among the believers "neither was there any among them that lacked," because distribution was made according to need — the picture of those without produce being satisfied appears again in the New Testament church; and 2 Corinthians 9:7 treats of giving as each man purposeth in his heart, not grudgingly or of necessity — the question of the heart in relief is set as a separate lesson in the New Testament. Registered only, not read back: this part is a clause of law, and the two New Testament texts are the church's practice and teaching, each with its own context.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] First, on the relation between the third-year tithe and Deuteronomy 26:12, two readings are set out side by side without adjudication: (a) the same-entry reading — the rule of the third year recorded at 26:12, in effect, "when thou hast made an end of tithing … thou shalt give it unto the Levite, and unto the stranger, to the fatherless, and to the widow" (registered as a report), is this same entry as verses 28–29 of this chapter, chapter 26 attaching to it a confession to be spoken at the offering, while this chapter has no confession; (b) the echo reading — the two places are two promulgations of one and the same system, one establishing the rule in this chapter and one supplying its spoken text in chapter 26. The two readings are set out side by side: the facts of this chapter go no further than the clauses themselves. Second, on how the two tithe entries of this chapter fit the festival calendar (how the overlapping year is reckoned, when the festival entry is yearly and the relief entry runs in a three-year cycle), the text does not make it plain; it is set out side by side as doubtful, without adjudication.

[Summary] The governing sentence of Part Five: every three years, the whole tithe stays in the town; four groups of people without fields come and eat and are satisfied; the chapter closes with a blessing — what is given out is remembered in all the work of thine hand.

### Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Fact] The chapter read back over: 29 verses, two sections; the divine name eleven times, concentrated in the identity sentences and the tithe section (vv. 1, 2, 21, 23, 24, 25, 26, 29), and not once in the list section (vv. 3–20). The identity sentence occurs twice (vv. 2, 21) enclosing the lists; "no part nor inheritance" occurs twice (vv. 27, 29) as the reminder concerning the Levite; the blessing occurs once (v. 29) and closes the chapter.

[Argument] This is an inference: this chapter puts faith in the place where it is hardest to hide — the table. The separation of the three meals of the day, the feast once a year, the relief of every third year: if holiness can stand here, it can stand anywhere else as well. The order of the chapter is still the whole of its method: first children, then the menu; first election, then boundaries; and within the boundaries, first the abundance of permission, then the satisfaction of giving. This is an inference.

[Summary] The governing sentence of the conclusion: you are children, therefore eat in this way; you have produce, therefore share in this way; and between the eating and the sharing stands a God who blesses.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotional use)

The first sentence Deuteronomy 14 speaks is not a menu but an identity: "Ye are the children of the LORD your God." "For thou art an holy people unto the LORD thy God, and the LORD hath chosen thee to be a peculiar people unto himself, above all the nations that are upon the earth." Separation is never something eaten into being: we are children first, and only then does the table receive its boundaries; chosen first, and only then do the days receive their separation. The place one passes most often in a day is this very table — the three meals of a day are the most daily testing ground of holiness. The second half of this chapter sets out another table: every third year, the whole tenth stays in the town, so that "the Levite, (because he hath no part nor inheritance with thee,) and the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow, which are within thy gates, shall come, and shall eat and be satisfied." Those who have produce learn reverence by eating whatsoever their soul desireth at the feast of the sanctuary; those who have no produce are remembered by eating and being satisfied at the store in the locality. The standard for giving to others is the standard of one's own feast — this is the measure this chapter sets for generosity. The promise at the chapter's end is: "that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all the work of thine hand which thou doest." Today, first remember who you are, and only then decide what you will eat and what you will give: identity first — boundaries and generosity alike are the daily life of children.

(Scripture quoted in the devotional excerpt: Deut 14:1–2, 14:29, KJV, verbatim from the anchor.)

### Devotional Extract (KJV)

The first word of Deuteronomy 14 is not a menu but an identity: "Ye are the children of the LORD your God." "For thou art an holy people unto the LORD thy God, and the LORD hath chosen thee to be a peculiar people unto himself, above all the nations that are upon the earth." Remember who you are first; then let identity govern both your boundaries and your giving.

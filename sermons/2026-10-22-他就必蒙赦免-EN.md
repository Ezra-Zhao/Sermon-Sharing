# It Shall Be Forgiven Him — The Unintentional Sinner and the Sin Offering

- Title: It Shall Be Forgiven Him
- Scripture: Leviticus 4:1–5:13 (King James Version, KJV)
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft · Pending cross-review (Sermon Mass-Production Batch 2, 2026-10-22, unpublished) — English version: first translation, pending review; translated from the Chinese original

## Abstract

The fourth chapter of Leviticus is not about the person who deliberately defies God, but about the person who only learns of the wrong after doing it. The anointed priest, the whole congregation, the ruler, and the common person — four ranks — follow one set of rules for the sin offering, with clear gradations in where the blood is applied and how the animal is disposed of. In chapter 5, confession becomes a requirement, and the offering steps down according to each person's means, until even the poorest person, bringing a handful of fine flour, still hears the same sentence: it shall be forgiven him.

## 00:00-08:00 Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all.

Today we come to the fourth chapter of Leviticus. In the first three chapters — the burnt offering, the grain offering, the peace offering — the worshiper could come willingly to draw near to God, and the blood and fat of the animals, the fine flour and frankincense, all had clear rules. In chapter 4, the mood changes. It still opens with the familiar words, "And the LORD spake unto Moses," but what follows no longer carries the willing tone of "if any man of you bring an offering." Instead it says: if a soul shall sin through ignorance against any of the commandments of the LORD concerning things which ought not to be done. Our sermon takes its starting point here: sin through ignorance — in Hebrew, bishgagah. Not deliberate defiance, not a fist shaken at heaven, but a deed done, and only afterward known to be wrong, and once known, impossible to set down in the heart.

Please note that today's text is long, from Leviticus 4:1 to 5:13. On the surface it reads like a ritual manual. Four ranks take their turns: the anointed priest, all the congregation of Israel, a ruler, and one of the common people. The wording repeats, the actions repeat, and it can feel repetitive. But I ask you to listen, within the repetition, for one voice. At the end of the rules for each rank, everything lands on the same sentence: the priest shall make an atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him. It is so with the bull, so with the goat, so with the lamb; and in chapter 5, even with the poor person's two birds and tenth of an ephah of fine flour, the paragraph still ends with the same words: it shall be forgiven him. That sentence is driven through the whole passage like a nail, one place after another. Today we will not hurry. We will follow those six nails down, and read this seemingly cumbersome code as a preview of the gospel for the unintentional sinner. One thing must be said clearly at the outset: how the New Testament reads this text belongs to the level of New Testament reading, which we will treat separately later. First, let us honestly read the text itself and see what this code actually says.

Let us read several key verses together, in the King James Version:

"If a soul shall sin through ignorance against any of the commandments of the LORD concerning things which ought not to be done, and shall do against any of them." (Lev 4:2, excerpted)

"Or if his sin, wherein he hath sinned, come to his knowledge; he shall bring his offering." (Lev 4:23, excerpted, cf. KJV)

"And it shall be, when he shall be guilty in one of these things, that he shall confess that he hath sinned in that thing." (Lev 5:5, KJV)

"And the priest shall burn it upon the altar... and the priest shall make an atonement for him as concerning his sin, and it shall be forgiven him." (Lev 5:13, excerpted, cf. KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, few of us set out to do evil, yet every one of us sins through ignorance: a word spoken wrongly that wounded someone without our knowing, a deed done that we thought was right, and then, once we know, unrest in the night. Speak to us today through this ancient code. Let us first see the seriousness of sin clearly, and then hear the certainty of forgiveness clearly. In Jesus' name, Amen.

First, let me draw the map of the whole passage, so we do not get lost in the details. Chapter 4 is the basic code for the sin offering, divided by the person who offers into four ranks: verses 1–12, the anointed priest sins; verses 13–21, the whole congregation sins through ignorance; verses 22–26, a ruler sins; verses 27–35, one of the common people sins through ignorance. The order is not random. It is arranged by nearness to the sanctuary and by the weight of status: the nearer a person stands to the sanctuary, and the more people he represents, the greater the impact of his sin, and the heavier the procedure. Leviticus 5:1–13 is a sequel of cases, no longer divided by person but illustrated by deed: three concrete sins — hearing the voice of swearing and withholding testimony, touching uncleanness without knowing it, and swearing rashly — followed by the requirement of confession and offerings graded by economic means. That is today's route: first the four ranks, then the three deeds; first how the blood is sprinkled, then what the mouth must say; and at the end, a count of how many times that sentence, it shall be forgiven him, actually appears, and upon whom each occurrence falls.

## 08:00-24:00 Part One: The Anointed Priest and the Whole Congregation — The Bull, Blood Before the Veil, Ashes Outside the Camp (4:1-21)

First, the first rank: the anointed priest.

The text says that if the priest that is anointed do sin according to the sin of the people, bringing guilt on the people, he shall bring for his sin a young bullock without blemish unto the LORD for a sin offering. Pause and think: who is the priest? He is the man who stands daily in the sanctuary offering sacrifices for the people, the man who knows the rules best and stands nearest to God. Even such a man can sin, and the text uses heavy words: his sin brings guilt on the people. The high priest's fault is not his private matter alone. As weighty as his status is, so wide is the impact of his sin. That is the first lesson of the four ranks: the nearer to the sanctuary, the heavier the responsibility. I set this down first as the factual layer: the arrangement of the text itself is the argument; no embellishment from us is needed.

Next, the procedure. The bull is brought to the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, the priest lays his hand on the bull's head, and it is killed before the LORD. The laying on of the hand appears in the earlier chapters as well; here the commentaries mostly read it as identification and belonging: this animal is offered for this sin and this person, not an animal seized at random. Then comes the most distinctive passage in the whole chapter: the priest takes some of the bull's blood and brings it into the tabernacle, dips his finger in the blood and sprinkles it seven times before the LORD, before the veil of the sanctuary; he puts some of the blood on the horns of the altar of sweet incense in the tabernacle; and he pours all the remaining blood at the bottom of the altar of the burnt offering, which is at the door of the tabernacle. Mark this route clearly: the blood enters the tabernacle, is sprinkled before the veil, and is applied to the incense altar. Of the four ranks, only the priest and the congregation receive this treatment, and it is the heaviest grade. Why must the priest's sin bring blood so deep? Scholarship offers one weighty explanation, set out in full in the notes: the blood of the sin offering acts mainly on the altar and the sanctuary, purging a sanctuary polluted by sin; the nearer the sin reaches to the core, the deeper the blood must go. This explanation belongs to commentary discussion, not to the explicit words of the text, and this sermon states the level honestly and draws no final conclusion.

The fat of the bull is removed according to the rules for the fat of the peace offering and burned on the altar of burnt offering. But the hide of the bull, all its flesh, its head, legs, inwards and dung — the whole remainder of the bull — is carried outside the camp to a clean place, where the ashes are poured out, and burned there on wood with fire. Of a whole bull, only the fat goes on the altar; everything else leaves the camp to be burned, and neither priest nor people eat a mouthful. We will contrast this later: for the common person's sin offering, the priest may eat the flesh; only in these two grades, where the blood enters the tabernacle, is the flesh wholly burned and nothing kept. The weight of the code is hidden in details like these.

Second, all the congregation of Israel sins through ignorance. The text says that if the whole congregation sins through ignorance against any of the commandments concerning things which ought not to be done, and the thing is hidden from the eyes of the assembly, then, when the sin is known, the congregation shall offer a young bullock for a sin offering. The elders lay their hands on the bull's head, and it is killed before the LORD. The blood procedure is exactly the same as for the priest: brought into the tabernacle, sprinkled seven times before the veil, applied to the horns of the incense altar, the remainder poured at the bottom of the altar of burnt offering, the fat on the altar, and the body burned outside the camp. That the congregation stands in the same grade as the priest is itself worth pondering: the collective fault of a whole community weighs, before God, the same as the personal fault of the high priest. A thing done wrongly together cannot be met with "it has nothing to do with me." When the congregation knows, the elders come forward, and the congregation offers together. Here the text draws a line for communal life: the sin of a community must be confessed, offered for, and cleansed by the community; it cannot be pushed onto one person to carry, nor pretended away.

At the end of this grade, the set formula appears for the first time, in the plural: the priest shall make an atonement for them, and it shall be forgiven them. The word for forgiveness will be treated in detail in the notes; note the location here: Leviticus 4:20, the first occurrence in the passage. The second rank stands beside the first and reminds us today: the church is one body, and when the body is ill, it is not the private affair of one member; confession and cleansing are never merely individual exercises either. Let me gather the common points of the first two grades into three sentences: first, the offering in both is a bull, the most costly of the four grades; second, in both the blood enters the tabernacle, going the deepest of the four grades; third, in both the remainder of the animal is burned outside the camp, the most thorough disposal of the four grades. Costly, deep, thorough — corresponding exactly to the weight of status and impact. In the next two grades, the procedure steps down, level by level. But what steps down is not the forgiveness; what steps down is the scale of the rite. That distinction is the key to the whole chapter, so carry it with you as we go on.

## 24:00-42:00 Part Two: The Ruler and the Common Person — Blood Only on the Altar of Burnt Offering, the Flesh to the Priest (4:22-35)

Third, a ruler sins.

The wording changes. For the priest and the congregation the text speaks of sinning and sinning through ignorance; for the ruler it says: when a ruler hath sinned, and done somewhat through ignorance against any of the commandments of the LORD his God concerning things which should not be done, and is guilty. Then comes a key sentence: or if his sin wherein he hath sinned come to his knowledge — whether someone makes it known to him or he comes to know it himself — he shall bring his offering, a kid of the goats, a male without blemish. Sin pointed out, or sin self-discovered: this is the trigger the ruler's grade spells out. A leader has a particular difficulty: few people dare to tell him his fault to his face. Yet the text writes being told into the code itself, as if God already knew that what a ruler needs most is precisely someone who dares to say to him, you were wrong. And once told, what then? Not excuses, not suppression — he brings a male goat, lays his hand on it, kills it, and offers it as a sin offering.

Here the procedure drops one grade. The priest takes the blood with his finger and puts it on the horns of the altar of burnt offering, pours out the blood at the bottom of the altar of burnt offering, and burns the fat on the altar. No entering the tabernacle, no sprinkling before the veil, no incense altar. The blood works only on the altar of burnt offering in the courtyard. Why? Read by the structure of the chapter, a ruler's influence does not reach the core of the sanctuary as the priest's and the congregation's do, and the range polluted by his fault stops at the outer altar. The scale has dropped, but the closing sentence has not dropped at all: the priest shall make an atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him. This is the second occurrence of the set formula in the passage, Leviticus 4:26. Note that the word of forgiveness is spoken to the ruler with exactly the same certainty as to the congregation. The code grades the rite; it does not grade the forgiveness. That is one of the most important theological facts of the chapter, and this sermon marks it as an exposition drawn from the structure, grounded in the fact of the formula's verbatim repetition itself.

Fourth, one of the common people sins through ignorance. This is the rank farthest from the sanctuary — ordinary people like you and me. The text says that if any one of the common people sins through ignorance by doing something against any of the commandments concerning things which ought not to be done, and is guilty, and the sin is known to him, or is made known to him, he shall bring his offering: a kid of the goats, a female without blemish, or a lamb, a female without blemish, for his sin. The offering drops another step: from bull to male goat, then to female goat and female lamb, cheaper at each level, more ordinary at each level of status. The blood procedure is the same grade as the ruler's: applied to the horns of the altar of burnt offering, poured at the bottom of the altar, the fat on the altar. But the ending repeats twice: for the female goat it says, it shall be forgiven him, Leviticus 4:31; for the female lamb it says it again, it shall be forgiven him, Leviticus 4:35. Twice in a single rank, as if God feared ordinary people might miss it the first time and said it again on purpose. Brothers and sisters, by this point in the chapter the order is already plain: the more ordinary the person, the lighter the offering — yet not one word less of forgiveness. With the four ranks counted, the set formula has appeared four times: once in the plural and three times in the singular, falling on the congregation, the ruler, the common person with a goat, and the common person with a lamb; with the two further occurrences in chapter 5, six in all, listed one by one in the notes.

Two further facts must be stated clearly. First, burning the remainder of the animal outside the camp applies only to the first two grades. Leviticus 4:11–12, for the priest's bull, and 4:21, for the congregation's bull: hide, flesh and inwards are all carried outside the camp to the place where the ashes are poured out and burned. For the goat and lamb of the last two grades, whose blood does not enter the tabernacle, the general rule later in Leviticus applies: the flesh of such a sin offering belongs to the officiating priest to eat; it is the priest's most holy portion. The same name, sin offering — one wholly burned, one eaten — and the dividing line is whether the blood entered the tabernacle. That dividing line is a ritual fact; this sermon states it and draws no further inference. Second, the boundary of sin through ignorance. One qualifier runs through the whole chapter: through ignorance, bishgagah. The Hebrew and its contrast are set out in the notes; here the tension must be stated plainly: by the contrast of Numbers 15, for the soul that does presumptuously — with a high hand — there is no sin offering to bring, and that soul shall be cut off from among his people. In other words, this code covers inadvertent failure, negligent failure, failure recognised only afterward; it does not cover the clenched fist of deliberation. This boundary is unsettling to read, yet it must be set out honestly side by side: on one side the text speaks the word of forgiveness very widely, including the most ordinary and the poorest; on the other side it draws the threshold very clearly, and deliberate sin is not within the list. Breadth and strictness stand in one chapter, and that is precisely Leviticus' way of speaking of forgiveness: forgiveness is real, but never cheap; there is a way out of sin, but the way opens only for the person willing to turn back. If someone among us today is reproaching himself over a thing done unknowingly that will not leave him alone, this text is read for you; if someone is planning to sin deliberately and counting on patching it up afterward with a ritual, this text is also read for you — only in the opposite direction.

Gather the four ranks into one picture: priest and congregation — a bull, blood into the tabernacle, the remainder outside the camp; ruler — a male goat, blood on the outer altar; common person — a female goat or a female lamb, blood on the outer altar. Four rows side by side: the greater the influence, the heavier the rite; the lighter the status, the cheaper the offering. But one column is identical in all four rows — the last column: it shall be forgiven him. The rite steps down by status; forgiveness is equal by promise. That is the skeleton of chapter 4. The greatest danger in reading sacrificial codes is reading them as lists; in fact, order hides inside the list: God measures sin not only by the deed itself but also by the position and influence of the doer; yet God grants forgiveness with no discount by position. The leader's sin is heavier, the commoner's forgiveness is no lighter — this heaviness and this lightness in one chapter are fairness for the whole congregation.

## 42:00-56:00 Part Three: Three Concrete Sins, Confession, and the Poor Person's Fine Flour (5:1-13)

In chapter 5 the form changes. Chapter 4 divided by person; chapter 5 illustrates by deed — three concrete sins in a row, all things that really happen in daily life.

The first: hearing the voice of swearing, called to testify, and withholding testimony. The text says that if a soul sin, and hear the voice of swearing, and is a witness, whether he hath seen or known of it, if he does not utter it, then he shall bear his iniquity. A witness in court who keeps silent is, today, obstructing justice; in Leviticus it is sin. Knowing the truth and staying silent, letting the guilty escape and the wronged go unheard — this thing, in which a person seems to have done nothing at all, Scripture counts as guilt. Silence here is not a virtue; it is concealment.

The second: touching uncleanness without knowing it. If a soul touch any unclean thing, whether the carcase of an unclean beast, or of unclean cattle, or of unclean creeping things, and it is hidden from him, he also shall be unclean and guilty — once he knows it. The sin here is not in the touching itself; at the time, he did not know. The sin lies after the knowing. Having learned that he has contracted uncleanness, he still enters the sanctuary as usual and lives as usual, carrying uncleanness into worship — that is the problem. While he did not know, it was sin through ignorance; knowing and not dealing with it slides, step by step, toward deliberation. Chapter 4's sin through ignorance and chapter 5's hidden-from-him meet precisely here.

The third: swearing rashly. If a soul swear, pronouncing with his lips to do evil or to do good, whatsoever it be that a man shall pronounce with an oath in haste, and it is hidden from him, when he knoweth of it, then he shall be guilty in one of these. The words left the mouth in a hurry, offhand, unconsidered; only when he recalls them does he realise he promised God and men things he cannot do. The sin of the rash oath is taking one's own words lightly. Once spoken, words are not finished just because they were said.

After the three deeds, Leviticus 5:5 gives the master action of the whole passage: and it shall be, when he shall be guilty in one of these things, that he shall confess that he hath sinned in that thing. Confession, hitvaddah — the Hebrew has its own note; mark its weight here first: in chapter 5, the sin offering for the first time writes open confession into the code as an explicit requirement. Chapter 4 spoke of hands: laying on hands, killing, sprinkling blood. Chapter 5 adds the action of the mouth: say it — I sinned in this thing. The sin must be named, not blurred. Not "I am no good," not "I have been in poor shape lately," but this thing: I heard and did not testify; this thing: I contracted uncleanness, knew it, and did not cleanse it; this thing: I treated a rashly spoken promise as nothing. Confession that reaches the specific is the only kind forgiveness can land on specifically.

Then comes the offering, and the most moving thing in chapter 5 is right here: the offering steps down according to each person's means. First the standard grade: a female lamb or a female goat as a sin offering. And if he cannot? The text says, if he be not able to bring a lamb — if his means fall short — he may bring two turtledoves or two young pigeons, one for a sin offering and one for a burnt offering. Birds are far cheaper than a lamb; the poor can keep them and can buy them. And below that, there are people who cannot bring even birds: if he be not able to bring two turtledoves or two young pigeons, he shall bring the tenth part of an ephah of fine flour for a sin offering; he shall put no oil upon it, neither shall he put any frankincense thereon, for it is a sin offering. Fine flour, without blood. After a whole chapter of the blood of sin offerings, at the very end of the passage appears a sin offering without blood: the priest takes a handful of the flour and burns it on the altar; the rest belongs to the priest, and the text says it is as the grain offering. And then the set formula appears for the fifth time, and for the last complete time in the sacrificial code: it shall be forgiven him, Leviticus 5:13. The other occurrence is in verse 10, for the grade of the birds: the priest shall make an atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him. Twice: once for the person who can afford birds, once for the person who can afford only flour.

This exception of a bloodless sin offering is much discussed in the commentaries, and this sermon sets out two readings honestly side by side: one reading holds that the flour here is a substitute, that the poor person's flour is reckoned as a sin offering through the fire of the altar and the priest's intercession, and that the point of the code is that God apportions grace according to means; the other reading reminds us that this handful of flour is burned on the altar together with the fire of the altar's other offerings, that the flour itself sprinkles no blood, and that the basis of forgiveness still stands within the whole sacrificial order — one cannot conclude from this case that blood does not matter. The two readings stand side by side, with no final verdict. But one thing is explicit in the text: the poorest person is not shut outside the door. Means step down in three grades — lamb, birds, flour; the grades are economic, the forgiveness is one and the same sentence. The well-off person brings a lamb and hears, it shall be forgiven him; the poor person comes holding a handful of flour and hears the same, it shall be forgiven him. Here God's code shows a kind of tenderness: He measures each person's means, and does not use the poor person's shortfall as a reason to refuse him.

Now let us make the full count of the set formula for the passage. Follow me: Leviticus 4:20, the congregation — it shall be forgiven them, plural; 4:26, the ruler — it shall be forgiven him; 4:31, the common person with a female goat — it shall be forgiven him; 4:35, the common person with a female lamb — it shall be forgiven him; 5:10, the person offering birds — it shall be forgiven him; 5:13, the person offering fine flour — it shall be forgiven him. Six occurrences across the passage. The priest's own grade has no separate sentence, because the plural of the congregation's grade — same grade, same rite — includes the priest within it; that is the reading of most commentators, and this sermon marks it as commentary discussion. Sixfold repetition is not verbosity of style; it is the punctuation of the code: at the end of each grade, God stamps the seal with His own hand — forgiveness is effective. People who find Leviticus tedious have mostly mistaken repetition for redundancy; in fact, repetition is this book's way of preaching: the important thing is said again for the next rank down, until the most ordinary and the poorest person has heard it with his own ears, and only then is it fully said.

Finally, in keeping with this sermon's format, the New Testament reading is marked as its own level, not filled back in as the plain sense of the original text. New Testament reading: Hebrews 9–10 looks back on this code and says that these offerings cannot take away sins, that it is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins, and that priests offering year by year the same sacrifices only bring sins to remembrance; and it says that Christ, having offered himself once, accomplished an eternal redemption. That is the New Testament's interpretation of the sin offering, the church's reading for two thousand years, and in sermon use this sermon continues that reading: the sixfold it shall be forgiven him of Leviticus is read, in the light of the New Testament, as a preview, pointing to a forgiveness accomplished once for all. First John 1:9 then takes up chapter 5's confession: if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins. That too is New Testament reading and sermon use: confession remains the requirement, forgiveness remains the promise; only the altar has become the cross, and the priest has become Christ. Distinguishing the levels does not weaken the text; it honours what each testament says in its own words: what Leviticus itself says, we preach in its own words; how the New Testament continues it, we preach with the level marked. When both levels are honest, faith stands firm.

Just two applications. First: once you know, do not delay any longer. The road to forgiveness for sin through ignorance begins at the moment of knowing: pointed out by another, or discovered by oneself — the text writes that starting point for both ruler and commoner. Left unconfessed, sin through ignorance ferments into deliberation; confess it today, and the sin still stands in the grade of ignorance, with an offering to bring and a word to hear. Second: let confession reach the specific. The confession of chapter 5 is not a mood; it is a naming: in that thing. Bring them one by one before God — the thing you heard and did not testify to, the thing you knew and did not cleanse, the rashly spoken word you did not honour — and say them out. The word of forgiveness is spoken six times in this text: enough for every one of us to receive it once, with some to spare.

## 58:00-60:00 Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this passage today we have walked past four ranks of people, three deeds, and six occurrences of one sentence. The anointed priest can sin; the whole congregation can sin through ignorance; the ruler needs someone who dares to point out his fault; the common person's sin must likewise be dealt with. The person who hears the call to testify and keeps silent is guilty; the person who contracts uncleanness, knows it, and does not cleanse it is guilty; the person who treats a rashly sworn word as nothing is guilty. All along the way, the rite steps down by status and by means, from a bull down to a handful of fine flour — only that sentence never steps down from beginning to end: it shall be forgiven him. Sin through ignorance is no small matter, and so there must be an offering; sin through ignorance is no dead end, and so after the offering there is forgiveness. This most ancient book of ritual, in its most repetitive phrasing, does the gentlest of things: it lets every rank of person hear, with his own ears, his own share.

Let us pray together: Lord, we place before You, one by one, the sins we know of today — without vagueness and without delay. Forgive us what we did wrong unknowingly, yet truly, wounding others and falling short of You; and restrain us, lest sin through ignorance slide into deliberation. Thank You that in the most ancient code You already left a place for the most ordinary and the poorest person, and that even a handful of fine flour counts. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Notes

[1] chattat: Hebrew חַטָּאת (chattat, H2403), with a double sense: it can denote sin itself, and it can denote the sin offering. It is the title word of this passage: at Lev 4:3, 4:8, 4:14 and elsewhere, sin and sin offering share the one word — the offering is named for the sin, and the sin is removed by the offering. Definitions per BDB, s.v.; HALOT, s.v. In this sermon, "sin offering" throughout renders the sacrificial sense of this word.

[2] shagag and shgagah: the verb שָׁגַג (shagag, H7683), to err unintentionally, to sin through ignorance; the noun שְׁגָגָה (shgagah, H7684), an inadvertent error, an unintentional fault. Lev 4:2, 4:13, 4:22 and 4:27 repeatedly use the noun shgagah to define the nature of the sin in the whole passage. Definitions per BDB and HALOT. For the contrast, see note [3].

[3] beyad ramah and the boundary of sin through ignorance: Num 15:30-31 states explicitly that the soul that does presumptuously — beyad ramah (בְּיָד רָמָה, "with a high hand," defiantly) — shall be cut off from among his people, with no offering to bring. This contrast is the tension this sermon sets out honestly in the main text: the sin offering covers only sin through ignorance, and deliberate sin lies outside the code. Fact and exposition kept distinct: the scope of the code is the explicit text; the reading "no offering for deliberate sin" is the standard exegesis drawn from the Numbers contrast.

[4] The kipper / kofer family: the verb כִּפֶּר (kipper, H3722, the Piel of kaphar), to make atonement, to cover, to purge; the cognate noun kofer denotes a ransom price. In this passage, Lev 4:20, 4:26, 4:31, 4:35 and 5:10, 5:13 all use this verb for atonement. Definitions per BDB and HALOT.

[5] Milgrom's sanctuary-purgation view: Jacob Milgrom, in the Anchor Bible Leviticus commentary, argues that the blood of the sin offering acts mainly on the altar and the sanctuary, its function being to purge a sanctuary polluted by sin; sin pollutes the sanctuary to different depths according to the offerer's status, which is why the blood for priest and congregation must enter the tabernacle, while the common person's blood stays on the outer altar. Set side by side with the traditional substitutionary reading (the blood acts directly on the offerer, the animal suffering in the person's place): the former stresses the purging of the sanctuary, the latter personal substitution. This sermon draws no final conclusion: its structural exposition follows the former, its sermon use also draws on the latter, with the levels marked separately.

[6] salach and venislach lo: the verb סָלַח (salach, H5545), to forgive; in the Old Testament its subject is always God. The set formula וְנִסְלַח לוֹ (venislach lo, "and it shall be forgiven him," Niphal form) is counted six times in the passage in all: Lev 4:20 (plural לָהֶם, lahem), 4:26, 4:31, 4:35, 5:10, 5:13. Definitions and form per BDB and HALOT; the count verified verse by verse.

[7] asham: Hebrew אָשָׁם (asham, H817), guilt, liability for guilt; also the name of the guilt offering. At Lev 5:2, 5:3 and 5:4, "shall be guilty" uses the verbal form of this word, denoting a person's entering a state of guilt and bearing liability for it. Definitions per BDB and HALOT. In this sermon, "is guilty" renders this word.

[8] hitvaddah: Hebrew הִתְוַדָּה (hitvaddah, H3034, the Hithpael of yadah), to acknowledge, to confess. Lev 5:5, "he shall confess that he hath sinned," uses this word, the classic Old Testament locus for the language of confession. Definitions per BDB and HALOT.

[9] Blood entering the tabernacle and the disposal of the flesh: Lev 4:11-12 and 4:21, the remainder of the bull burned outside the camp where the ashes are poured out, are explicit text. That the flesh of a sin offering whose blood is brought into the tabernacle may not be eaten but must be burned, while where the blood stays on the outer altar the flesh belongs to the priest, is the general rule of Lev 6:24-30. This sermon's statement that in the last two grades the flesh belongs to the priest follows that general rule, not an explicit statement in chapter 4, as marked in the main text.

[10] The tenth of an ephah of fine flour: Lev 5:11, a tenth of an ephah of fine flour, without oil and without frankincense, is the exceptional case of a bloodless sin offering. Discussion set side by side: both Milgrom and Wenham treat the substitutionary character of this case and its aspect of grace toward the poor; the relation between the necessity of blood and this case has no settled conclusion in the commentaries, and this sermon sets out both readings, as in Part Three of the main text.

[11] Hearing the voice of swearing and testifying: Lev 5:1, rendered in Chinese Union Version as hearing the sound of a public adjuration; the Hebrew includes the sense of a summons to testify, and scholarship mostly reads it as hearing a public oath summoning witnesses. The divergence of readings follows the commentaries of Milgrom and Hartley, set side by side; the main text adopts the summons-to-testify reading, marked as the sense of the original.

[12] Sources for the New Testament reading: Hebrews 9–10, on the blood of bulls and goats being unable to take away sins and on Christ offered once; 1 John 1:9, on confession and forgiveness. In this sermon these two passages are throughout marked as the level of New Testament reading / sermon use, and are not filled back in as the plain sense of the Leviticus text, in keeping with the format of the earlier sermons in this series.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And the priest shall make an atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him." (Leviticus 4:26, KJV) "And it shall be, when he shall be guilty in one of these things, that he shall confess that he hath sinned in that thing." (Leviticus 5:5, KJV, excerpted)

The hardest part of an unintentional sin is that, once you know, you cannot unknow it. Leviticus leaves a complete path for such a person: confess the one specific thing, bring the offering according to your means, and then hear the sentence spoken six times over — it shall be forgiven him. The poorest person with only a handful of fine flour counts; the most ordinary person with a lamb counts. Do not delay today, and do not be vague: name that one thing and bring it to God. Forgiveness is not a feeling; it is a word God has stamped. Speak it, bring it, hear it, and you can live today again with a clean heart. May none of us let an unintentional fault fester into an ulcer of the heart: confess in time, cleanse in time, sleep at night, and stand upright in the day. And may we be gentler with the unintentional faults of others: who has not spoken a wrong word or done a wrong thing? As God apportions grace to us according to our means, so let us bear with others' weakness according to their circumstance — walk with them first on the road of confession, rather than pronouncing judgment first. A person who has heard forgiveness for himself can also steadily pass the word of forgiveness into another's ear.

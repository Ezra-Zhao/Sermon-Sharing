## Summary (about 100 words)

The garments are ready; chapter 29 prepares the people: washing, clothing, anointing, and sacrifice over seven days of ordination, so that the priests are first atoned for and first set apart before they stand at the altar. Once the rite is complete, the tabernacle's days settle into the continual burnt offering of two lambs, morning and evening. At the end God states His purpose plainly: He brought Israel out of Egypt in order to dwell among them and be their God.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Let us first join the order together again, lest in reading this chapter we see only rituals and miss where it stands. In chapters 25 to 27 God prepared the place: ark, table, lampstand, tabernacle, court, and bronze altar, commanded piece by piece according to the pattern shown on the mountain, until the dwelling was complete. In chapter 28 God prepared the garments: ephod, breastplate, robe, tunic, turban, and gold plate, all made ready. The place is there, the garments are there — what is still lacking is the most important thing of all: the people. A man cannot put on the holy garments himself and walk into the holy place; he must first pass through a rite — washed, clothed, anointed, cleansed by blood, and formally placed in the office. Chapter 29 speaks of precisely this rite, and Scripture calls it ordination, consecration for the priestly office. In other words, chapters 25 to 28 prepared the place and the garments; chapter 29 prepares the people who will stand in the place and serve in the garments. This positioning is a structural explanation drawn from the order of the chapters, identified here first, and then developed.

The phrase "ordination" itself must be explained from the original, or the whole chapter will be read vaguely. "Ordination," in the original, is millu'im (מִלֻּאִים, H4394) [1], whose sense is bound up with filling, with making the hand full; the ordination rite is often understood as the rite of filling the hand: at his installation the priest's hands were filled with portions of the offering, and filled with the office itself; from then on he was not a man standing empty-handed at the altar, but one whose hands were full of what God had entrusted to him, to use those hands daily in service. This manuscript takes only the factual layer of the word's meaning: millu'im shares its root with "filling," appears many times in this chapter, and denotes this whole set of rites and offerings by which a priest was formally installed; the picture of the hand being filled is preached along the line of the word's meaning, and is not turned into teaching about spiritual asceticism or merit. The priest's hands are full not because he grasped anything for himself, but because God placed the offering and the office into his hands. Hold this proportion in the word's meaning, and the reading of the whole chapter will not go astray: ordination, from beginning to end, is God installing a man, not a man exalting himself.

One further textual relationship must be stated honestly at the outset, lest the reader mix blueprint and performance into one. This chapter is the command Moses received on the mountain; it is the blueprint of the rite. The rite was actually performed after the tabernacle was set up, and is recorded in Leviticus chapter 8: Moses, following this chapter's order, assembled the congregation at the door of the tabernacle, washed Aaron and his sons, clothed and anointed them, offered the sacrifices, and kept them at the door of the tabernacle for seven days. The sequence is a matter of fact: Exodus 29 commands first, Leviticus 8 performs afterwards; the two are not two different rites, but the command and the performance of one rite. This manuscript preaches this chapter — the blueprint itself — and does not pour details from Leviticus' performance back into it as though they were this chapter's text; where comparison is needed, it will say plainly that this is Leviticus' record of the performance. One sentence at this chapter's end, verse 46, should also be noted here: God brought them out of Egypt in order to dwell among them. From the first day of the exodus, then, the purpose was not only to leave Egypt, but for God to dwell in. This chapter, at this point in the book, is the first place where that purpose is stated in its fullest words. When we preach the rite, we must never preach until only the rite is left, forgetting the God who will dwell in, for whose sake the rite exists.

Hold the chapter's contents in three sections first. The first section, verses 1 to 37, is the ordination rite: a bull, two rams, unleavened bread and cakes are prepared; there is washing, clothing, anointing, and three offerings; blood is put on the priests' ear, hand, and foot; and the rite continues for seven days, with a bull offered daily to purify the altar. The second section, verses 38 to 42, is the daily continual burnt offering: once the rite is complete, how are the tabernacle's days to be lived? A one-year-old lamb morning and evening, with flour, oil, and wine, offered continually for all generations. The third section, verses 43 to 46, is the promise of God's presence, and the eye of the whole chapter: I will meet with the children of Israel there; I will dwell among the children of Israel and be their God. However long the order of the rite, it lands at last on meeting and dwelling; however many the offerings, their end is the unbroken fellowship between God and man. This manuscript's title is taken from this promise at the chapter's end: I Will Dwell Among the Israelites. All the washing, clothing, anointing, blood, and fire before it exist for the sake of this one sentence.

In preaching a ritual chapter, this manuscript still keeps the three rules of this batch of manuscripts. First, the procedure is taken only where it carries the main line, and is not spread out item by item: how many cakes, how many tenths of flour, how the blood was sprinkled — the text records these in detail, but this manuscript preaches through only the procedure that bears the main line, and does not turn the whole chapter into a ritual checklist. Second, symbolic uses of blood and anointing are identified by their level: what the text plainly says belongs to the text, lexical observations belong to lexicography, and where the New Testament later looks back on the priestly office, this manuscript touches on it only in passing and identifies it each time as a later reading; symbolism is not passed off as teaching the original text states on the spot, and still less are ritual details turned into ascetic teaching that tells men to afflict themselves and earn merit — washing and anointing, blood and oil, are in this chapter marks of cleansing and setting apart established by God; the one who receives them receives passively, and does not buy his qualification by suffering. Third, facts are distinguished from exposition and identified as such: the facts of the ritual order, structural observations, and homiletical exposition — three levels kept apart, so that the hearer knows where he stands. Holding these three rules, a ritual chapter will be preached neither dry nor astray.

Let us read several key passages of today's text together:

"And this is the thing that thou shalt do unto them to hallow them, to minister unto me in the priest's office." (Exodus 29:1, excerpt, KJV)

"And Aaron and his sons thou shalt bring unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, and shalt wash them with water." (Exodus 29:4, excerpt, KJV)

"Then shalt thou take the anointing oil, and pour it upon his head, and anoint him." (Exodus 29:7, excerpt, KJV)

"Now this is that which thou shalt offer upon the altar; two lambs of the first year day by day continually. The one lamb thou shalt offer in the morning; and the other lamb thou shalt offer at even." (Exodus 29:38-39, excerpt, KJV)

"And there I will meet with the children of Israel, and the tabernacle shall be sanctified by my glory. And I will sanctify the tabernacle of the congregation, and the altar: I will sanctify also both Aaron and his sons, to minister to me in the priest's office. And I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will be their God. And they shall know that I am the LORD their God, that brought them forth out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them: I am the LORD their God." (Exodus 29:43-46, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You prepared the dwelling and You prepared the garments; now teach us to see how You prepare people. Teach us not to despise cleansing, not to despise being set apart, and not to despise the ordinary fire of the continual offering, morning and evening; and teach us above all to hear Your own words at the chapter's end: You brought us out in order to dwell among us. Let what we hear today be not only an ancient rite, but Your heart to meet with us. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Washing, Clothing, Anointing — First Cleansed, First Set Apart (29:1-14)

This section speaks of the first half of the ordination rite: preparation, washing, clothing, anointing, and then the first offering, the bull for the sin offering. Before the rite begins to offer sacrifice, God first commands the preparation of things: one young bull and two rams without blemish, unleavened bread, unleavened cakes mingled with oil, and unleavened wafers anointed with oil, put into one basket and brought, with the bull and the rams, while Aaron and his sons are brought near to the door of the tabernacle. The preparation list is not spread out item by item; one point only is taken: the man being installed stands at the rite's beginning with empty hands — the bull, the rams, and the bread were all prepared beforehand according to command and brought before him; the filling of his hands comes later, and the beginning is first to draw near empty-handed. Ordination is not a man enrolling with his own qualifications; he is first led before God — and the exposition adds only one sentence: the order of office should be first to draw near, first to receive, and only then to speak of offering oneself.

The rite's first step is washing. The text says Aaron and his sons were to be brought to the door of the tabernacle and washed with water. Note the place: at the door of the tabernacle — not a private washing in his own tent, but publicly, before the congregation, at the entrance of the holy place. And what was washed was the whole body, not hands and feet only — later, in the priests' daily service, when they entered the tabernacle or came near the altar, they were to wash hands and feet in the laver, as chapter 30 commands; but at this installation, the whole man was washed. This manuscript states this distinction clearly, as a fact of the ritual order: the office begins with a washing of the whole body, signifying that this man is set apart from head to foot; the daily washing of hands and feet comes after this, as the cleansing of daily service. The meaning of the washing is kept within the text's bounds: in the tabernacle ritual, washing with water is a mark of cleansing and setting apart; a man is first washed, and only then can he bear the holy garments and draw near to the holy place. As for reading the doctrine of baptism directly into this verse — that is a reading from the church's later looking back, after the New Testament was written; this manuscript does not develop it here, but identifies its level and does not mix it into this chapter's original meaning. The most important lesson of this step is in fact very plain: the first thing at a priest's installation is not work, but being washed. He does not first prove himself clean so that God will use him; he is first washed, and only then stands in his post. Passivity is this step's keyword, and it is identified here.

The second step is clothing. Moses was to put on Aaron the holy garments made in chapter 28, piece by piece — the tunic, the robe of the ephod, the ephod itself, bound with its skilfully woven band, the breastplate set on the ephod, the Urim and the Thummim put in the breastplate, the turban on his head, and the plate of the holy crown, that gold plate engraved HOLINESS TO THE LORD, set on the turban. The order of clothing is given by the text without omitting a piece, but this manuscript does not re-preach the making of each piece as chapter 28 already did; it takes the main line only: the garments were not taken and put on by Aaron himself; Moses put them on him piece by piece. Every garment's meaning was spoken in chapter 28 — the breastplate bearing the names of the tribes of Israel, the gold plate inscribed HOLINESS TO THE LORD — and by chapter 29 those meanings are no longer words on a drawing, but rest in reality on one man's shoulders, breast, and forehead. From then on, when Aaron stood before God, he no longer stood simply as himself alone; he stood bearing the names of the tribes and wearing the plate of holiness to the LORD. This step of clothing tells us: office is not an overcoat that can be taken off in idle hours; worn on the body, office is identity, is burden, is the form in which this man henceforth stands before God. This layer is exegetical explanation drawn from the order of clothing and the garments' meaning in chapter 28, its root in the intertextuality of the two chapters, and is identified here.

At the same time, Aaron's sons were given only tunics, sashes, and turbans — far plainer, with no ephod and no breastplate. The high priest and the priests together passed through washing, anointing, and blood, yet their garments differed and their offices had their order: being set apart is not making all men into one mould, but each man standing well in his own place according to the office he has received. The exposition takes only one sentence: to wear one's own garment rightly and well is the first thing a man ordained should learn.

The third step is anointing. The text says it very briefly: take the anointing oil, pour it upon his head, and anoint him. "Anoint," in the original, is mashach (מָשַׁח, H4886) [2] — to smear, to pour over; the oil was not dabbed in a spot on the forehead, but poured upon the head, running down. The oil's composition is given in detail in chapter 30 — a holy anointing oil compounded and set apart, not to be imitated privately; this chapter uses one sentence only: take the anointing oil and pour it upon his head. Anointing here opens a long line, and its levels must be identified: from then on, being anointed became the term for the formal installation of priests and kings — kings such as Saul and David were also established through anointing; and the word for "the anointed one" later became the special title of the King who was to come, rendered in New Testament Greek as "Christ." That is the factual layer of later development, kept apart from this chapter and not read back into it. As far as this chapter is concerned, oil poured on the head signifies only this: the man is marked by God Himself, set apart by God Himself; the office is God's anointing, not an honour voted by the congregation.

Washing, clothing, and anointing are finished, and only then does the rite come to sacrifice. The first thing offered is the bull for the sin offering — and this order is the weightiest stroke in the whole rite, and this manuscript will take it slowly. The text says the bull was to be brought before the tabernacle, and Aaron and his sons were to lay their hands on the bull's head — laying on of hands signifying that the offering and the man offering it are joined together: the offering stands in the man's place, and the man owns the offering. Then the bull was killed before the LORD, by the door of the tabernacle; some of the bull's blood was taken and put with the finger on the horns of the altar, and all the blood poured out at the bottom of the altar; the fat covering the inwards, the caul above the liver, the two kidneys and their fat were taken and burned on the altar; but the bull's skin, flesh, and dung were to be burned with fire outside the camp. This manuscript gives the procedure only as far as it bears the main line; the weight lies not in the complication of the steps but in an order that may not be reversed: the first offering at a priest's installation was not offered for the people, but for himself. Aaron had not yet made atonement for anyone; he first had to make atonement for himself and his sons. That bull, hands laid upon it, was killed, its blood put on the horns of the altar, its inwards burned on the altar, and its unclean parts burned outside the camp — outside the camp, the place where unclean things were dealt with, farthest from the holy place. Set the factual layer of this stroke out clearly first: the ordination rite opens with a sin offering; before the priest serves God, he himself is first a man who needs atonement. "To make atonement," the original verb is kipper (כִּפֶּר, H3722) [3] — to cover, to make atonement; the offering covered the sin of the man who offered, and only then could he stand at the altar. The exposition stays close to this fact and takes one step only, identifying it as such: no service in this world begins from a man's own righteousness; first atoned for, first confessing sin, first standing under the covering of blood — only then is a man qualified to speak of serving others. Whoever skips this bull and goes straight to wearing the holy garments and standing in the pulpit has not skipped a formality; he has skipped the foundation of the whole priestly office. Later, the Epistle to the Hebrews, looking back on this passage, says the high priest offered for the people's sins and also for his own, because he himself was compassed with infirmity (Hebrews 5:2-3; 7:27, cited in sense) — that is the New Testament's own plain looking back, identified here as a New Testament reading, corresponding to this chapter's ritual fact: the New Testament did not abolish this stroke; it pointed out its shortcoming — a priest who offered for himself year after year himself needed a High Priest. This layer of contrast is noted only in passing and does not develop Christology in this chapter; it lets the hearer remember this chapter's own words: before a priest takes office, he himself first needs atonement.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Blood on Ear, Hand, and Foot — The Whole Man Brought into the Office, Seven Days of Consecration (29:15-37)

The sin offering has been offered, and the rite moves on. The second offering is the ram for the burnt offering. The text says the first ram was to be brought, Aaron and his sons laid their hands on the ram's head, the ram was killed, its blood sprinkled round about upon the altar, the ram cut into pieces, its inwards and legs washed, and all of it, with its head and fat, burned on the altar — the whole burned on the altar as a burnt offering to the LORD, a sweet savour, an offering made by fire. The distinction between burnt offering and sin offering needs only its factual layer stated clearly in this manuscript: the sin offering deals with sin — the bull's blood was put on the horns of the altar and its flesh burned outside the camp; the burnt offering deals with the belonging of the man himself — the whole ram, no part held back, was burned entirely on the altar, rising little by little as smoke before God, and so was called a sweet savour. Atonement first, burnt offering afterwards — this order likewise may not be reversed: first covering, then complete offering up; before sin has been dealt with, talk of complete surrender is empty words; once sin has been dealt with, the whole man burned on the altar is what a priest ought to be. This layer is exegetical explanation drawn from the two offerings' manner and order, identified here. The exposition gathers it into one sentence: a priest's life stands first under the bull's blood, and then upon the ram's fire — first atoned for, then burned; first a sinner shown grace, then a whole man belonging to the Lord. Every man who serves today should engrave this order on his heart.

The third offering is the centre of the whole rite: the ram of ordination, also called the ram of consecration to the LORD. This ram was offered differently from the two before it, and this manuscript follows the text's order along its main line. Aaron and his sons likewise laid their hands on the ram's head, and the ram was killed; Moses took the ram's blood and put it on the tip of Aaron's right ear, on the thumb of his right hand, and on the great toe of his right foot, and on his sons' right ear, right hand, and right foot likewise, and sprinkled the rest of the blood round about upon the altar. Then he took the blood on the altar and the anointing oil and sprinkled them on Aaron and on his garments, and on his sons and their garments, so that he and his garments, his sons and their garments, were sanctified together. Then he took the ram's fat, the fat tail, the fat covering the inwards, the caul of the liver, the kidneys, and the right shoulder, and from the basket one unleavened cake, one cake of oiled bread, and one wafer, and put them into the hands of Aaron and his sons, to be waved before the LORD as a wave offering; then he received them back from their hands and burned them on the altar upon the burnt offering, as a sweet savour, an offering made by fire. The breast and the right shoulder, as the wave and heave offering, were afterwards given to the priests as their portion ordained for ever from the children of Israel. The procedure is taken in this manuscript only to this point, without numbering every portion of the offering piece by piece; what must be grasped is three lines: blood put on the man, oil sprinkled on the man, and the offering filling the man's hands.

First, the blood on ear, hand, and foot — the weightiest picture in the whole rite, and the one most easily preached beyond its measure; this manuscript distinguishes its levels and preaches it carefully. First, the facts: the places touched by blood were the tip of the right ear, the thumb of the right hand, and the great toe of the right foot — three places, all on the right side, all extremities: the extremity of hearing, of doing, and of walking. The text itself records only the rite and attaches no explanation in this chapter; the same putting of blood appears later in the rite for cleansing a leper, where blood was likewise put on the right ear and the right hand and foot (Leviticus 14, cited in sense), showing that this set of places was an established form in cleansing rites, not devised for priests alone. There the factual layer stops. What follows is homiletical exposition, and this manuscript says so plainly here: blood on the ear is taken in preaching as hearing under the blood — the priest's ear henceforth hears God's word and hears the people's cry only through the cleansing of blood, hearing no slander, no flattery, only truth; blood on the hand as doing under the blood — the priest's hands henceforth handle the offerings, uphold the people, and do the holy work only within the covering of blood, never reaching out for unrighteous gain; blood on the foot as walking under the blood — the priest's steps, in and out of the holy place and through the camp, walk on a road set apart by blood. Read this way, hearing, doing, and walking — the whole man is under the blood, and no part is left outside it. This layer of reading has often been taken up in preaching through the ages; it fits the facts of the places touched, it preaches through, and it is easy to remember; but this manuscript must say it firmly: this is homiletical exposition, not something Exodus 29 states plainly on the spot; the original text records only the rite, and the exposition is a development later men drew along the rite's places. Only when the levels are distinguished may it be preached — preached with its level identified, so that the hearers are edified and the text is not misused. One further proportion must be kept: blood put on a man is a mark of cleansing and setting apart, not a summons to wound or afflict oneself in exchange for holiness; this manuscript does not turn ritual details into ascetic teaching — the priest was a man on whom Moses put the blood; he himself did nothing; he only stood there and received.

Next, the blood and anointing oil sprinkled on the garments. The text says Moses took the blood on the altar and the anointing oil and sprinkled them on Aaron and his garments, and on his sons and their garments also, so that they and their garments were sanctified together. This stroke joins man and garment together: every garment in chapter 28 had its meaning, and now garment and man are sprinkled together with blood and oil, signifying that office and man are henceforth inseparable — not the man holy and the garment not, nor the garment holy and the man not, but the man who wears the garment and the garment he wears placed together within being set apart. That oil appears here alongside blood is also worth noting: blood governs cleansing, oil governs anointing — one washing and one anointing, one atoning and one setting apart; the ordained man is a man marked by both together. This layer is exegetical explanation drawn from the order of sprinkling blood and oil, identified here. Then, the filling of the hands: the wave offering was placed in the priests' hands, waved before God, then received back and burned on the altar — this is precisely the most concrete picture of millu'im, the filling of the hand: the priest's hands did their first formal work, and the work was to present the offering before God. His hands were full — not full of his own ability, but full of God's offering and God's entrusting. From this moment the priest also received his own portion: the breast and shoulder of the ordination ram were given to Aaron and his sons, ordained as a statute for ever — even the daily provision of the man set apart God arranged within the office itself.

The rite's offerings seem complete here, yet the text stretches the time out: it is to be done for seven days. Do so for seven days; every day a bull shall be offered for a sin offering, and the altar shall be cleansed and anointed, so that the altar is sanctified; and on the seventh day the altar shall be most holy, and whatsoever touches the altar shall be holy. This line of seven days this manuscript draws out on its own, because it corrects a very common misunderstanding: ordination is not a ceremony performed once and then finished with, not one day of excitement and the next day as before. For seven whole days the sin offering was repeated daily and the altar cleansed daily, and Aaron and his sons remained for seven days at the door of the tabernacle and did not go out (the performance record in Leviticus 8:33-35, cited in sense; this manuscript gives it here as an account at the level of performance, not as this chapter's original meaning) — being set apart needs time; it needs a man to remain before God long enough for the rite's weight to sink in. Why seven days? The text states no reason plainly, and this manuscript invents no allegory of numbers, offering only a restrained observation: seven in Scripture's order is often the mark of a complete round — a round completed, and the matter is accomplished; that belongs to structural observation. And as for the rite itself, the altar too had in these seven days to be cleansed and anointed — the altar and the man alike had to pass through blood and oil before becoming most holy; man and altar were set apart together within one and the same rite, and that is a fact the text states plainly. "Whatsoever touches the altar shall be holy" must be read carefully: it speaks of the altar's most holy standing, by which the vessels and offerings touching it were set apart; it is not the superstitious reading that a man is automatically saved by touching the altar; this manuscript states its ritual meaning honestly and draws no more from it. The exposition gathers only one sentence and identifies it: an office made in haste is the most dangerous thing; a man willing to spend seven days before God — dealt with, cleansed, and anointed — will afterwards stand firm before the altar; the church's solemnity in ordination and sending has its root in these seven days.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Continual Offering Morning and Evening, and the Promise of Dwelling in the Midst (29:38-46)

However solemn the ordination rite, it was performed for seven days only; after the seven days, how were the tabernacle's days to be lived? This section speaks first of the daily continual offering, and then of the chapter's eye, the promise of presence. The text says: what you shall offer daily upon the altar is two lambs of the first year — one lamb in the morning and one at evening; with each lamb, as a meal offering, a tenth of an ephah of fine flour mingled with a quarter of a hin of beaten oil, and a quarter of a hin of wine for a drink offering. The continual lamb was to be offered at the door of the tabernacle, before the LORD; this was to be the continual burnt offering throughout your generations. The continual burnt offering, in the original, is 'olat tamid (עֹלַת תָּמִיד, 'olah, H5930, a burnt offering; tamid, H8548, continually, without ceasing) [4] — the unceasing burnt offering, the burnt offering offered continually. This word tamid we met in the previous chapter, speaking of the lamp: the lamp was to burn always, the bread was always to be set out on the table, and now the offering was always to be offered on the altar. "Always" is the ground colour of tabernacle life: not remembering God only at festivals, but every morning and every evening, fire on the altar, a lamb on the altar, flour and oil and wine offered up. Set the facts out clearly first in this manuscript: the continual offering's number was not large — one lamb morning and evening, neither lavish nor ostentatious; its value lay in its never ceasing. The solemnity of the seven days of ordination would pass; the ordinariness of two lambs morning and evening was not allowed to pass. Once the priestly office was established, its form in daily life was this rhythm: one in the morning, one at evening, more than three hundred days a year, day after day the same. This is precisely the deep meaning of the ordination rite and the daily continual offering being recorded side by side in one chapter — God first used seven days to set a man up, and then used the continual offering to keep him standing; standing up depended on the rite, standing firm depended on "always." This layer is exegetical explanation drawn from the structure of the chapter's two sections set side by side, identified here.

The continual offering's accompaniments are also worth pausing over: besides the lamb there were fine flour, beaten pure oil, and wine — livestock, grain, oil, and wine, all the most everyday produce of the people's life, laid on the altar daily. What the sacrifice used was life itself: exposition, identified as such — the continual offering God wanted was not far away; when a man set aside a little of his daily goods and offered it morning and evening, his days were set apart. Then look at the time: morning and evening are a day's two ends — before a man went out to work the altar had fire first, and after a day's work was done the altar's fire was still burning; a man's day was enclosed by the fire of the continual offering, and the exposition takes only this one sentence: days enclosed by the continual offering are days enclosed by God's presence. A proportion must also be identified: the continual offering was an ordinance of the Old Testament priestly system; reading this passage, what is taken is the rhythm of "always" and of offering, not a performance of the rite, lest anyone take the shadow of the ritual for today's substance.

Now read the chapter's end, verses 43 to 46 — the eye of the whole chapter, and the weightiest passage in the whole book of Exodus up to this point. God says: There I will meet with the children of Israel, and the tabernacle shall be sanctified by my glory. The place of meeting is not on the top of Sinai, nor in the depths of the pillar of cloud, but at the door of the tabernacle, at the place of the continual burnt offering — the place where men offered daily is precisely the place where God meets with men. "Be sanctified" — the underlying root is qadash (קָדַשׁ, H6942) [5], and the forms in these two verses must be stated precisely: in verse 43 the verb is וְנִקְדַּשׁ (weniqdash), the Niphal (passive) stem — the tabernacle "shall be sanctified" by God's glory; in verse 44, "I will sanctify" is וְקִדַּשְׁתִּי (weqiddashti) and אֲקַדֵּשׁ ('aqaddesh), the Piel stem — God himself makes holy. Neither verse uses the Hiphil form hikdish (הִקְדִּישׁ); an earlier draft of this manuscript (following the Chinese original) mislabelled the form, and the label is corrected here: the tabernacle became holy not because its wood was overlaid with gold, but because God's glory was there, because God met with men there. Then God states the full range of sanctifying: I will sanctify the tabernacle and the altar, and I will sanctify Aaron and his sons, to minister to me in the priest's office. Place sanctified, vessels sanctified, men sanctified — all the setting apart since chapter 25 gathers here into one sentence: it is not that the objects are holy in themselves; it is God's presence that makes all this holy. This manuscript fixes this layer as the chapter's exegetical centre, identifying it first, with everything that follows coming from here: the source of holiness is not the rite — the rite is holiness's channel; the source is God Himself, who will come to meet, who will dwell in.

Then verse 45, one of the very heart-passages of the whole book: And I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will be their God. "Dwell," in the original, is shakan (שָׁכַן, H7931) [6] — to dwell, to settle, to take up residence; the word for tabernacle, mishkan, shares this same root — this manuscript states honestly that this is an observation about words of the same root: the tabernacle is called the tabernacle precisely because it is the dwelling of the God who dwells; the connection at the level of the root is a factual statement visible at a glance to a Hebrew reader, and no excessive etymological development is made of it. What God uses here is not a passing visit, nor an inspection tour, but dwelling: dwelling is long-lasting, it is settling one's home down, it is being there morning and evening. He dwells in the midst — not at the edge of the camp, not on the mountain, but among the children of Israel, in the very centre ringed by the people's tents. "And will be their God" — these words settle the relationship: He is not a God issuing commands from far away; He is the God who has dwelt in among His people, entered into relationship with them, and owned them as His own. From Genesis, where God covenanted with Abraham saying, I will be God to you and to your seed, to this point, that sentence falls for the first time into the very centre of a whole nation's life, with a tabernacle, an altar, and a continual offering morning and evening as its daily mark.

At last, in verse 46, God Himself restates the purpose of the whole book, and this manuscript asks you to hear it word by word: And they shall know that I am the LORD their God, that brought them forth out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them: I am the LORD their God. "That I may dwell among them" — the purpose of the exodus is here fully revealed. At first we thought the exodus was for escaping the house of bondage; later we thought it was for entering the land of Canaan; at the end of chapter 29 God Himself says the exodus was for dwelling: bringing out was in order to dwell in. Salvation was never the end point; presence is the end point; leaving Egypt was only half the road walked, and God dwelling in the midst completes the whole journey. This manuscript here marks the structure that forms a ring from head to tail, identified as a structural observation drawn from the two passages: the purpose sentence of chapter 25 verse 8, "that I may dwell among them," and this chapter's verse 46, the same purpose sentence, stand one at the head and one at the tail, holding the whole blueprint of chapters 25 to 29 between them — the blueprint begins with dwelling in the midst and closes with dwelling in the midst, and speaks not of empty ritual for its own sake, but of a God preparing everything in order to dwell in among His people. The exposition gathers one sentence and identifies it: if all the church's worship, offices, and ministries today forget this sentence, only programmes and procedures are left; remembering this sentence, the altar's fire and the morning and evening offerings have their home — that God may dwell among us, and we in Him.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today we have learned three things. First, the order of preparing people: washing first, clothing next, anointing after, and the first offering a bull for one's own sin offering — no man can skip atonement and stand directly before the altar. Second, the setting apart of the whole man: blood put on the right ear, the right hand, and the right foot — in the exposition's words, hearing, doing, and walking all under the blood; and the rite continued for seven days, altar and man sanctified together. Third, the continual offering and the presence: the days settled into the continual offering of two lambs, morning and evening, and at the end God stated His purpose plainly — brought out, in order to dwell in. May we also keep this order: first washed, first atoned for, and only then standing in our post; morning and evening, with the fire of the continual offering and the hour of meeting with God. May God dwell among us, and be our God.

Let us pray together: Lord, we thank You: You did not only bring us out of Egypt; You will also dwell among us. Wash us, as You washed those who were ordained, from head to foot, leaving no place outside Your cleansing; cover our ears, our hands, and our feet with Your blood, so that what we hear, what we do, and the road we walk are all within Your setting apart; and teach us not to despise the ordinary continual offering, morning and evening — willing every day to offer up our days and offer up our hearts, and to keep the altar's fire. If we remember only the rite and forget the meeting, remind us: You prepared all this in order to meet with us, in order to dwell among us. We are willing to keep the central place for You, to set You in the very centre of our life, our family, and our church. Dwell in, and be our God. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will be their God. And they shall know that I am the LORD their God, that brought them forth out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them: I am the LORD their God." (Exodus 29:45-46, excerpt, KJV)

God brought the children of Israel out of Egypt, over so long a road, and ordained so solemn a rite, and at the last His purpose is one sentence only: to dwell among them. Have you ever thought that in all your years of believing, God's purpose for you is not only to have brought you out — out of sin, out of the old life? To be brought out is grace; to be dwelt in is His heart. But dwelling needs a place. Before the priests were ordained, they had to be washed, and blood had to be put on ear, hand, and foot, the whole man set apart, before God's dwelling could be set up. Your heart is the same: you need not wait until you are complete before inviting Him in — the washing and the blood of the priests were both prepared by God, and man only received them; if you will empty the central place, give your ear to hear His word, your hands to do His work, and your feet to walk His way, He is willing to dwell. Add to this the continual offering, morning and evening: in the morning, offer a few minutes first; in the evening, before sleep, offer a few minutes more; let the day be enclosed at both ends, and His presence will be not doctrine only, but the true centre of your day. Pray one sentence tonight: Lord, You brought me out, that You might dwell in my midst; I keep the centre for You.

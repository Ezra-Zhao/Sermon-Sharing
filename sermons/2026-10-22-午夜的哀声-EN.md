## Summary (about 100 words)

After the nine plagues, God announced through Moses the last plague: at midnight He would go out through the land of Egypt, every firstborn would die, and Egypt would have a great cry such as never was before; yet Israel would have utter peace, so that not even a dog would move its tongue against them. Slaves received honor, servants would bow down, and the silver and gold they asked for became the repayment of four hundred years of wages. Once the words of judgment are fully spoken, only the execution remains; a person should turn back while there is still one plague left.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Let us first recall the end of the previous chapter. The plague of darkness in chapter 10 lasted three days: thick darkness over all the land of Egypt, people could not see one another, and no one dared rise from his place, but all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings. Pharaoh was desperate and called Moses; first he relented and let the people go, then he wanted to keep back the cattle, and Moses pushed the words right back: our cattle also shall go with us, there shall not an hoof be left behind. At last Pharaoh was furious and issued an expulsion order combined with a death threat: get thee from me, take heed to thyself, see my face no more, for in that day thou seest my face thou shalt die. Moses answered calmly and decisively: thou hast spoken well, I will see thy face again no more.

By a human script, the story should have broken up here: negotiations collapsed, each went his own way. But the biblical narrative pauses precisely here and inserts chapter 11. Readers should notice one detail: the first three verses of chapter 11 are like the narrator lowering his voice to explain to us that God had long ago told Moses the ending; only from verse 4 do we hear the last words Moses spoke standing before Pharaoh. That is to say, after Moses answered, thou hast spoken well, he did not turn and leave at once; he still stood in the palace and spoke God's final words completely, face to face and in public, and only then came out in great anger. Before the prophet leaves the scene, he must finish speaking the words of judgment. This is the atmosphere of our chapter today: no more bargaining, no new conditions, only an announcement, word by word, driven like nails into the night of Egypt.

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several key passages:

"And the LORD said unto Moses, Yet will I bring one plague more upon Pharaoh, and upon Egypt; afterwards he will let you go hence: when he shall let you go, he shall surely thrust you out hence altogether. Speak now in the ears of the people, and let every man borrow of his neighbour, and every woman of her neighbour, jewels of silver, and jewels of gold. And the LORD gave the people favour in the sight of the Egyptians. Moreover the man Moses was very great in the land of Egypt, in the sight of Pharaoh's servants, and in the sight of the people." (Exodus 11:1-3, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses said, Thus saith the LORD, About midnight will I go out into the midst of Egypt: And all the firstborn in the land of Egypt shall die, from the firstborn of Pharaoh that sitteth upon his throne, even unto the firstborn of the maidservant that is behind the mill; and all the firstborn of beasts. And there shall be a great cry throughout all the land of Egypt, such as there was none like it, nor shall be like it any more. But against any of the children of Israel shall not a dog move his tongue, against man or beast: that ye may know how that the LORD doth put a difference between the Egyptians and Israel." (Exodus 11:4-7, excerpt, KJV)

"And all these thy servants shall come down unto me, and bow down themselves unto me, saying, Get thee out, and all the people that follow thee: and after that I will go out. And he went out from Pharaoh in a great anger." (Exodus 11:8, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses and Aaron did all these wonders before Pharaoh: and the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart, so that he would not let the children of Israel go out of his land." (Exodus 11:10, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today's Scripture is heavy, with judgment, with crying, and with death at midnight. Do not let us read it with a spectator's heart, and do not let us flee from it in fear. Let us hear the two things behind this announcement: how long Your patience toward a hardened heart is, and how real Your remembrance of the oppressed is. Teach us to turn back while grace still leaves room to turn, and to bow down when peace comes. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: About Midnight — The Announcement of the Last Plague (11:1-5)

First look at what God said to Moses in verse 1: Yet will I bring one plague more upon Pharaoh, and upon Egypt. One plague. Nine plagues had already passed: water turned to blood, frogs, lice, flies, pestilence on the livestock, boils, hail, locusts, and darkness — each plague closer to the body than the one before, each shaking Egypt's gods and order more deeply. Before the tenth plague, God first made the number clear: one more, not endless torment, but one fixed execution. This point matters greatly in the theology of judgment: God's judgment is not a loss of emotional control, nor is it endless; it is limited, restrained, and arrives as spoken. The first nine plagues were warnings, opportunities to turn back; the tenth plague is execution, the result after the warnings were exhausted. People most easily misread God's heart, thinking the warnings will continue forever and the opportunities will be extended without limit. Today's Scripture pierces that thin layer: warnings have a number, the door of grace has a time limit, and when God says there is one plague more, it means only one road remains.

Next look at the announcement of the time: About midnight will I go out into the midst of Egypt. Midnight, in Hebrew chatsot hallaylah (חֲצוֹת הַלַּיְלָה, H2677 plus H3915), literally the middle of the night, the moment when the night is split open [1]. The Bible never chooses a time casually. The Passover lamb was killed at twilight on the fourteenth day of the first month; God's going out to strike was at midnight; Israel's departure from Egypt came under urgent pressure in the night. Midnight is the darkest, quietest hour of the day, when people are most defenseless. Egypt's king sat on his throne in the daytime issuing orders, and Egypt's army could muster and count its troops in the daytime, but at midnight no one could assemble an army, and no one could light a lamp and organize resistance. That judgment chose midnight shows precisely this: the one acting this time was not Moses' rod, not Aaron's hand, not even pestilence or wind, but God Himself going out. All human defenses come too late before the personal arrival of God.

Please note that subject: I will go out. In many of the nine plagues, Moses lifted the rod or Aaron stretched out his hand, and the plague came. This time God said, I will go out. The God of Israel is not a God who controls things remotely from heaven; He comes down, He enters, He goes out through the land. In Genesis, God walked in the garden, seeking Adam who was hiding; in Exodus, God remembered the covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and came down personally to save His people. Judgment and salvation are in fact two sides of one and the same going-out of the same God: for Pharaoh, striking; for Israel, passing over. The same God, the same midnight, two outcomes; the difference does not lie in God showing partiality, but in which side of the covenant a person stands. We will unfold this solemnly again later; keep it in your heart for now.

Then look at the scope: all the firstborn in the land of Egypt shall die, from the firstborn of Pharaoh that sitteth upon his throne, even unto the firstborn of the maidservant that is behind the mill, and all the firstborn of beasts. Firstborn, in Hebrew bekhor (בְּכוֹר, H1060), the firstborn, the eldest son [2]. In an ancient Near Eastern household, the firstborn was not merely a birth order; he was the heir of the family, the firstfruit of his father's strength, the future of a household and even of a dynasty. Pharaoh's firstborn was the heir to the throne, the continuation, in Egyptian eyes, of the son of god; the maidservant's firstborn was a poor family's only hope; the firstborn of the beasts was the first portion of a farming household's property. The scope of this plague ran from the throne to the millstone, from human beings to beasts; no class could be exempted, and no roof could provide shelter. What it struck was the thing Egypt's whole order treasured most: inheritance, future, and strength.

Here we must read intertextually with chapter 4 of Exodus. When God sent Moses to Pharaoh in chapter 4, He gave him in advance the most important sentence: thou shalt say unto Pharaoh, Thus saith the LORD, Israel is my son, even my firstborn: and I say unto thee, Let my son go, that he may serve me: and if thou refuse to let him go, behold, I will slay thy son, even thy firstborn (Exodus 4:22-23). Brothers and sisters, do you hear it? The tenth plague was not a heavy hand God improvised on the spot; it was the principle announced at the very beginning of the book: Israel is God's firstborn, and Pharaoh seized God's firstborn and would not release him, afflicted him, and killed his infant boys; therefore God's judgment would be measured firstborn for firstborn. Pharaoh in that year threw the Hebrew baby boys into the Nile; today God's hand came upon the firstborn of every Egyptian house. The shape of judgment often corresponds to the shape of the sin, so that people recognize without excuse: this is no accident; this is exactly what we ourselves have done. The Psalms later retold this night, saying, He smote also all the firstborn in their land, the chief of all their strength (Psalm 105:36), and, He smote the firstborn of Egypt, and brought Israel out from among them (Psalm 136:10-11, paraphrased closely from the KJV wording). Israel sang of this night for generations, not to celebrate the death of others, but to remember how God avenged His firstborn.

Also pay attention to the order of the announcement. Moses first told Pharaoh about midnight and the firstborn, and only then about the great cry. That is to say, the cry was not an atmosphere fixed in advance; the cry was the result of the judgment. When the Bible speaks of judgment, it first speaks God's word, then human reaction; first righteousness, then tears. This order reminds us of the proper attitude in reading Scripture: do not be led first by emotion; first hear clearly why God acts in this way. Pharaoh had ten opportunities to listen to counsel, the warnings of nine plagues, the magicians' identification, the servants' pleading, and even a moment when his own mouth confessed sin: I have sinned this time: the LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked (Exodus 9:27). But every time a plague stopped, his heart hardened again. Before judgment arrived, God's words had been fully spoken. Fully spoken — these are the two heaviest words in this chapter. Many human tragedies do not happen because no one warned; they happen because the warnings were heard one by one and let pass time after time, until at the last time, once the words were finished, only execution remained.

In this part we must also honestly face a question modern readers often have: was the striking of the firstborn not too severe? The text itself gives us angles from which to answer. First, this was a judgment announced against a regime of collective oppression that had lasted four hundred years and still refused to repent after nine plagues of warning; it was not revenge over a private grievance. Second, Egypt's sin was not abstract: from Pharaoh's order to kill infants, to affliction with heavy labor, to beatings by taskmasters, to a whole land treating slavery as a matter of course, the sin had a concrete shape. Third, the Bible never lets us speak of this night in a gloating tone; what it immediately records is the cry, the tears of the whole land, and it commands Israel to remember its own deliverance through the Passover for generations, not to remember the destruction of its enemy. A person who preaches judgment should first tremble himself: that we stand within the covenant today is not because we are nobler than the Egyptians, but because we have received mercy. We will make this clear again in the second part.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: A Great Cry and Not a Dog Moving Its Tongue — Distinction, Honor, and Four Hundred Years of Wages (11:6-8a)

Verse 6 says: there shall be a great cry throughout all the land of Egypt, such as there was none like it, nor shall be like it any more. Great cry, in Hebrew tse'aqah gedolah (צְעָקָה גְּדֹלָה, H6818 plus H1419), a great crying out, a great cry of distress [3]. This word tse'aqah appears in Exodus, and it appears meaningfully. In chapter 3 God said to Moses that the cry of the Israelites had come unto Him, and that He had also seen the oppression wherewith they were oppressed. The people's cry God heard for four hundred years. Pharaoh could not hear it — or rather, Pharaoh pretended not to hear it. So by chapter 11, the word cry changed owners: formerly it was the slaves crying in the brick kilns, with no one paying attention; now it was all Egypt, from palace to cottage, crying together, as never before and never again. There is a plain justice among human beings: if you stop your ears for a long time against the cries of others, one day the cry will become too great to stop out. This is not a folk saying about retribution; it is the text itself using the same word for a before-and-after correspondence: God is the God who hears cries; He hears the cries of slaves, and in the end He will also make the oppressor taste what a cry is like.

Immediately in verse 7, the stroke of the pen turns sharply: but against any of the children of Israel shall not a dog move his tongue, against man or beast. Move his tongue, in the original yecheretz lashon (יֶחֱרַץ לָשׁוֹן, H2782), literally to move or wag the tongue, extended to mean barking and disturbing [4]. This sentence has an extremely strong sense of picture: at midnight, the cries shook the whole land of Egypt, and behind every door someone was holding a firstborn and weeping; while in the land of Goshen, it was so quiet that not even a dog barked. Brothers and sisters, when the Bible depicts distinction, it rarely uses abstract language; it uses an acoustic picture: on one side, a great cry such as never was before; on the other side, utter peace in which not even a dog barked. Utmost crying and utmost peace coexisted at the same midnight, on the same land. This was not a geographical distinction — Goshen and Egypt were only a short distance apart; it was a distinction of the covenant. God Himself stated the purpose of this verse: that ye may know how that the LORD doth put a difference between the Egyptians and Israel. In Exodus, putting a difference is not a slogan; it has concrete shapes: at the plague of flies there were no flies in Goshen, at the pestilence Israel's livestock suffered no loss, at the hail there was no hail in Goshen, and at the darkness there was light in Israel's dwellings. At the tenth plague the distinction reached its peak: death went out through the whole land, yet passed over the houses that had the mark of blood on their lintels.

Here we must speak of the foundation of the distinction solemnly and carefully, lest it be heard as superiority. Israel's distinction was founded neither on nobility of race, nor on moral superiority, nor on their being more deserving than the Egyptians. The Bible is unsparing in its evaluation of Israel: Deuteronomy says plainly that the LORD set His love upon them and chose them not because they were more in number than any people, for they were the fewest of all people, but because the LORD loved them and because He would keep the oath He had sworn unto their fathers (Deuteronomy 7:7-8). The foundation of the distinction is God's own active love and the promise of the covenant; it is grace, not a medal of bloodline. It is the same for us who are in Christ today: the line between peace and crying is not drawn between the clever and the foolish, nor between success and failure, but only between being in the covenant or not, being hidden under the blood of the Lamb or not. When Hebrews speaks of the faith of this night, it says: through faith Moses kept the passover, and the sprinkling of blood, lest he that destroyed the firstborn should touch them (Hebrews 11:28). That they were not touched rested on faith and blood, not on Israel's own ability. If a person preaches distinction as rank and grace as superiority, he has preached this chapter exactly backwards. This chapter is meant to make people bow down, not boast.

Besides the distinction, verse 3 contains a record very easily read past: the LORD gave the people favour in the sight of the Egyptians, and the man Moses was very great in the land of Egypt, in the sight of Pharaoh's servants and in the sight of the people. Very great — that is, held in great honor. That slaves of four hundred years, at the moment of their departure, should be regarded as very great in the eyes of their masters — what a reversal this is. Israel came into Egypt as sojourners fleeing famine to survive; later a new king arose, regarded them as a threat, ruled over them and afflicted them, and in Egyptian eyes they were a base people, labor, slaves who could be beaten and scolded at will. After the nine plagues, the Egyptians' view of Moses and the people changed completely: the servants privately pleaded with Moses, the people respected Moses, and even those in Pharaoh's palace knew that this fugitive shepherd was the messenger of God. The honor was not something Moses earned for himself; it was God personally lifting him up before Egyptian eyes. Brothers and sisters, this is a great comfort to people who are despised today: human evaluations do reverse, and the timing of the reversal is not in our hands; what we are to do is stand firm in the years of being despised, and wait for God to lift up the head of His people before everyone's eyes. Honor seized by oneself cannot be kept; honor given by God no one can take away.

In verse 8 Moses also spoke a future-tense prophecy within the announcement: all these thy servants shall come down unto me, and bow down themselves unto me, saying, Get thee out, and all the people that follow thee, and after that I will go out. Note the object and the posture of the speech: those who would bow down and come to him were not Pharaoh himself, but Pharaoh's servants. Moses did not prophesy that Pharaoh would bow his head; he prophesied that the servants of the court would be the first unable to hold out, bowing down and begging the people to go out. Formerly it was Moses who entered the palace again and again begging Pharaoh to let the people go; now it would be the servants coming in a body, bowing down and begging the people to leave quickly. Begging and being begged were completely reversed. Chapter 12 fulfilled it without missing a word: Pharaoh called Moses and Aaron by night and pressed them to go out, the Egyptians also pressed the people urgently, as if saying, we be all dead men, and they even said, bless me also. The prophet's words came first, the fulfillment followed, and between them there was only one midnight. The future tense within an announcement is the mark of the prophet's certainty: he was not guessing the outcome; he was sent to speak in advance the outcome God had already determined. Reading the Prophets and reading Revelation, we should keep this perspective: when God's word is spoken, the fulfillment is already on its way.

This part also contains a matter that must be explained truthfully, namely asking for silver and gold. In verse 2 God commanded: speak in the ears of the people, and let every man borrow of his neighbour, and every woman of her neighbour, jewels of silver and jewels of gold. (The KJV renders sha'al here as borrow; see note [5].) As early as chapter 3, God had foretold the same thing: I will give this people favour in the sight of the Egyptians, and it shall come to pass that when ye go, ye shall not go empty, but every woman shall borrow of her neighbour, and of her that sojourneth in her house, jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and raiment (Exodus 3:21-22). In chapter 12, the fulfillment is recorded: the children of Israel did according to the word of Moses, and they borrowed of the Egyptians jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and raiment, and the LORD had given the people favour in the sight of the Egyptians, so that they lent unto them such things as they required, and they spoiled the Egyptians (Exodus 12:35-36; on the rendering spoiled, see the explanation below).

To ask, in the original sha'al (שָׁאַל, H7592), to ask, inquire, request [5]. The most common use of this word in the Bible is simply to open one's mouth and request, to borrow by asking; it does not in itself contain the meaning of robbery or theft. Scholarship has long held serious discussion of this passage, and this manuscript presents both main readings truthfully side by side, without asserting a single interpretation alone. One reading emphasizes giving and favour: the text says again and again that the LORD gave the people favour in the sight of the Egyptians, and the Egyptians gave their goods voluntarily; their motives could have been reverence, could have been averting disaster, could have been the urgency of sending away a plague, and could also have been a measure of compensation for years of labor; the requesting sense of sha'al fits this exactly, and the KJV's rendering at Exodus 11:2, "borrow," carries precisely the sense of requesting. The other reading emphasizes that this was wages repaid and spoil ordained by God: Israel labored without pay for four hundred years, Egypt built its store cities by their hands, and the accumulated wages could not be calculated; God therefore used this night's asking to make the oppressor return part of what had been exploited, as when spoil is taken from an enemy after a war; the KJV's chapter 12 rendering, "they spoiled the Egyptians," reflects this layer of theological understanding. The two readings are not wholly opposed: in the act itself, it was requesting and giving between neighbours; in God's intention, it was the repayment of four hundred years of wages and the provision for the exodus journey. We must avoid two ways of preaching it wrongly: first, preaching this passage as God instigating deceit or theft, which does not fit the meaning of sha'al or the context of favour; second, preaching it lightly, as if it were merely borrowing some jewelry for the road, which does less than justice to four hundred years of blood and sweat. The solemn way to preach it is this: God remembered the wages of His people for four hundred years, without missing a fraction, and on the night of their departure He personally settled the account for them. The people in this world who are most owed often think no one keeps account of their wages; this chapter tells us there is a ledger in heaven, and God keeps it Himself and repays it Himself.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: God Let Pharaoh Harden, and Moses Went Out in Great Anger — Words Finished and the Turning of Man (11:8b-10)

Finally, look at Moses' posture as he left the palace and at the close of this chapter. After Moses had spoken all this, he went out from Pharaoh in great anger. Anger, in the original chori af (חֳרִי־אַף, H2750 plus H639), literally the burning of the nostrils, that is, fierce anger, an outburst of wrath [6]. The KJV renders Moses' state as he left the palace as "in great anger." Brothers and sisters, please do not read the prophet's anger as a loss of composure. Of Moses, the Bible says he was very meek, above all the men which were upon the face of the earth (Numbers 12:3). How many times had he interceded for Pharaoh: as soon as a plague came and Pharaoh confessed fault, he went out and prayed, and the plague stopped; he even, when God was about to consume Israel, laid himself down and said, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book, yet interceded for the people to be forgiven. How did a man of such long patience and repeated intercession come to be in great anger at the last moment? This anger was anger at a hardened heart; it was the grieved anger of watching a life that could plainly have turned back walk step by step onto a road of no return. The prophet's anger and his long patience do not contradict each other; they complete each other: precisely because he truly hoped Pharaoh would turn back, he left the scene in anger when Pharaoh utterly refused. False prophets never grow angry, because they only speak pleasant words; a true prophet has anger, because his words are in earnest, and he watches with his own eyes as people refuse the way of life. Those who preach today, who are parents today, who counsel friends today, should all learn this measure: patience must be long, long enough for ten warnings; anger must also be real, real enough not to treat judgment as a trifle. Only, our anger must always stop at announcing and leaving the scene; the hand that executes judgment belongs to God alone, not to man. Moses went out in anger and left the night to God, without taking one step more himself. That boundary remains in force today.

Verses 9 and 10 are the seal of the whole chapter: the LORD said unto Moses, Pharaoh shall not hearken unto you, that my wonders may be multiplied in the land of Egypt. And Moses and Aaron did all these wonders before Pharaoh, and the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart, so that he would not let the children of Israel go out of his land. God's letting Pharaoh harden is a theme repeated throughout Exodus: the narrative order is the reverse of what is often assumed: through the first five plagues it is Pharaoh who hardens his own heart, or his heart is simply said to be hardened, and the statement that the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart appears only from Exodus 9:12 onward. The two statements coexist, and the Bible does not evade the tension between them. A sound understanding is this: Pharaoh first hardened his own heart again and again, and made his own heart heavy himself; God's action was to give him over to walk to the end of the road he himself had chosen, and to take even this hardening into the course of His saving plan, so that His wonders were multiplied and His name declared throughout all the earth. This is not God tempting a person to do evil; it is the giving-over of justice: if a person insists on refusing the light, God lets him taste the darkness to the end. For those who hear the word, this is the most serious reminder: a hard heart is not formed in one moment; it is hardened gradually, by repenting each time a plague stopped and going back on it, by hearing each time and postponing. Whoever hears a warning today should not say tomorrow; tomorrow's heart will only be harder than today's.

Then where does this chapter land for us today? At least three sentences should be taken home.

The first sentence: once the words of judgment are fully spoken, only execution remains; a person should turn back while there is still one plague left. Pharaoh's opportunities were not absent; they were exhausted. The nine plagues were nine invitations to turn back: the magicians said, this is the finger of God; the servants said, knowest thou not yet that Egypt is destroyed; even Pharaoh's own mouth once confessed sin. But the invitations were torn up one by one, until the tenth sheet contained not an invitation but the time of execution: about midnight. Who among us who hear preaching today dares say his opportunities are more than Pharaoh's? When God's word comes to us — through a sermon, through a change of circumstances, through an uneasy sentence in our conscience — it is a voice from before midnight. Turn back early: not when the plague arrives, but while the words are still being spoken. While the words are still being spoken, the door is still open; once the words are finished and only footsteps remain, it will be too late. The prophet Isaiah later counseled Israel: seek ye the LORD while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near — which speaks of exactly this timing. Brothers and sisters, do not misread God's patience as God's indifference, and do not treat warning after warning as wind past the ear. Pharaoh's story is written in Scripture precisely for every person who thinks he still has time.

The second sentence: God remembers the wages of His people for four hundred years. God had long ago prophesied to Abram: thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs, and shall serve them, and they shall afflict them four hundred years; and also that nation, whom they shall serve, will I judge: and afterward shall they come out with great substance (Genesis 15:13-14). For four hundred years, God did not misremember a single year. The wages of slaves, the blood and sweat in the brick kilns, the infants in the Nile — not one item fell outside the account. The silver and gold they asked for were not unexpected wealth; the day of settlement had arrived. Those today who are owed in the workplace, overlooked in the family, and feel in their years that no one sees their labor — please hear this sentence well: people may default on a debt, God does not default; people may fail to remember, God remembers. Your wages may not be repaid at the time you expect, nor in the way you expect, but God remembers, and God will settle. This is not a wish of prosperity preaching; it is the ledger of Exodus: even four hundred years can be waited out, and God's hand is never absent.

The third sentence: between peace and crying, the distinction lies in being in the covenant, not in any foundation a person imagines for himself. That night in Egypt, life and death shared one land, and peace and crying shared one night; the distinction lay only in the blood on the lintel and the word of God. Our distinction today likewise lies neither in origin, nor in achievement, nor in our moral self-feeling, but in whether we are in the covenant of Christ and hidden under His precious blood. To speak this sentence is to dismantle every spiritual sense of superiority: that night Israel, too, was to be in the house trembling, keeping watch, not going out to watch a neighbor's calamity. The rite of the Passover is from beginning to end remembrance and thanksgiving, not celebration and boasting. If we have peace within the covenant, our first response should be to bow down and worship, and our second should be to intercede for those still in the crying, hoping that they, too, will come in through the door before midnight. A sermon on judgment that produces coldness has been preached wrongly; preached to the end, a sermon on judgment should produce tears and a sense of mission: those who have themselves received grace should be the most anxious for those who have not, just as Moses interceded for Pharaoh to the very last moment.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have finished reading Exodus chapter 11. The whole chapter has only ten verses, yet they are the quietest ten verses before the storm. The words were all finished: midnight, firstborn, great cry, not a dog moving its tongue, servants bowing down, silver and gold repaid. God spoke the ending in advance and completely; then Moses went out from Pharaoh in great anger, Pharaoh still sat on his throne, and Egypt's night deepened little by little. The Bible does not record the striking in this chapter; that belongs to chapter 12. This chapter stops between the announcement and the execution, at the last moment when the door is still open. This pause is precisely the empty place left for those who hear the word: which side do you stand on, and will you turn back — now, while the words are not yet finished?

Let us pray together: Righteous and merciful Lord, thank You; Your judgment is never hasty. You gave Pharaoh ten opportunities, and You have given us opportunity after opportunity to hear the word. Do not let our hearts harden until they cannot hear; teach us to turn back while there is still one plague left, and to repent while the words are not yet finished. Remember also every one among us who has been owed, despised, and feels that no one settles his wages; as You remembered four hundred years of blood and sweat in Egypt, so remember them today. Let those of us who have peace in the covenant not boast, but only bow down, and intercede for those outside the door. In the name of the Passover Lamb, the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] chatsot hallaylah: Hebrew חֲצוֹת הַלַּיְלָה, chatsot (H2677, half) plus hallaylah (H3915, night); Exodus 11:4, Moses announced about midnight, and Exodus 12:29 records the fulfillment at midnight; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's exposition — that midnight, the darkest and most defenseless hour, shows by its choice that God Himself went out — is homiletical exposition, marked separately from the lexical facts.

[2] bekhor: Hebrew בְּכוֹר (H1060), firstborn, eldest son; Exodus 11:5, all the firstborn from Pharaoh to the maidservant, and the firstborn of beasts; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Exodus 4:22-23, where the LORD calls Israel my firstborn and foretells that He will slay Pharaoh's firstborn, as the scriptural basis for this manuscript's firstborn-for-firstborn intertext; Psalm 105:36 and Psalm 136:10-11 are later retellings.

[3] tse'aqah gedolah: Hebrew צְעָקָה גְּדֹלָה, tse'aqah (H6818, cry, cry of distress) plus gedolah (H1419, great); Exodus 11:6, there shall be a great cry throughout all the land of Egypt; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Exodus 3:7-9, where the cry of the Israelites came up unto God, as the scriptural basis for this manuscript's observation that the same word for crying changes its subject between the two passages.

[4] yecheretz lashon: Hebrew יֶחֱרַץ לָשׁוֹן, charats (H2782, to move, to decide) plus lashon (tongue); Exodus 11:7, not a dog shall move his tongue against the children of Israel; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The phrase is an idiom meaning that not even a dog would bark or disturb; this manuscript's picture of utter peace is a homiletical unfolding based on that idiom.

[5] sha'al: Hebrew שָׁאַל (H7592), to ask, inquire, request; Exodus 11:2, ask of one's neighbour jewels of silver and jewels of gold (the KJV renders it borrow), with the foretelling in Exodus 3:21-22 and the fulfillment in Exodus 12:35-36; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Scholarship holds two main readings of this passage, the giving-and-favour reading and the wages-and-spoil repayment reading; the text of this manuscript presents both readings side by side and does not assert a single interpretation alone. That asking is the word's basic meaning belongs to the factual layer; the two theological readings belong to the layer of exposition.

[6] chori af: Hebrew חֳרִי־אַף, chori (H2750, fierce anger) plus af (H639, nose, anger); Exodus 11:8, Moses went out from Pharaoh in great anger; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Numbers 12:3, Moses was very meek, as cross-evidence for this manuscript's discussion of the prophet's anger coexisting with long patience.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Moses said, Thus saith the LORD, About midnight will I go out into the midst of Egypt: And all the firstborn in the land of Egypt shall die, from the firstborn of Pharaoh that sitteth upon his throne, even unto the firstborn of the maidservant that is behind the mill; and all the firstborn of beasts. And there shall be a great cry throughout all the land of Egypt, such as there was none like it, nor shall be like it any more. But against any of the children of Israel shall not a dog move his tongue, against man or beast: that ye may know how that the LORD doth put a difference between the Egyptians and Israel." (Exodus 11:4-7, excerpt, KJV)

Pharaoh had nine warnings, and every time a plague stopped, he hardened his heart again, until God said, yet one plague more, and the words were finished. When God's word comes to us, the door is still open; do not wait. Two words of comfort remain today: God remembers four hundred years of wages, without missing a fraction, and His people's peace is so deep that not even a dog moves its tongue. The difference does not lie in our being better than anyone, but in whether we are in the covenant. Before the words are finished, turn back, bow down, and place yourself under the blood of the Lamb. Whoever turns back before midnight will find peace at midnight. May we take this word in today: do not delay, do not harden your heart; receive the warning as grace and the peace as a reason to bow down, and dwell quietly in His covenant until daybreak.

## Summary (about 100 words)

Joseph brings his father Jacob before Pharaoh; the sojourning old man twice blesses the king, and describes his pilgrimage as one hundred and thirty years, few and evil. The whole family receives a possession in Goshen and is settled, yet the famine deepens step by step: Joseph exchanges grain for money, for livestock, and for land, and establishes the law of one fifth. At the end of the chapter Jacob charges Joseph not to bury him in Egypt: life is a pilgrimage, and the direction faced at death matters more than the place of burial.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter, the whole family arrived in Egypt. Seventy people, supporting the old and carrying the young, driving livestock and bringing all they had, journeyed south from Canaan to escape a great famine that had already lasted two years, and to see one person — Joseph, whom they had believed dead long ago. The tears of the reunion between father and son had not yet dried when today's text moves the camera into the palace.

What kind of place was the palace? It was where the most powerful man in the whole world at that time sat. With one sentence Pharaoh could let a person live or let a person die; with one sentence he could open the storehouses and release grain, or shut the door to all petitioners. People who entered the palace usually came to plead, to offer tribute, to beg for a way to survive. The one who walked in today, however, was a man one hundred and thirty years old: his back most likely bent, his eyes most likely dim, leaning on a staff, supported by his son. He came to give thanks, and he came to ask for a place to settle. By human reckoning, he was the person of least weight in that room.

Yet the Bible, in this very chapter, records one unexpected thing twice: it was not Pharaoh who blessed Jacob, but Jacob who blessed Pharaoh. Once on entering, once on leaving — twice. The sojourning old man stood before the king and, before asking for anything, first spoke a blessing. This is the picture we will look at together today. And the old man's own summary of his life is only this: one hundred and thirty years, few and evil. The hardship is in the words, and the weight is also in the words.

Let us read several key passages of today's text together:

"And Joseph brought in Jacob his father, and set him before Pharaoh: and Jacob blessed Pharaoh. And Pharaoh said unto Jacob, How old art thou? And Jacob said unto Pharaoh, The days of the years of my pilgrimage are an hundred and thirty years: few and evil have the days of the years of my life been, and have not attained unto the days of the years of the life of my fathers in the days of their pilgrimage." (Genesis 47:7-9, excerpt, KJV)

"And Joseph placed his father and his brethren, and gave them a possession in the land of Egypt, in the best of the land, in the land of Rameses, as Pharaoh had commanded. And Joseph nourished his father, and his brethren, and all his father's household, with bread, according to their families." (Genesis 47:11-12, excerpt, KJV)

"If now I have found grace in thy sight, put, I pray thee, thy hand under my thigh, and deal kindly and truly with me; bury me not, I pray thee, in Egypt: but I will lie with my fathers, and thou shalt carry me out of Egypt, and bury me in their buryingplace. And he said, I will do as thou hast said." (Genesis 47:29-30, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, every one of us is a sojourner, and the length of our days is in Your hand. Through these scenes of Jacob's old age, teach us how, in days that are few and evil, we may still stand with dignity, live as people who can bless others, and face the right direction at the end of life. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Sojourner Before the King — Two Blessings and One Hundred and Thirty Years (47:1-12)

First look at how Joseph arranged this audience. Joseph went first and told Pharaoh: my father and my brothers, with their flocks and herds and all that they have, have come from the land of Canaan, and are now in the land of Goshen. He also chose five of his brothers and presented them before Pharaoh. Pharaoh asked about their occupation, and they answered as Joseph had taught them beforehand: thy servants are shepherds, both we and also our fathers. Pharaoh then granted it: the land of Egypt is before you; in the best of the land make your father and brothers dwell, in the land of Goshen let them dwell; and if you know any men of activity among them, make them rulers over my cattle.

This arrangement shows Joseph's wisdom, and it also shows this family's situation: they were sojourners. Sojourners must speak carefully and carry themselves fittingly; all they ask for is a place where the old and the young can settle and the livestock can graze. They had no capital for putting on airs, and no plan to stay by force. Goshen, in the eastern Nile Delta, had rich water and grass, suited to pasturing, and lay at some distance from the centers where Egyptians lived — the Egyptians traditionally regarded shepherds as an abomination, and this separation actually became a protection: this family could preserve its own identity there and multiply in quietness. God's arrangements are often like this: the place people regard as marginal is exactly the place of settling He has prepared for His children.

Then Joseph brought his father in. Notice the order in the text: the first thing Jacob did on entering the palace was not to pour out hardships, nor to give thanks, but to bless. "And Jacob blessed Pharaoh." When the conversation ended and he was leaving, the text records it again: "And Jacob blessed Pharaoh, and went out from before Pharaoh." Once entering, once leaving — two blessings, with a brief question and answer between them. The Bible's pen never wastes anything: what appears twice is what it wants us to stop and examine carefully.

Pharaoh asked a very practical question: How old art thou? This was probably a king's natural curiosity toward a very aged man, and perhaps routine palace courtesy. Jacob's answer, however, went far beyond courtesy. He first reported his age: one hundred and thirty years. Then he pronounced a verdict on his own life: "few and evil have the days of the years of my life been, and have not attained unto the days of the years of the life of my fathers in the days of their pilgrimage."

Let us chew on the wording first. The word "pilgrimage" in the original is megurim (מְגוּרִים, H4033), a noun from "to sojourn, to dwell as a stranger," denoting the condition and the years of a person temporarily residing in a foreign land [1]. Jacob did not answer Pharaoh's question "how many years have you lived" in those terms; he said "the days of the years of my pilgrimage." One word states the position of a whole life: I am not the owner of this land; I am a guest. My days are not property clutched in my hand; they are borrowed, a temporary stay. His father Isaac lived one hundred and eighty years, his grandfather Abraham one hundred and seventy-five; compared with them, he at one hundred and thirty called his own days "few" — this is both fact and a sojourner's self-awareness: before the years of his fathers, he did not dare to boast of longevity.

"Few and evil": the original is me'at vera'im (מְעַט וְרָעִים, H4592 and H7451); "few" is few in number, and "evil" translated literally is "bad, grievous, hard" [3]. Jacob did not whitewash anything. His life truly had been hard: in his youth he deceived his brother and fled, sleeping in the wilderness; in his uncle's house he was deceived for fourteen years and wore his heart out over the strife of two wives and two maidservants; Rachel died early, Joseph "disappeared," Benjamin was nearly lost as well; in old age he met a seven-year famine and left his home to go down to Egypt. Another person summarizing such a life might well be full of complaints: God has wronged me, fate is unfair, my children are unfilial. Jacob was not. He simply stated the facts calmly: few and evil. The hardship is in the words, neither exaggerated nor concealed; and the weight is also in the words — because the man who said this was standing before the king, and his back did not bend.

Then consider the two blessings. "To bless" in the original is barakh (בָּרַךְ, H1288), meaning to bless, to praise, to wish good to come upon the other [2]. In the etiquette of the ancient Near East, it was normally the higher in rank who blessed the lower, the elder who blessed the younger, the king who blessed his subjects. But in this palace the ranks were entirely reversed: Pharaoh was the king, Jacob was a sojourner fleeing famine; Pharaoh held the grain, and Jacob's whole family was waiting for grain to live. By human rules, Jacob was the one who should receive a blessing, and Pharaoh the one who should speak it. Yet the text says Jacob blessed Pharaoh — and says it twice.

The New Testament Book of Hebrews has a related sentence: "And without all contradiction the less is blessed of the better." (Hebrews 7:7, KJV) The context of that sentence concerns Melchizedek and Abraham, and its author argues an order of rank from the direction of blessing. We cite it today only as a theological comparison, and only in passing: the narrative of the text itself stands first — in Genesis 47, it is Jacob who blesses Pharaoh. The Bible does not explain on what basis Jacob did this, and Jacob did not claim any basis for himself. His basis was not status, not wealth, not armies; it was the God he knew, the God of his fathers, the God of the promise. The sojourner had nothing, yet he had a God; the king had everything, yet did not necessarily know the God who gives life. Here is the reversal of dignity: a person standing before those in power has a dignity that does not come from their permission, but from his relationship with God. He can bless the other because his own good is not supplied by the other.

Brothers and sisters, this scene is spoken to everyone who feels a head shorter before others. You may feel, before a supervisor, before an official, before a wealthy and powerful relative, that your weight is light and your words do not count. Jacob tells us: a sojourner can still stand straight, and can still open his mouth to bless. Blessing those in power is not flattery and not currying favor — flattery seeks something, blessing gives something. Jacob brought no gift into the palace; the gift he brought was the blessing itself. That living people bless those in authority is the sojourner's distinctive office in this world: we do not curse the rulers of this age; we bless them, because we know that days, like authority, have all been measured in God's hand.

When the conversation ended, the whole family received its settlement. Pharaoh commanded that the best of the land be given to them, and Joseph obeyed, giving his father and brothers the land of Rameses as a possession. Note the word "possession": in the original 'achuzzah (אֲחֻזָּה, H272), meaning property actually held in the hand, land that can be handed down [4]. Sojourners received a possession in a foreign land. This does not mean they became the owners of Egypt from then on — they continued to call themselves sojourners — but that God, in the days of sojourning, also gives people a portion for settling and living. This possession later became the cradle of Israel's multiplication in Egypt: there they were fruitful and increased, growing from seventy persons into a great nation. Sojourning and taking root are not contradictory: the hand may hold a possession while the heart knows clearly that it is a guest; the feet may be settled while the eyes look beyond all of this toward a farther place. This is exactly what Jacob would deal with next, and the direction of the second half of this chapter: the first half speaks of settling, the second half of how the land of Egypt changed in the famine, and at the end the old man would personally fix the direction he faced after death.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Famine Deepens — Grain, Money, Livestock, Land, and One Fifth (47:13-26)

The camera turns from the palace to the whole nation. The famine was very sore, and the land of Egypt and the land of Canaan fainted by reason of the famine. The text then uses a large amount of space to record how Joseph governed this crisis. Let us look at it sentence by sentence, because readings of this passage have long diverged greatly, and the pulpit must above all be honest: state only what the text writes, lay out the positive and negative readings and the divergence of later evaluations as they are, and do not hurry to conclude for either side.

The first stage: money for grain. The people came with money to buy grain, and Joseph gathered up all the money found in the land of Egypt and in the land of Canaan and brought it into Pharaoh's house. The famine continued, the money was spent, and the people came asking: we cannot hide it from my lord; our money is spent; give us bread, we beg you — why should we die in your presence?

The second stage: livestock for grain. Joseph said: if your money is spent, bring your cattle, and I will give you bread for your cattle. So the people brought their horses, their flocks, their herds, and their donkeys, and Joseph fed them with bread in exchange for all their livestock, and sustained them that year. Note that the people themselves proposed the order of exchange: first money, then livestock. Joseph did not seize anything by force; in their extremity the people step by step brought out what little they had to exchange for life. This is not a defense; it is simply the order of the text, and we read it as it stands.

The third stage: land and their own persons. In the second year the people came to Joseph and said that heavy sentence: We will not hide it from my lord, how that our money is spent; my lord also hath our herds of cattle: there is not ought left in the sight of my lord, but our bodies, and our lands: Wherefore shall we die before thine eyes, both we and our land? buy us and our land for bread, and we and our land will be servants unto Pharaoh: and give us seed, that we may live, and not die, that the land be not desolate.

So Joseph bought all the land of Egypt for Pharaoh, for the Egyptians sold every man his field because the famine prevailed over them, and the land became Pharaoh's. Only the land of the priests did not become Pharaoh's, because the priests had a portion assigned them by Pharaoh. Then Joseph said unto the people, Behold, I have bought you this day and your land for Pharaoh: lo, here is seed for you, and ye shall sow the land. And it shall come to pass in the increase, that ye shall give the fifth part unto Pharaoh, and four parts shall be your own, for seed of the field, and for your food, and for them of your households, and for food for your little ones. The people's answer is surprising: "Thou hast saved our lives: let us find grace in the sight of my lord, and we will be Pharaoh's servants." And Joseph made it a law over the land of Egypt unto this day, that Pharaoh should have the fifth part, except the land of the priests only, which did not become Pharaoh's.

The divergence in later readings of this passage is real, and we lay the readings out side by side as they are. One reading stresses the effect of the relief: a seven-year famine was a catastrophe of extinction level, and Joseph, forewarned through dreams years earlier, had stored grain in advance and opened the storehouses in the years of disaster, so that Egypt and the surrounding peoples "did not die" — Jacob's family in Canaan was also saved by this; the people's own words, "Thou hast saved our lives," are the testimony within the text; and a rate of one fifth was not unheard of in the ancient Near East, and afterward continued as an established law. Another reading stresses the consequence for power: after the disaster, the land all belonged to the crown, and the people farmed the king's land and paid the king's tax generation after generation; the independence of the freeholding farmer was gone for good. Whether or not it began voluntarily, a famine completely rewrote a nation's land system, and later readers may well question whether this concentration was just. A third reading reminds us of the narrative's position: this passage stands in Genesis mainly to explain what kind of country Egypt was, in which Jacob's family sojourned, and to lay a foreshadowing for Israel's later descent from guests to slaves in Egypt — when a nation's land and persons are all tied to royal power, the sojourner's settlement today may not still be a settlement tomorrow.

The position of this manuscript is: the pulpit states the text only; it neither defends the text nor uses the pulpit to accuse Joseph. Joseph was the man God raised up to prepare life-saving grain — the overall narrative of Genesis says this clearly. At the same time, the text records every step of the exchanges and every word of the people exactly as they were, without beautifying and without concealing, and this itself invites readers to face honestly the real tension within ancient systems of relief: life-saving grain and lost land can both be true at the same time. We who read Scripture need not hurry to flatten the tension. The Bible records these things to show us how fragile the human condition is before famine and power: money gets spent, livestock gets led away, land changes hands, and only the God who prepared the way before the famine governs the outcome of it all. For Jacob's sojourning family this lesson was especially close to home: the possession they received in Goshen and the grain they ate — not one of them was an eternal guarantee. Their true possession was not in Egypt.

One contrast is also worth noticing: in the same famine, Jacob's family received a possession and provision in Goshen, while the people of Egypt step by step handed over money, livestock, and land. The text does not explain this difference, and we need not invent a reason for it; but sojourners who have been preserved should keep reverence and gratitude in their hearts, rather than treating the loss of others as a matter of course. A person who has received grace and forgets that he too is a guest living on grain will soon find that grace changes flavor in his hands.

And one detail is worth pausing over: the people asked not only for grain, but also for seed. "Give us seed, that we may live." Grain solves today; seed decides tomorrow. Good governance in a disaster year does not only distribute relief; it also gives people the capital to begin producing again. And a person in difficulty should not only ask to get through today, but should ask God to leave him one seed — something that can be sown again: perhaps a craft, a relationship, a hope not yet extinguished. The famine will pass, and the one who still has seed will have a harvest after the lean years.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Do Not Bury Me in Egypt — The Direction at the End of Life (47:27-31)

The text draws the camera back to Goshen. Israel dwelt in the land of Goshen, and they held possessions there and grew and multiplied exceedingly. Jacob lived in the land of Egypt seventeen years — he held Joseph's children, saw Ephraim and Manasseh, and enjoyed seventeen years of old age in Egypt. A man who had fled all his life finally had a stretch of settled years. But the settling did not change his position: he always remembered that he was a sojourner.

"And Jacob lived in the land of Egypt seventeen years: so the whole age of Jacob was an hundred forty and seven years." When the time drew near that Israel must die, he called his son Joseph. Note that the one he called was not the eldest son Reuben, but Joseph. At the end of his life he entrusted the most important matter to the son most able to accomplish it. What he said carried weight in every sentence:

"If now I have found grace in thy sight, put, I pray thee, thy hand under my thigh, and deal kindly and truly with me; bury me not, I pray thee, in Egypt: but I will lie with my fathers, and thou shalt carry me out of Egypt, and bury me in their buryingplace."

First look at the gesture of the oath. "Thigh" in the original is yerek (יָרֵךְ, H3409), denoting the thigh, the hip [5]. Placing the hand under the thigh to swear was a solemn covenant gesture in the ancient Near East, related to descendants and to the continuation of the covenant. In Genesis 24, when Abraham sent his old servant to take a wife for Isaac, he also told him to put his hand under his thigh and swear. That time, the oath concerned a son's marriage and looked toward the future of the descendants; this time, the oath concerned Jacob's own burial and looked toward the return to the promise. Two generations of the same family, the same gesture: at the great passages of life, words alone were not enough — there had to be an oath, and it had to count. By Jacob's generation, faith transmitted down the family had become a habit carved into the bones: God's word is not something to be merely heard, but something to be guaranteed to one another in the most solemn way.

Then look at the content of the charge: "Bury me not in Egypt." Note that Jacob was not despising Egypt. Egypt had saved his whole family's lives, Pharaoh had given them the best of the land, and Joseph had nourished them there for seventeen years. Gratitude was gratitude, and burial was burial; Jacob distinguished the two clearly. The place he wanted to return to was the cave of Machpelah in Canaan — the field Abraham had bought, where Sarah was buried, where Isaac and Rebekah were buried, and where Leah was buried too. He said "bury me in their buryingplace," and "I will lie with my fathers." This was not merely the homesickness of fallen leaves returning to the root; it was the direction of faith: the land God promised to his fathers, he acknowledged to his death. Most of his days were not spent on that land — he was born there, fled and left, sojourned twenty years in Haran, and went down to Egypt in old age — but his grave was to be dug there. In life one may sojourn anywhere; in death one returns into the promise. When Hebrews speaks of the patriarchs, it says they confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth, and that if they had been mindful of the country they left, they might have had opportunity to return, but they desired a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Jacob's grave faced Canaan; his heart faced somewhere farther than Canaan.

Joseph's answer was direct: "I will do as thou hast said." Jacob said: "Swear unto me." And Joseph swore to him. Then Israel bowed himself upon the bed's head. This closing scene is worth contemplating slowly: a man one hundred and forty-seven years old, having just settled the matters after his death, does not weep and does not panic, but worships at the head of his bed. The grave's matter was settled, and his heart was at rest; at rest, he worshiped. Brothers and sisters, this is the posture of a sojourner finishing his life: he did not clutch Egypt's possession and refuse to let go, and he left his children no pile of inheritance disputes; what he left was a direction and an act of worship. He told his children: where we come from and where we are going matter far more than where we live now.

Gathering the whole chapter, the application lands in four sentences. First: life is a pilgrimage, and the years are measured by God. At one hundred and thirty Jacob met Pharaoh and said his days were few; afterward he lived another seventeen years, one hundred and forty-seven in all. He said few, and God measured out one hundred and forty-seven for him; he said evil, and in those evil days God never left him for one step. Your days are not in Pharaoh's hand, not in an employer's hand, not in a doctor's hand; they are measured in God's hand. Not one day more, not one day less; every day is a stop on the pilgrimage, and every day counts.

Second: a narrative of suffering does not equal being forsaken. Before Pharaoh's face, Jacob said his life had been few and evil; this was not a failure of faith but precisely the honesty of faith. Scripture never requires us to call hardship sweet. Your prayers may contain "few and evil"; your testimony may contain tears and unanswered questions. God did not forsake Jacob for telling the truth; He let him stand and bless the king. A person who can speak of his hardship can still be a person of dignity; a person who admits his hardship can still become a person who blesses others.

Third: living people bless those in authority. In whatever city and whatever country we sojourn, we bless and pray for those in authority there. This is not a political statement; it is the sojourner's duty: we acknowledge that we are guests, yet we do not stand as cold-eyed guests; we do not belong here, yet we are willing for the people here to receive good. Jacob entered and left, blessing Pharaoh twice, and Pharaoh treated his family with grace — blessing is never a one-sided loss; it changes the air in a room.

Fourth: the direction faced at death matters more than the place of burial. What Jacob contended for was not a plot of grave land, but a whole family's sense of direction: do not let Egypt become the endpoint; do not let the children forget who we are and where the promise lies. Today it is our turn to think: besides houses and savings, are we leaving our children a clear direction? Do they know that life is a pilgrimage, and that ahead there is a better homeland? Where a person faces at death is where his whole life gathers. While you can still speak, state the direction clearly; while the children can still hear, explain the promise plainly.

One more sentence for those who are settling their lives: pilgrimage is not rootless drifting, and it is not wanting nothing. Jacob accepted the possession in Goshen, raised his family earnestly, and arranged his descendants' future earnestly; he simply did not let settling become burial, and did not let a temporary home impersonate the eternal home. Buying a house, acquiring property, planning for our children — all of these are good, and all can be done earnestly and responsibly; as long as the heart always keeps that confession — the days of my pilgrimage on earth are limited, and the place I truly face is not here — the possession in our hands will not turn around and possess us.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have seen an audience, a piece of national governance, and a dying charge. In the palace, the sojourning old man twice blessed Pharaoh and honestly said that the days of his pilgrimage had been few and evil; in Goshen, the whole family received a possession, was settled, and multiplied; throughout the country, the famine pushed money, livestock, and land step by step into the king's hand — we have read the text as it is, without flattening its tension; at the bedside, the old man swore not to be buried in Egypt, turning his grave toward the promised land and his heart toward a better homeland. Life is a pilgrimage, and the years are measured by God; the person who can speak of his hardship can still stand straight and can still bless others; the direction faced at death matters more than the place of burial.

Let us pray together: Lord who measures our days, thank You: Jacob sojourned one hundred and forty-seven years, and You were absent not one day. Among us, some feel right now that their days are few and evil, some are making a living for their families in a foreign land, some hold resentment toward those in authority, and some have begun to plan for the matters after death. Teach us, like Jacob, to speak of hardship honestly, to stand straight and bless others, to settle the possessions in our hands well, and yet to turn our hearts toward the direction of the promise. May we, having finished the road of pilgrimage, also worship at the bed's head and say: Lord, I have come home. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[1] megurim: Hebrew מְגוּרִים (H4033), a noun denoting sojourning, the condition and years of dwelling as a guest or stranger, from the verb gur (גּוּר, to sojourn, to dwell temporarily); Genesis 47:9, where Jacob says "the days of the years of my pilgrimage are an hundred and thirty years" and "have not attained unto the days of the years of the life of my fathers in the days of their pilgrimage," twice defining his own life and his fathers' lives by this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "borrowed, temporary days" is a homiletical exposition based on the word, kept separate from the lexical facts.

[2] barakh: Hebrew בָּרַךְ (H1288), to bless, to praise, to wish good to come upon another; Genesis 47:7 "Jacob blessed Pharaoh" and 47:10 "Jacob blessed Pharaoh, and went out" both take Jacob as subject and Pharaoh as object; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's discussion of the reversal of rank is an observation on the narrative; Hebrews 7:7, "the less is blessed of the better," has Melchizedek and Abraham as its original context, and this manuscript uses it only as a theological comparison, in passing, without rewriting this chapter's narrative by means of that verse.

[3] me'at vera'im: Hebrew מְעַט (me'at, H4592, few, not many) and רָעִים (ra'im, the plural of H7451 ra, evil, grievous, bad); Genesis 47:9, Jacob's self-description of the days of his life as "few and evil"; the Chinese Union Version renders it "few and bitter" （又少又苦）; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the hardship is in the words, and the weight is also in the words" is homiletical exposition.

[4] 'achuzzah: Hebrew אֲחֻזָּה (H272), a possession, landed property held in the hand, an estate that can be handed down to descendants; Genesis 47:11, where Joseph, as Pharaoh commanded, gave his father and brothers the land of Rameses "for a possession"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Genesis 17:8 and 23:4, where Abraham in Canaan also called himself a sojourner and received the cave of Machpelah as a possession ('achuzzat qever, a possessing of a buryingplace), echoing this chapter's theme of sojourning and possession coexisting; this is this manuscript's cross-observation.

[5] yerek: Hebrew יָרֵךְ (H3409), thigh, hip; Genesis 47:29, where Jacob told Joseph to "put thy hand under my thigh" and swear — a solemn gesture of covenant making; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Genesis 24:2 and 24:9, where Abraham made his old servant swear with the same gesture, as the scriptural basis for this manuscript's "two generations of the same family, the same gesture." The association of the gesture with the continuation of descendants is a general exegetical view concerning ancient Near Eastern custom, and this manuscript marks it as exegetical reference, not as the lexical meaning itself.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Jacob said unto Pharaoh, The days of the years of my pilgrimage are an hundred and thirty years: few and evil have the days of the years of my life been, and have not attained unto the days of the years of the life of my fathers in the days of their pilgrimage." (Genesis 47:9, KJV) "And Jacob blessed Pharaoh, and went out from before Pharaoh." (Genesis 47:10, KJV)

Standing before Pharaoh, Jacob did not disguise his life: it had been few and evil. Yet after saying this, on his way out, he blessed Pharaoh again. Honestly naming your hardship and standing upright to bless others can live in the same person. Your years may not be many, your road may be hard, and what you hold may be little, but you know the God who measures the days, and you are no empty-handed sojourner. Consider also Jacob at the end of the chapter: he settled his whole family, earnestly gave his charge about returning after death, and then worshiped at the head of his bed. A pilgrim is not a homeless person, but a person whose home lies far away; not a person indifferent to this life, but one who lives this life earnestly and sees eternity clearly. Today, speak a blessing over someone near you who holds power and carries burdens of his own; treat today as one stop on the pilgrimage and walk it well — give thanks where thanks is due, give the charges that must be given, and when it is time to worship, open your mouth and worship. When the direction is right, no suffering is wasted, and not one day is lived in vain.

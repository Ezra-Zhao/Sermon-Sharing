## Summary (about 100 words)

The Ten Commandments have been given and the Book of the Covenant spoken; the final step is to make the covenant. Chapter 24 first calls Moses and the seventy elders up the mountain, then builds an altar and pillars below: Moses reads the Book of the Covenant, the people answer a third time — we will do and be obedient; half the blood is sprinkled on the altar and half on the people, and Moses declares: this is the blood of the covenant. Then seventy-four men behold God on the mountain and eat and drink, while Moses enters the cloud alone for forty days and nights to receive the tablets of stone written with the law.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Reading through Exodus, we have come, step by step, to a mountain. In chapter 3, Moses met the flame in the bush at Horeb; in chapter 19, the people camped below Mount Sinai, and God descended in thunder, lightning, thick cloud, and the sound of the trumpet, and boundaries were set around the mountain which no one might cross. In chapter 20, the Ten Commandments were given from the cloud; in chapters 21 to 23 came the Book of the Covenant, unfolding the Ten Commandments into concrete ordinances about servants, property, the vulnerable, feasts, and the land of conquest. The words have all been spoken, sentence by sentence; the promises have all been given, one by one. But the covenant has not yet been made. For two people to have agreed is not yet for a covenant to stand; for a nation to have heard God's words is not yet for that nation to stand within the covenant. Chapter 24, which we read today, is the chapter in which the covenant is made: eighteen verses, no new ordinances, almost entirely ceremony and action — going up the mountain, building an altar, reading the book, sprinkling blood, eating and drinking, entering the cloud. The actions are few, yet every one of them is weighty, because they must turn everything heard over the past three months into a covenant with a seal, with witnesses, and with blood.

Let us first see the structure of the whole chapter clearly. The chapter falls roughly into four parts. The first is the call up the mountain and the preparation for the covenant, verses 1 to 8: God first calls Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel to come up and worship afar off, while Moses alone may come near; then the scene shifts below the mountain, where Moses relates God's words to the people, builds an altar, sets up twelve pillars, offers sacrifices, and sprinkles blood, and after the reading the people give the third — and fullest — answer in the whole book. The second part is the meal on the mountain, verses 9 to 11: seventy-four men go up, see the God of Israel, and eat and drink before Him, and God does not lay His hand on them. The third part is the ascent alone, verses 12 to 18: God calls Moses to go up still further by himself to receive the tablets of stone; Joshua accompanies him partway, the elders wait halfway down, and Moses enters the cloud and remains on the mountain forty days and nights. Beyond these parts, one quiet line runs through the whole chapter: the ordering of nearness. The people are below the mountain, the elders worship on the mountain afar off, and Moses alone draws near to God and enters the cloud. At the very moment the covenant is made, nearness is layered — and that layering is itself a lesson this chapter wants us to see clearly.

In preaching this chapter, this manuscript still keeps the three rules of this series, stated honestly at the outset. First, fact and exposition are kept separate: the actions and numbers the text states directly are preached as stated; observations drawn from the structure — for example, that the covenant was made, the boundary remained, but a door had opened a crack — will be marked as homiletical observation, not as the words of the text. Second, New Testament echoes are marked as New Testament readings: in Mark's Gospel the Lord Jesus says, "this is my blood of the new testament," and Hebrews cites this chapter's blood-sprinkling scene to speak of the blood of the new covenant — that is how the New Testament authors, in Christ, looked back on this chapter; this manuscript will note that when it cites them. This chapter's original setting is the covenant ceremony below Sinai, and the later reading is not poured back into the scene of that day. Third, difficulties are set side by side honestly, with no dogmatic ruling: verses 10 and 11 say plainly that they saw the God of Israel, while Exodus 33:20 says that no man can see God's face and live; there is a real tension, and when this manuscript reaches it, it will place the two passages side by side and state the common scholarly explanation plainly, silencing neither text.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And he said unto Moses, Come up unto the LORD, thou, and Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel; and worship ye afar off. And Moses alone shall come near the LORD: but they shall not come nigh; neither shall the people go up with him." (Exodus 24:1-2, KJV, excerpt)

"And Moses came and told the people all the words of the LORD, and all the judgments: and all the people answered with one voice, and said, All the words which the LORD hath said will we do." (Exodus 24:3, KJV, excerpt)

"And Moses took the blood, and sprinkled it on the people, and said, Behold the blood of the covenant, which the LORD hath made with you concerning all these words." (Exodus 24:8, KJV, excerpt)

"Then went up Moses, and Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel: and they saw the God of Israel: and there was under his feet as it were a paved work of a sapphire stone, and as it were the body of heaven in his clearness. And upon the nobles of the children of Israel he laid not his hand: also they saw God, and did eat and drink." (Exodus 24:9-11, KJV, excerpt)

Let us pray: Lord, we have heard many of Your words, yet You do not let us stop at hearing; You will make a covenant with us and seal Your words upon us with blood. Open our ears today to hear every sentence of the Book of the Covenant clearly; open our hearts to understand why the blood had to be sprinkled on the altar and also on the people; and teach us to long for that meal on the mountain, learning from the seventy elders to draw near to You in reverence. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: From Worshipping Afar Off to Reading and Answering — The Call Up the Mountain, the Altar, and the Book of the Covenant (24:1-8a)

The first part begins with a summons. Verse 1: the LORD says to Moses, Come up unto the LORD — you, and Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel — and worship afar off. Notice first where this summons stands: it is recorded at the head of chapter 24, yet in content it reads like a command issued before Moses went up to receive the ordinances — Moses had gone up and come down several times before this (chapters 19-23, which this manuscript follows without retelling the details here); this chapter places the summons first, then narrates the covenant ceremony below the mountain, and only in verse 9 records that the company actually went up. The order of the narrative is not the order of a clock; this manuscript offers one reminder here only: read this chapter by the progress of the ceremony, not by the timetable. The summons itself contains three layers of people. The first layer is the seventy elders, together with Aaron and his two sons Nadab and Abihu — seventy-three men in all; they may go up the mountain, but they must worship afar off. The second layer is Moses alone; verse 2 is explicit: Moses alone shall come near the LORD, but they shall not come nigh. The third layer is the people: neither shall the people go up with him; they remain below the mountain.

These three layers of nearness must be read alongside the boundaries of chapter 19. In chapter 19, God commanded Moses to set bounds around the mountain; the people might not break through and go up, and whoever even touched the mountain was to be put to death; at that time even Moses had first to go down and warn the people. The boundary was severe and closed. By chapter 24, the boundary is still there — the people still may not go up, the elders may only worship afar off, and Moses alone may draw near — yet within the boundary a door has appeared: more than seventy representatives may go up the mountain and worship at a distance. Here this manuscript marks a homiletical observation, not the words of the text: the covenant was made, the boundary remained, but the door had opened a crack. The covenant did not turn the holy God into a God who may be casually surrounded and watched; His holiness is not discounted. Nor did the covenant shut the people outside the mountain forever; He Himself opened the way, letting the people's representatives ascend step by step. This ordering of nearness is the ground-plan of Israel's later worship: the tabernacle divided into court, holy place, and most holy place; priests, Levites, and people each with their appointed station; the high priest entering the most holy place once a year — the source of that whole order is this one ascent at Sinai. Drawing near to God has never been a disorderly crowding, nor an eternal separation, but an ordered approach that God Himself has laid down.

The scene shifts below the mountain. Verse 3: Moses came down and told the people all the words of the LORD. What he related was all these words just received — the Ten Commandments and the ordinances of the Book of the Covenant, the contents of the preceding chapters. The people's answer came with one voice: All the words which the LORD hath said will we do. This is the second time the people say this sentence. The first was in chapter 19, verse 8: before the commandments had even been given, the people answered first, All that the LORD hath spoken we will do — an answer given after hearing the covenant proposed. This time, the words have been spelled out and the ordinances laid out article by article, and after hearing the contents clearly, the people say it again: we will do. This manuscript offers one homiletical observation here: the first answer was willingness; the second answer is confirmation — not a slogan shouted along with the mood, but a signature made a second time after the details have been heard. Brothers and sisters, many failures of faith fail precisely here: signing the willingness and never reading the details. Israel this time, at least in the record of the ceremony, spoke only after hearing the words to the end.

Verse 4: Moses wrote all the words of the LORD. The scroll thus written receives its formal name in verse 7: the Book of the Covenant, in the original sefer haberit (סֵפֶר הַבְּרִית, H5612 plus H1285) [2] — the book of the covenant. Writing is an extremely important act in this chapter: spoken words scatter, but written words can be read again, examined, and handed down to descendants who never came to Sinai. Having written, Moses rose early in the morning and built an altar below the mountain, and twelve pillars, according to the twelve tribes of Israel. The altar is the place of sacrifice toward God; the twelve pillars represent the twelve tribes standing, not one missing, before the altar. Keep this picture in mind: the altar represents God's side, the pillars represent the people's side, and the covenant ceremony is about to unfold between altar and pillars. Then young men were sent to offer: the young men of Israel offered burnt offerings and sacrificed peace offerings of oxen unto the LORD. The blood of the sacrifices was collected in basins. Verse 6: Moses took half of the blood and sprinkled it on the altar. Notice the order: half the blood is sprinkled on the altar first; the other half is kept back, to be sprinkled on the people only after the Book of the Covenant has been read and the people have answered a third time — that is the eye of the next section; fix the order in mind here: nothing about the sprinkling of the blood is careless.

Verse 7: Moses took the Book of the Covenant and read in the audience of the people. A covenant is not signed behind the people's backs; its terms must be read aloud, read to everyone who is entering it. When the people had heard, they spoke a third time, and this answer carried one layer more than the previous two: All that the LORD hath said will we do, and be obedient. The Chinese Union Version's sentence contains two sets of verbs, and the word order of the original deserves still closer attention: na'aseh venishma' (נַעֲשֶׂה וְנִשְׁמָע, H6213 plus H8085) [3], literally, we will do and we will hear — doing first, hearing after. By ordinary reasoning, a person first understands, then decides whether to do; Israel's sentence reverses the order: undertaking to do first, and continuing to hear afterward. Jewish tradition treasures this word order greatly, regarding it as the people's most precious answer at Sinai; this manuscript cites that as a later traditional reading, not as a dogmatic ruling on the text's original meaning. As for the text itself, at least one thing is stated directly: this answer adds the word obedient (hear-and-obey), showing that what the people undertook was not a one-time gesture but a life of continuing to hear and continuing to obey afterward — a covenant is not a document filed away once signed; a covenant is a life that must be listened to every day after it is signed. By this point in the ceremony, the words have been read, the names signed, the altar built, and the pillars raised; one thing only is still missing — the blood.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Blood Divided in Two and the Meal on the Mountain — The Declaration of the Sprinkling and Beholding God, Eating and Drinking (24:8-11)

Verse 8 is the eye of the whole chapter and the source of this chapter's title: Moses took the blood, and sprinkled it on the people, and said, Behold the blood of the covenant, which the LORD hath made with you concerning all these words. The blood of the covenant, in the original dam haberit (דַּם־הַבְּרִית, H1818 plus H1285) [1] — blood and covenant joined together here for the first time, a phrase that then runs through the whole Bible until the Lord Jesus lifts the cup in the upper room. Look first at the act of sprinkling itself. Of the blood of the sacrifices, half was sprinkled on the altar in verse 6 — the altar represents God; the other half is here sprinkled on the people — the people are the other party of the covenant. The blood of the same sacrifices is sprinkled on both sides. Here this manuscript makes a direct statement of the text's structure, with no exposition mixed in: the blood is divided in two, half on the altar and half on the people, and in this way the covenant builds a bridge between God and man — blood on God's side, blood on the people's side, the same blood joining the two sides within one covenant. Why must a covenant use blood? Stated directly from this chapter's setting: blood is the sign of life, and a covenant is a pledge of life itself; to use blood as the seal is to declare that this covenant is no child's play, and that breaking it costs life; at the same time, sprinkling also carries the sense of cleansing and belonging — the person sprinkled with blood is marked as belonging to this covenant. This manuscript states these two layers and stops there; both are explanations drawn from this chapter's ceremony and the Bible's common symbolism of blood.

Every word of what Moses said as he sprinkled the blood carries weight: Behold — not "you shall remember in the future," but pointing at the blood being sprinkled at that very moment: see it with your own eyes, now. This is the blood of the covenant — the identity of the blood is declared on the spot; it is no ordinary sacrificial blood, but the seal of this covenant. Which the LORD hath made with you concerning all these words — "all these words" are the Book of the Covenant just read; blood and words are bound together: without the words, the blood is mere ritual; without the blood, the words are mere clauses. The words define the covenant's content; the blood seals the covenant's solemnity. Brothers and sisters, this is a mirror for us today: in our age, covenants are made by signature, by fingerprint, by electronic authentication; the lighter the seal, the easier the breaking. The seal below Sinai was blood sprinkled on the body — it could not be wiped off and could not be denied. As a homiletical exposition, marked as such: Israel went home that day with spots of blood on their garments and skin, and every time afterward that they were tempted to break the covenant, they first had to remember how this blood had been sprinkled on them — a covenant is not an abstract idea, but a mark that falls on the body.

How the New Testament looks back on this verse, this manuscript marks, by its rule, as the New Testament authors' reading. At the Last Supper, the Lord Jesus took the cup and said, "For this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins" (Matthew 26:28; Mark 14:24 has "this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many"). [7] Jesus used the very phrase of Exodus 24:8, and the New Testament authors plainly meant the disciples to hear the echo of Sinai: the new covenant, likewise, is made with blood. Hebrews chapter 9 goes into finer detail: the author cites this chapter's scene directly — Moses sprinkled the blood of calves and goats on the book itself and on all the people, and said, this is the blood of the testament which God hath enjoined unto you — and argues from it that even the first covenant (the old) was dedicated with blood, and that the new covenant takes effect through the blood shed by Jesus, the mediator of the new covenant (Hebrews 9:18-22). [7] This manuscript cites these two passages to point out the intertextual relation according to the New Testament's own reading; this chapter's original setting remains the covenant ceremony below Sinai, and the Israelites present that day could not have foreknown the supper in the upper room — that line must be drawn clearly. With the line drawn, the echo stands, and it is precious: the old covenant was made with the blood of sacrifices, the new covenant with the blood of Christ; the old covenant's blood was sprinkled on the altar and on the people, and the New Testament author says that Christ's blood purges the conscience — that further step is the New Testament's exposition, which this manuscript conveys honestly and attributes.

After the sprinkling, the ceremony does not disperse; instead it moves upward. Verse 9: Then went up Moses, and Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel. The summons of verse 1 at the chapter's opening is fulfilled here. Seventy-four men, representing all the people, ascend the mountain they previously dared not even touch. What follows is among the strangest passages in all of Exodus, and the one that makes every expositor most careful: verse 10, And they saw the God of Israel: and there was under his feet as it were a paved work of a sapphire stone, and as it were the body of heaven in his clearness. Sapphire, in the original sappir (סַפִּיר, H5601) [4], a deep blue precious stone. The text describes no shape and no face of God; it describes only the pavement under His feet — as it were paved with sapphire, clear as the body of heaven itself. Notice the restraint of this description: the text's eyes stop at the feet and do not presume to rise higher; what it gives is a scene beneath the feet, not a portrait of a face. That restraint is itself information — the God of Israel can be truly met by His people, and yet is greater than any shape can frame.

Immediately after comes the hardest passage in the chapter, the one that must be treated most honestly. Verse 11 says: And upon the nobles of the children of Israel he laid not his hand. The nobles of the children of Israel, in the original 'atsile bene yisra'el (אֲצִילֵי בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, H678) [5], denotes the chosen leadership of the people. God did not lay His hand on them; also they saw God, and did eat and drink. Yet in Exodus 33:20 God says to Moses with His own mouth: Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live. The two passages — one saying they saw God and lived, eating and drinking; the other saying no man can see God's face and live — stand in real tension, and this manuscript sets them side by side honestly, silencing neither. The common scholarly explanation reads the two with a distinction: what this chapter records is a limited form of theophany — the description reaches only to the feet and says only "as it were," an indirect appearing, veiled; what chapter 33 prohibits is a direct sight of the face of God's glory in itself. Other commentators stress that this chapter deliberately records that God laid not His hand on them, showing that this beholding was an arrangement of God's own grace — a meeting exceptionally preserved, not a routine one. This manuscript sets out the common reading without issuing a dogmatic ruling; but one thing the text states directly and may not be discounted: God did not harm them, and they truly beheld, and truly ate and drank.

"They did eat and drink" — in the King James wording, a handful of words of very great weight. With this, the chain of the covenant ceremony is complete: the book read, the answer given, the blood sprinkled — and then, eating and drinking. After the blood covenant comes the feast; this is a structural observation this manuscript draws from the order of the ceremony: the covenant made, God sits at table with the people's representatives, and the meal of the peace offering serves as the covenant's celebration — the peace offering itself carries the nature of vow-fulfilment and shared eating (and what chapter 24, verse 5 offered was precisely peace offerings), and through this meal eaten by the people's representatives, God declares that the relation this covenant brings is not a confrontation across a judgment bench, but a relation in which they may sit at one table. In the ancient Near East, covenant-making and shared meals often went together; those who had eaten at one table had entered a relation of mutual obligation. Brothers and sisters, this table on the mountain is the source of many tables after it: the Passover meal, the Lord Jesus's supper with His disciples, the church's table of the broken bread — one line running through them all, with a common mark: the covenant is made first, the blood is shed first, and only then do people sit down to eat. The order cannot be reversed: man does not first spread a feast to win God's favour; God first makes the covenant and sprinkles the blood, and then invites man to the table.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Alone into the Cloud for Forty Days and Nights — The Tablets, the Waiting, and the Fire of Glory (24:12-18)

After the feast, the elders went down, but the story leaves Moses alone on the road that climbs higher. Verse 12: And the LORD said unto Moses, Come up to me into the mount, and be there: and I will give thee tables of stone, and a law, and commandments which I have written. The tables of stone make their first formal appearance here: the law and commandments are not Moses's notebook; God says, which I have written — in the wording of verse 12, God says He will give Moses the tables of stone with the law and commandments He has written. Look back one step: the Book of the Covenant was written by Moses (24:4); the tablets, by contrast, are written by God Himself (this chapter, verse 12; see also 31:18, which specifies they were written with the finger of God — cited here as an intra-book intertext). The words have two carriers: the book written by man can be read and handed down; the tablets written by God are the covenant's original, to be kept in the ark as its seal. When Moses later comes down the mountain, it is these two tables he carries in his hands; what the people will examine for generations is that one book. Book and tablets — one soft, one hard; one transmitted, one preserved — both are witnesses of this covenant.

Verses 13 and 14 are an arrangement full of human warmth, and no preacher should skip them: Moses rose up, and his minister Joshua with him; and before going up, he said to the elders, Tarry ye here for us, until we come again unto you: and, behold, Aaron and Hur are with you: if any man have any matters to do, let him come unto them. Notice three things in this arrangement. First, Joshua accompanies him: at this time Joshua is still only Moses's minister; he goes up partway with Moses, yet does not enter the cloud — the cloud opens for Moses alone. Later this minister will succeed Moses and lead the people into Canaan (Deuteronomy chapter 31 and the sequel of this book; cited here as whole-book background); his schooling began with accompanying Moses partway up the mountain, and then waiting forty days below it. Second, the elders are told to wait: Tarry ye here for us, until we come again unto you — this word "until" soon shows its weight: this journey of Moses lasts forty days and nights, and when those waiting lose patience, below the mountain comes the affair of the golden calf (chapter 32; cited here as foreshadowing for the next chapter, not developed here). Third, the arrangement for governing: while Moses is away, Aaron and Hur judge in his place, and the people's disputes do not stop. The man called by God up the mountain does not leave behind him a stalled operation, but an order clearly handed over. This manuscript offers one homiletical exposition, marked as such: a true spiritual leader arranges what is below the mountain before ascending it; able to enter the cloud and draw near to God, and able also to entrust the people's daily cases properly — lacking neither, he is fit to remain long on the mountain.

Verses 15 to 18 are the chapter's landing point, and the quietest yet most overwhelming passage in the book so far. Moses went up into the mount, and a cloud covered the mount. And the glory of the LORD abode upon mount Sinai, and the cloud covered it six days: and the seventh day he called unto Moses out of the midst of the cloud. The glory of the LORD, in the original kevod YHWH (כְּבוֹד־יְהוָה, H3519 plus H3068) [6]. See first the rhythm of the six days and the seventh: Moses reached the mountain, but did not enter the cloud at once; the cloud covered the mountain six days, and he waited outside the cloud for six days; on the seventh day, God called to him out of the midst of the cloud, and only then did he go in. Even a nearness like Moses's must wait for God's call — no rushing, no breaking in. Back in chapter 19, God's descent had thunder and lightning; in this chapter there is no thunder, only cloud, silence, six days of waiting, and then one call. This manuscript offers one observation, marked as such: in the depths of God's fellowship with man, what there is, very often, is not noise but waiting — the words all spoken, the covenant all made, what remains is to wait quietly at the edge of the cloud until He speaks.

Verse 17 gives the sight as seen by those outside the cloud: And the sight of the glory of the LORD was like devouring fire on the top of the mount in the eyes of the children of Israel. The same glory is, within the cloud, a voice calling Moses; in the eyes of the people below, it is fire on the mountaintop. The distance differs, and what is seen differs: what the people behold is a fire that may not be approached; what Moses enters is a cloud in which a voice can be heard. Verse 18 closes the chapter: And Moses went into the midst of the cloud, and gat him up into the mount: and Moses was in the mount forty days and forty nights. This number, forty days and nights, becomes from here on a marker number in Scripture for trial and preparation: Israel's forty years in the wilderness after it, Elijah's forty days and nights to Horeb, the Lord Jesus's forty days in the wilderness — all echo this first forty days and nights at a distance; this set of echoes is a common observation on the biblical narrative, offered here as a pointer only, not developed. Here, too, one difference of source must be marked by the rule: this chapter records only that Moses was on the mountain forty days and nights; it does not say that he neither ate nor drank. Moses himself later recalls in Deuteronomy 9:9: I abode in the mount forty days and forty nights, I neither did eat bread nor drink water. The two sources differ, and when preaching, Deuteronomy's words must not be stuffed back into this chapter as this chapter's record — facts belong to their sources; that is this series' rule. Through those forty days and nights, on the mountain there are the cloud of glory and the tablets; below the mountain, a waiting crowd and elders judging in Moses's place. The covenant is already made, yet the people's testing has only just begun. What will await Moses when he comes down carrying the tablets is the story of the next chapter; this chapter stops inside the cloud, and stops at exactly the right place: the covenant's seal written on tables of stone, the covenant's people waiting at the foot of the mountain, and between them, forty days of cloud.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 24. Remember first its order: the book read first, the blood sprinkled after; the words heard clearly first, the covenant signed after. The people answered three times, each answer weightier than the last, until the final one said: will we do, and be obedient. Remember next the eye of the chapter: Behold the blood of the covenant — the same blood, half on the altar and half on the people, the covenant building a bridge between God and man. Remember also that meal on the mountain: the blood sprinkled, and only then men at the table; seventy-four men beholding God, eating and drinking — the boundary still there, yet the door opened a crack. Remember last the forty days and nights in the cloud: Moses waited six days, waited for God's call, and only then entered the cloud; the glory was fire in the people's eyes and a voice in Moses's ears. May we walk this road today as well: read His words clearly, hear them clearly, and answer for them — not stopping at being moved, but landing in doing and in obeying; and may we treasure the blood sprinkled upon us — in the New Testament's reading, the blood of Christ's covenant, which cannot be wiped off and cannot be denied, the mark that we belong to Him.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You that You did not merely drop Your words upon us; You Yourself made a covenant with us, used blood as its seal, and spread a feast, so that we might draw near to You in reverence. Forgive us for hearing much, answering quickly, and obeying slowly; write the words of the Book of the Covenant, sentence by sentence, into our days, so that we do first and keep on listening day by day. Teach us to long for fellowship in the cloud, and to learn from Moses to wait for Your call — never rushing ahead, never breaking in. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who made the covenant, Amen.

[2] sefer haberit: Hebrew סֵפֶר הַבְּרִית (sefer, H5612, book, scroll; berit, H1285, covenant), the Book of the Covenant; Exodus 24:7 — "And he took the book of the covenant, and read in the audience of the people" — uses this name, denoting the scroll Moses wrote in 24:4, whose content is the Ten Commandments and the ordinances of chapters 21-23. In 2 Kings 23:2 and 23:21, the book of the covenant that Josiah found in the temple bears the same name. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[3] na'aseh venishma': Hebrew נַעֲשֶׂה וְנִשְׁמָע ('asah, H6213, to do, to carry out; shama', H8085, to hear, to obey), "we will do and we will hear (be obedient)"; Exodus 24:7, the people's third answer, whose order is doing first and hearing after, differing from the two earlier answers at 19:8 and 24:3, which speak of doing only. Jewish tradition (e.g., Talmud, Shabbat 88a) praises this order highly as an obedience that acts before it fully hears; the text of this manuscript cites that as a later reading. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[4] sappir: Hebrew סַפִּיר (H5601), sapphire, a deep blue precious stone of the lapis lazuli kind; Exodus 24:10 — "they saw the God of Israel: and there was under his feet as it were a paved work of a sapphire stone." Ezekiel 1:26, describing what is above God's throne, also uses this word; the exact mineral intended is rendered with slight differences in ancient and modern versions (sapphire / lapis lazuli); this manuscript follows the common rendering, sapphire. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[5] 'atsile bene yisra'el: Hebrew אֲצִילֵי בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל ('atsil, H678, nobles, eminent men), the nobles of the children of Israel; Exodus 24:11 — "And upon the nobles of the children of Israel he laid not his hand" — denoting the company of Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and the seventy elders who went up in 24:9. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[6] kevod YHWH: Hebrew כְּבוֹד־יְהוָה (kavod, H3519, glory, weight; YHWH, H3068, the LORD), the glory of the LORD; Exodus 24:16-17 — the glory of the LORD abode upon Mount Sinai, and its sight was like devouring fire. Exodus 16:10, Leviticus 9:23, Numbers 14:10, and other passages also record the appearing of God's glory with this phrase. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[7] The New Testament's citation of this chapter: at Matthew 26:28 and Mark 14:24 the Lord Jesus, instituting the Supper, calls His own blood the blood of the (new) testament (covenant), directly echoing Exodus 24:8; Hebrews 9:19-22 recounts in detail Moses sprinkling blood on the book and the people (and also on the tabernacle and its vessels — the author's composite account of the sprinkling rite, whose range is somewhat broader than the record of Exodus 24 itself; where this manuscript's text cites it, it rests on Exodus 24's two sprinklings, on the altar and on the people, and notes Hebrews' expansion here only), and 9:20 cites the declaration of Exodus 24:8, arguing that the first covenant was dedicated with blood. Wherever this manuscript's text cites a New Testament echo, it marks it as the New Testament authors' retrospective reading of this chapter, not as a direct statement of this chapter's original setting.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "All that the LORD hath said will we do, and be obedient." (Exodus 24:7, KJV) "And Moses took the blood, and sprinkled it on the people, and said, Behold the blood of the covenant, which the LORD hath made with you concerning all these words." (Exodus 24:8, KJV)

Meditate today on one order of things: first the book was read, then the blood was sprinkled. Israel was not sprinkled in confusion; Moses first read the Book of the Covenant to them word by word, they heard it clearly and answered, and only then did the blood come down. God honours our understanding: His words are laid open first, and His covenant is sealed afterward. We so often want to reverse the order — unwilling to read, eager only to be blessed; unwilling to hear the terms clearly, eager only for the seal. Sinai tells us the seal is given to those who have heard the words clearly and are willing to answer for them. Meditate also on the half of the blood sprinkled on the people: a covenant is not a document hung on a wall but a mark that falls on the body; Israel went home with the blood upon them, and from that day, in everything they did, they were to remember this day. We who live by the new covenant in Christ's blood are no different: it is not finished when Sunday's hearing ends; on Monday, at home and at work, the words are to be obeyed one by one, and the ear kept open day by day. Ask the Lord today for a quiet heart to read His word, and for the obedience that does first and keeps on listening: the words heard clearly, the covenant remembered firmly, and a life lived worthy of the blood sprinkled upon us.

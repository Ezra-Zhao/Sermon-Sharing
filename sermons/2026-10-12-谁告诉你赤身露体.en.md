## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace be with you!

Yesterday we looked at Genesis 2: Adam fell into a deep sleep, and God formed Eve from his rib and brought her to him with His own hands. As soon as Adam opened his eyes, he uttered humanity’s first words of love: "This is now bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh."

Today, skipping forward just a few verses, the atmosphere has completely changed. The fruit has been eaten, their eyes are opened, but they are hiding. God walks in the garden, calling to the man: "Where are you?" Adam replies: "I heard Your voice in the garden, and I was afraid because I was naked; and I hid myself."

Then, God asked two consecutive questions. It is these two questions that open up today's four verses. Please turn to Genesis chapter 3, verses 11 to 14. Let me read:

"And He said, 'Who told you that you were naked? Have you eaten from the tree of which I commanded you that you should not eat?' Then the man said, 'The woman whom You gave to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I ate.' And the Lord God said to the woman, 'What is this you have done?' The woman said, 'The serpent deceived me, and I ate.' So the Lord God said to the serpent: 'Because you have done this, you are cursed more than all cattle, and more than every beast of the field; on your belly you shall go, and you shall eat dust all the days of your life.'" (Gen. 3:11-14)

Four verses, three dialogues. God asks Adam, Adam shifts the blame to Eve—and casually shifts it onto God as well; God asks Eve, Eve shifts the blame to the serpent; when it comes to the serpent, God asks not a single question, but directly pronounces judgment.

Today we will examine this in three parts:

First, why does God ask when He already knows the answer?—His questions are not to gather intelligence, but to give you an opportunity to open your mouth and confess sin;
Second, why do humans state only facts without confessing sin?—Every single word Adam and Eve spoke was true, yet not a single word was a confession of sin;
Third, why does God not question the serpent?—God has never used the same tone with humans as He does with the serpent.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. You are the God who personally walked into the garden to seek man even when he hid himself. Today, we pray that You would ask us through these two questions as well: Who told you? Have you eaten? May we not dodge before You, not shift blame, not cover up, but stand honestly in Your light. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: God Asking When He Already Knows—Questions Are the Door of Grace

### 1.1 Does God Not Know the Answer?

Let us first ask a most fundamental question: When God asked, "Who told you that you were naked?" did He really not know?

Of course not. Nowhere in the entire Bible does it say that God needs to gather information from man. Psalm 139 says, "You know my sitting down and my rising up; You understand my thought afar off. You comprehend my path and my lying down, and are acquainted with all my ways. For there is not a word on my tongue, but behold, O Lord, You know it altogether" (cf. Ps. 139:1-4).

Then why did He ask?

Brothers and sisters, this is a pattern that appears repeatedly throughout Scripture: God's questions are never meant to obtain information for Himself, but to lead the one answering to face themselves. Before Abraham offered Isaac, God already knew the outcome; after Cain murdered Abel, God asked him: "Where is Abel your brother?" (Gen. 4:9). Did He not know? He knew. He asked to give Cain one last chance to speak.

In his Genesis commentary, Warren W. Wiersbe gives a reminder regarding this kind of questioning, to the effect that: God's interrogation is not a forced confession like questioning a criminal, but a fatherly calling—He places the words right at your lips, waiting for you to say that word you have refused to say. (Source: The general teaching of Warren W. Wiersbe's *Bible Exposition Commentary* on Genesis 3; paraphrased, not a direct quote.)

Therefore, you must understand today: When God questioned Adam, it was not that Adam was lucky enough to slip by, nor that God was kept in the dark. God's questioning was grace speaking first.

### 1.2 Two Questions That Strike at the Root

Looking closely at these two questions, one asks about the source, and the other asks about the deed.

The first: "Who told you that you were naked?"

The Hebrew word for "told" is *higgid* (derived from the verb *nagad*), meaning to declare, make known, or reveal what is hidden. What God asked about was not a matter of clothing. Adam was naked before as well; Genesis 2:25 says, "And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed." The body had not changed; what changed was the voice within: Previously, what told him "you are very good" was God's evaluation; now, what told him "you are shameful, you must hide" was another voice.

God was actually asking: Adam, whose voice are you listening to now? Does your understanding of yourself come from Me, or from the tempter?

The second: "Have you eaten from the tree of which I commanded you that you should not eat?"

The word "commanded," in Hebrew *tsivvitikha* (from *tsavah*), refers to a covenantal command, not a suggestion or a piece of advice. God brought the matter back to the starting point: Have you plainly violated what I clearly declared?

Notice the order of God's questions. He did not ask about the fruit first; He asked about the voice first. Because before man ate the fruit, he first changed his ears. The entrance of sin is often not the hand, but an ear that first listened to the wrong words. Eve first listened to "Has God indeed said," and only then did she stretch out her hand to take the fruit. It is the same for us today: a person first agrees with a lie in their heart, and then their steps follow.

The comparison among English translations is also very clear:
- KJV: "Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree, whereof I commanded thee that thou shouldest not eat?"
- The question structures of the NIV and NRSV are consistent with the Chinese Union Version, both asking "Who told you" first, and then "Have you eaten of the tree of which I commanded you not to eat."
- The Lu Zhenzhong Version likewise preserves the order of these two questions, first asking the source of the message, then the transgression of the command.

### 1.3 The Holy Spirit Is Still Asking the Same Questions Today

A verse in the New Testament connects directly to this. The Lord Jesus said that when the Holy Spirit comes, "He will convict the world of sin, and of righteousness, and of judgment" (John 16:8).

Therefore, when you feel restless, unable to sleep, and dodging before God after doing something wrong, that is not necessarily the enemy's accusation; it may be the Holy Spirit gently asking you, just as in Genesis 3: Who told you? Did you do it?

King David had the most authority to speak on this. After he sinned, he kept his mouth shut. Psalm 32 says: "When I kept silent, my bones grew old through my groaning all the day long." Later he opened his mouth: "I will confess my transgressions to the Lord, and You forgave the iniquity of my sin" (Ps. 32:3, 5).

In the same situation, silence brings wasting away, while confessing brings forgiveness. Proverbs puts it even more plainly: "He who covers his sins will not prosper, but whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy" (Prov. 28:13).

To summarize the first part in one sentence: **God's question is not an interrogation exam, but a door left open for you to walk through. He asks because He still wants to hear it from your own mouth.**

## (24:00 - 40:00) Part 2: Stating Only Facts Without Confessing Sin—Two Ways of Shifting the Blame

### 2.1 Every Single Word in Adam's Statement Was True

It was Adam's turn to answer. His response is worth examining word by word:

"The woman whom You gave to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I ate." (Gen. 3:12)

Did Eve hand it to him? Yes. Did he eat the fruit? Yes. On a factual level, there is no flaw in this statement.

Yet here is where the problem lies: he recounted all the facts, leaving out only one thing—confession. In the entire sentence, there is not a single "I was wrong." The subject keeps changing: it was the woman You gave me, she handed it to me, and only at the very end does it come down to "and I ate"—and that "ate" is framed as though it were a step he was nudged into taking.

What is even more piercing is the contrast. Yesterday we read Genesis 2:23, where Adam called her "bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh"; today in Genesis 3:12, he calls her "the woman whom You gave to be with me." The name is gone, the intimacy is gone, he does not even call her his "wife" anymore; all that remains is a title pointing toward blame. The very first thing sin does is not merely separate man from God; it first drives a wedge between those who are closest.

There is yet another layer we must not overlook: "whom You gave to be with me." The spearhead of this half of the sentence has already bypassed Eve and is aimed squarely at God. The implication is: "Had You not given her to me, this would never have happened today." In a single sentence, Adam passed the buck to two parties—to the person beside him, and to the God in heaven.

Brothers and sisters, does this not sound like us? Whenever trouble arises, our statement of facts is usually accurate; we remember the time, the place, and who spoke first with utter clarity. The only thing that is forever blurry is our own part in it.

### 2.2 Eve’s Answer: Half Truth, Half Passing the Blame

God turned to the woman and asked even more concisely: "What is this you have done?"

The woman said, "The serpent deceived me, and I ate." (Gen. 3:13)

The Hebrew word for "deceived" is *hissi'ani*, meaning to beguile, to lead astray through deception. Paul later confirmed this, saying that Eve was deceived and fell into transgression (cf. 1 Tim. 2:14; 2 Cor. 11:3). So what Eve said was not untrue; she genuinely had been deceived.

Yet take note: her words follow the exact same sentence structure as Adam's: first pointing to another party who took the initiative, and only at the very end lightly dropping the clause, "and I ate." Being deceived was real, but reaching out her hand was just as real. Scripture never relieves man of his own responsibility simply because he was tempted.

The Book of James articulates this principle most thoroughly: "Let no one say when he is tempted, 'I am tempted by God'; for God cannot be tempted by evil, nor does He Himself tempt anyone. But each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed." (Jas. 1:13-14)

Did you hear that?—"Let no one say, 'I am tempted by God.'" That is the precise counter-example to Adam's words, "the woman whom You gave to be with me." More than two thousand years later, James effectively took this phrase of blame-shifting from the Garden of Eden and penned it into church doctrine, admonishing every generation: stop speaking like this.

### 2.3 David and Adam: Separated by a Single Sentence

Also questioned after falling into sin, 2 Samuel presents us with an entirely different scenario.

Nathan the prophet came before David, finished telling his story, and pointed at him, saying, "You are the man!" David did not recount the facts; he said nothing about Bathsheba, nothing about Uriah, nothing about the battlefield. He spoke only one sentence: "I have sinned against the Lord." (2 Sam. 12:7, 13)

Just a few words. No shifting of the subject, no reciting of causes and effects—simply "I."

Psalm 51 is the unfolding of that one sentence. David said, "I was brought forth in iniquity"; he also said that he acknowledged his sin to God and confessed against Him alone he had sinned (cf. Ps. 51:3-5). Throughout that psalm, David did not invoke a single other person's name to exonerate himself.

Place Adam's answer sheet beside David's, and the difference lies not in whose sin was lighter, but in who was the first to stop deflecting. For the one who shifts blame, the more facts he recites, the further he drifts from forgiveness; for the one who confesses, the fewer excuses he offers, the wider the door opens.

In Romans 5:12, Paul looks back at the Garden of Eden and says that through one man sin entered the world. Pay close attention to Paul's assessment: he did not write "sin entered the world through the woman," nor did he write "through the serpent"—the responsibility for sin fell ultimately upon the one who received the commandment yet shifted the blame away. God's righteousness is not derailed by grammatical evasions; what He looks for is whether, before each person's "and I ate," there is a genuine "I was wrong."

To wrap up this second part: **Shifting blame is not lying; shifting blame is arranging the truths into a lineup just so you can stand at the very back of the line. What God wants is not for you to recount the whole story, but for you to put yourself back in the place of the subject.**

## (40:00 - 50:00) Part Three: God Does Not Question the Serpent—The Boundary Between Judgment and Grace

### 3.1 When It Comes to the Serpent, There Are No Questions

The third dialogue is the most extraordinary: God did not ask the serpent a single question.

He questioned Adam, He questioned Eve, but when He came to the serpent, the tone changed completely: "Because you have done this, you are cursed." (Gen. 3:14) No "Where are you?", no "What is this you have done?"—only direct sentencing.

Why? Because questioning is grace extended to human beings, not to the devil. When God questions man, He speaks within relationship, leaving room for repentance; toward the source of temptation, God does not negotiate, He does not converse, and He provides no platform for explanation.

This point is vital for the church today. Some people expend a great deal of energy "dialoguing" with the powers of darkness, analyzing them, and chasing after them asking why. That is not the biblical way: toward man, God asks questions patiently; toward the serpent, God directly defines and directly pronounces judgment. What we must learn is to reserve patience for people, and to draw the line against evil.

### 3.2 The Words in the Curse: On Your Belly You Shall Go, and You Shall Eat Dust All Your Days

The verdict reads: "On your belly you shall go, and you shall eat dust all the days of your life." (Gen. 3:14)

The word "dust" ties together the threads of the entire chapter. Man was formed from the dust of the ground (Gen. 2:7), and the serpent's destiny is to eat dust; after sinning, man must eat bread in the sweat of his face until he returns to the dust (cf. Gen. 3:19). Eating dust is a posture of shame, a position of being brought low. The prophet Isaiah later employed this very imagery to describe the end of God's enemies, saying they would "lick up the dust of your feet" (cf. Isa. 49:23).

Yet this verse is merely the beginning of the sentence. The very next verse, 3:15, is what we will examine tomorrow—the Seed of the woman shall bruise the serpent's head. That is the first promise of the gospel in the entire Bible. Today, let us leave the door open right here: God's sentence upon the serpent is not yet finished, but His search for man today is already halfway told. He questioned, He listened, and He did not turn away and walk off.

### 3.3 That God Is Still Speaking Here Is the Greatest Grace

Looking back over this entire scene, the detail most easily missed is this: from Genesis 3:9 to 3:14, God never stopped speaking.

Man hides, God seeks; man deflects, God questions; man frames sin as someone else's fault, yet God continues to probe question by question. He could have remained entirely silent and simply driven them out immediately. But He did not. The interrogation itself is proof that He refuses to let go.

Psalm 103 says: "The Lord is merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in mercy. He will not always strive with us, nor will He keep His anger forever." (Ps. 103:8-9) The God who asked questions in the Garden of Eden is the exact same God under David's pen.

Proverbs 15:29 says that the Lord is far from the wicked, but He hears the prayer of the righteous. And in God's eyes, the person who confesses is walking the very path that leads from being "one who hides" back to being "one who prays."

To wrap up this third part: **God fell silent and judged the serpent, but opened His mouth and questioned man. O man, discern this special favor—that He is still willing to question you means He has not yet given up on you.**

## (50:00 - 60:00) Applications for Six Groups of People · Altar Call · Response Prayer

### Application One: For Seekers Who Have Not Yet Believed in the Lord

Friend, you may feel that the word "sin" is very distant from you. But shifting blame is not distant from you: whenever something goes wrong, our very first reaction is almost always to explain, not to admit. This conversation in the Garden of Eden describes the instinct of all humanity.

Today, this God did not wait for Adam to polish his excuses; He first stepped into the garden, and He was the first to speak and ask questions. His attitude toward you is identical: He does not stand far off waiting for you to clean yourself up; He comes looking for you first. Are you willing to let Him find you?

### Application Two: For Husbands, Wives, Parents, and Children in Marriage and Family

Yesterday we said that the wife is taken from the husband's side, meant to be restored to a place of equality, protection, and cherishing. Today let us add another word: what wounds a couple most deeply is not an argument, but that phrase, "It's all your fault."

With one sentence—"the woman whom You gave to be with me"—Adam turned the "bone of my bones" into a stranger. The next time a dispute flares up, hold back that impulse to shift the blame, and learn from David to speak in short sentences: "In this matter, I have my own fault." In a family, whoever is first to own their part is the one who opens the door. For parents to admit a fault before their children is not shameful; it is the most authentic lesson in faith those children will witness in their entire lives.

### Application Three: For Those in the Workplace and on Teams

When a blunder occurs in the workplace, the most common scene in the conference room is that the facts become increasingly detailed while the responsibility becomes increasingly diluted. The project belongs to everyone, yet the mistake seems to belong to no one.

Today's scripture offers you a different approach: by all means, keep your report accurate, but place your own part right at the front, not hidden at the end of the sentence. Proverbs 11:1 says that dishonest scales are an abomination to the Lord, but a just weight is His delight. The weights in our speech must be just as accurate—neither magnifying others' faults nor minimizing our own. Such a person God can entrust, and the team will dare to trust as well.

### Application Four: For New Believers Learning to Pray

Confessing sin is not berating yourself. Biblical confession is very specific: state the exact thing God asked about in a short sentence, without adding explanations, and without bringing in a "but."

Tonight when you pray, you can try it like this once: instead of saying, "Lord, I'm sorry, because of... that's why I...," just say, "Lord, I was wrong in this matter." Then stop, and listen. David said that after he confessed, God forgave him. First John also promises us: "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9).

### Application 5: For Leaders, Parents, and Those Who Serve

Those who lead others fall most easily into a habit: only questioning others, never letting God question them.

Adam was the steward of the garden; when trouble arose, God sought him out first. The higher your position, the more you must stand beneath the problem first. The mark of a healthy leader is not always having the right answer, but being able to be the first in your small group, or in your home, to say, "For this part, I confess to God first." When you do this, the people you lead will dare to bring out their own hiding.

### Application 6: For the Whole Church

What kind of place is the church? It is a place where we no longer hide. We come here every week not to put on a show of "I'm doing great," but to respond to the God who personally walked into the garden searching for us.

The God of Genesis 3 keeps searching—all the way from Genesis to Luke 15, where that father stands at the intersection waiting for his younger son to return home; the son had rehearsed his speech all along the way, but the father didn't let him finish it—he embraced him and kissed him. Every single one of us is someone who was sought and found first before learning how to turn back (cf. Luke 15:20-24).

### The Spark Moment

[Pause, slow down tone]

Brothers and sisters, let me ask you one question—just one. You don't need to answer out loud; answer the God in your heart:

**The last time you confessed your sin to God, was the first phrase out of your mouth "I was wrong," or was it "Because of them"?**

Think about it. Think about the last time you apologized to your wife, to your husband, to your child, to your coworker—how did you phrase it? Did you spend three minutes explaining the background first before finally getting around to yourself?

Adam stated an entire sentence of facts; he missed only three words: *I was wrong*. David spoke only four words: *I have sinned against the Lord*. One drifted farther and farther the more he spoke; the other drew close the moment he spoke.

[Pause 5 seconds, intensify tone]

It is today, not tomorrow.

Stop waiting until your emotions settle down before confessing; stop waiting for the other person to bow their head first before you bow yours; stop pushing that confession to the very end of your explanations. **God is walking into your garden Himself today, and He is asking you: Who told you? Have you eaten?**

You can answer Him right now.

### Altar Call

I am making two calls right now:

**The first call**: You know clearly in your heart that there is a matter you have shifted for a long time—shifted onto your spouse, onto your parents, onto your circumstances, even shifted onto God. Today, if you are willing to stop shifting blame, and from your seat say to God, "Lord, it was my fault," please stand up, and we will pray for you together. There is no shame in it; those who stand up before God are those who have been found by Him.

**The second call**: For friends who have not yet accepted Jesus. That God who searched for Adam in the Garden of Eden later became man Himself, bearing on the cross everything that man dared not bear. Today He is searching for you just the same. If you are willing to accept Him and let Him cleanse you from all unrighteousness, please raise your hand, and I want to pray for you.

[Wait for response, pray for those standing and raising hands]

### Concluding Response Prayer

Let us bow our heads and pray together:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. Man hides, yet You seek; man makes excuses, yet You ask. The two questions You asked Adam have pierced into our hearts today as well.

Lord, we confess that we are far too good at stating facts and far too poor at confessing sin. We call our spouses 'the one You gave me,' we turn our circumstances into justifications, and we turn Your sovereign arrangements into excuses. Today we take all these words back, and we say to You only this: Lord, I was wrong; I have sinned against You.

We pray that You would forgive us and cleanse us from all unrighteousness. We ask You to bring our families back from 'it’s all your fault' to 'bone of my bones'; bring our mouths back from explanations to confession; bring our hearts back from hiding into Your light.

We also pray for the friends who stood up and raised their hands today: just as You found Adam in the garden, we ask that You would personally find them today, embrace them, and lead them home.

Thank You, for You are the God who pronounces judgment on the serpent, and even more, the God who speaks to man, asks questions, and extends grace to seek us out. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

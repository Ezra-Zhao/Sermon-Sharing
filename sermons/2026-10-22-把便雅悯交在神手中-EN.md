## Summary (about 100 words)

The famine was severe and the family's grain was eaten up, so Jacob urged his sons to go down to Egypt again. Judah stepped forward as surety, willing to bear the blame forever, in contrast to Reuben's empty pledge. Jacob finally let go: he sent gifts and double silver, and placed Benjamin in the mercy of God Almighty. Joseph saw Benjamin and could not hold back his tears; he set a feast before them, and no one envied Benjamin's fivefold portion — a rehearsal of reconciliation.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The previous chapter's story stopped at a frozen household. The ten sons had carried grain back from Egypt, and had also carried back a problem: that lord in Egypt had said that next time they must bring their young brother Benjamin with them, or they would not see his face again. Simeon had already been detained in Egypt as a hostage. As soon as Jacob heard the three words "Benjamin," he made his words final: Joseph is gone, Simeon is gone, and now you want to take Benjamin away too. If harm comes to Benjamin, you will bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to the grave. In Jacob's heart, Benjamin was not just one of eleven sons; he was the last one Rachel had left behind, the whole darling of his old age. Whoever touched Benjamin was touching his life.

But days do not wait for anyone. A famine does not stop just because a father cannot bear to let go. This chapter opens with only two plain sentences in the Bible, yet they lie like two stones on the whole family's chest: "And the famine was sore in the land. And it came to pass, when they had eaten up the corn which they had brought out of Egypt..." The grain was eaten up. These words are the most real lesson of many families: you think that if you drag a problem along, the problem will pass by itself; but on the day the grain jar is empty, the problem returns before you, bigger and more urgent than before, exactly as it was. Jacob had to make the decision once more, and this time he could not dodge it.

This chapter has no thunder and no fire, no parting of the Red Sea, no dream of a ladder. It tells how an old man opened the hand he had clenched all his life, finger by finger; how a middle-aged son stood up and pledged his whole life as surety for his brother; and how a man in a high position in a foreign land, when he saw his brother, turned away, hid in an inner room, and wept aloud. Three people, three kinds of tears, circling the same matter: placing what you can least bear to part with into the mercy of God.

Let us read several key passages from today together:

"And Judah said unto Israel his father, Send the lad with me, and we will arise and go; that we may live, and not die, both we, and thou, and also our little ones. I will be surety for him; of my hand shalt thou require him: if I bring him not unto thee, and set him before thee, then let me bear the blame for ever." (Genesis 43:8-9, excerpt, KJV)

"Take also your brother, and arise, go again unto the man: And God Almighty give you mercy before the man, that he may send away your other brother, and Benjamin. If I be bereaved of my children, I am bereaved." (Genesis 43:13-14, excerpt, KJV)

"And he lifted up his eyes, and saw his brother Benjamin, his mother's son, and said, Is this your younger brother, of whom ye spake unto me? And he said, God be gracious unto thee, my son. And Joseph made haste; for his bowels did yearn upon his brother: and he sought where to weep; and he entered into his chamber, and wept there." (Genesis 43:29-30, excerpt, KJV)

"And he took and sent messes unto them from before him: but Benjamin's mess was five times so much as any of theirs. And they drank, and were merry with him." (Genesis 43:34, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, every one of us holds a Benjamin in his hand — the one we can least bear to part with and least dare to hand over. Today, through Jacob's opened hand and Judah's words of surety, teach us: entrusting is not giving up; it is placing what is most precious into Your mercy. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Grain Was Eaten Up — Judah Stepped Forward as Surety (43:1-10)

First look at how the deadlock was broken by the famine. When the grain was eaten up, Jacob said to his sons, "Go again, buy us a little food." Please note the tone of this sentence: go again and buy a little grain, as if it were no more than a trip to a neighboring village. He did not mention Benjamin at all, as if by not mentioning him, that condition did not exist. This is the way we often live: we say only the half we are willing to hear, and pretend we have not heard the hardest half. The father here did not really not know; he could not bear to know.

Judah spoke the other half aloud: "The man did solemnly protest unto us, saying, Ye shall not see my face, except your brother be with you." Judah did not go around in circles, nor did he blame his father. He first laid the facts out level: there is only one road, and only one condition; for every day we linger at home, the whole family goes hungry one more day. He also added a very practical sentence: "For except we had lingered, surely now we had returned this second time." This sentence is calm, yet hard to bear: how many days has this family wasted in vain because it could not bear to let go? Dear brothers and sisters, delay is never free. Many families and many churches clearly know there is only one road to take, yet because they fear pain and fear loss, they drag on and on, and in the end the interest they pay is dearer than the principal. Judah here shows a growth: he began to face the facts directly, and no longer pretended together with his father.

Then comes the first peak of the whole chapter. Judah said, "Send the lad with me, and we will arise and go; that we may live, and not die." "I will be surety for him; of my hand shalt thou require him: if I bring him not unto thee, and set him before thee, then let me bear the blame for ever." Please place these words beside Reuben's words in the previous chapter and listen. What did Reuben say at that time? "Slay my two sons, if I bring him not to thee:" Jacob listened and, without a word, said only that his son would not go down with them. Why? Because Reuben's pledge was empty talk. Slaying his own two sons to pay with their lives sounds fierce, but on careful thought it is all hollow: if Benjamin truly did not return, would Jacob really kill two grandsons? What could two more deaths exchange back? What Reuben produced was emotion; what he staked was other people's lives; the fiercer his words, the more they showed he could not bear the weight. What he was anxious about was proving his own filial devotion, not truly carrying his brother's life on his own shoulders.

Judah's pledge was completely different. What he staked was himself. "Of my hand shalt thou require him" — Father, the one you hold responsible is me, not my children, not anyone else. "If I bring him not unto thee, then let me bear the blame for ever." This "bear the blame," in the original chata' (חָטָא, H2398), means at its root to sin, to be at fault, to bear the consequences of sin [2]. What Judah was saying was: if I fail, let this account be recorded under my name all my life; before you I will forever be the sinner who lost your son. This was not fierce talk in a moment of impulse; this was a signature. He handed the right to hold him responsible into his father's hand, and left the consequences to himself. This is what the growth of a surety looks like: from staking other people's lives to staking his own name; from talking tough to bearing consequences.

The word for being surety, in the original 'arav (עָרַב, H6148), is to be surety, to guarantee, to pledge something as security [1]. This word has another moving echo in the Old Testament: in the Psalms, the hand of His servant who becomes "surety for good" is the hand of faithfulness that keeps covenant. Judah used exactly this word. He was not saying "I will try my best"; he was pledging his whole self as security: if Benjamin loses a single hair, you may take it out on me.

We must also see the change in Judah the man in order to understand the weight of these words. The one who in that year proposed selling Joseph to the Ishmaelites was Judah himself — "What profit is it if we slay our brother? ... sell him." For more than twenty years he knew best what this family owed Joseph. Later in Canaan, confronted face to face by his daughter-in-law Tamar, he admitted with his own mouth, "She hath been more righteous than I" — the first time in his life he bowed his head and confessed his own sin. The Judah standing before his father now was no longer the man who had sold his brother: he had begun to stake himself for his brother. This line has not finished running; in the next chapter, before the judgment seat of the lord of Egypt, he will go even further, willing to remain himself as a slave in exchange for Benjamin's return home. The evidence of a person's true repentance is not tears; it is whether he is willing, in the very same kind of matter in which he once fell, to live in a different way. Judah sold his brother; now he pledged himself for his brother. When God restores a person, He often begins right here.

Of course, the Bible does not praise being a surety indiscriminately. Proverbs 11:15 says plainly: "He that is surety for a stranger shall smart for it: and he that hateth suretiship is sure." This word of wisdom must not be omitted: rashly striking hands as surety for people unconnected with you, and staking yourself and your family into someone else's risk, is folly, and the Bible says so without politeness. Where is the difference between Judah's suretyship here and the reckless suretyship Proverbs warns against? What Proverbs speaks of is for strangers, for vanity, for a moment of loyalty; what Judah pledged was his own brother, the road by which the whole family would live, and the debt he had personally caused in that year. This is not reckless suretyship; this is repayment, this is taking responsibility. The wisdom of suretyship does not lie in never being a surety, but in distinguishing clearly: what is yours to bear, and what you cannot bear and should not bear. To step forward for the life of your closest kin and for the account you yourself owe is taking responsibility; to strike hands for face, for favors, and for greed is rashness. May God give us Judah's sense of responsibility, and also give us the clarity of Proverbs.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Israel Let Go — Placing His Son in the Mercy of God Almighty (43:11-15)

After Judah had spoken, the narrative changed the form of address: the one speaking was no longer "Jacob," but "their father Israel." This detail is worth pausing over. In the biblical narrative, when the old man is helpless, grasping, and scheming, he is mostly called Jacob; when he has been touched by God and remembers the covenant, he is called Israel. The one who spoke this time was Israel. He said, "If it must be so now," and then made the arrangements one by one. Note that his letting go was not a collapse, nor giving up in despair; he did everything that could be done to the best, and then handed what could not be done to God. This is exactly the order of entrusting; let us look slowly.

First, take gifts. He told his sons to take the best fruits of the land: balm, honey, spices, myrrh, nuts, and almonds, naming them one by one. These things in famine-stricken Canaan were precious, item by item, yet Jacob was willing to bring them out. This was not bribery; this was humbling himself: in that year, when he went to meet his brother Esau, he also sent gifts ahead of him; he understood that a hardened man must first be softened by gifts. He did not put on lofty airs, nor did he assume the posture of a patriarch; for the sake of the whole family's life, he was willing to bow his head and willing to pay the cost. When a person grows old, what is hardest to lay down is often not money, but face. Israel here laid it down. Second, take double silver. The silver that had been put back in their sacks the first time was to be carried back in its original amount, and another amount again besides. Jacob also left a considerate sentence: that silver was perhaps an oversight. Perhaps the other side had made a mistake; do not condemn first, but first make the account clean. When a person reaches this point, it is honesty, and it is also clearness: our hands must be clean and our accounts clear; only then can we stand upright before others. Third, he handed Benjamin over with his own mouth: "Take also your brother." How much strength it took for this sentence to come out of his mouth, we do not know. In the previous chapter he had said Benjamin could not go down; in this chapter he said with his own mouth: take him. This is how a person's hand opens: not because he no longer loves, but because he loves to the point of knowing that clenching is also a dead end, and letting go is where the road of life is.

Then comes the highest prayer of the whole chapter, only one short sentence, yet the crystallization of Jacob's faith in his whole life: "And God Almighty give you mercy before the man, that he may send away your other brother, and Benjamin." God Almighty, in the original 'el shaddai (אֵל שַׁדַּי, H7706) [3]. This form of address is not a polite formula; it is Jacob's family line: God appeared to Abraham and said, "I am the Almighty God," promising him many descendants; God appeared to Jacob at Bethel and also blessed him with this name, that he would be fruitful and multiply. In his old age, what he remembered was not his own cleverness, but the God of his fathers, the God of Bethel. The God who at the ladder said He would not leave him, and the God who wrestled with him at the ford of Jabbok — now he entrusted his youngest son into His hands. Please also listen carefully to the content of his prayer: he did not ask that his sons' road be without shock or danger; he asked that they "find mercy before the man." Mercy, in the original rachamim (רַחֲמִים, H7356), compassion and pity, from the same source as a mother's heart for her child [4]. Jacob was very clear: that lord of Egypt has power and position; whether his heart is hard or not is not in my hand, nor in the hand of gifts and silver, but only in God's hand. So he did not ask to control the situation; he asked that that man's heart be touched by God. How often when we pray, we ask God to settle everyone for us; Jacob asked God to do a work of mercy in one man's heart. For an old man to speak to this point in prayer is truly to understand prayer.

The last sentence is the heaviest sentence of the whole chapter: "If I be bereaved of my children, I am bereaved." Some hear this sentence as resignation, as despair. Please listen once more: these are the words of a father who, after doing everything and praying everything, hands the outcome over. He did not say his sons would certainly return; he had already "lost" a Joseph in his life, and he knew that entrusting does not equal God's acting according to our script. Psalm 37:5 says, "Commit thy way unto the LORD; trust also in him; and he shall bring it to pass." The point of entrusting has never been "He must accomplish it according to my intention," but that after the matter is handed over, my heart can rest. Entrusting is not giving up: a person who gives up does nothing and lets go with his eyes closed; Jacob prepared gifts, prepared silver, obtained a surety, and prayed, and only then let go. Entrusting is also not a transaction: it is not that if I hand over Benjamin, God owes me a Benjamin intact. Entrusting is moving the one I can least bear to part with out of my own hands — hands that will grow old, will tremble, and cannot protect him — into those almighty hands full of mercy. After he is moved over, whether loss or gain, I accept it. Brothers and sisters, only parents understand the price it costs to say this sentence. But only the person who says this sentence truly experiences what rest is: not rest only on the day the son arrives home safely, but rest first, at the very moment of letting go.

There is also a detail to note: after Jacob said, "If it must be so now," he bargained no further. He did not send someone to test the waters again, did not say to send half the sons first as a trial, and did not sew some mark into Benjamin's clothing. When a person has truly entrusted, his actions become clean and direct instead: once the arrangements were made, he let them set out. The Scripture says they arose and went again to the man, and it shows the old man standing where he was, handing his row of sons, together with the youngest one, over in prayer as he sent them away. There is no description of tears in this scene, yet we who read it all understand: a father watching the back of Benjamin disappear on the road down to Egypt — for as many days as that road would take, so many days would he stand at the door. Entrusting is not a ceremony finished in one prayer; it is every day afterward, while the grain has not yet returned and there is no news of the people, still not stretching out a hand to snatch back what was handed over. Those of us who are parents all have this lesson: we entrust at the moment the child walks out the door, snatch it back when we wake in the middle of the night, and hand it over again in prayer the next day. Never mind — Jacob also came through like this: in the previous chapter he would not let go for anything; in this chapter he let go. God does not mind that we learn slowly; He only fears that on the day the grain is gone, we still clench our hands and refuse to learn.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: A Fivefold Portion — A Rehearsal of Reconciliation (43:16-34)

When the brothers arrived in Egypt, they first passed through a gate of fear. Joseph commanded the steward to bring them into the house, and they were afraid, thinking it was because of the silver from the first time that hands would be laid on them. At the door they hurried to explain themselves to the steward, and the steward's answer was beyond everyone's expectation: "Peace be to you, fear not: your God, and the God of your father, hath given you treasure in your sacks." Please note: an Egyptian steward spoke the words "your God" from his mouth. Who taught him this sentence, we do not know; what we know is that the God Almighty to whom Jacob prayed in Canaan had already arrived in Egypt a step ahead with His work of mercy, and even the steward's mouth had become an outlet reporting peace. Children, the tears you shed and the prayers you prayed for your sons and daughters in Canaan — God, in places you cannot see, had already begun to work. Simeon was also brought out from prison to them, and the brothers were reunited in a first step.

Joseph came home at noon, and the brothers presented the gifts and bowed themselves to him to the earth — the sheaves and stars he had dreamed of in his youth were fulfilled before his eyes once more, but this time the hand writing the story was not revenge, but mercy. Joseph asked about his father's welfare: "Is your father well, the old man of whom ye spake? Is he yet alive?" They answered, "Thy servant our father is in good health, he is yet alive." He asked like an outsider, yet those who hear it know clearly in their hearts: this "Is he yet alive?" was what a son who had left home for more than twenty years had endured so many years to ask aloud.

Then Joseph saw Benjamin, his brother by the same father and the same mother; in the year he was born, their mother Rachel had died on the road. "Is this your younger brother, of whom ye spake unto me? God be gracious unto thee, my son." These were the first words Joseph spoke to his brother, and they were a blessing. Next the Scripture writes: "And Joseph made haste; for his bowels did yearn upon his brother: and he sought where to weep; and he entered into his chamber, and wept there." This yearning of the heart, in the original, uses the same root as rachamim: his compassion, his inward parts, surged and grew hot within him [4]. Brothers and sisters, please read this verse against the father's prayer: Jacob in Canaan asked God Almighty to give his sons "mercy before the man"; when they reached Egypt, the first thing that stirred in that man's heart was precisely mercy. The father asked that mercy come upon that man, and God first raised mercy up in that man's own heart. Joseph's tears were the echo of Jacob's prayer. He finished weeping, washed his face, came out, refrained himself, and commanded the meal to be set on. A man in office cannot lose composure in public; he shut his tears in the inner room and set grace on the table. How many of us are also like this: holding up in front of people, yet in the inner room we have wept so many times that only God knows. That God knows is enough.

The arrangement at the meal was everywhere a test, and everywhere a lesson. The Egyptians ate by themselves, Joseph by himself, and the brothers by themselves, because the Egyptians abhorred eating with the Hebrews. The brothers were seated according to their ages, from the firstborn to the youngest without the slightest error, and they "marvelled one at another": how does this foreign lord know even the order of our brotherhood so clearly? They marvelled, yet still did not recognize that the one sitting across from them was exactly the one they had sold. People are often just this close to God's answer: sitting at the same table, eating his food, yet not recognizing him. At last the dishes were served; Joseph sent portions to them from before him, portion by portion, and Benjamin's portion was five times as much as any of theirs. Portion, in the original mas'eth (מַשְׂאֵת, H4864), refers to the portion of a gift divided from the table of an honored person and sent to the guests [5]. The fivefold portion set before the youngest brother was visible to everyone at the table.

Please hold your breath and watch this scene. This family's tragedy had in that year begun from favoritism: one coat of many colors, given by the father to Joseph alone, and the brothers' hatred had burned up. Now the same scene was repeated: the youngest, the most favored one, received a portion five times more than the others. If the fire of jealousy in the brothers' hearts had not yet gone out, they would have changed expression on the spot, exchanged glances with one another, and recorded another account in their hearts. But the Scripture writes only one sentence: "And they drank, and were merry with him." Not one person was jealous; not one person complained. They ate, they drank, and they were glad together with this "outsider." This is a rehearsal of reconciliation: the formal recognition had not yet arrived, and the last gate before the judgment seat had not yet been passed, but this family's oldest sickness — jealousy — did not break out at this table. When God heals a family, He often begins with a meal; the evidence that a heart has melted is often not some earth-shaking event, but that when favoritism is before your eyes, you no longer hate. Of course, the story is not finished: in the next chapter, the silver cup hidden in Benjamin's sack will drive everyone into a corner again, to see whether they will abandon Benjamin and flee for their own lives, or all return to stand trial with him. Today's gladness at the table is a rehearsal; the choice in the next chapter is the examination. But the rehearsal already tells us that God's hand has worked in this family: Judah the surety has changed, the brothers who do not envy have changed, and even the father's clenched hand has opened.

Please also note another layer of meaning in this meal: this was the first time in twenty-two years that this family ate at the same table — although the brothers did not yet know that the lord across from them was the little brother whose colored coat they had stripped off, whom they had thrown into a pit and sold to a caravan in that year. Joseph knew; they did not know. Joseph remembered every account, yet chose first to set a table, first to bless, first to weep a while, and only then settle accounts slowly. The order of God's work of reconciliation is often like this: not first spreading out the old accounts for confrontation, but first letting everyone eat a meal in peace, letting the heated heart soften first, letting the oldest wound stop bleeding first. Many families long for reconciliation and long for the other side to admit fault first; Joseph's table tells us that reconciliation sometimes begins from the stronger side setting the table first, from the side whose heart first stirs with mercy handing out the first portion of food. If you are the one in the family who was hurt most deeply and who now has ability in hand, God may be giving you the place of setting the table: tears are shed in the inner room, grace is divided at the table — this is exactly the road Joseph walked in this chapter to show us.

### Application: Four Kinds of People

The first kind: people whose hands are clenching a Benjamin. It may be a child's future, the person you have protected all your life, or that matter about which you say "let go" ten thousand times yet still cannot sleep at night. Israel's words today are spoken for you: do everything that should be done — prepare the gifts well, make the accounts clean, speak the words clearly, pray the prayers through — and then let go, placing it in the mercy of God Almighty. Your clenched hand cannot protect him; God's hand can protect him. Letting go is not ceasing to love; it is admitting that you are not God.

The second kind: people who ought to be a surety but are shrinking back. Someone in the family is waiting for you to step forward, staking yourself like Judah, rather than talking two fierce sentences like Reuben. Taking responsibility costs a price: you must bear the name of "bearing the blame forever," and stand forward when the account is demanded. Take up what you ought to bear for the one you ought to bear it for, and at the same time ask God for the clarity of Proverbs: do not lightly strike hands for what you should not bear, for what is unconnected with you, or for the sake of face.

The third kind: people learning a lesson at the table. Someone else receives a greater portion than you, the colored coat is worn on someone else's body, the blessing falls on someone else's head — is the fire in your heart still there? The fivefold portion is a mirror God has set out. To be able to feast gladly together at another person's grace is the mark that this person has truly been healed. May God remove jealousy from our hearts by the root, until we can sincerely raise a cup for a brother's fivefold blessing.

The fourth kind: people like Joseph: in a high position, holding up in front of others, able to shed tears only back in the inner room. How much you have endured for family, for the church, and for those you love, God has recorded it all. He can enter your inner room. After you have wept a while, wash your face, then come out and set the table, dividing grace to those who once owed you — this is the hardest, and it is also the most like Christ. A pledge must pay the cost, and a pledge that bears blame points afar to a greater Surety: our Lord Jesus, who personally became the Mediator and Surety of a better covenant for us, bringing us back one by one before the Father, and not losing one. If even Judah's shadow is like this, how much more the Substance Himself.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked a road: from an emptied grain jar to a father's opened hand; from Judah's pledge staking himself to the fivefold portion at the Egyptian table. Entrusting is not giving up; it is, after doing everything and praying everything, placing what you can least bear to part with into the mercy of God. A pledge is not fierce talk; it is handing the right to demand an account to the other side and leaving the consequences to yourself. And the way God answers prayer is often to first raise up mercy in that man's heart, and to first heal jealousy at one table.

Let us pray together: God Almighty, God of our fathers, God of Bethel, we hand the Benjamin in our hands to You: our children, our family, and all that we cannot protect yet cannot let go. Give us mercy before people, and first give us mercy in our own hearts. Give us Judah's sense of responsibility — not speaking empty words, and willing to pay the cost; give us Israel's rest — opening our hands and still able to sleep; and heal the jealousy within us, so that we can feast gladly together with the one who received the fivefold portion. Thank You for sending Your Son Jesus to be our Surety forever, to bear our sins, and to bring us back before You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[5] mas'eth: Hebrew מַשְׂאֵת (H4864), a portion, a present, food sent from a table, also carrying the senses of lifting up and tribute; the "portion" in Genesis 43:34, "Benjamin's mess was five times so much as any of theirs," uses this word; see the narrative earlier in Genesis 43:34 of Joseph sending food from his own table; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "a gift divided from the table of an honored person" is stated directly from this word's root meaning, and "the fivefold portion is a test and a lesson" is a homiletical exposition, noted as exposition.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "I will be surety for him; of my hand shalt thou require him: if I bring him not unto thee, and set him before thee, then let me bear the blame for ever:" (Genesis 43:9, KJV) "And God Almighty give you mercy before the man, that he may send away your other brother, and Benjamin. If I be bereaved of my children, I am bereaved." (Genesis 43:14, KJV)

Everyone holds a Benjamin in his hands: the one person or thing we can least bear to let go. Jacob clutched his youngest son all his life, and only when the grain was gone did he learn to open his hand. His letting go was not giving up: the gifts were prepared, the silver was doubled and taken back honestly, Judah's pledge was given, and the prayer was prayed through — and then he said, if I am bereaved, I am bereaved. That is entrusting: do everything that is yours to do, and place the outcome in the mercy of God Almighty. Judah also holds up a mirror to us: a true pledge does not talk tough; it stakes oneself and is willing to bear the consequences. Today, hand your Benjamin over in prayer, and stand up for the one who is waiting for you to take responsibility. Do not wait until the grain is gone to learn this lesson, and do not wait until your feelings have settled before you are willing to let go — when Jacob let go, his heart was bleeding too; he simply got the order right: first do everything that can be done, then hand the outcome over. You can do this tonight: for that matter and that person, prepare what you should prepare, say what you should say, then kneel down and place the names, one by one, into the hands of God Almighty. God's mercy is more reliable than your clenched hand; whatever His hand holds up will never fall.

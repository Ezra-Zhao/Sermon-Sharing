## Summary (about 100 words)

The covenant has been made, and now God will dwell among His people. Those whose hearts are willing lift up gold and silver, fine linen, acacia wood, and precious stones as an offering; the craftsmen are to make the ark, the table, and the golden lampstand according to the pattern shown on the mountain. Above the mercy seat is the voice of God meeting and speaking; on the table the bread of the Presence is always set out; the lampstand's seven lamps light the holy place. Worship is not invented by man; it is built according to the pattern. This chapter opens with the pattern, and with the pattern it closes.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

First set the order of our reading straight in your hearts. In the previous chapter the people were still below the mountain: Moses read the book of the covenant in their hearing, and they answered with one voice, All that the LORD hath said will we do; Moses took the blood, half of it sprinkled on the altar and half on the people, saying, this is the blood of the covenant. The elders went up and saw the God of Israel, and under His feet there was as it were a paved work of sapphire. Then Moses went alone into the cloud and was on the mountain forty days and forty nights. Below, the people waited; on the mountain, Moses received. What did he receive? We might guess: a route map for the march into Canaan, decrees for governing the people, detailed rules for judging hard cases. But chapter 25 opens, and it is none of these. The first great thing God commands on the mountain is the building of a tabernacle: let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them. This manuscript pauses here first, because this sentence is heavy enough to carry the whole six chapters that follow. God saved His people out of Egypt and made a covenant with them at Sinai, and now He takes one step further: He will not only issue commands from afar, not only show His glory on a high mountain — He will come and dwell, dwell in the middle of the camp, dwell among His people. The exodus, by chapter 25, finally shows its full purpose: to be redeemed is not only to leave the house of slavery; it is to become the place where God dwells.

Hold the contents of this chapter in hand first; it is far neater than we imagine. Verses 1 to 9 are the opening of the whole blueprint: God tells Moses to receive an offering from the people — whoever gives it willingly with his heart may bring it — for the making of the sanctuary; and the opening ends by laying down one general rule: the tabernacle and all its furnishings are to be made according to the pattern shown on the mountain. Verses 10 to 22 are the ark and the mercy seat: a chest of acacia wood overlaid with gold, with rings and poles, the tablets of the testimony inside it, a mercy seat of pure gold above it, two cherubim spreading their wings face to face, and God's promise to meet Moses there and speak with him. Verses 23 to 30 are the table of the bread of the Presence: likewise acacia wood overlaid with gold, with poles to carry it and dishes and bowls, and the bread of the Presence always set out before God on it. Verses 31 to 40 are the golden lampstand: hammered out of pure gold, one talent of it, six branches coming out of its sides, with cups and knops and flowers, and seven lamps lighting the space in front of it; and at the end of the chapter the sentence sounds once more: see that you make them after the pattern shown on the mountain. The opening has the pattern, and the ending has the pattern; the whole chapter is framed head and tail by this sentence, and so this manuscript's title is taken from it: According to the Pattern Shown on the Mountain.

In preaching this chapter, this manuscript still keeps the three rules laid down in the previous chapters. First, grasp the main line and do not spread out every inch: the tabernacle's measurements, materials, and workmanship are recorded in fine detail, but this sermon takes only the details that carry the main line and preaches them through; it does not unfold every cubit and every finger — that belongs to the hour of drawing diagrams in a Bible-study class. Second, symbolic readings and New Testament readings must be identified by their source and their level: how the ark, the bread of the Presence, and the lampstand were later read and fulfilled in the New Testament, this manuscript will preach — but every instance is identified as a New Testament reading, a homiletical use, and typology is never passed off as the original, on-the-spot meaning of Exodus 25; what the text plainly says belongs to the text, what the New Testament sees in retrospect belongs to the New Testament, and what the sermon extends belongs to the exposition — the three levels are never mixed into one. Third, facts are distinguished from exposition and identified as such — that old rule needs no more words. Holding these three rules, we can preach this chapter with flesh and blood, without stuffing our own ideas into the mouth of the text.

Let us read several key passages of today's text together:

"Speak unto the children of Israel, that they bring me an offering: of every man that giveth it willingly with his heart ye shall take my offering." (Exodus 25:2, excerpt, KJV)

"And let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them. According to all that I shew thee, after the pattern of the tabernacle, and the pattern of all the instruments thereof, even so shall ye make it." (Exodus 25:8-9, excerpt, KJV)

"And there I will meet with thee, and I will commune with thee from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubims which are upon the ark of the testimony, of all things which I will give thee in commandment unto the children of Israel." (Exodus 25:22, excerpt, KJV)

"And thou shalt set upon the table shewbread before me alway." (Exodus 25:30, excerpt, KJV)

"And look that thou make them after their pattern, which was shewed thee in the mount." (Exodus 25:40, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You are the Lord who is willing to dwell among men. You came down from heaven to the mountain, and from the mountain You commanded that a dwelling place be prepared for You in the camp; for this willingness we bow in worship. Open our eyes today to see the heart of Your blueprint: the place where speaking and atonement are together, the bread always set out before You, the lamp that lights the holy place. Cleanse our hands and hearts, so that what we offer is not a reluctant duty but a willing lifting up, and what we build is not after our own ideas but after Your pattern. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Opening of the Blueprint — The Willing Offering and the Promise to Dwell Among Them (25:1-9)

The opening of this section is only nine verses, yet the root of the six chapters that follow is planted here, so let us read it slowly, sentence by sentence. The first sentence is this: the LORD spoke to Moses, saying, Speak unto the children of Israel, that they bring me an offering: of every man that giveth it willingly with his heart ye shall take my offering. The offering brought here, in the original, is terumah (תְּרוּמָה, H8641), a heave offering, a contribution. Within this word's root there is the motion of lifting up, of raising high [1]: it is not a thing thrown away, not a tax paid up, but a thing lifted up, raised to a height, presented to God. So when it is translated as a heave offering, it keeps close to the original: the first picture of giving in the Bible is not a hand stretched out demanding, but two hands lifted up. Note also the condition for receiving it: of every man that giveth it willingly with his heart — only those whose hearts stir them, who are willing, bring it. The text sets no amount, no proportion, and sends no one from tent to tent to press for payment. And what did a people just delivered from slavery have in their hands? Some had gold and silver vessels they had asked of their neighbours on leaving Egypt; some had the fine linen and wool they carried with them in the wilderness; some had a little stored up from home. God says: these things — let whoever is willing lift them up. The materials of the place of worship do not come from forced levies; they come from willing hearts. This manuscript must identify this: it is the principle of receiving that the text states in so many words, not a sentiment added by preaching. Of course, speaking of giving today, this manuscript adds one identified piece of exposition: what God has always valued is not the figure first, but the hands lifted up — willingness first, the gift after; reverse that order, and however large the gift, it is no longer terumah.

The list of gifts, verses 3 to 7, runs down in one breath; let us read it as the text gives it, neither inflating nor trimming: gold, silver, and brass; blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine linen, and goats' hair; rams' skins dyed red, and badgers' skins, and acacia wood; oil for the light, and spices for anointing oil and for sweet incense; onyx stones and other stones to be set in the ephod and in the breastplate. This list deserves a second look. First, it walks almost in the order of its later uses: metals first, then fabrics and threads, then skins and wood, then oil and spices, and at the end the stones — from the frame of the tabernacle to the stones on the high priest's breastplate. Second, every item is something the people really had at hand: not one was conjured out of nothing in the wilderness, and not one required the people to go back to Egypt to buy it. The materials God had prepared had already come out of Egypt with the people; what the people took for farewell gifts from their neighbours turned out to be the building materials of the sanctuary. This manuscript strings this together as intertextual explanation drawn from Exodus chapter 12, where the people asked gold and silver vessels of the Egyptians, and identifies it as such. Third, the list holds both the costly and the cheap: gold and onyx are costly, goats' hair and acacia wood are cheap. Whoever has gold offers gold; whoever has no gold offers a handful of goats' hair, and it stands on the list all the same. The sanctuary is the sanctuary of the whole people, and on its materials list rich and poor alike have a share; this is what "willingly with his heart" looks like when it lands on the ground.

After the list, verse 8 is the purpose sentence of the whole six-chapter section, and the heart of this chapter is here: And let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them. "Dwell" — in the original, to reside in the midst, to settle down. God did not say, that I may be served by you, nor, that you may have a place to remember me; He said: dwell. To dwell is long-term, daily, and not departing. A God who is willing to settle down is altogether different from a God who appears only for great events: a God who dwells will hear the crying of children in the camp, see the smoke rising from each tent in the morning, walk in the pillar of cloud when the people journey, and rest above the tabernacle when they stop. Read this sentence against the whole book: at the opening of Exodus, God appears as the Lord who hears cries — He heard the groaning of His people in the brick kilns; at Sinai, God appears as the Lord who makes a covenant — He proclaimed the law and cut a covenant with the people; by chapter 25, God appears as the Lord who dwells — He will live among them. From hearing, to covenant, to dwelling, God draws step by step nearer to this people of slave origin. This summary is exegetical explanation made from the book's own progression, rooted in the movement of the text, and is identified here as such. The proportion must also be stated clearly: God's dwelling is His own gracious stooping; the tabernacle is not a box for containing God — later, Solomon prayed at the dedication of the temple that heaven and the heaven of heavens cannot contain Him (1 Kings 8:27, cited in sense). What the tabernacle declares is that God is willing to be present with His people in covenant; it does not declare that God's immensity can be measured by a tent. This is a theological boundary that must be kept in reading this chapter, and this manuscript sets it down here first.

The last sentence of the opening, verse 9, is this chapter's first general rule: According to all that I shew thee, after the pattern of the tabernacle, and the pattern of all the instruments thereof, even so shall ye make it. "Pattern," in the original, is tavnit (תַּבְנִית, H8403), a pattern, a model, a form [2]. In his forty days and nights on the mountain, what Moses saw was not only a heap of instructions but a shape: what the tabernacle was to look like, God showed him first, and he was to come down and build according to it. Note the proportion of the verb: build according to it — not build according to the general idea, and still less according to Moses' own taste. Why so solemn? Because if the pattern of worship comes from man, man will unknowingly set his own preferences, his own respectability, his own inventions before God, and call it worship. What had just happened at the foot of Sinai is the counter-example: the people, waiting for a Moses who did not come down, cast a golden calf after their own idea and proclaimed it the god who had brought them out of Egypt (Exodus 32; this manuscript mentions it in advance by the book's internal order as a contrast, noting that in the narrative of the sermon this event comes later). Worship invented by man, however lively the invention, makes God in man's image. The pattern on the mountain is exactly the reverse: God sets the rule, and man builds according to it; first the blueprint, then the work. This manuscript fixes this point in the opening, because verse 40 at the end of the chapter will fix it once more: worship is not invented by man; it is built according to the pattern. This judgment is the straight reading of this chapter's two "pattern" verses, not a slogan added from outside.

The opening's nine verses plant the root here: willing materials, the promise of dwelling, and the rule of building according to the pattern. Now let us enter the sanctuary, beginning with the innermost furnishing of all — the ark.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Ark and the Mercy Seat — The Heart of the Whole Tabernacle (25:10-22)

The first furnishing of the blueprint is not the altar outside, nor the lampstand that everyone could see, but the ark, hidden in the Holy of Holies, into which only the high priest might enter once a year. The order itself is an argument: God's blueprint is drawn from the inside out — first fix the centre, then fix the surroundings. When we read the church today, and read the life of faith, we should learn this order too: ask first what the centre is, then ask how things are arranged. If the centre does not stand, however tidy the arrangement outside, it is only an empty tent.

The making of the ark, the text says: they shall make an ark of acacia wood. Acacia is a wood common in the wilderness, hard and durable, found locally, not to be fetched from afar; within and without it is overlaid with pure gold, and a crown of gold runs round about it — wood inside, gold outside, a humble material wrapped in a precious one. Of this picture this manuscript states only the fact: the text plainly specifies the pairing of wood and gold; the many likenesses of human nature and divine nature drawn from it in later preaching are a common homiletical use which this manuscript does not adopt, and it does not pass a likeness off as the text's original meaning. The ark's dimensions: two cubits and a half its length, a cubit and a half its breadth, and a cubit and a half its height. A cubit being roughly forty-five centimetres today, two and a half cubits is about one metre ten, and a cubit and a half about sixty-eight centimetres — in the end, this is not a large chest. The ark's smallness is worth pausing over: the sacred centre of all Israel measures out to no more than a small table. God's presence does not lean on size for its dignity — that sentence is homiletical exposition, but it is rooted in a fact of the text: the dimensions given are not grand, yet they are exact; they do not overawe, yet not a finger's breadth may slip. On the ark's four feet are four rings of gold, and through the rings pass the poles. The poles too are acacia wood, overlaid with gold. Verse 15 holds a very particular command — mark it well: the poles shall be in the rings of the ark: they shall not be taken from it. The poles of other furnishings could be drawn out when the carrying was done; the ark's poles alone, once put through, were never to be drawn out again. Why? The text does not state the reason, and this manuscript presents the commentators' common views together without deciding: one view holds it was for readiness to journey — the wilderness road might start at any moment, and with the poles always in place the ark could be lifted and gone, with no hurried threading of poles at the last minute; another holds it was for the boundary of holiness — the ark was not to be touched directly by hand, and poles always in the rings removed the danger of hands coming near the chest at every moving, and the later matter of Uzzah, struck down for putting out his hand to steady the ark (2 Samuel 6), is often cited alongside this rule. This manuscript takes the factual layer of the first view — the poles stayed in as readiness for carrying — and identifies the second as a reading of the commentary tradition, presented alongside. Whichever view is taken, one thing the text states plainly: God's presence in the wilderness was kept ready to journey with His people at any time; the poles are not withdrawn, and the Presence is not withdrawn. This is exposition drawn from verse 15, and is identified here.

What was to go inside the ark? Verse 16 says: And thou shalt put into the ark the testimony which I shall give thee. The testimony — the two tablets of stone written by God's own hand at the covenant, bearing the commandments. Picture it clearly: in the Holy of Holies there was no image of God, no portrait, nothing representing God's shape — Israel's worship was imageless from the root, the boundary laid down by the second commandment. What the Holy of Holies held was a chest, and in the chest, God's words. The mark of God's presence was not a figure but His speech; the warrant of His covenant with the people was not a totem but the commandments He had spoken with His own mouth, for men to keep. This manuscript holds this to be the deepest stroke in the faith of the Old Testament: God dwells among His people, and the surety of His presence is the word He has already spoken and had written down. Reading this verse today, there is no need to go around in circles: what a community places at its centre decides what that community worships. If the centre holds a man's achievements, a human leader, a human tradition, that is another religion; the ark teaches us to put God's word in the innermost, deepest place, where it is least allowed to be moved. This application is exposition; the fact of the text is that the tablets were put into the ark — and the two are identified separately here.

Above the ark sat the weightiest thing in the whole chapter: the mercy seat. The text says: And thou shalt make a mercy seat of pure gold: two cubits and a half shall be the length thereof, and a cubit and a half the breadth thereof — fitting exactly the mouth of the ark, as its cover. "Mercy seat," in the original, is kapporet (כַּפֹּרֶת, H3727), the golden cover of the ark specifically [3]. Here is a lexical fact that the pulpit should state honestly: kapporet shares its root with the Hebrew verb kipper (כִּפֶּר), to cover, to make atonement — the whole family of words for atoning, for covering sins, and this noun "mercy seat" come from one root. So translations that render it "mercy seat" are all expressing the same fact: this plate of gold is the place where grace and atonement meet. Later, Leviticus chapter 16 records the rite of the Day of Atonement: once a year the high priest entered the Holy of Holies with blood and sprinkled the blood on the mercy seat, to make atonement for the sins the people had committed through the year (Leviticus 16, cited in sense). Here in this chapter the place is built first: where the blood is to be sprinkled, where grace is to be dispensed — God fixes the location here first. One piece of homiletical exposition: atonement is not a temporary remedy; it is a place reserved beforehand in the blueprint; before the people had committed their first great sin, the mercy seat had already been commanded on the mountain and set upon the ark. That grace was written into the drawing before judgment — that sentence is exposition, but its root is the order of this chapter, which builds the mercy seat first and only later uses it in Leviticus; the level is identified first, and then it may be preached with confidence.

At the two ends of the mercy seat were to be made two cherubim. "Cherubim," in the original, is keruvim (כְּרֻבִים, H3742), the plural of keruv, the heavenly beings who guard the boundaries of holiness in the Old Testament [4]. These two cherubim were to be beaten out of gold, made at the two ends of the mercy seat, of one piece with the mercy seat — not made separately and then joined on. Their posture the text writes in fine detail: the cherubim stretch forth their wings on high, covering the mercy seat with their wings, and their faces look one to another; toward the mercy seat shall the faces of the cherubim be. Note the two directions: the wings spread upward, the faces bend downward toward the mercy seat, and face each other. The cherubim first appear in Scripture at the east of Eden: after God drove the man out of the garden, He placed cherubim to guard the way of the tree of life (Genesis 3:24, cited in sense). Then they were guards, with a flaming sword, keeping man from drawing near. Now, on the mercy seat, the cherubim's faces are turned toward the place of atoning grace. One pair standing guard at the gate of the lost garden, one pair bowed low before the seat of atonement — this manuscript reads the two passages together as intertextual contrast drawn from both texts: the same cherubim, in one place witnessing that man, for sin, is shut out from life, and in the other witnessing that God Himself has prepared a way back into His presence. When this contrast is used in preaching, it must be identified: it is a homiletical use gained by reading the two passages together across the canon, not something Exodus 25 says of itself on the spot.

Then, verse 22 — the summit of the whole chapter. Read this verse slowly: And there I will meet with thee, and I will commune with thee from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubims which are upon the ark of the testimony, of all things which I will give thee in commandment unto the children of Israel. "There I will meet with thee" — where is "there"? Above the mercy seat, between the two cherubim. The place where God spoke with Moses was not in the fire on the mountain top, not in the wilderness outside the camp, but within the Holy of Holies, above the ark, directly over the cover of atonement. Lay these things together and look: below is the ark; in the ark are the tablets, the commandments God established with man; above is the mercy seat, the place where blood is sprinkled and sin is covered; and above the mercy seat, between the cherubim, is the place where God speaks. That is to say, the position of God's speaking to man stands exactly between His commandments and His atonement: His word comes to man carrying the gravity of the law, and it comes carrying the grace of atonement. The heart of the whole tabernacle is the ark, and the holiest point of the ark is the place where speaking and atonement are together. This summary sentence is the axis of this chapter's whole sermon; its basis is the laying together of three things — verse 16, verse 22, and the lexical fact of the mercy seat — and the level is identified here: the fact of the text is that God would meet and speak above the mercy seat; the lexical fact is that the mercy seat's name shares its root with atonement; read together, they yield the conclusion that speaking and atonement occupy one place. That is exegetical summary, not a new doctrine started elsewhere.

How does the New Testament look back at this mercy seat? This manuscript speaks of it, as its rule requires, with the levels identified. In Romans chapter 3, verse 25, Paul says that God set forth Christ Jesus as a propitiation; the Greek word used there, hilasterion, is precisely the word the Septuagint uses to translate kapporet, the mercy seat. To say that Jesus is the true mercy seat — the place where God and the sinner meet and sin is atoned for, no longer a plate of gold, but a Lord crucified and risen — this is the New Testament's reading, the apostle's retrospective reading unto fulfilment, and this manuscript identifies it honestly: it is not the original meaning of Exodus 25 on the spot — the readers of Exodus at the time knew only that here was the place where God met and showed grace; but the New Testament's author did indeed use this very line and read the mercy seat onto Christ. Both levels are preached, both levels are identified, and the levels are never mixed; this is the iron rule of this manuscript in preaching the tabernacle chapters, and the bread of the Presence and the lampstand below are handled the same way.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Table and the Golden Lampstand — The Bread of the Presence and the Light of the Holy Place (25:23-40)

One layer out from the Holy of Holies is the holy place. In the holy place, two furnishings face each other — a table on one side, a lampstand on the other; between them stands also the altar of incense, whose ordinance is recorded in chapter 30 (Exodus 30:1-10), so this chapter takes these two first. On one side bread, on the other light: provision, and illumination. In God's house, first there is the centre where He speaks and atones, and then there is the daily life of His people eating and drinking and walking; the order of this chapter, once again, is itself the argument.

Look first at the table of the bread of the Presence. The text says: Thou shalt also make a table of acacia wood: two cubits shall be the length thereof, and a cubit the breadth thereof, and a cubit and a half the height thereof. Round about it is overlaid with pure gold and a crown of gold runs around it, as with the ark — acacia wood within, pure gold without; on its four feet are rings of gold, and through the rings pass poles, acacia wood overlaid with gold here as well, with which the table is carried. The vessels on the table the text lists in four kinds: dishes, spoons, covers, and bowls for pouring out — all made of gold. These vessels were prepared for the service at the table, and this manuscript lists them as the text gives them and does not develop them piece by piece here, holding to the rule set down in the opening and not spreading out every inch. The table's real business is verse 30: And thou shalt set upon the table shewbread before me alway. "Shewbread" — in the original lechem panim (לֶחֶם פָּנִים), literally "bread of the face," bread of the presence, the bread of being before Him [5]: lechem is bread, panim is face, presence. And this bread is not set out once and finished with; the key lies in one word — alway: always set before me. Later, Leviticus chapter 24 states the rule completely: the bread was changed every Sabbath, twelve loaves at a time, representing the twelve tribes, set before God, the old bread going to the priests to be eaten in the holy place (Leviticus 24:5-9, cited in sense). A new week, new bread; the old bread was not thrown away but went into the mouths of those who served. This manuscript takes three points tied to this chapter's main line. First, this bread was permanently set out: unlike the manna, which fell once and was eaten once, it was the standing order before God — three hundred and sixty-five days a year, the table was never empty. Presence is not an occasional emotion; it is a permanent setting-out. This is exegetical explanation drawn from the word "alway." Second, in this bread the people's provision and remembrance are joined together: the bread came from the people's fine flour, was brought before God, and then went to those who served Him to eat — man offers God the produce of the ground, and God with it feeds those who serve Him; provision and remembrance happen at once on one table. Preached this way, it must be identified as homiletical exposition drawn from reading this verse together with the completed institution in Leviticus: provision means that the people's produce belongs to God; remembrance means that the twelve loaves set before God stand as the twelve tribes held always in God's remembrance, never forgotten. Third, the position is worth noticing: the table stood in the holy place, outside the veil, where the priests served daily under the lamplight and would see this table morning and evening. That is to say, though the people could not enter the Holy of Holies, their bread was before God every day; where the men themselves could not always be, their remembrance always was. This last layer is a homiletical use, preached with comfort, its level identified and not passed off as the text's own statement. Extending it in preaching to today, this manuscript adds only one sentence: a table always set out reminds us that God's provision is not emergency charity but daily faithfulness — there yesterday, there today, and when the Sabbath comes, the new bread is set out again.

Now the golden lampstand, the furnishing whose making this chapter describes most finely. The text says: And thou shalt make a candlestick of pure gold[6]: of beaten work shall the candlestick be made: his shaft, and his branches, his bowls, his knops, and his flowers, shall be of the same. Mark two words: pure gold, and beaten work. The ark was wood overlaid with gold; the table was wood overlaid with gold; the lampstand was solid pure gold with no wooden core; and it was not cast but beaten — the craftsman took one whole piece of gold and, blow by blow, hammered shaft, branches, cups, and flowers into shape, with no soldering or joining in between. The text also records its weight: the lampstand and all its vessels were to be made of a talent of pure gold. One talent is roughly thirty-four kilograms today. One whole piece of gold, one whole stretch of work, one whole furnishing of one piece — this is the fact the text sets down. The lampstand's form: from its shaft, out of its two sides, come six branches — three branches out of one side, and three branches out of the other side, coming out in symmetry; on the branches are cups, knops, and flowers, made in the same way as the shaft; the six branches together with the central shaft make seven places for lamps. Verse 37 says: And thou shalt make the seven lamps thereof: and they shall light the lamps thereof, that they may give light over against it. The seven lamps lit together lit up the holy place — the tabernacle had no windows; all the light in the holy place came from these seven lamps; and the priests' service inside, their seeing the table and seeing the altar of incense, depended wholly on the lampstand's light. This manuscript adds one reminder: Israel at the time knew only that this was the light of the holy place, made according to the pattern shown on the mountain and lit according to the rule. Later, the prophet Zechariah saw in a vision a golden lampstand and olive trees supplying its oil, and heard the words, Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit (Zechariah 4, cited in sense); and later still, Revelation chapter 1 states plainly that the seven golden candlesticks are the seven churches (Revelation 1:20, cited in sense), and the Lord Jesus Himself said, I am the light of the world (John 8:12, excerpt). These are all later readings and fulfilments — the depth the whole Bible, looking back at this lamp in later ages, came to see — and this manuscript identifies them together here: they are New Testament readings and the prophets' retrospective reading, not the on-the-spot meaning of Exodus 25; the on-the-spot meaning is only this: the holy place was to have light, and the light's form, its material, and the rule for lighting it were all set by God, and man built according to it.

At the end of the lampstand passage, verse 40, the sentence that closes the whole chapter sounds once more: And look that thou make them after their pattern, which was shewed thee in the mount. "Look that thou make" — carefulness is the duty laid on Moses; "the pattern" is the blueprint God has already given. Set verse 40 beside verse 9, and head and tail of this chapter lock together: verse 9 says the tabernacle and all its instruments are to be made after the pattern; verse 40 says these furnishings are to be made after the pattern. Head and tail, the same sentence — the chapter is framed strictly by the word "pattern." Here this manuscript sums up its way of preaching the tabernacle chapters in one sentence: the details of the furnishings are recorded finely in Scripture because the blueprint came from God; when preaching takes details, it takes only those that can carry the main line, and does not trade details as curiosities. And what is the main line? That God will dwell among His people, and His presence has a centre — above the mercy seat, speaking and atonement together; a daily life — the bread always set out on the table; and light — the lamp in the holy place that does not go out. None of these three was invented by man; all were made according to the pattern.

Finally, concerning the pattern in heaven, this manuscript must set out one plain New Testament passage clearly, with its level identified. Hebrews chapters 8 to 9, speaking of the earthly tabernacle, say that the earthly service is the example and shadow of heavenly things, as Moses was admonished of God when he was about to make the tabernacle: for, See, saith he, that thou make all things according to the pattern shewed to thee in the mount (Hebrews 8:5, cited in sense); and that the earthly sanctuary was made as a copy of the true sanctuary in heaven, and that Christ has entered into the greater, true tabernacle itself. This is the New Testament's reading: it reads this chapter's word "pattern" as the relation between an earthly copy and a heavenly reality. This manuscript's attitude toward it is to report it honestly and identify it honestly: this layer of meaning is the theological exposition the author of Hebrews reached when, in the light of Christ already come, he read the books of Moses back — in faith we receive it as New Testament teaching; but returning to Exodus 25 itself, the reader on the spot received only a rule of workmanship — make it after the pattern shown on the mountain, and do not take matters into your own hands. Both levels are preached, identified separately, and neither passes itself off as the other; this is the honesty this manuscript has kept from first to last in preaching this chapter.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today, from the nine opening verses to the ark and the mercy seat, and then to the table and the golden lampstand, we have learned three things. First, the material and the heart of worship: the materials of the sanctuary were lifted up and offered by willing hearts — terumah is willingness first, gift afterwards; what God wants is not a levied sum but two hands lifted up. Second, the centre and the daily life of worship: the heart of the whole tabernacle is the ark, and its holiest point is the mercy seat — inside the ark lie God's words, upon the seat is the place of atonement, and above the seat, between the cherubim, is the place where God meets and speaks; and the daily life of the holy place is the bread of the Presence always set out on the table, and the unquenchable light of the lampstand — the provision is constant, the remembrance is constant, and the light is constant too. Third, the rule of worship: all things according to the pattern shown on the mountain. This chapter opens with the pattern and closes with the pattern; worship is not invented by man — God shows first, and man builds according to it; and the thing our generation must guard against most is precisely casting our own preferences into a golden calf and then calling it God. May what we offer be willing, what we keep be the centre, and what we walk in be the pattern — so that His dwelling among us is not a slogan, but a life in which the bread is set out and the lamp is lit every day.

Let us pray together: Lord, Lord who is willing to dwell among us, we thank You. Your blueprint goes down to a single ring, a single pole, a single lamp, because in Your presence no place may be made do with. Forgive our reluctant, perfunctory offerings; forgive our pride in worshipping after our own ideas; forgive our neglect in moving Your word out of the centre and letting it gather dust. Keep the tablets in our deepest place still, and set the mercy seat on the road we return by every day, so that our words have Your commandments and our sins have Your covering; and let our table always bear the bread of Your provision, and our house the lamp of Your lighting. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them." (Exodus 25:8, excerpt, KJV) "And there I will meet with thee, and I will commune with thee from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubims which are upon the ark of the testimony, of all things which I will give thee in commandment unto the children of Israel." (Exodus 25:22, excerpt, KJV)

At this point the story of the exodus suddenly slows down. Before it came the parted sea, the falling manna, the giving of the law — each greater than the last; by chapter 25, God is telling men to measure timber, hammer out rings of gold, set out bread, and light lamps. It turns out that the end of redemption is not the noise of great events, but a quiet dwelling: God will live among them. And the place where He meets is telling — not on a mountain peak, but above the mercy seat, that cover of gold whose very name is kin to atonement. Which is to say: beneath the place where you come back to God each day there is first spread grace, not your record. If today you feel unfit to pray, think of the mercy seat: inside the ark are the tablets, reminding you that God's word counts; above it is atonement, reminding you that God's grace also counts — and only when both count can you stand steady. Think, too, of that table: the bread was always set out, never once for all. God's provision is not one great favour dispensed a single time; it is daily faithfulness — there tomorrow, and there the day after. What you lack today He may not fill in one miracle; He may set it before you week by week, like the bread. Offer willingly the little in your hand — a handful of goats' hair is on the list too — and do each day's work after His pattern, piece by piece. He has already come to dwell; for the road that remains, He walks with you.

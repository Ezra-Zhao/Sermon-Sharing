## Summary (about 100 words)

There was a famine in the land, and Isaac went to Gerar. God stopped him from going down to Egypt; the promise was still in this land. Yet he repeated his father's weakness, calling his wife his sister, and nearly brought disaster. God still caused him to sow in that land and reap a hundredfold that year; the Philistines envied him and stopped up his father's wells. Isaac did not fight or seize; he gave way well after well, dug his father's wells again, and named them contention and hostility, until Rehoboth — God made room for him. At the end he built an altar at Beersheba, and Abimelech came of his own accord to make a covenant with him. This sermon looks at three things: weakness is repeated, but grace is never absent; the harvest is in obedience, not in contention; spaciousness is yielded, and it is also given by God.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Today we open Genesis chapter 26. This chapter holds a very special place in Genesis: it is the only chapter in the whole book devoted entirely to Isaac. Abraham has the most chapters, Jacob's chapters are the longest, and Isaac is sandwiched in the middle, often seeming like a transitional figure. But God left a whole chapter for him, because in his life he is to demonstrate for us something not spectacular, yet extremely difficult: on someone else's territory, in famine, when bullied again and again, not to flee, not to seize, but to live out spaciousness by digging wells.

Let us first read several key passages:

"And there was a famine in the land, beside the first famine that was in the days of Abraham. And Isaac went unto Abimelech king of the Philistines unto Gerar." (Gen 26:1, KJV)

"And the LORD appeared unto him, and said, Go not down into Egypt; dwell in the land which I shall tell thee of: Sojourn in this land, and I will be with thee, and will bless thee." (Gen 26:2-3, excerpt, KJV)

"Then Isaac sowed in that land, and received in the same year an hundredfold: and the LORD blessed him. And the man waxed great, and went forward, and grew until he became very great." (Gen 26:12-13, KJV)

"And he removed from thence, and digged another well; and for that they strove not: and he called the name of it Rehoboth; and he said, For now the LORD hath made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land." (Gen 26:22, KJV)

"And the LORD appeared unto him the same night, and said, I am the God of Abraham thy father: fear not, for I am with thee, and will bless thee." (Gen 26:24, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, many of us are also in Gerar, also in famine, and have also suffered the grievance of having our wells stopped up. Through this chapter on Isaac, teach us not to run to Egypt, not to strive with others, but to pick up the spade and dig open again the well that has been stopped with earth. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Famine Comes Again, Weakness Is Repeated — The Crossroads of Going Down to Egypt and Calling His Wife His Sister (26:1-11)

First, look at the famine. The text deliberately adds a note: there was also such a thing in Abraham's time. Famine is not something new; it is a periodic lesson of this land. Abraham met famine and went to Egypt — on that trip, in Pharaoh's palace he called his wife Sarai his sister, Sarai was taken into the palace, and God sent plagues on Pharaoh's house before she was brought back (Gen 12). Now the son meets the same famine, and his first reaction is also to pack his bags and head south. Brothers and sisters, circumstances repeat, and temptations repeat; where the previous generation fell, the next generation, if it is not watchful, will fall in the same way. This is not fate; it is a reminder: in a family's character there is faith, and there will also be weakness, and both will be passed on. This is precisely what we as parents must watch.

Fortunately, this time God Himself stopped him at the crossroads: "Go not down into Egypt." Notice the measure: God did not forbid him to move at all; He forbade him to move in that direction. Isaac could live in Gerar, could sojourn, could sow, but he alone could not go down to Egypt. Why? Because the land of promise, however barren, is still the land of promise; Egypt, however fertile, is not his destiny. The word God gave him restated, word for word, the promise He had given his father: I will give all these lands to you and to your descendants, and all the nations of the earth shall be blessed in your descendants — and He added a reason: because Abraham obeyed My voice and kept My charge. What Isaac heard was the summary judgment on his father's whole life. God was saying, in effect: Child, the road of faith your father walked, you are to walk on; the promise has not expired, only the one who receives it has changed. Isaac obeyed, and he stayed in Gerar. This is his first act of obedience in this chapter: his feet did not step toward Egypt.

But though his feet stopped, the fear in his heart remained. When the men of Gerar asked about his wife Rebekah, he said: She is my sister. What was he afraid of? The text says he thought in his heart: Lest the men of the place kill me for Rebekah's sake, because she was fair to look on (26:7). This sentence is all too familiar to us — his father said almost the same words on this same ground (Gen 20, Abraham in Gerar called Sarah his sister, and it was also to Abimelech). Two generations, father and son, the same place, the same royal name, the same lie. Rebekah was the wife he had personally brought from his homeland, whom he had waited for while meditating in the field, a wife obtained through prayer; yet once famine came and danger drew near, to save his own life he first demoted his wife's status. Brothers and sisters, the saddest thing about weakness is not that we do not know right from wrong — Isaac knew it all — but that when fear arrives, the order in which we protect ourselves always comes before protecting our family.

The way the matter was exposed was very true to life: after a long time, King Abimelech looked out of a window and saw Isaac sporting with Rebekah his wife — the natural intimacy between husband and wife; a lie cannot cover it. The king summoned him and rebuked him very reasonably: What is this thou hast done unto us? one of the people might lightly have lien with thy wife, and thou shouldest have brought guiltiness upon us (26:10, excerpt in sense). Notice, even a pagan king understood the weight of this matter better than Isaac. The king then immediately commanded: Whoever touches Isaac and his wife shall be put to death. This command, in turn, became God's amulet for Isaac's family. The way God protects people sometimes uses no appearing angel, only the conscience and decree of an offended king. Our weakness caused trouble, yet God still set boundaries in the trouble, not letting the trouble reach its worst. This is not indulgence toward the lie — not one word of rebuke was spared — this is grace catching us from below. Isaac must remember this lesson for a lifetime: to go down to Egypt, God stopped him; to tell a lie, God also stopped him; twice He stopped him, once by an appearing, once by the rebuke of a pagan king. The ways God stops us vary, but stop us He does.

One more thing must be added: the Abimelech of this chapter is most likely a throne name rather than the same person, just as the king of Egypt is called Pharaoh. What Isaac faced was the same kind of power and the same kind of culture his father had faced. The lesson is examined again, not because God forgot to give the grade, but because in this subject his father also had barely passed.

There is also a question worth pausing over at the end of this section: Why did God not remove Isaac's weakness once for all? He appeared, He stopped him, He blessed him, He restated the promise — yet Isaac turned around and lied. Brothers and sisters, God's order of working is often first to make a covenant, then to sanctify; first to bring a person into the promise, then slowly to repair the fear inside the person. Isaac's fear did not grow in a day: he was the child offered on Mount Moriah, the child who watched his brother being sent away, the child who grew up behind his mother's tent. Fear was the air of his childhood. God did not annul the covenant because he feared, nor did He pretend, because of the covenant, that he did not fear. He healed this fear slowly through the events of a lifetime: at Gerar He healed it with the rebuke of a pagan king, at Beersheba He healed it with an appearing and with "fear not," until in Genesis 31, when Jacob spoke to Laban, he called God "the fear of his father Isaac" (Gen 31:42), and swore by that same name (Gen 31:53) — not a self-introduction by God, but a son's testimony to his father's faith. Do you hear it? Isaac, who as a child feared men, in old age had a son who called God "the fear of his father Isaac." The man who feared people in the end became a man who feared God — and between the two lie, chapter after chapter, well after well, altar after altar. So do not despise the weakness that still remains in you today, and do not use it as an excuse to lie flat: if God did not give up on Isaac, He will not give up on you; He healed Isaac over a lifetime, and the slowness of your lesson does not mean you are not learning it.

A word in passing about Rebekah. In this chapter her name appears only once, in her identity as wife at the beginning. Yet it was this wife who was almost taken away by an outsider because of one sentence from her husband — last time it was Sarah, this time Rebekah. Scripture does not record Rebekah's reaction; the silence of the narrative is itself an accusation: as a husband, one life-saving lie costs your wife taking the risk for you. When we speak and make decisions at home, we often count only our own risk, forgetting that those beside us are paying the bill for our weakness. Isaac later took only one wife in his whole life, the only monogamist among the patriarchs; perhaps it was precisely this lesson at Gerar that made him remember: a wife is not a sister who can be demoted in danger, but a person to be guarded with one's whole life. If a lesson is truly learned, weakness too can become teaching material.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: A Hundredfold Harvest and Three Wells — Digging Out Contention and Hostility Through Yielding (26:12-22a)

After the storm of the lie had passed, Isaac did something very bold in the land of his sojourning: he sowed. Sojourners usually only keep livestock and do not sow — sowing is staking your life on this piece of land; it is believing you will still be here next year to harvest. God had stopped him from going down to Egypt, so he lived seriously in this land: he held the plow, scattered the seed, and waited for rain. And the result? "That year he received a hundredfold." A hundredfold — in the original, me'ah she'arim, literally "a hundred measures," meaning what he reaped was a hundred times what he sowed [5]. In a year of famine, on someone else's land, a sojourner received a hundredfold — this is not agricultural technique; this is the fingerprint of blessing. When the Lord Jesus told the parable of the sower, the good soil bore fruit thirtyfold, sixtyfold, a hundredfold (Mark 4:8); that year, Isaac lived out the upper limit of good soil. Brothers and sisters, the prerequisite for the hundredfold harvest was that he first heard that sentence: "Go not down into Egypt." How many people run toward Egypt while asking for a hundredfold harvest — when the direction is wrong, diligence only speeds up departure from the promise. The order of obedience cannot be reversed: first stay in the land God indicates, then talk about the fruit of sowing.

When the harvest grew large, trouble grew with it. The text says he waxed great and grew, with many servants and many flocks and herds, and the Philistines envied him. The expression of envy was very concrete: they filled with earth all the wells his father Abraham had dug in Abraham's time (26:15). In a dry land, stopping a well is no small scuffle — a well is life; where the water is, livestock and people live; to stop one well is to declare: you are not welcome to live here. And the wells they stopped were precisely his father's wells. Abraham had dug wells, planted a tree, and called on the name of the LORD in this land; those wells were monuments to his father's faith. Now the monuments had been pushed over and the well mouths filled with earth. What did Isaac do? He did not summon his servants to argue, nor did he go to the king to complain. Abimelech spoke first: Depart from us, for thou art much mightier than we (26:16, in sense). The words were polite; in substance it was an order to leave. Isaac left, moved to the valley of Gerar, and dwelt there.

In the valley, he did one thing, and this thing is the title of this chapter: he dug open again his father's wells. Those wells had been stopped by the Philistines after Abraham's death; Isaac's servants dug the earth open again, and he still called them by the names his father had given the wells (26:18). Brothers and sisters, this spade of earth digs three things: it digs water, the resource for staying alive; it digs memory, the inheritance of faith his father left; it digs names — the names were not changed, still the original names. He did not set up a new stove or chase novelty; he first restored what his father's generation had already dug through. How many churches, how many families, have had the wells of prayer their fathers dug through, the wells of Scripture reading, the wells of worship, slowly filled level by this generation's busyness and worldly soil. Revival is often not inventing a new well, but shoveling the soil off an old well. Isaac gives us this order: first dig again, then dig anew.

The newly dug wells immediately drew contention. Isaac's servants dug in the valley and found a spring of living water, and the herdsmen of Gerar came to strive: This water is ours. Isaac named that well Esek, meaning "contention" [2] — what was dug out first was contention, not water. He yielded. The servants dug another well, they strove again, and he named it Sitnah, meaning "hostility, opposition" [3]. He yielded again. Brothers and sisters, let us count: one well, two wells, plus his father's wells that had been filled — Isaac's yielding, in human eyes, is retreat after retreat, and economically it is real silver-and-gold loss: how much labor, how many days does it take to dig a well, how much dry earth must be passed through to dig out living water. To yield once may be called cultivation; to yield a second time is character; to yield a third time is faith. But I also want to ask honestly: Is such a person too weak? Scripture's answer is in the next section — God was leading him, step by step, from the valley of contention into spaciousness. With every step he retreated, the land grew wider.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The Spaciousness of Rehoboth and the Covenant at Beersheba — God Appears, Enemies Seek Peace (26:22b-35)

He left there and dug another well. This time, no one strove. Isaac named that well Rechovot, Rehoboth, meaning "a spacious place" [4], and declared: "For now the LORD hath made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land." Please note the theological order of this declaration: spaciousness was not seized by him; it was given by the LORD; flourishing is not the terminus, it is what comes after spaciousness. People often reverse the order: first flourish, then seek spaciousness; the more they flourish, the more crowded they get, and the narrower the heart. Isaac's road is: first obey, then sow, be bullied, do not strive, dig wells again — and then spaciousness comes of itself. Spaciousness — the root of this word in the original has the sense of breadth and unobstructed passage; in the Psalms David says: He set my feet in a large place (Ps 31:8, in sense). The first spaciousness of a spacious place is spaciousness of heart: no longer needing to prove, no longer needing to seize, no longer calculating night after night who stopped my well.

After he had stood firm in the spacious place, Isaac went up to Beersheba. That night, the LORD appeared to him. In God's word there are three main points: first, identity — "I am the God of Abraham thy father"; Isaac's faith from then on was no longer only inherited; he himself met his father's God; second, the driving out of fear — "fear not, for I am with thee"; being with him is the promise of this chapter from beginning to end (26:3 "I will be with thee"; in 26:28 even Abimelech could see it); third, the restatement of blessing — to bless you and multiply your descendants. Isaac's response was to build an altar, call on the name of the LORD, pitch his tent, and order his servants to dig a well. The altar before the well: first worship, then work; first the altar, then the water. Beersheba was the place where his father had made a covenant with Abimelech, planted a tamarisk tree, and called on the name of God (Gen 21). What the father had done here, the son did again — only this time, it was not a repetition of weakness, but a repetition of worship. The good part of a family's character can likewise be repeated, and it must be repeated.

Then came the most glorious scene of the whole chapter: Abimelech, with his friend and the captain of his host, came from Gerar to Beersheba to seek peace of his own accord. The king who had once issued the order to leave now stood outside Isaac's tent and spoke a verdict-like sentence: "We saw certainly that the LORD was with thee... let there be now an oath betwixt us... and let us make a covenant with thee." (26:28, excerpt) Brothers and sisters, Isaac had not fought a single battle, had not seized back a single well, had not said a single harsh word — yet the enemy came in person to his door and acknowledged that God was with him. How did Isaac respond? He made them a feast, and they ate and drank; early the next morning they swore to one another, and Isaac sent them away, and they departed from him in peace. A feast on top of hostility, an oath after the feast. That very day his servants came and reported: the well we dug has water. Isaac named that well Shebah — this is the origin of the name Beersheba (26:33). At the end of the chapter, water had been found, the covenant had been made, the altar had been built. The people who stopped his wells came to eat his food, the people who troubled him swore with him, the king who drove him away sought peace from him. This is the final ledger of the man who does not strive: nothing is missing, everything is repaid in full and doubled, and one thing more is added — testimony. It was the testimony spoken by the Philistines' own mouths: the LORD is with you.

At the end of the chapter there is a seemingly idle stroke that is not idle: Esau was forty years old and took two Hittite women as wives, and they were a grief of mind to Isaac and Rebekah (26:34-35). The story of the spacious place ends by burying the mine of the next chapter: the blessing and the grief of this family will collide head-on in the tent next time.

Looking back over the whole chapter, Isaac's greatness is a quiet greatness. He had no peak like his father's building an altar and offering his son, nor a legend like his son's wrestling with God; he had only famine, a lie, stopped wells, and contention — things that do not make a stage. Yet it was precisely to such a person that Scripture left a whole chapter. Brothers and sisters, God remembers people not by the size of the scene: faithfulness on a spade and faithfulness on an altar have equal weight in His eyes. Every spade you dig silently today in the valley of Gerar, He is counting.

Look one level deeper at the wells of this chapter. The order in which Isaac dug wells is actually the order of his life: first, others' wells were stopped — that was the encounter he could not control; next, his father's wells were dug again — that was his attitude toward the past; then, his own newly dug wells were contested — that was his patience toward the present; finally, the well at Beersheba yielded water of itself — that was God's arrangement for the future. Four kinds of wells, four kinds of lessons, none can be missing. Many people are stuck at the first kind of well, counting all their lives how much others have stopped up for them; Isaac's preciousness lies in his not camping beside the stopped well. He acknowledged his encounter, but he did not accept fate; he honored his father, but did not lie down on his father's estate; facing contention, he did not entangle himself or take revenge, but simply walked a stretch further and dug another well. A detail of the biblical narrative is worth reading slowly: 26:22 says "he removed from thence, and digged another well" — "removed" appears more than once in this chapter (see 26:16-17, 21-22). Leaving, sometimes, is not failure but wisdom: leaving the atmosphere of contention, leaving the ground that consumes you, leaving the self that must fight to a win or loss. Some battlefields you lose even when you win, because you have lost your heart narrow; the one who leaves, his land instead grows wide.

There is another layer: Isaac's own temperament. Scripture's record of Isaac has no conversations under the starry sky like Abraham's, no night of wrestling with the angel like Jacob's; Isaac's theology is well-side theology, field theology — Scripture describes him as "meditating in the field" (Gen 24:63, in sense), and describes him sowing, digging wells, and building altars. His reverence is not in great scenes, but in day-after-day repetition: earth dug spade by spade, water sought mouthful by mouthful, altars built one by one. Brothers and sisters, is this not what most of our lives are like? No Red Sea, no pillar of fire, only wells to dig, seed to sow, accounts to settle, children to raise. This chapter is the gospel for ordinary people: the evidence of God's presence need not be a vision — it can also be a well no one finally comes to strive over, a year that actually yields a hundredfold, an enemy who suddenly comes to your door wanting to eat with you and make a covenant. Ordinary obedience, walked to the end, is spaciousness.

Do not forget either that after Rehoboth, Isaac did not stop: he went up to Beersheba, built an altar again, and dug a well again. Spaciousness is not retirement; spaciousness is the foundation of a new round of service. How many people slacken once they reach a spacious place and treat Rehoboth as the terminus; Isaac treated Rehoboth as a step — from the spacious place he went up, to the place where his father had made a covenant with God, and built his own altar there. If your spaciousness does not lead to an altar, it will soon grow narrow again; if your ease does not become worship, it will not be long before it is filled up by new contention. This is what this chapter leaves for those who "have already become spacious."

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: those who want to go down to Egypt. Your Gerar is in famine, and Egypt's lights look bright and near. What God says to you today is still that sentence: Go not down into Egypt. If the place you are going to violates the instruction God has already given you, however fertile it is, it is a wilderness; if the place where you stay is however dry, with His presence it is the land of promise.

The second kind: those who repeat their parents' weakness. You find yourself telling the lies your father told, losing the temper your mother lost, walking the road the previous generation walked wrongly. Do not despair: Isaac's weakness was repeated, and grace was repeated too. God's way of stopping you may be an appearing, or it may be the rebuke of an outsider. When you hear rebuke, do not be angry; first admit: this is God stopping me.

The third kind: those whose wells have been stopped. Your effort has been erased by others, your merit filled with earth by others, the road you dug through cut off at the waist by others. Learn from Isaac: do not spend your life striving over that well. The spade is in your own hand — leave the place of contention and dig again. Your life is not tied to that one well stopped by others; it is tied to the God who makes people spacious.

The fourth kind: those who dare not sow. The sense of sojourning is too heavy, so you will only keep livestock and will not sow: since it will not last long, why be serious? But the hundredfold harvest is given only to those who sow. If God has placed you in this city, this job, this church, take the plow and sow. To sow in a year of famine is an act of faith; the person who does not sow does not even have the chance of a hundredfold.

The fifth kind: those who have yielded until their hearts are cold. You yielded once, twice, and the third time you fear before you have even dug. Let me read Isaac's ledger to you: after contention and hostility comes Rehoboth; after Rehoboth, the appearing at Beersheba; after Beersheba, the enemy comes to your door seeking peace. God keeps account of all your yielding, and spaciousness lies behind. Do not get off at the station of Sitnah.

The sixth kind: those who have not yet dug their father's well open again. In your home there once was an altar of prayer, a table of Scripture reading, a song of worship, and the earth has already filled it halfway. Today, do not rush to dig a new well; first return to your father's well, shovel the earth away, and still call it by its original name. The spaciousness of the next generation mostly begins with digging open again the wells of the previous generation.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we walked with Isaac through one chapter: in famine he almost went down to Egypt, and God stopped him; in weakness he repeated his father's lie, and God also stopped him; in sowing he received a hundredfold harvest, in digging wells he met contention and hostility in succession, and he did not fight or seize, until Rehoboth — God made room for him; at the end, at Beersheba, he built an altar, made a covenant, and obtained water, and even his enemies acknowledged: the LORD was with him.

Let us pray together: God who makes people spacious, thank You. You stopped our feet from going down to Egypt, and You stopped our mouths from telling lies; You caused the obedient to have a hundredfold harvest in a year of famine, and the yielding to obtain a spacious place in a land of contention. Lord, in our hands there are stopped wells, and in our hearts earth not yet shoveled away. Grant us Isaac's quietness and endurance: not to strive, not to run away, to pick up the spade, first to dig open again our father's well, then to dig our own well, until we hear the sound of water, until even our enemies recognize that You are with us. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] be'er: Hebrew בְּאֵר (H875), a well, a water well; it appears repeatedly in the well-digging narrative of Genesis 26 (e.g., 26:15, 18-22, 25, 32-33); for the meaning, see BDB, HALOT. The statement in this manuscript that "a well is life" is a homiletical exposition based on the survival context of a dry land.

[2] Esek: Hebrew עֵשֶׂק (H6230) (26:20), a well name, meaning contention, striving, a matter of dispute; for the meaning, see BDB, HALOT (the etymological association of this entry with the verb to strive is discussed in many commentaries).

[3] Sitnah: Hebrew שִׂטְנָה (H7856), hostility, accusation, opposition; the well name in Genesis 26:21; for the meaning, see BDB, HALOT.

[4] Rechovot: Hebrew רְחֹבוֹת (H7344), a spacious place, a broad place; the well name in Genesis 26:22 (KJV: Rehoboth), and Isaac himself explained its meaning: "For now the LORD hath made room for us"; for the meaning, see BDB, HALOT.

[5] me'ah she'arim: Hebrew מֵאָה (H3967, a hundred) and שְׁעָרִים (H8180, measures, units of estimation, plural); Genesis 26:12, "received a hundredfold," literally, what was measured reached a hundred measures; for the meaning, see BDB, HALOT. The correspondence drawn in this manuscript with the hundredfold of the good soil in Mark 4:8 is a homiletical comparison, not an identity of meaning, and is noted here expressly.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture (KJV): "And he removed from thence, and digged another well; and for that they strove not: and he called the name of it Rehoboth; and he said, For now the LORD hath made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land." (Genesis 26:22) Again and again Isaac was wronged at Gerar: his father's wells were stopped up, and his new wells were named Esek, contention, and Sitnah, hostility. He neither fought nor quit; he simply took up his spade, moved on, and dug again. Room to flourish is not seized; it is given by God after we pass through contention and hostility. Our lives have stopped-up wells too: work erased, kindness misunderstood, roads blocked. If we spend all our strength fighting over one well, our hearts narrow first. Isaac teaches us: keep the spade in your own hand, walk away from the quarrel, and dig again. God remembers every quiet yielding, and the spacious place lies ahead. Today you do not need to win back that well; you only need to dig another, trusting that the God who gives room will make you flourish in the place He assigns. Nor should you despise re-digging your father's well: if the prayer, Scripture reading, and worship your family once had have been filled in by the soil of busyness, clear that well today. Revival is often not the invention of something new but the recovery of an old grace; spaciousness is often not a faraway place but one more spade of digging where you stand.

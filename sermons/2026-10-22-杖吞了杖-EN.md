## Summary (about 100 words)

God set Moses before Pharaoh as God, with Aaron as his prophet to speak His word; two old men of over eighty entered the palace under orders. Aaron's staff became a great serpent; the Egyptian magicians could imitate it, but Aaron's staff swallowed up their staffs. In the first plague the Nile turned to blood as God judged the river god Egypt worshiped; yet Pharaoh's heart was hardened and he would not listen. The confrontation had only just begun.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

At the end of the previous chapter Moses was still declining. He said, how will Pharaoh listen to me, a man of slow speech? The people will not listen, Pharaoh even less; this mouth of mine really will not do. God did not continue debating the question of eloquence with him; He simply sent him and Aaron before Pharaoh. This chapter is the first day the two men truly walked into the palace.

Interestingly, chapter 7 does not open with a miracle but with office. God first sets Moses in place: you shall be as God to Pharaoh; then He sets Aaron in place: he shall be the one who speaks for you. Only after that comes the staff — thrown down, turned into a serpent, and swallowing a floor full of serpents. Then comes the first plague: the water of the Nile turned to blood, the fish died, the river stank, and the Egyptians could not drink. But Pharaoh watched all of this, turned back into his palace, and his heart did not move at all.

What this chapter sets before us is the first face-to-face encounter between the true and the counterfeit. The counterfeit can produce a serpent; the counterfeit can turn water to blood; the counterfeit lacks nothing in show. But God used one single action to separate true from false: swallowing. The real does not rely on numbers; the real relies on authority. Today we will look together at how God establishes His servants, how He exposes imitations on the spot, and how, through a river turned to blood, He pronounces judgment on all the gods of Egypt.

Let us read several key passages of today's Scripture together:

"And the LORD said unto Moses, See, I have made thee a god to Pharaoh: and Aaron thy brother shall be thy prophet." (Exodus 7:1, excerpt, KJV)

"And the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD, when I stretch forth mine hand upon Egypt, and bring out the children of Israel from among them." (Exodus 7:5, excerpt, KJV)

"And Moses was fourscore years old, and Aaron fourscore and three years old, when they spake unto Pharaoh." (Exodus 7:7, KJV)

"For they cast down every man his rod, and they became serpents: but Aaron's rod swallowed up their rods." (Exodus 7:12, excerpt, KJV)

"Thus saith the LORD, In this thou shalt know that I am the LORD: behold, I will smite with the rod that is in mine hand upon the waters which are in the river, and they shall be turned to blood." (Exodus 7:17, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, we live in an age when imitations fill the streets and true and false are often hard to tell apart. Teach us today, through Your word, to discern: what is mere shape, and what is Your authority; and remind us that You do all this not for show, but so that people may know that You are the LORD. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Office Established First, Signs Afterward — As God Before Pharaoh (7:1-7)

First hear the first thing God said to Moses: "See, I have made thee a god to Pharaoh." The sense of the Hebrew is: See, I have set you as God before Pharaoh. Moses had just finished saying that he was of slow speech, and God's answer was not first to fix his eloquence, but first to give him his office. When Moses stood before Pharaoh, he was not there as a representative of slaves begging for mercy, nor as a diplomatic envoy coming to negotiate; he was a man carrying God's word, standing in God's place, speaking to Pharaoh. For Pharaoh to hear Moses' words was to hear God speaking to him; for Pharaoh to reject Moses was to reject God Himself.

For everyone who proclaims God's word, this is both a great comfort and a great trembling. The comfort is this: the weight of the one who proclaims does not come from eloquence, nor from degrees, nor from whether his voice sounds good, but from the One whose word he proclaims. Moses' mouth did not suddenly become fluent, yet his office was already settled. The trembling is this: since the office is received from God, the one who proclaims must not mix his own things into the word — he may not embellish it to please people, nor water it down for fear that people will leave. A man who speaks standing in God's place must himself first stand under God's word.

Then look at Aaron's office: "Aaron thy brother shall be thy prophet." The word used here in the original is "prophet." A prophet in Hebrew is not a fortune-teller, not a sorcerer who predicts luck and disaster; the prophet's duty is to pass on God's word exactly as it is — God puts the word in his mouth, and he speaks it out. Aaron as Moses' prophet means Aaron as Moses' mouthpiece: God's word came to Moses, Moses passed it to Aaron, and Aaron spoke it out before Pharaoh. This chain of transmission links together link by link: God, Moses, Aaron, Pharaoh. The chain is not long, but not one link may falsify. Brothers and sisters, those in the church who proclaim, who teach, who tell the gospel to their neighbors all stand on this chain. Our duty is not to invent words but to transmit them faithfully; the power of the word is not in us, but in the source of the word.

There is another layer, hidden in the order of the two brothers. Aaron was the elder brother, three years older; by human custom, the younger should listen to the elder. But God's arrangement is precisely the reverse: the elder brother becomes the younger brother's mouthpiece, and the words the younger received, the elder speaks out in public, without changing a syllable. Aaron did not feel he had lost face, nor did he snatch at being the source of the chain. This is what the office of proclaiming is: it does not care which link of the chain you stand on, only that the chain does not break and the word does not change flavor. What is scarcest today is not people who can invent new ways of preaching, but people willing to stand at their own link and pass on, as received, the word they received. The elder brother was willing to open his mouth for the younger; the younger was willing first to receive quietly; these two brothers of over eighty, one saying no more than needed, the other doing no more than needed, together delivered one sentence before Pharaoh.

Next, God states the purpose up front, so that we do not misread the direction of the signs that follow. He said, I will harden Pharaoh's heart [3], and I will multiply my signs and wonders in the land of Egypt; Pharaoh will not listen to you, but I will stretch out My hand upon Egypt and bring out My people, "and the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD." Please circle this sentence: the purpose of the signs was declared from the very beginning — not a contest of magic, not a performance, not Moses' vindication, but that people might know that He is the LORD. Knowing God is the endpoint of all these works. Verse 5 says it to the Egyptians; verse 17 will say it again to Pharaoh. The ten plagues that follow grow heavier one after another, and the reason lies here too: every time Pharaoh refused, God used the next plague to let Egypt know Him one degree deeper. If people will not know God in grace, they will come to know Him step by step in judgment.

The Scripture also says: "And Moses and Aaron did so; as the LORD commanded them, so did they." No bargaining, no added conditions. The Moses who was still declining in the previous chapter, in this chapter is left with only one response: they did just so. Growth in faith is sometimes exactly this quiet: not that the feelings suddenly improved, but that the feet stepped out first.

Finally, note a record that looks like an aside: "And Moses was fourscore years old, and Aaron fourscore and three years old, when they spake unto Pharaoh." Why does the Bible deliberately write down the two men's ages at the opening of the confrontation? Because this is God's normal way of working, not the exception. Abraham was seventy-five when he was called and ninety-nine when he was confirmed again; Caleb at eighty-five still said that his strength, great or small, was the same as on the day Moses sent him out. Moses' life — the first forty years in the palace, the middle forty years shepherding in the wilderness — those seemingly wasted eighty years were precisely the eighty years in which God prepared him: the palace taught him Egypt's ways; the wilderness taught him patience and humility. By the time he stood before Pharaoh, he lacked nothing except action — and action, beginning at eighty, is not late at all. Brothers and sisters, age has never been a reason for God not to use a person. Those of you who feel you are old, late, past your season, please hear this verse: God handed the most important battle to an eighty-year-old and an eighty-three-year-old. Being used in one's later years has never been the exception in the Bible; it is the rule.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Staff Swallowed the Staffs — The Real Does Not Win by Numbers (7:8-13)

Before the formal clash, there is first a rehearsal. God told Moses and Aaron in advance: Pharaoh will say to you, perform a miracle, show us your credentials. At that time, Aaron shall throw his staff down before Pharaoh, and the staff will become a great serpent. Note that even Pharaoh's challenge was scripted by God beforehand. This Pharaoh thought he was testing others, yet every step he took was within God's foreknowledge. The confrontation was unequal from the very start: one side improvising, the other side acting according to a script whose ending was already known.

Here there is a word of terminology at which we must stop and speak clearly, because the cutting edge of the whole rehearsal is hidden in this word. In chapter 4, at the burning bush, Moses' staff became a snake, and the word used was nachash — an ordinary snake. But in chapter 7, what God commanded Aaron's staff to become was tannin (תַּנִּין, H8577) [1] — a great serpent, a great monster of the sea, an enormous creature of the crocodile kind. Genesis 1:21 says that God created the great creatures of the waters, and the original uses this same word: they are the largest and most feared class among created things, and the text makes clear from the very beginning that they were made by God and are under God. By Ezekiel chapter 29, God, rebuking Pharaoh king of Egypt through the prophet, calls him directly the great crocodile lying in the midst of his rivers — and uses this same word again. That is to say, the word choice in Exodus chapter 7 was not picked at random: Pharaoh wore a serpent-shaped crown on his head — that rearing cobra was the emblem of Egyptian kingship; Pharaoh regarded himself as the great monster in the river, whom no one could shake. Now God had Aaron throw down a staff, and what it became was exactly such a great serpent: what you take to be your emblem and your power is, in My hand, nothing but what a shepherd's staff turned into. Before the rehearsal had even begun to swallow, it had already pronounced judgment in its wording: that bit of majesty Egypt was so proud of was originally a created thing, and a created thing cannot sit as an equal beside the God who made it.

Aaron did as commanded. The staff was thrown down and became a great serpent. What was Pharaoh's reaction? He was neither flustered nor convinced; he summoned the wise men and the sorcerers. The Egyptian magicians (חַרְטֻמִּים, H2748) [2] came on stage. These men were no street frauds; they were priestly scribes in Egypt's temples, who for generations had studied their sacred books and secret arts — the most accomplished people in that religious system. How did they respond? "they also did in like manner with their enchantments." Each man threw down his staff, and the staffs also became great serpents. The Bible does not explain how exactly they did it — whether sleight of hand or the working of evil spirits, the text does not indulge curiosity and does not explain; it records truthfully only this: they achieved the same shape. Here we must be honest: an imitation can copy the shape. The false is not a paper tiger that crumples at a touch; the false can transform, can move, can writhe all over the floor, and for a moment the scene looked evenly matched. Whoever thinks that following God means never meeting a high-quality imitation has not yet read this passage carefully. What the powers of darkness are best at is not open opposition, but doing the same likewise: whatever you have, I have one almost like it too.

Please also note the staff Aaron used. It was no ceremonial staff of the palace, inlaid with no gold and set with no jewels; it was a wooden staff a shepherd leaned on every day walking the mountain paths; back in Exodus chapter 4 the Bible already called it the staff of God — not that the staff itself had any magic power, but that it was held in the hand of an obedient man, thrown down and lifted up at God's word. Egypt's court had no lack of fine staffs; Pharaoh held in his hand the scepter of a whole nation, and the magicians each had one of their own. But that day, what decided victory and defeat was not the material or pedigree of the staff, but which side the most ordinary staff stood on. Brothers and sisters, God has always been like this: He does not rely on the most respectable vessel to overpower people; He uses the most obedient vessel to reveal Himself. The staff in your hand may be very ordinary — ordinary gifts, an ordinary post, an ordinary life — but ordinariness has never been the problem; the problem is whose command it obeys and for whom it is lifted up.

But the next scene settled true and false with one hammer blow: "But Aaron's rod swallowed up their rods." Think carefully about the arithmetic of this victory. By numbers, the other side had many staffs, and Aaron had only one; by spectacle, the other side's serpents covered the floor. God did not produce ten more staffs to overwhelm the lineup, nor did He make Aaron's serpent grow three times its size to frighten the crowd away. The method He used was: swallow. One swallowed all of them. The real has never won by numbers. Truth does not need a crowd to be true, and authority does not need pageantry to prove itself. Note even more the nature of the ending: the magicians' staffs were not driven off, nor broken, but swallowed — taken into God's staff, gone without return, with not even a carcass left to find. Such has always been the end of imitations in the Bible: it may strut for a while, but its final destination is to be taken away and finished off by the true authority. Later, in the New Testament, the apostle Paul mentions these magicians and, following Jewish tradition, calls them by the names Jannes and Jambres, and says that they resisted the truth with corrupt minds — yet before God, even their names had to be handed down by tradition, while their staffs remained forever in the belly of Aaron's staff.

The rehearsal ends here, and the conclusion is written in verse 13: "And Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he hearkened not unto them; as the LORD had said." A credential as great as the swallowing of the staffs lay before his eyes, and Pharaoh was utterly unmoved. Brothers and sisters, this reminds us of a solemn matter: a sign can prove authority, but it cannot compel a heart to turn. Seeing is not the same as being willing to bow. Pharaoh's problem was never insufficient evidence; it was that he was unwilling for this LORD to be King. This foreshadowing will be unfolded in detail in the third part.

Why, moreover, was this rehearsal arranged before the ten plagues? Because the credential had to be established before the judgment. God first let Pharaoh see clearly by whom Moses and Aaron were sent, and that the authority of this staff was higher than all of Egypt's magicians, and only then sent down the plagues one by one — so that Pharaoh could not plead that he had not known who his opponent was. God always works this way: He declares first and acts afterward; He gives the credential first and executes judgment afterward. He never ambushes anyone; He lays the evidence on the table, so that those who refuse have no excuse. This rehearsal was also prepared for Moses and Aaron themselves: the real hard battles were still ahead, and God first let the two old men taste a small victory, so that they would know the staff in their hands could swallow a floor full of serpents. Later, when the river water reddened again and the skies darkened again, they could look back on this day and have a settled heart. This is often how God trains His workers: first a rehearsal, then ten hard battles; not because He is sparing, but because He feeds a person's faith step by step until it grows.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Water Turned to Blood — Judgment Falls on the Gods of Egypt (7:14-25)

After the rehearsal, the first plague formally arrived. God told Moses: in the morning Pharaoh will go out to the water; you shall stand on the bank of the river to meet him, holding in your hand the staff that was turned into a serpent. Consider this picture first: early morning, the riverbank, Moses already standing there waiting. This is the rhythm of the ten plagues from here on — not one passionate showdown, but a meeting kept again and again, on time and at the appointed place. Pharaoh came out of the palace, and Moses was there; Pharaoh came to the riverbank, and Moses was still there. The one who delivers the word does not win by one burst of momentum, but by continually standing in the place to which God sent him. Brothers and sisters, much ministry, and many prayers for the souls of family members, follow this rhythm: early morning, the riverbank, wait for him to come out, and say it once more. Until God's word has been accomplished, the one who keeps the meeting does not leave his post.

Note also which staff was in his hand. God specifically commanded: take the staff that was turned into a serpent. The very one that had swallowed the staffs in the palace the day before was now to be lifted up again to strike the river water. God did not issue some new instrument; He told Moses to fight battle after battle with the same staff. This is concrete comfort for us: the testimony God gives us has continuity — as He used this small credential in your hand to win yesterday, so He will use the same credential to carry you across the river today. You need not, at every new difficulty, long for some great ability you have never had; first look at the old staff in your hand — God Himself has used it. Moses' faith was not built on an endless stream of novelties, but on a faithfulness fulfilled again and again. The staff was still the same staff, but the river was about to change color.

The word Moses was to deliver again made the purpose plain: "Thus saith the LORD, In this thou shalt know that I am the LORD: behold, I will smite with the rod that is in mine hand upon the waters which are in the river, and they shall be turned to blood." The word went first; the plague followed. God never strikes first and explains afterward; He declares first and executes afterward, leaving people room to hear and to turn. Then Aaron lifted the staff and struck the water of the river, and all the waters of Egypt — rivers, canals, ponds, even the water stored in vessels of wood and stone — were turned to blood (דָּם, H1818) [6]. The fish in the river died, the river stank, and the Egyptians could not drink; they could only dig around the riverbank looking for water, and they dug for seven days.

The Scripture also coldly records Pharaoh's reaction: he turned and went into his house, and did not lay even this to heart. The people were digging for water at the riverbank, the children at home were crying with thirst, and the man who was king shut his door and acted as though nothing had happened. When disaster comes, the difference between people is often right here: on one side the suffering digging for water, on the other side those in power shutting the door. Pharaoh's hardness of heart was not only hardness toward God's word, unmoved; it was also hardness toward the people's suffering, unmoved — and these two always go together: the more numb a person is to God's word, the more numb he is to people's suffering. The text also says this plague lasted a full seven days. Seven days is not long, but for a nation that lived off the river, seven days of nothing but bloody water was enough for every Egyptian to experience personally: their river could not save them, and the god they worshiped could not save them either. God gave Egypt seven days to think; Pharaoh used the seven days to wait for the plague to pass. When an opportunity lies before you, whether it is used for turning back or for riding it out depends entirely on whether the heart is soft or hard.

Why did the first plague strike the Nile? This was no randomly chosen target. The Nile was Egypt's lifeline: without the Nile's regular flooding and irrigation, Egypt would be a desert. It was also Egypt's deity: the Egyptians took the rising and falling of the Nile to be the work of Hapi, the river god, and offered sacrifices to him and sang to him; half of Pharaoh's authority was built on this river's blessing. By striking the Nile, God pronounced judgment before the face of the whole nation of Egypt: this god you have worshiped for thousands of years cannot even protect himself before Me; the source of life as you suppose it, with one stroke of My staff, becomes a river of death and blood. Blood in the Bible is originally the sign of life; once a source of life leaves the Lord who made it, even a river that gives life becomes a river exhaling death. This is precisely the beginning of that sentence in Exodus 12:12 — God said He would execute judgment "against all the gods of Egypt"; and Numbers 33:4, looking back on this history, also says that the LORD "executed judgments" on their gods as well. On the surface the ten plagues are disasters; in substance they are a judgment conducted case by case: the gods of Egypt were named one by one, and fell one by one. The opponent of the signs was never only the man Pharaoh, but the whole system of idols behind Pharaoh.

The magicians' reaction once again exposed the underside of imitation: they also used their secret arts to turn water into blood. Picture how absurd this scene is: all Egypt was already bloody water, and people were suffering for lack of anything to drink, and the magicians produced some more blood — what kind of skill is that? An imitation can reproduce a disaster, but it cannot undo a disaster; it can copy a shape, but it has no authority to reverse the shape. By the next chapter they could not even produce the lice, and could only say to Pharaoh: This is the finger of God. Imitation has its boundary; every step it takes forward brings it one step closer to the end of its ability. That they could still do the same likewise in chapter 7 was because God permitted them to lay out all their cards; once the cards were laid out, the end was not far away.

Now we must face squarely the most important matter in this chapter, and the one most easily preached out of balance: Pharaoh's hardened heart. In this chapter and in the whole book, the Bible uses three different ways of speaking, and the one who preaches must present them all honestly, side by side, and must not pick out a single sentence and pronounce a final verdict. The first kind: God hardens. In Exodus 4:21, at the very beginning of the commission, God prophesied: I will harden his heart, so that he will not let the people go; and in chapter 7 verse 3 He said with His own mouth: "I will harden Pharaoh's heart." The verb used in the original is qashah (קָשָׁה, H7185) [3], to make hard, to make stubborn — this is the statement of advance declaration. The second kind: Pharaoh hardens his own heart. Reading on, 8:15, 8:32, and 9:34 all state plainly that when Pharaoh saw the plague was past, he hardened his own heart, made his heart stubborn — the hardening was a choice he made again and again himself. The third kind is the statement that names no agent: the heart simply became hard. Verses 13 and 22 of chapter 7 are both like this: the original uses chazaq (חָזַק, H2388) [4], the heart became firm, became hard, stating only the condition and naming no one who made it so; and when God describes Pharaoh in verse 14 He uses yet another expression, kaved lev (כָּבֵד לֵב, H3515) [5], a heavy heart, a stubborn heart, — in the Hebrew, literally, "Pharaoh's heart is heavy." The three kinds of statement appear in alternation; this is the balance the Bible itself maintains.

Scholars and interpreters through the ages have discussed this at length; this manuscript only relays it honestly and does not hand down a single conclusion on everyone's behalf. One reading emphasizes the order: in the narrative of the ten plagues, statements of Pharaoh hardening his own heart appear many times earlier, while "the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart" as an explicit statement of action does not appear within the course of the plagues until 9:12; on this basis some interpreters hold that God's hardening is judicial in nature: Pharaoh first chose hardness again and again, and God then gave him over to, and confirmed him in, the direction he had chosen — just as Romans chapter 1 says, God gave them up to a reprobate mind. Another reading emphasizes sovereignty: the prophecies of 4:21 and 7:3 come first, showing that the whole matter was within God's plan from the beginning, and that God's hardening was not a passive response but an active arrangement He made in order to make His name known and to let the Egyptians know Him. The two readings dispute the emphasis of theological interpretation, but on one undisputed fact there is no disagreement: Pharaoh had a real choice every single time, and every single time he truly refused; hardness was not made in one day, but is a road that grows deeper the further one walks it — refuse a little today, grow harder tomorrow, until at last the heart is so hard that even when his firstborn dies in his arms he will not turn back completely. So the warning this chapter gives us carries a sense of time: turn back while it is early. While the heart is still soft, that is the time to turn; Hebrews says, today if you will hear His voice, harden not your hearts. It was not that Pharaoh had no opportunity; it was that he used his opportunities up, one by one.

One more reminder about the method of reading: these three kinds of statement all make their entrance already within chapter 7 itself — verse 3 is God's prophecy, verses 13 and 22 record the state of the heart, and verse 14 is God's description of Pharaoh's heart. The same author, in the same chapter, places the three expressions side by side, feeling no contradiction between them at all. Those who interpret should learn this honesty: we should not flatten out, for the sake of preaching smoothly, the tension the Bible itself maintains. Speak every sentence as it stands, and do not exaggerate any one of them into the only explanation; this is not vagueness — this is reverence for the text. And whichever reading one holds, the lesson that lands on us points to the same place, which is the few sentences gathered below.

Gathering up the application of this chapter, we are left with four sentences. The first, for those who proclaim: your office comes from the word entrusted to you, not from your eloquence; stand firm, and do just so. The second, for those who discern: do not be frightened by the numbers and noise of imitations; look at the ending — who swallowed whom, who can reverse, and who cannot. However well the church copies the shapes of the world's methods, if it lacks the authority of God's word, in the end it cannot produce life. The third, for those who rely on idols: what is the Nile of your life? Is it income, is it status, is it some relationship or habit you cannot leave? Sometimes God strikes what we rely on, not in order to destroy us, but in order to let us know that the source of life is not that river, but the Lord behind the river. The fourth, for those whose hearts are now hardening: the fact that you can still hear the warning shows your heart is not yet hard to the bottom; hear today, and turn back today — do not leave turning back for the next plague.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have seen a staff, a river, and a heart. A staff swallowed a floor full of staffs — the real does not rely on numbers, but on authority; what can copy the shape cannot swallow the real. A river was turned to blood — God judged the gods Egypt worshiped, and let people know in whose hand the sovereignty of life lies. A heart, before the signs, grew harder each time — reminding us that hardness is a road, and that if we are to turn back, we should do it while today is still in time. And the center of all this is the sentence God spoke twice with His own mouth: that the Egyptians may know that I am the LORD, and that you may know that I am the LORD. He worked signs not in order to win a contest of magic, but in order that people might know Him.

Let us pray together: LORD God, thank You; You establish Your servants not by looking at eloquence, but by looking at Your own word; You made one staff swallow all the staffs, and taught us not to fear the noise of imitations. Search us, we pray: where is the Nile we rely on, and at what sentence has our heart begun to harden? While our hearts are still soft, pull us back; and use us, even at eighty, even of slow speech, to stand in the place to which You have sent us and to do just as You have commanded. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[1] tannin: Hebrew תַּנִּין (H8577), a great serpent, sea monster, or enormous water or reptile creature of the crocodile kind; Exodus 7:9, 10, and 12 use this word both for what Aaron's staff became and for what the magicians' staffs became, differing from the nachash (נָחָשׁ, H5175, an ordinary snake) into which Moses' staff was turned in Exodus 4:3; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The second part of this manuscript's "the wording itself is the verdict — the emblem of Egyptian kingship is, under God's hand, nothing but a created thing" is a homiletical exposition based on the distribution of this word, and is noted as exposition.

[2] chartummim: Hebrew חַרְטֻמִּים (H2748), the magicians of Egypt, priestly scribes versed in secret arts; Exodus 7:11 and 22 twice call those who worked the secret arts by this word (Genesis 41:8 and Daniel 1:20 also use it of men who kept the secret arts and sacred writings at court and in the temples); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "priestly scribes of the temples, the most accomplished people in that religious system" is a factual statement based on the usage of this word; the mechanism of their arts is not explained by the text itself, and this manuscript makes no determination about it, noting only that the text records that they "did the same."

[3] qashah: Hebrew קָשָׁה (H7185), to be hard, to be stubborn; its causative form appears in Exodus 7:3, "I will harden Pharaoh's heart," and in the prophecy of Exodus 4:21, in both cases with God as subject, as an advance prophetic declaration; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. At the level of fact: this way of speaking, in narrative order, precedes the unfolding of the ten plagues. The two scholarly readings concerning "God hardens" given in the third part of this manuscript (a judicial giving-over and confirmation of the direction Pharaoh chose, and an active arrangement of sovereignty) are a relaying of exegetical discussion; this manuscript presents both side by side and does not pronounce a single final conclusion.

[4] chazaq: Hebrew חָזַק (H2388), to be strong, to be firm; Exodus 7:13 and 22, "Pharaoh's heart was hardened," use the corresponding form of this word to state the condition of the heart, without naming an agent; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. At the level of fact: the same way of speaking recurs in 8:19, 9:35, and elsewhere; together with 8:15, 32, and 9:34, which state plainly that Pharaoh hardened his own heart, and with 9:12 onward, which state plainly that the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart (the same word), the three kinds of expression appear in alternation throughout the plague narrative, and the third part of this manuscript presents them honestly side by side according to that alternation.

[5] kaved lev: Hebrew כָּבֵד (H3515), heavy, stubborn; in Exodus 7:14 God's description of Pharaoh, "his heart was hardened," is literally "his heart is heavy," in the Hebrew the sense is "a heavy, stubborn heart"; Exodus 8:15, 32, and 9:34, where Pharaoh made his heart stubborn, also use this root; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "a heavy heart is a stubborn heart" is a homiletical expression based on the literal sense; the facts of the root have been noted separately from the exposition.

[6] dam: Hebrew דָּם (H1818), blood; it recurs throughout the first plague in Exodus 7:17-21 (striking the river water so that it became blood; blood throughout all the land of Egypt); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. That the Nile was bound up in Egyptian religion with the blessing of the river god Hapi and was the lifeline of Egyptian agriculture and life is a statement of historical background; Exodus 12:12, where God says He will execute judgment "against all the gods of Egypt," and Numbers 33:4, where the LORD "executed judgments" on their gods as well, are the scriptural basis for this manuscript's "the ten plagues are a judgment pronounced on the gods of Egypt"; this manuscript's "once a source of life leaves the Lord who made it, it becomes a river of death" is a homiletical exposition, and is noted as exposition.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Each one threw down his staff and it became a snake. But Aaron's staff swallowed up their staffs." (Exodus 7:12, NIV) "This is what the LORD says: By this you will know that I am the LORD: With the staff that is in my hand I will strike the water of the Nile, and it will be changed into blood." (Exodus 7:17, NIV)

The most reassuring thing in this chapter is that God's truth never wins by numbers. On Pharaoh's side there were many staffs, snakes all over the floor, an imposing show of force; on God's side there was only one staff — a shepherd's staff worn by use. But truth is not counted by the piece: Aaron's staff opened its mouth and swallowed all the others. In your life you will also meet "a floor full of snakes": falsehood spoken by many, crooked reasoning argued on a grand scale, unbelief pressing in with its momentum, while in your hand there seems to be only one thin staff. Do not be afraid, and do not imitate their way of making up numbers. Simply throw down and lift up, according to His word, the staff God has given you. The shape can be copied; the authority cannot. Only what can swallow is real. Remind yourself today as well: God did all this not for spectacle, but so that you would know that He is the LORD. Knowing Him matters more than winning. Watch your heart as well: Pharaoh saw the swallowing of the staffs and saw the river of blood, yet his heart grew harder each time. Seeing many signs does not by itself make a heart soft; only hearing, and being willing to turn back, counts.

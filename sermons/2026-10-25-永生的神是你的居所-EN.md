## Summary

Deuteronomy 33 is the blessing with which Moses, the man of God, blessed the children of Israel before his death. The blessing opens with a recollection of the theophany — he came from Sinai, he loved the people, he was king in Jeshurun — and then blesses the tribes by name: for Reuben, fading away, he asks that he may live; for Judah in battle he asks for help; for Levi he asks for faithfulness and the charge of teaching; for Benjamin, safety; for Joseph, the abundance of heaven above and earth beneath; until at last, "as thy days, so shall thy strength be." The whole chapter closes with the highest declaration of all: the eternal God is your dwelling place, and underneath are the everlasting arms.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, we thank you. What we read today is not a doctrine but a string of blessings from an old man at the end of his life. Moses' one hundred and twenty years have reached their last station, and what his hands leave behind is not complaint but blessing. Open our hearts, we pray, and let these blessings, sentence by sentence, fall into each of our own circumstances: let the weak receive strength, those in battle receive help, and the wandering receive a dwelling place. When we leave this place, may every head carry one word that has come from you. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: Why Is a Dying Man's Mouth Full of Blessing?

The song of Deuteronomy 32 is warning; chapter 33 is blessing. Moses first sang the song of judgment, and then spoke the words of blessing — the order itself is a sermon: God's word first deals with sin, and then releases grace; Moses' heart first bears witness, and then plays the father.

The text first gives Moses a title: "Moses the man of God" (Deut 33:1) — "And this is the blessing, wherewith Moses the man of God blessed the children of Israel before his death." The heading of Psalm 90 carries the same words: "A Prayer of Moses the man of God." As his life draws near its end, his identity card in Scripture has only these words left on it — not lawgiver, not prophet, not leader, but "the man of God." And he himself has no tribe: the list of blessings for the twelve tribes includes Levi, but there is no "tribe of Moses"; his lifelong portion was God himself. This is precisely the root of Levi's blessing, as we shall see below.

This blessing also has an elder standing beside it for comparison: Genesis 49, where Jacob on his deathbed blesses his twelve sons. Read the two side by side and the interest is endless: Jacob's words dwell mostly on each man's past and character, Moses' words mostly on each tribe's future and inheritance; Jacob's prophecy over Reuben is severe (Gen 49:3–4: "Reuben, thou art my firstborn, my might, and the beginning of my strength, the excellency of dignity, and the excellency of power: Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel; because thou wentest up to thy father's bed; then defiledst thou it: he went up to my couch."), yet Moses prays for Reuben, "Let Reuben live, and not die" (Deut 33:6). Centuries have passed, and in God's hand the curse has been held up by prayer. Matthew Henry's commentary observes that Moses' blessing breathes the air of the New Testament: he does not recount the people's sins; he looks only toward their blessing. [1] That a man at the point of death can have a mouth full of blessing rests not on cultivation but on his certainty that the One to whom he is going is a God who blesses.

One textual fact must be registered first: in this chapter's list of tribes, Simeon does not appear. Scholars mostly read this together with the historical fact that the tribe of Simeon gradually faded and was later absorbed within the territory of Judah (Josh 19:1–9; Josh 19:1, "And the second lot came forth to Simeon, even for the tribe of the children of Simeon according to their families: and their inheritance was within the inheritance of the children of Judah."; Josh 19:9, "Out of the portion of the children of Judah was the inheritance of the children of Simeon: for the part of the children of Judah was too much for them: therefore the children of Simeon had their inheritance within the inheritance of them."), and with Jacob's severe words in Genesis 49:5–7, where Simeon and Levi are named together ("Simeon and Levi are brethren; instruments of cruelty are in their habitations. O my soul, come not thou into their secret; unto their assembly, mine honour, be not thou united: for in their anger they slew a man, and in their selfwill they digged down a wall. Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce; and their wrath, for it was cruel: I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel."). The list in Revelation 7 likewise makes its own selection (Rev 7:4–8, fetched and checked in this round: Dan is not listed there, while Joseph and Manasseh are listed separately, and Simeon and Levi both appear); the agreements and differences among the three lists have always been stated side by side in scholarship and discussed, and from the pulpit they are registered only, not adjudicated. [2]

Notice also the posture of the one who blesses. When Moses blesses others, he himself is standing under the sentence "thou shalt not go thither" (Deut 32:52: "Yet thou shalt see the land before thee; but thou shalt not go thither unto the land which I give the children of Israel."). A man shut by God outside the door of the promised land turns around and blesses, one by one, the people who are about to enter — and there is not one sour word in between. What completeness this requires: he knew that his own portion was not in the landed inheritance of Canaan but in God himself — the frame of mind of Psalm 16:5 (David's words): "The LORD is the portion of mine inheritance and of my cup: thou maintainest my lot." Brothers and sisters, the greatest lesson a person has to learn after middle age is exactly this: to watch others walk through a door you cannot enter, and still bless them sincerely. The one who can do this — his dwelling place is certainly not inside that door; it is in God. Moses did it, and that is why Deuteronomy 33 has not a trace of bitterness when you read it; it is honey from beginning to end.

## Part One: The Foundation of the Blessing — He Came from Sinai, and Was King in Jeshurun (Deut 33:1–5)

### 1. The blessing begins from God's appearing, not from man's need

"And he said, The LORD came from Sinai, and rose up from Seir unto them; he shined forth from mount Paran, and he came with ten thousands of saints: from his right hand went a fiery law for them. Yea, he loved the people; all his saints are in thy hand: and they sat down at thy feet; every one shall receive of thy words." (Deut 33:2–3)

Moses' blessing is not a wish made out of thin air. He first takes the people back to Sinai: God has come, God has appeared, God has with his own hand delivered the fiery law. The foundation of blessing is history, not emotion. The prayer of Habakkuk chapter 3 recalls God's appearing along the same route — he came from Teman and from Paran (Hab 3:3: "God came from Teman, and the Holy One from mount Paran. Selah. His glory covered the heavens, and the earth was full of his praise."); and Psalm 68 likewise records, "the Lord is among them, as in Sinai, in the holy place" (Ps 68:17: "The chariots of God are twenty thousand, even thousands of angels: the Lord is among them, as in Sinai, in the holy place."). When you bless your children, when you bless a friend, learn from Moses: first tell what God has done in the past, and then declare what he will accomplish in the future. When the foundation is sunk into accomplished fact, blessing is no longer a courtesy.

"and they sat down at thy feet; every one shall receive of thy words" — this is the quietest picture in the whole chapter. In Luke's Gospel, Mary "sat at Jesus' feet, and heard his word" (Luke 10:39: "And she had a sister called Mary, which also sat at Jesus' feet, and heard his word."), and the Lord said that was the good part that shall not be taken away. The entrance to blessing is always sitting at his feet; for a person who will not sit down and hear the word, the blessing passes over his head and cannot settle.

Look again at the middle of verse 3: "Yea, he loved the people." The original word is חֹבֵב (ḥōbēb, from ḥābab, H2245), a rare Old Testament word for "loved, cherished," pointing to the tenderness of holding the beloved in one's arms (according to BDB). [10] At the head of the blessing the lawgiver does not speak first of the law's majesty; he speaks first of the warmth of God's embrace: all his saints are in his hand, and the people sit at his feet — held in one hand, listening with attentive ear; the blessing begins from exactly this posture. If anyone among us today feels that God is far from him and that only rules and requirements remain, come back to this verse: he first loves, and then commands; he first embraces, and then teaches. Never reverse the order; reversed, the faith is left with nothing but pressure.

### 2. The law is an inheritance, and God's kingship is the blessing

"Moses commanded us a law, even the inheritance of the congregation of Jacob. And he was king in Jeshurun, when the heads of the people and the tribes of Israel were gathered together." (Deut 33:4–5)

In Moses' eyes the law is not a burden; it is an "inheritance" — a family estate that can be handed down to children and grandchildren and grows more precious the more it is handed down. The psalmist of Psalm 119 says it even more plainly: "Thy testimonies also are my delight and my counsellors." (Ps 119:24) "Jeshurun," יְשֻׁרוּן (Yəšurûn, H3484), appeared in Deuteronomy 32: Jeshurun grown fat will kick (Deut 32:15: "But Jeshurun waxed fat, and kicked"); but here, the mark of Jeshurun blessed is that he "was king in Jeshurun" — the LORD himself reigning in the midst. The same people: kicking, or blessed — it depends only on who the king is. If you are king yourself, you kick; if God is king, you are blessed. Brothers and sisters, the best words with which to bless a person are not "may all your wishes come true," but "may he be king in your midst."

## Part Two: The Blessing Comes to Each Tribe — God Knows His People by Name (Deut 33:6–25)

This section is the main body of the whole chapter. Moses blesses the tribes one by one, naming each, like a grandfather before leaving home calling his grandchildren to him one by one, laying his hand on each head and speaking. God's blessing has never been wholesale; it is by name.

### 1. For the fading, he asks that they live; for those in battle, he asks for help (Deut 33:6–7)

"Let Reuben live, and not die; and let not his men be few." (Deut 33:6) Reuben was the firstborn, yet through indulgence he lost the birthright (Gen 49:3–4, quoted above), and the tribe faded day by day. Moses' blessing is one short sentence only — not flourishing, only living. This one sentence is a great comfort to many people today: some blessings are not soaring success; they are "not die" — a marriage shall not die, faith shall not die, this little shop, this small group, this relationship shall not die. God hears prayers like that. Proverbs says: "The blessing of the LORD, it maketh rich, and he addeth no sorrow with it." (Prov 10:22) But Reuben's blessing is plainer still: to be alive is still to be inside God's story.

"And this is the blessing of Judah: and he said, Hear, LORD, the voice of Judah, and bring him unto his people: let his hands be sufficient for him; and be thou an help to him from his enemies." (Deut 33:7) Judah's blessing is help in the midst of battle. Judah afterwards indeed stood at the very front of the kingdom. The blessing for a person in battle is not "may you have no enemies," but "may he help you." If anyone among us is battling over a lawsuit, a project, an illness, this sentence is yours: your hand must fight, and his hand undergirds.

### 2. Levi: he blesses the faithful man, and he blesses his office (Deut 33:8–11)

"And of Levi he said, Let thy Thummim and thy Urim be with thy holy one, whom thou didst prove at Massah, and with whom thou didst strive at the waters of Meribah; Who said unto his father and to his mother, I have not seen him; neither did he acknowledge his brethren, nor knew his own children: for they have observed thy word, and kept thy covenant. They shall teach Jacob thy judgments, and Israel thy law: they shall put incense before thee, and whole burnt sacrifice upon thine altar. Bless, LORD, his substance, and accept the work of his hands" (Deut 33:8–11, quoted through the first half of v. 11, following the source's excerpting practice).

"Thummim," תֻּמִּים (Tummîm, H8550), and "Urim," אוּרִים (ʾÛrîm, H224), are the objects in the high priest's breastplate used for inquiring of God's will (Exod 28:30: "And thou shalt put in the breastplate of judgment the Urim and the Thummim; and they shall be upon Aaron's heart, when he goeth in before the LORD: and Aaron shall bear the judgment of the children of Israel upon his heart before the LORD continually."); their meanings are connected with "perfection" and "light," and their exact form has no settled conclusion ancient or modern — registered only, nothing fabricated. [3] "Thy holy one" is חָסִיד (ḥāsîd, H2623), from ḥesed, "lovingkindness, covenant love" — one who is loyal within the covenant and loved within it (according to BDB). [4] The reason Levi is blessed is not bloodline but faithfulness: when all Israel worshipped the golden calf, the Levites stood on Moses' side (Exod 32:26–29: "Then Moses stood in the gate of the camp, and said, Who is on the LORD'S side? let him come unto me. And all the sons of Levi gathered themselves together unto him. And he said unto them, Thus saith the LORD God of Israel, Put every man his sword by his side, and go in and out from gate to gate throughout the camp, and slay every man his brother, and every man his companion, and every man his neighbour. And the children of Levi did according to the word of Moses: and there fell of the people that day about three thousand men. For Moses had said, Consecrate yourselves to day to the LORD, even every man upon his son, and upon his brother; that he may bestow upon you a blessing this day."). "Who said unto his father and to his mother, I have not seen him" is not heartlessness; it is order: God's word stands above every human tie. The Lord Jesus carries the same line over to his disciples: "He that loveth father or mother more than me is not worthy of me: and he that loveth son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me." (Matt 10:37)

Levi's office has three parts: teaching, burning incense, and offering sacrifice — teaching God's word to men, and bringing men's prayers to God. The words of verse 11, "accept the work of his hands," answer across the distance to Moses' own prayer in Psalm 90: "And let the beauty of the LORD our God be upon us: and establish thou the work of our hands upon us; yea, the work of our hands establish thou it." (Ps 90:17) What Moses had prayed for himself, at the end of his life he also blesses out loud for Levi. You who serve, listen: the work of your hands — the lesson preparation, the visiting, the cleaning, the intercession that no one sees — God accepts it, and God establishes it. That is not a courtesy; it is Moses' last testament.

### 3. Benjamin's safety and Joseph's abundance (Deut 33:12–17)

"And of Benjamin he said, The beloved of the LORD shall dwell in safety by him; and the LORD shall cover him all the day long, and he shall dwell between his shoulders." (Deut 33:12) Benjamin was Jacob's youngest son, and his blessing is also the shortest, yet the most intimate: "cover him all the day long," "dwell between his shoulders" — like a father carrying a child on his shoulders. Afterwards God's house was in fact built on the borderland of Benjamin and Judah (Josh 18:11–28 records Benjamin's inheritance, with the temple mount lying along its southern edge; the details of the attribution are registered only, not adjudicated), and this tribe that "dwells between his shoulders" became the landlord of the place where God dwells. Safety, it turns out, is the position closest to God.

Joseph's blessing is the longest in the whole chapter: "And of Joseph he said, Blessed of the LORD be his land, for the precious things of heaven, for the dew, and for the deep that coucheth beneath, And for the precious fruits brought forth by the sun, and for the precious things put forth by the moon, And for the chief things of the ancient mountains, and for the precious things of the lasting hills, And for the precious things of the earth and fulness thereof, and for the good will of him that dwelt in the bush: let the blessing come upon the head of Joseph, and upon the top of the head of him that was separated from his brethren." (Deut 33:13–16) "Dew," טַל (ṭal, H2919), is the water on which the crops depended for survival in the dry season of Palestine (according to BDB). [5] Joseph's life was to be sold by his brothers, falsely accused by his master, and forgotten by the cupbearer; yet Moses heaps the richest blessing of all on his head, and calls him "him that was separated from his brethren." Separation is not isolation — it is being set apart in order to bless others. Jacob's blessing in Genesis 49 and this one confirm each other (Gen 49:22–26: "Joseph is a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough by a well; whose branches run over the wall: The archers have sorely grieved him, and shot at him, and hated him: But his bow abode in strength, and the arms of his hands were made strong by the hands of the mighty God of Jacob; (from thence is the shepherd, the stone of Israel:) Even by the God of thy father, who shall help thee; and by the Almighty, who shall bless thee with blessings of heaven above, blessings of the deep that lieth under, blessings of the breasts, and of the womb: The blessings of thy father have prevailed above the blessings of my progenitors unto the utmost bound of the everlasting hills: they shall be on the head of Joseph, and on the crown of the head of him that was separate from his brethren."). You who suffer, hear this well: the stretch of road on which you were separated from others is precisely the stretch of road on which blessing will be heaped; God did not waste one of Joseph's years, and he will not waste one of yours.

Verse 17 lands Joseph's blessing on the image of a warrior: "His glory is like the firstling of his bullock, and his horns are like the horns of unicorns: with them he shall push the people together to the ends of the earth: and they are the ten thousands of Ephraim, and they are the thousands of Manasseh." (Deut 33:17) Joseph's two sons are here named for the first time at the scale of "ten thousands" and "thousands" — the boy who was once sold into Egypt has now become the measure of multitudes. Notice the path of the blessing: it was not something Joseph fought for; it was something Moses spoke. And it was not spoken in a time of ease; it was spoken when Joseph was long gone from the world and Moses was also about to depart. Words of blessing have the power to cross time: they go on growing beyond the grave of the person they concern. The blessings parents speak over their children, the words elders leave to the young, often do not begin to bear fruit until after the speaker has departed. That is why we must speak more blessings while we are still here: the sentence you speak today may, on some night thirty years from now, become the strength by which your grandson stands up.

### 4. The remaining tribes, and the sentence that belongs to everyone (Deut 33:18–25)

Zebulun rejoices in his going out, and Issachar in his tents (Deut 33:18–19: "And of Zebulun he said, Rejoice, Zebulun, in thy going out; and, Issachar, in thy tents. They shall call the people unto the mountain; there they shall offer sacrifices of righteousness: for they shall suck of the abundance of the seas, and of treasures hid in the sand.") — the one who goes out and the one who stays at home each has his own joy; that is the fairness of blessing. And they will also "call the people unto the mountain; there they shall offer sacrifices of righteousness," drawing on "the abundance of the seas, and of treasures hid in the sand": Zebulun is by the sea, Issachar inland, and the blessings of the two tribes join into a single supply chain — the one by the sea calls the nations in, and the one in the tents offers the sacrifices of righteousness. The church should be the same: the outgoing and the quiet are not rivals; they are two organs of one body, and neither needs to envy the other's blessing. Gad's blessing carries a warrior's hardness: "he dwelleth as a lion, and teareth the arm with the crown of the head. And he provided the first part for himself, because there, in a portion of the lawgiver, was he seated" (Deut 33:20–21, excerpted). Gad chose the land east of the Jordan, the land of the battlefront; inheritance and responsibility were one and the same piece of ground. Of Dan the space is shortest, one short sentence only: "And of Dan he said, Dan is a lion's whelp: he shall leap from Bashan." (Deut 33:22) Shortest in space does not necessarily mean lightest in weight — in Genesis 49 Dan received a long blessing (Gen 49:16–18: "Dan shall judge his people, as one of the tribes of Israel. Dan shall be a serpent by the way, an adder in the path, that biteth the horse heels, so that his rider shall fall backward. I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD."); only when the two chapters are read together does a tribe's complete face come into view. Naphtali is "satisfied with favour, and full with the blessing of the LORD: possess thou the west and the south" (Deut 33:23); his land lay around Galilee, and afterwards, when the Lord Jesus came out to preach, it was exactly this land that received the light first, as was fulfilled (Isa 9:1–2: "Nevertheless the dimness shall not be such as was in her vexation, when at the first he lightly afflicted the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali, and afterward did more grievously afflict her by the way of the sea, beyond Jordan, in Galilee of the nations. The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light: they that dwell in the land of the shadow of death, upon them hath the light shined."; Matt 4:13–16: "And leaving Nazareth, he came and dwelt in Capernaum, which is upon the sea coast, in the borders of Zabulon and Nephthalim: That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying, The land of Zabulon, and the land of Nephthalim, by the way of the sea, beyond Jordan, Galilee of the Gentiles; The people which sat in darkness saw great light; and to them which sat in the region and shadow of death light is sprung up.").

Having read the whole list, please notice one thing: the length and the scale of the blessings are extremely uneven — Joseph takes five verses, Reuben only one sentence. But within this unevenness there is no favouritism, only office. What God measures out to each tribe is according to that tribe's road, its character, and its future need. Comparison is the fastest hand for stealing a blessing: if Reuben kept staring at Joseph's five verses, his own sentence, "live," would lose its sweetness; and if Joseph had not had the thirteen years of being sold, he could not have borne the weight of those five verses either. Your blessing is written under your name; someone else's portion, however thick, is not yours. Receive your own, give thanks for your own — that is the contentment Deuteronomy 33 teaches ordinary people.

Last comes Asher. Hidden in Asher's blessing is a sentence that may be the most widely transmitted in the whole chapter:

"And of Asher he said, Let Asher be blessed with children; let him be acceptable to his brethren, and let him dip his foot in oil. Thy shoes shall be iron and brass; and as thy days, so shall thy strength be." (Deut 33:24–25)

"as thy days, so shall thy strength be" — the original reads כְּיָמֶיךָ דֹּבְאֶךָ (kəyāmêkā dobeʾekā), and the last word, דֹּבֶא (dobeʾ, H1679), occurs in the Old Testament only here (a hapax); its meaning has been disputed from ancient times, interpreted variously as strength, rest, or abundance. The Chinese Union Version renders it "strength," and the Lü Zhenzhong version renders it, in the source's report, "your energy" (「你的日子如何，你的精力也必如何」 — registered as reported in the Chinese source; the Lü Zhenzhong wording was not verified word for word in this round); this translation follows the KJV's own wording, "strength" (and the Chinese base text likewise), and the alternative senses are registered only, not adjudicated. [6] But whatever the exact sense of the last word, the promise-structure of the whole sentence is clear: what God measures out to his children is never a lifetime's strength paid in advance; it is apportioned by the day — today's trouble, today's strength; tomorrow's will come with tomorrow morning's new mercies (Lam 3:22–23: "It is of the LORD'S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness."). Wiersbe, expounding this verse, reminds us that what God promises is not days without trouble, but strength equal to the days. [7] Take this sentence away with you today: the day you fear has not yet come, and fearing it is useless; when that day comes, the strength will arrive on time — earlier than the day itself.

**Ignition point (1)**: Brothers and sisters, Moses blessed by name — Reuben, Judah, Levi, Benjamin, Joseph, one by one. Today God also knows you by name. He knows which one is the fading Reuben, which one is Judah worn out by fighting, which one is Levi serving in silence, which one is Joseph forgotten by men. Never say again, "God cannot get to me": on his list of blessings, your name is written in his own hand. Today itself, according to your own circumstance, receive the sentence that belongs to you.

## Part Three: The Guarantee of the Blessing — The Eternal God Is Your Dwelling Place (Deut 33:26–29)

### 1. There is none like unto God (Deut 33:26)

"There is none like unto the God of Jeshurun, who rideth upon the heaven in thy help, and in his excellency on the sky." (Deut 33:26) When the blessings of the tribes have been spoken one by one, Moses draws the lens back to the whole people: the guarantee of the blessing does not lie in any single promise but in God himself. In the Psalms the psalmist asked, "Who is like unto the LORD our God, who dwelleth on high," (Ps 113:5); here Moses answers for all Israel: there is none like him. A nation that has walked in the wilderness for forty years is the best qualified to make this comparison — they had seen the gods of Egypt and the Baals of Canaan, and when the comparing was done, the conclusion was one sentence only: there is none like unto God.

### 2. Underneath are the everlasting arms (Deut 33:27–28)

"The eternal God is thy refuge, and underneath are the everlasting arms: and he shall thrust out the enemy from before thee; and shall say, Destroy them. Israel then shall dwell in safety alone: the fountain of Jacob shall be upon a land of corn and wine; also his heavens shall drop down dew." (Deut 33:27–28)

"Dwelling place" — the word rendered "refuge" in the KJV — in the original is מְעֹנָה (məʿōnâ, H4585), a dwelling, a place of refuge; and "the eternal God" in the original is אֱלֹהֵי קֶדֶם (ʾĕlōhê qedem), where qedem (H6924) is "from of old, from ancient times," so that modern punctuated Chinese editions render it "the eternal God," while the Lü Zhenzhong version renders it "the God of ancient times" (according to BDB). [8] Moses' prayer in Psalm 90 unfolds from exactly this sentence: "Lord, thou hast been our dwelling place in all generations." (Ps 90:1) A person's address changes many times in a lifetime — Moses himself changed his countless times: the basket on the Nile, Pharaoh's palace, the wilderness of Midian, the camp at Sinai. At the end he says: my true dwelling place was never any tent; it is God himself. "and underneath are the everlasting arms" — attend, please, to the direction of these words: not ahead of you pulling you, not behind you pushing you, but underneath you, holding you up. When you fall downward, the first thing you meet is not the ground; it is his arms. This and the eagle of chapter 32 bearing its young on its wings (Deut 32:11) are the same God and the same tenderness, a motif joined from head to tail. [9]

Verse 28 writes the everyday life of the blessed land: "Israel then shall dwell in safety alone: the fountain of Jacob shall be upon a land of corn and wine; also his heavens shall drop down dew." There is no war epic here — only corn, wine, and the dew that drops in the night. It turns out that when blessing lands, most of the time it is not spectacular: there is food on the table, wine in the cup, dew in the night, and people in the morning. Many believers imagine blessing as a great event, and then fail to recognise blessing in the ordinary day after day. Moses corrects us here: the four words "dwell in safety" are themselves the greatest blessing — how many nations, how many families, have sought exactly these words and could not obtain them. When you go home today, give thanks once for that ordinary table: that is Canaan; that is the evidence that the blessing has already landed.

### 3. Happy art thou (Deut 33:29)

"Happy art thou, O Israel: who is like unto thee, O people saved by the LORD, the shield of thy help, and who is the sword of thy excellency! and thine enemies shall be found liars unto thee; and thou shalt tread upon their high places." (Deut 33:29)

"Happy" in the original is אַשְׁרֵי (ʾašrê, H835) — the very word with which Psalm 1 opens: "Blessed is the man that walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly, nor standeth in the way of sinners, nor sitteth in the seat of the scornful." (Ps 1:1) Moses' blessing ends with "O people saved by the LORD" — the root of all the blessing is not that the people deserve it, but that they are "saved." Shield and sword, one for defence and one for attack, both carry the same sentence written on them: he is yours. When the blessing had been spoken to the end, Moses left the people no inventory of estates; he left them one identity only: a people saved by the LORD. Whoever enters Canaan carrying this identity has everything.

**Ignition point (2) · Closing call**: Brothers and sisters, this blessing today comes at last to its final question: are you willing to move into this dwelling place? Some people have believed in the Lord for many years, yet their address is still written on anxiety, written on the stock market's movements, written on other people's opinions. Moses says: the eternal God is your dwelling place, and underneath are the everlasting arms — right beneath the place where you are standing, lower than your fear, lower than your failure, lower even than your grave. No fall can pass through these arms. Today itself, not tomorrow: change your address. Those who are willing, please stand, and together with the prayer below let us formally move the dwelling place of this lifetime into him.

## Application to Six Groups of People

**1. Seekers — people who do not yet have a home.** You have drifted for years, changed cities, changed jobs, changed relationships, and the "dwelling place" in your heart has been empty all along. Today's message says one sentence to you only: there is a God of ancient times who is willing to be your dwelling place, and his arms are underneath you; he has waited for you a long time. You do not need to tidy yourself up first before moving in; even a fading one like Reuben is on the list of blessings, and so are you. The way to move in, the Bible calls believing: confess your sin, and receive Jesus Christ as Saviour. It can be today.

**2. New believers — people who have just moved in.** Please copy out Deut 33:25 and put it where you can see it: "Thy shoes shall be iron and brass; and as thy days, so shall thy strength be." Do not pay tomorrow's worries in advance, and do not overdraw today to carry tomorrow's hardships. The road of a new believer is walked day by day, and the dew drops night by night. You only need to sit at his feet today and receive the word; tomorrow's strength will be delivered on time tomorrow.

**3. Believers in the workplace — people in the midst of battle.** Judah's blessing is yours: "be thou an help to him from his enemies." For the battles of the workplace, God does not promise to route you around them; he promises to fight alongside you. Asher's shoes of iron and brass (in the Chinese base text, his bolts are of brass and iron — see the Finalization Record on the difference of wording and order between the versions) are for holding the boundary: your integrity, your Sabbath rest, your time with your family are the bolts of brass and iron, and they do not open at any price. Learn also from Gad: when you expand, first reserve the lawgiver's portion — the larger the map of your enterprise, the more you must leave God the first share.

**4. Parents — people who must learn from Moses to leave a deathbed inheritance.** What Moses left the next generation was not real estate; it was a string of blessings by name. From today, practise speaking one specific, by-name blessing over each of your children: not "all well, all at peace," but spoken according to that child's own circumstance — a Reuben kind, a Judah kind, a Joseph kind, one sentence for each. A child will forget many things you gave him, but the blessing spoken over his own name he will remember for a lifetime.

**5. The elderly — people walking in Moses' years.** Moses was one hundred and twenty years old, and his eye was not dim (Deut 34:7: "And Moses was an hundred and twenty years old when he died: his eye was not dim, nor his natural force abated."), and the last great thing he did in his old age was to bless the next generation. Your later years can be positioned in the same way: do not recount who owes you; only practise blessing people. What many young people in the church lack is exactly one serious blessing spoken from an elder's mouth. This Lord's Day, go and lay your hand on a young person's shoulder, and speak one sentence of Deuteronomy 33 to him.

**6. People who are suffering — people who feel forgotten.** Joseph was "him that was separated from his brethren," yet his blessing was the thickest; Reuben was left with only the word "live," yet he was not deleted from the list either. When the feeling of being forgotten is strongest, please re-read 33:27: his arms are underneath you — you feel that you are falling, and he says you are falling into the crook of his arm. Falling and being caught are two accounts of the same distance; Moses wants you to take the second.

## Closing Response Prayer

God of ancient times, eternal Lord, today we formally move our dwelling place into you. The strength you measure out to us is apportioned by the day; your arms are underneath us; your blessing comes to each one of us by name. The fading one, you cause to live; the one in battle, you make yourself his help; the one who serves, you accept the work of his hands; the one who suffers, you heap the thickest blessing on his head. When we leave this place, may everyone remember that there is a word of yours over his head. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotional use, 300–500 words)

Moses at the end of his life left no complaints; he left only blessing. He blessed each tribe by name: for the fading Reuben he asked only, "Let Reuben live, and not die"; for Judah in battle he asked for help; for faithful Levi he asked that God would accept the work of his hands; for Benjamin, the youngest, he asked to dwell in safety; and on Joseph, who had suffered every injustice, he heaped the richest blessing of all. The blessings differ in length, yet not one person is outside the list; your portion too has been measured out under your name and according to your road — there is no need to take someone else's five verses and despise your own one sentence. When he came to Asher, he spoke a sentence that has comforted countless generations: "Thy shoes shall be iron and brass; and as thy days, so shall thy strength be." God does not pay out a lifetime's strength in advance; he apportions it by the day: today's trouble, today's strength; tomorrow's will come with tomorrow morning's new mercies. The whole chapter closes at the highest place: "The eternal God is thy refuge, and underneath are the everlasting arms." Today, wherever you may fall, the first thing you meet is not the ground but his arms; and tonight, whichever bed you sleep in, your true dwelling place is himself. Please copy these two verses out and put them where you can see them today, and let them walk with you through this day.

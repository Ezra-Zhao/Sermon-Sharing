## Summary (About 100 Words)

Isaac is old and blind, and before he dies he wants to bless his beloved Esau. Rebekah overhears and urges Jacob to impersonate his brother: wearing goatskins, putting on Esau’s clothes, and carrying the savory food his mother has made, he deceives his father’s hands, nose, and ears, and receives the blessing of the firstborn. Esau returns with his game, only to learn that the blessing has already been taken, and he cries aloud, begging his father for another blessing, but receives only a tearful remainder. The father trembles, and Jacob flees. This sermon looks at three things: a blessing cannot be stolen, it can only be received; deceit won once and lost twenty years; and God’s purpose still moves forward amid human corruption, yet every person must settle his own account in full.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

Genesis chapter 27 is one of the most unsettling chapters in Genesis. There is no foreign enemy, no famine, no Pharaoh — only a family of four, inside one tent, carrying out a deception: the mother devises the plan, the son acts the part, the father is fooled, and by the time the other son returns, everything is already too late. As we read this chapter, none of us is clean enough to point a finger at anyone, because the sickness of this family is one we all know more or less: favoritism, comparison, using schemes to snatch the things of God, and treating blessing as merchandise that can be calculated.

Let us first read several key passages:

"And it came to pass, that when Isaac was old, and his eyes were dim, so that he could not see, he called Esau his eldest son, and said unto him, My son: and he said unto him, Behold, here am I." (Gen 27:1, excerpt, KJV)

"Now therefore, my son, obey my voice according to that which I command thee." (Gen 27:8, excerpt, KJV)

"And thou shalt bring it to thy father, that he may eat, and that he may bless thee before his death." (Gen 27:10, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob went near unto Isaac his father... And he came near, and kissed him: and he smelled the smell of his raiment, and blessed him." (Gen 27:27, excerpt, KJV)

"And when Esau heard the words of his father, he cried with a great and exceeding bitter cry, and said unto his father, Bless me, even me also, O my father. And he said, Thy brother came with subtilty, and hath taken away thy blessing." (Gen 27:34-35, excerpt, KJV)

"And Isaac trembled very exceedingly, and said... yea, and he shall be blessed." (cf. Gen 27:33, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, this chapter exposes the hands in our hearts that steal blessings. Make us honest: help us see clearly the cost of deceit, and also see that Your purpose is not annulled by men; teach us not to rely on deception or snatching, but to wait for and receive the blessing You give with Your own hand. In Jesus’ name, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Favoritism Planted the Fuse First — The Blind Father and the Mother Listening at the Tent Wall (27:1-17)

The story begins with Isaac’s eyes: old, dim, unable to see. This detail is not merely a physical note. Chapter 25 had already written this family’s medical record: Isaac loved Esau, because he did eat of his venison; but Rebekah loved Jacob (Gen 25:28). One household, two children, each parent loving one — favoritism is the earliest fuse of this tragedy. Before the blessing was ever stolen, this home had already been divided by favoritism into two camps: father and Esau, joined by taste; mother and Jacob, joined by scheming. Brothers and sisters, favoritism is no small matter; it cuts in two, at the dinner table, the blessing God gave to the whole family.

Isaac wants to bless Esau before he dies, and he handles it like a private transaction: he calls Esau in, whispers his instructions — go hunt, make savory food, "that my soul may bless thee before I die." Notice, he does not gather the whole family, he does not call Rebekah, he does not call Jacob. Why so secretive? Because in his heart he knows clearly that, according to God’s oracle, this blessing should not be given this way — while Jacob and Esau were still in the womb, God had already said: the elder shall serve the younger (Gen 25:23). Isaac is not ignorant; he wants to use taste to overturn the oracle: what I love to eat is in my mouth, so my blessing shall fall on the one I love. It is not frightening for a man to grow old and blind in his eyes; what is frightening is blindness of heart: knowing full well what God has said, yet still arranging a private ceremony to try to make it a fait accompli. How many families, how many churches, have begun their corruption with exactly this kind of "while there is still time... hurry up and..."

And Rebekah was listening outside the tent. The text says Rebekah heard — she had been listening all along. As soon as she heard, she called Jacob over, repeated it word for word, and added her own plan. Her plan is as precise as a battle plan: two good kids of the goats, which she will make into savory food; Esau’s best clothes, which are right there in the house, for Jacob to wear; and since Jacob’s hands and neck are smooth, the goatskins will be put on them. Jacob raises only one concern, and that concern tells us a great deal: my brother is a hairy man and I am a smooth man; if my father feels me, it will be exposed, and "I shall bring a curse upon me, and not a blessing." What he fears is not doing evil, but being exposed; what he calculates is not right and wrong, but risk. Rebekah’s answer pushes a mother’s favoritism to its peak: "Upon me be thy curse, my son: only obey my voice." These words sound great, but they are in fact frightening: she is willing to bear the curse in her son’s place, yet she still pushes her son into the deception. In order to "help God fulfill His oracle," she chooses to stop at nothing. Here lies the deepest lesson of this chapter: God’s oracle is true — the elder shall serve the younger; but using deceit to fulfill God’s purpose is still deceit. However spiritual the end may be, it cannot bleach the means. After this, Rebekah never appears again in Genesis. When she sent her beloved son away she said, stay a few days — she never imagined that this parting would be forever, and that she would never see Jacob again before she died. The account for deceit is collected first from the one who devised the plan.

The kids are slaughtered, the savory food is made, the clothes are put on, the skins are wrapped on. Jacob, carrying the bread and the savory food, walks into his father’s tent. A play begins.

Before leaving the tent, there is one more line we must not miss: when Rebekah instructs Jacob, she says, go, fetch me from the flock two good kids of the goats. Note the word "good" kids — even a forgery must use the finest materials. Deceit never saves on cost; what it saves on is honesty. Jacob’s whole outfit is genuine in every item: real goatskin, real clothes, real savory food, real cooking — the only fake thing is one thing: identity. This is precisely where deception is hardest to guard against: nine parts true, one part false, and the false part hidden inside the true. Isaac’s nose, hands, and tongue were all checking materials, and the materials were all real; the one thing he never checked to the bottom was who the person in front of him was. Brothers and sisters, how many deceptions today follow this same ratio: the documents are real, the data are real, the story is real, and what is fake is only the motive and the identity. The lesson in guarding against deception is not to sharpen the senses further, but to quiet the heart further: quiet enough to hear the voice that was already heard correctly, and not in a hurry to let desire decide in place of evidence. If Isaac had stopped at that moment of "the voice is Jacob’s voice," asked one more question, waited a little longer, waited for the real Esau to return and confront him, there would have been no tears in this chapter. Sadly, he was hungry, and he was in a hurry to finish the matter before he died — hurry is deceit’s best accomplice. The more we rush a decision with "while we still can" and "before...", the more we must remind ourselves: the things of God never need stolen time to be fulfilled; what needs stolen time is mostly our own selfish desire.

Consider also Jacob’s hand carrying the dish. In chapter 25 Scripture introduces him as "grasping the heel"; in chapter 27 his hand is wrapped in goatskin; from chapter 28 onward his hands will crack with cold and be oppressed in Laban’s flocks. The hand is the thread running through Jacob’s whole life story: the grasping hand, the deceiving hand, the laboring hand, the hand that held on and would not let go in wrestling (Gen 32), until in old age those same hands would be laid on the heads of Joseph’s two sons, crossed in blessing, placing the younger before the elder (Gen 48) — that time, he deceived no one; awake and openly, he blessed according to God’s heart. The same pair of hands, used to steal in youth, used in old age to bestow according to God’s will. When God repairs a person, He does not discard even the hand that sinned; He remakes it into a vessel of blessing. This is a ray of light shining through the darkness of chapter 27: the hands you have done wrong with today do not need to be cut off; give them to God, and He has a way to lay them one day on the heads of others as hands of blessing.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Three Questions, Three Answers, and One Hand — How the Deception Passed Through the Senses (27:18-29)

Jacob enters the tent, and his first sentence is a lie: "My father... I am Esau thy firstborn." Isaac’s first reaction is suspicion: My son, how is it that thou hast found it so quickly? Jacob’s answer is the most jarring sentence in the whole chapter: "Because the LORD thy God brought it to me." He borrows the name of God to stamp his lie. Brothers and sisters, this is the heaviest layer of deceit: not merely deceiving his father, but using God as cover. Wrapping calculation in pious words, covering schemes with spiritual language — this trick is still in use four thousand years later: how many unclean things have been done under the banner of "God’s leading." God’s name being misused is more serious than the venison being falsely claimed.

Isaac does not fully believe him. He says: Come near, my son, that I may feel thee, whether thou be my very son Esau or not. Jacob comes near, and his father feels him and speaks that immortal line: "The voice is Jacob’s voice, but the hands are the hands of Esau." (27:22, KJV) The hearing tells the truth, the touch tells a lie; the old man chooses to believe the hand. The hand feels goatskin, while the voice heard is his son’s own voice. When a person wants to believe something, his senses fight each other, and he will always pick the one that pleases him to believe. Isaac wants to bless Esau, so the hand wins and the ear loses. This is not merely the dullness of old age; it is the normal state of every one of us: we are not persuaded by evidence, we are persuaded by desire, and then we gather evidence to match.

He asks a third time: "Art thou my very son Esau?" Jacob says: "I am." Then he eats and drinks, and says again: Come near now, my son, and kiss me. The father smells — on the clothes is the scent of the field, for they are Esau’s clothes; the goatskin is the hand, the clothes are the smell, the savory food is the taste. By this moment, of the five senses — ear, eye (already dim), hand, nose, mouth — four have been bought by props; only the ear had protested once, and he himself had pressed it down. The deception passes through the senses as if through an unguarded land. Then the blessing comes out of his mouth.

We must listen carefully to the content of the blessing, for this is what Esau later weeps for and can never get back: "Therefore God give thee of the dew of heaven, and the fatness of the earth, and plenty of corn and wine: Let people serve thee, and nations bow down to thee: be lord over thy brethren, and let thy mother’s sons bow down to thee: cursed be every one that curseth thee, and blessed be he that blesseth thee." (Gen 27:28-29, excerpt, KJV) This is the measure of a patriarchal blessing: dew of heaven and fatness of earth are inheritance, peoples and nations are authority, being lord over his brethren is the status of the firstborn, and the final line echoes God’s promise to Abraham. A blessing here is not a pleasant, auspicious remark — in the patriarch’s world, words from a father’s mouth are a declaration carrying authority and effect; once spoken, they stand. This is exactly why Isaac later "trembled very exceedingly," and exactly why, once this blessing was stolen, even the father himself could not take it back. The weight of words is the physics of this chapter: a spoken blessing is like water poured out; Esau’s tears cannot catch it, and the father’s regret cannot gather it back.

Jacob has barely stepped out of the tent after receiving the blessing when Esau returns. Scripture’s narration times it to the second: one goes out, one comes in; one walks out with an empty dish, one walks in with hot venison. From this day on, Jacob begins to learn another lesson for the rest of his life: a man who has deceived to obtain something can never slow his steps again.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: Weeping and Trembling — Esau Begs for the Blessing That Is Left (27:30-46)

Esau prepares his venison and brings it to his father, saying respectfully: Let my father arise, and eat of his son’s venison, that thy soul may bless me. With one sentence Isaac tears open the heavens: "Who art thou?" Esau says: I am thy son, thy firstborn Esau. The text says Isaac "trembled very exceedingly" — this trembling is double: the old man discovers he has been deceived by his own son, and discovers that his own attempt to secretly change the arrangement of heaven’s decree has been intercepted by the decree itself. He then speaks the sentence that sets the tone: Thy brother came with subtilty [1], and hath taken away thy blessing [2]... I have blessed him, yea, and he shall be blessed. Isaac does not say, "That does not count, let us start over." In his world, once a blessing has gone out of the mouth, it belongs to the one who received it, and even the one who blessed has no right to revoke it. In his trembling, he submits to a fact: through this ugly deception, God’s hand has still placed the blessing on the person to whom the oracle pointed. Man can corrupt the process, but he cannot annul God’s purpose; but note carefully — God’s fulfillment of His purpose in no way means God approves the means. This chapter separates these two things very clearly, and Jacob’s twenty-year account that follows is the evidence.

Then comes one of the most heartbreaking pleas in Scripture. Esau "lifted up his voice, and wept" — in the original, a great and exceedingly bitter cry, tse’aqah — a loud wailing [3]. He says: Bless me, even me also, O my father! Pleading and asking, he asks a piercing question: "Hast thou but one blessing, my father?" In Esau’s understanding, his father’s blessing is like stock in a warehouse: take one portion away, and there should still be another. But a patriarch’s blessing is not stock; the firstborn’s portion is one only. Hebrews later delivers its verdict on Esau: for one morsel of meat he sold his birthright; afterward, when he would have inherited the blessing, he was rejected, "for he found no place of repentance, though he sought it carefully with tears" (Heb 12:16-17, excerpt) [4]. The bowl of red stew is the cause, the weeping in the tent is the effect; what was despised that day cannot be bought back later even with weeping. Brothers and sisters, spiritual things have their seasons: to despise them while there is still time, and to weep when they are gone, is man’s most common and most useless regret.

In the end Isaac does give Esau a word: Behold, thy dwelling shall be the fatness of the earth, and of the dew of heaven from above; and by thy sword shalt thou live, and shalt serve thy brother; and it shall come to pass when thou shalt have the dominion, that thou shalt break his yoke from off thy neck (27:39-40, paraphrased excerpt) [5]. Is this a blessing? It is more like a prophecy: there is land, there is a sword, there is service, and there is also a thread of hope of breaking free. Esau receives this "remainder," but from then on a murderous intent is planted in his heart: The days of mourning for my father are at hand; then will I slay my brother Jacob. For a person whose blessing has been stolen, if the wound is not healed, weeping will be simmered into murderous rage — this is the chapter’s word to victims: your grievance is real, and God remembers your tears; but if you let bitterness grow into hatred, the one who stole the blessing has destroyed you once, and you will destroy yourself a second time with your own hands.

There is another account to be entered under Esau’s name, and for every one of us as well: his word of hatred against Jacob waits for the days of mourning for his father — he schedules his murderous intent, hiding it behind the days of filial mourning. If hatred in the human heart is not dealt with, it will quietly queue up like this, waiting for a "suitable" moment. God in the end did not let this murder happen; He first cooled the knife with twenty years of separation, and then slowly melted it with Jacob’s humility and gifts, and with Esau’s own prosperity; by Genesis 33, when the brothers meet, Esau runs to meet him, embraces his neck, and kisses him — that is a later story. But note: reconciliation was possible because in between lay years and lessons arranged by God Himself. Time itself does not heal people; God’s work within time heals people.

When Rebekah hears the murderous intent, she quickly sends Jacob to flee to Haran to her brother Laban. As he leaves, she changes her story with Isaac: I am weary of my life because of the daughters of Heth: if Jacob take a wife of the daughters of Heth, what good shall my life do me? (27:46, paraphrased) — she uses the topic of daughters-in-law as cover, and presents the flight as matchmaking. Isaac indeed follows suit, calls Jacob, formally blesses him, and sends him to Padan-aram. This time the blessing is given by a father who is awake and open (Gen 28:1-4). Beyond the portion stolen by deceit, God lets Jacob, at the doorway of his home, receive Abraham’s blessing once more from his father’s mouth — not stolen, but given as a farewell. Jacob leaves home carrying two blessings, and also carrying twenty years of exile: under Laban, he will be treated with the same deceit — his bride switched, his wages changed ten times. The deceiver must taste what it is to be deceived; the one who stole a blessing must learn in a foreign land over twenty years: a blessing can only be received, it cannot be stolen.

We must also read this chapter together with Hebrews’ retelling to read it fully. Hebrews 11:20 says: "By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning things to come." Is that not wonderful? This blessing, born of favoritism and hijacked by human deceit, is nevertheless recorded by Hebrews on the account of faith. Why? Because after his trembling, Isaac did not overturn the blessing that had gone out — he acknowledged it: in this ugly event, God’s hand had still placed the blessing on the person to whom the oracle pointed, and his faith was shown in afterward submitting to the accomplished fact, without bargaining with God. And Hebrews 12, on Esau, treats the other side: the one who despised the birthright cannot beg it back even with weeping. The two passages side by side are the two doors of one chapter: on one door is written, God’s purpose is not annulled by men; on the other is written, man’s contempt must pay a real price. Those who read only one door fall either into cheap grace or into hopeless fatalism; walking through both doors together is the right path of Genesis 27.

Say one more word for Jacob, which is also for ourselves. Jacob lives through three name-stages in his life: the heel-grasper, the deceiver, and the one who wrestles with God. Chapter 27 is the definitive work of his "deceiver" title, yet God does not abandon him in this chapter. Why? Not because his means are lovely, but because God’s election precedes his performance: while still in the womb, God had already said the elder shall serve the younger, when he had not yet even cooked a bowl of red stew. Election is not a reward for deceit; election is a track of grace that God lays down apart from human corruption. But note the direction of the track: God does not let Jacob, clutching the stolen blessing, walk straight into Canaan to enjoy it; the track first turns toward Haran — into twenty years of being deceived, oppressed, and having his wages changed. Grace does not cancel discipline, and election does not exempt from lessons. In Laban’s tents Jacob will recognize, again and again, what it feels like when his own methods of that year are used on him by another: the bride switched, just as the brother was switched; the wages changed ten times, just as the blessing was intercepted. This is the hand of God at work: He fulfills His purpose and He repairs His vessel, and He uses the same stretch of time for both.

Finally, return to the quietest person in that tent — Isaac. He felt, he smelled, he tasted, he erred, he trembled, and then he spoke the weightiest sentence: I have blessed him, yea, and he shall be blessed. At this moment the old man finally stops wrestling with God. What he wanted to give Esau he can no longer give; what God wanted to give Jacob, he confirms. A man who had wrestled with his own favoritism for decades lets go in his trembling. Brothers and sisters, this is also the door this chapter leaves for parents: what you have arranged for your child may not be what God has arranged for your child; when the two conflict and God’s intention has become clear, letting go in acknowledgment shows more faith than clinging to the end. Isaac’s greatness lies not in his never having erred, but in his, after erring, acknowledging the hand of God.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: those who are scheming right now. In your hands is a precise plan, all the props are ready, and all that remains is that the father cannot feel the difference. I beg you to hear the lesson of Jacob walking out the door: the moment of stealing is the proudest moment, and from that moment the bill begins to accrue interest. Twenty years in a foreign land, a deceived wedding, wages changed ten times — the interest on deceit is far heavier than you think. Turn back; there is still time to stop at the doorway of the tent.

The second kind: those who bleach their means with God’s words. "Has God not promised?" "Was the elder not meant to serve the younger anyway!" The oracle is true, and God will surely fulfill it — but He does not want you to help Him with lies. Whenever a so-called "will of God" can only be accomplished if you lie, impersonate, or cross a boundary, stop first: that is most likely your own desire, wearing Esau’s clothes.

The third kind: parents who favor one child. Isaac loved venison, Rebekah loved Jacob, and favoritism cut the blessing in two and pushed the children into being rivals. Every partiality at your dinner table today is planting a fuse for a tent twenty years from now. Ask God to give you eyes that are not dim: both children are people who are to receive blessing.

The fourth kind: those with Esau-like regret. You once despised something precious — faith, marriage, calling, birthright — and when you wanted to turn back, it was no longer where it had been. Esau’s tears tell us: some doors close when the season is missed, so do not despise what is still in your hands today; at the same time, Esau later also had his own land and descendants, and God did not let his story end with only weeping. Your story, too, need not stop at weeping.

The fifth kind: those whose blessing was stolen. Your portion was taken away by unclean hands — credit, position, opportunity. God sees, and you may weep, and you may ask the Father. But do not simmer weeping into murderous rage: bitterness is a poison you drink yourself. Leave vindication to God, and walk out your road ahead; even Esau had a day of breaking the yoke.

The sixth kind: those who fear there is not enough blessing to go around. Esau asked: Hast thou but one blessing? In Christ, the answer has changed: blessing is not stock in a warehouse, where taking one portion leaves one less. He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things (Rom 8:32, paraphrased)? You do not need to steal another’s, nor to snatch your brother’s — your portion the Father has long stored up for you, not in a tent, but in His hand.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Responsive Prayer

In this chapter today, four people, four conditions: the blind father trembled, the mother who listened at the wall sent her son away forever, the one who stole the blessing fled with the blessing, and the one who wept embraced the remainder and planted murderous intent. God’s purpose did not fall empty, yet not one human account was recorded any less. This is the honesty of Genesis: it covers no one’s shame, and lets no one’s shame annul God’s plan; it records tears and trembling, and it records God’s unfailing purpose, so that every family struggling in a tent in later generations can find here both its own shadow and its way out.

Let us pray together: Lord who gives blessing, we confess that in all our hearts there is a pair of Jacob’s hands, wanting to bypass waiting, bypass order, and get the blessing into our hands first. Forgive our calculations that misuse Your name, heal our Esau-like bitterness, and brighten our Isaac-like dim eyes. Teach us to believe: Your blessing can only be received, it cannot be stolen; what is ours, You will surely give with Your own hand; what is not ours cannot be kept even if stolen. Let us be quiet before You, waiting for You to open Your mouth and bless. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] mirmah: Hebrew מִרְמָה (H4820), deceit, fraud, treachery; in Gen 27:35 Isaac’s words "Thy brother came with subtilty" refer to Jacob receiving the blessing by impersonation; for the meaning see BDB, HALOT. The exposition in Part Two of this manuscript on "using God as cover" is based on Jacob’s answer in 27:20, and is noted here as sermon application.

[2] berakhah: Hebrew בְּרָכָה (H1293), blessing, gift of blessing; the pivotal word of Genesis 27 throughout, seen repeatedly in the dialogue of Isaac and Esau (e.g. 27:12, 34-36, 41); for the meaning see BDB, HALOT. This manuscript’s statement that "a blessing is like water poured out; once spoken, it stands" is an exegetical summary based on the narrative of this chapter (27:33, Isaac does not revoke it) and the tradition of patriarchal blessing, not the lexical meaning itself.

[3] tse’aqah: Hebrew צְעָקָה (H6818), cry, outcry, wailing; Gen 27:34 records that Esau "cried with a great and exceeding bitter cry"; for the meaning see BDB, HALOT.

[4] On Hebrews 12:16-17’s verdict on Esau: Hebrews takes Esau’s selling of his birthright, and his later seeking with tears yet finding no place for a change of mind, as a warning, to be read together with Heb 11:20, "By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning things to come" — the former treats Esau’s contempt, the latter treats Isaac’s blessing as from faith; Part Three of this manuscript accordingly handles the two lines of the father and of Esau separately.

[5] On the rendering of Genesis 27:39-40: the Hebrew preposition min can be partitive ("from / of") or separative ("away from"). The KJV, like the traditional Chinese Union Version, takes the partitive sense — "thy dwelling shall be the fatness of the earth, and of the dew of heaven from above"; the NIV, ESV and other modern versions take the separative sense — "away from the earth's richness." The Korean (개역개정), Hindi (BSI OV) and Vietnamese (BTT 1934) versions used by the companion translations read the separative sense at this verse. This is a well-known crux of translation (see Keil & Delitzsch, among others), not a contradiction in the text, and the preacher should make the congregation aware of it.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "The voice is Jacob's voice, but the hands are the hands of Esau." (Genesis 27:22) Isaac was old and his eyes were dim. He felt with his hands, smelled with his nose, and tasted with his mouth, and every sense was fooled by the props; only his ears heard rightly, and he did not obey them. Jacob stole the blessing by deceit, but from that day began twenty years of exile, in which he was himself deceived in the very same ways. A blessing can be stolen for a moment but never kept for a lifetime; deceit wins once, yet its interest is charged by the decade. We too carry hands that want to steal blessings — bypassing the waiting, the order, and God's timing. This verse warns us twice over: do not be Jacob — what is yours, God will place in your hands Himself, for His blessing can only be received, never stolen; and do not be Isaac — when senses and desires conflict, heed the voice that is right instead of believing what you wish to believe. Withdraw your hands, quiet your heart, and wait for the God who is never deceived and never deceives, to bless you in His own time. Remember also Isaac's words after his trembling: I blessed him, and indeed he will be blessed. The old man let go of his struggle with his own favoritism and acknowledged the hand of God. Parents, and all who are anxious about their own future, may take comfort here: once God's intention has become clear, letting go in acknowledgment takes more faith than clinging to our own arrangements.

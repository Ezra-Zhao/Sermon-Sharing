# The Willing Heart

Preacher: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries

Status: Reviewed and published (passed cross-review on 2026-10-23: Gemini independent review + two self-review rounds; see review record, group X). Translated from the Chinese original.
Review (2026-10-23): Passed cross-review (group X); corrected during review: "rams' skins dyed red" (35:7), "Oholiab" in narrative, and note 4 verse reference 35:27.

Scripture: Exodus 35:1-35 (KJV)

Connection: In Exodus 34, Moses came down the mountain a second time bearing the newly cut tablets, his face shining, and God renewed His covenant with Israel in grace. Entering Exodus 35, the covenant people are to put that grace into concrete practice — yet the first thing is not to start work, but to reaffirm the Sabbath.

Summary: Moses assembled the whole congregation, first reaffirming the Sabbath, then calling on the willing-hearted to bring offerings, and then restating the call of the craftsmen. After the congregation departed, men and women came together; women spun thread with their own hands, the leaders brought gems and spices, and Bezalel and Oholiab, filled with the Spirit of God, not only worked themselves but could also teach others. This chapter shows that the tabernacle was not built by levies, but by a people at rest who, with willing hearts, offered all they had to God and passed their gifts on to others.

## I. Before Starting Work, First Hear the Sabbath (00:00-08:00)

Brothers and sisters, as we come to Exodus 35, notice first the order of Moses' words. The text says Moses gathered all the congregation of the children of Israel together, and said unto them, These are the words which the LORD hath commanded, that ye should do them. Israel had just passed through the failure of the golden calf, and had just passed through the mercy of the renewed covenant. Before them lay a great project, the building of the tabernacle. By human logic, since the project was urgent, a leader's first word should have been the assignment of tasks, his second the schedule, and his third an urging to hand over the materials. But Moses' first word was none of these. He said, Six days shall work be done, but on the seventh day there shall be to you an holy day, a sabbath of rest to the LORD: whosoever doeth work therein shall be put to death. Ye shall kindle no fire throughout your habitations upon the sabbath day.

This order is no idle stroke; it is the theological skeleton of the whole chapter. God did not let Israel prove their repentance by busyness, nor redeem the sin of the golden calf by a project. After a person stands again in grace, the first lesson is still to stop. Rest is not a reward after the project is finished; it is the foundation before the project begins. If a person does not first learn to rest before God, he will turn offering into anxiety, service into competition, and the tabernacle into another stage for displaying himself. God set the seventh day before them first, to make them confess that neither time nor life is of their own making: the six days of work are received, and the ceasing of the seventh day is also received.

This passage also forms an inclusio with chapter 31. In chapter 31, after God had finished speaking of the furnishings of the tabernacle and the priestly garments, and had designated Bezalel and Oholiab, He immediately reaffirmed the Sabbath, as if deliberately inserting a pause into the very middle of all the instructions. Now in chapter 35, as Moses is about to put the instructions into practice, he again speaks first of the Sabbath. There is rest before the instructions, and rest before their execution. The two passages echo each other, reminding us that the Sabbath is not an incidental rule attached to the tabernacle project, but the central order of the life of worship. The tabernacle is the sign that God dwells among His people; the Sabbath is the sign that the people acknowledge God as Lord in their time. One concerns space, the other time, and together they declare that God will dwell among His people and will also rule their days.

Verse 3 says, Ye shall kindle no fire throughout your habitations upon the sabbath day. This sentence is a supplement found nowhere else in all the Sabbath regulations; other texts on the Sabbath do not restate it in the same words. Whether its literal scope means every act of kindling, or specifically kindling for cooking or for work, has long been discussed in scholarship. The Samaritan tradition has its own transmission and understanding of this commandment; rabbinic interpretation, reading it in context, often places the emphasis on fire used in connection with work; and the Karaite reading insists more strictly on a literal, comprehensive prohibition, so that even a fire lit beforehand is not kept on the Sabbath. Each reading has its grounds; here we set them side by side honestly and draw no final conclusion. But whatever the debated scope, the direction of the sentence is clear: it carries rest from public worship into the dwellings and daily life, so that Israel had to arrange even the preparations at home in advance. Rest is not reluctantly stopping when the seventh day arrives; it is learning trust from the sixth day onward.

For us today, this is an equally sharp reminder. We easily take ceaseless busyness as faithfulness, and exhausting ourselves as love for the Lord. But God's word asks first not how much you can do, but whether you are willing to stop and confess that He is God. A person who cannot rest often does not have too much to do; he does not believe that after he stops, God still works. The tabernacle project began in just such rest. God's house is not built by human anxiety, but by a people whom God has first settled.

There is another layer worth pausing over. The roots of the Sabbath reach in one direction to creation — God worked six days and rested on the seventh — and in the other direction to the Exodus — God brought His people out of the house of bondage in Egypt with a mighty hand. Creation shows that man is not a machine, and work is not the whole of human existence; the Exodus shows that those who were once slaves may now stop, because their master has changed. Pharaoh only said, Go and work, and the tale of bricks shall not be diminished; the LORD said, Six days shall work be done, but on the seventh day ye shall rest. Two voices, two worlds. If Israel did not first come out of Pharaoh's rhythm in their time, then even though their bodies had left Egypt, their hearts still lived in Egypt. Before building the tabernacle, Moses reaffirmed the Sabbath precisely so that this freed people would first learn to stop freely, and only then offer freely and work freely. A heart still enslaved will hand over what it has only out of fear; a heart set free can offer all it has out of willingness. This is why chapter 35 begins with the Sabbath.

## II. Not a Levy, but a Willing Offering (08:00-24:00)

Only after speaking of the Sabbath did Moses speak of the offering. The text says Moses spake unto all the congregation of the children of Israel, saying, This is the thing which the LORD commanded, saying, Take ye from among you an offering unto the LORD: whosoever is of a willing heart, let him bring it, an offering of the LORD. Note the verbs and the tone here: what Moses conveyed was not a levy, but an appeal. He did not say how much each household must pay, did not assign quotas by tribe, and did not drive people by shame or comparison. He drew the line very clearly: the gift must come from a willing heart. In its Hebrew setting, this willingness is not an emotion squeezed out under pressure, nor a momentary impulse of zeal, but a heart inwardly moved, willing to present what it has. If an offering leaves willingness behind, it is corrupted. It may become a transaction, in which a person tries to exchange a gift for God's favour; or it may become a performance, in which a person tries to prove his spirituality by the size of his gift. Moses blocked both roads from the start.

Then comes a concrete list: gold, and silver, and brass, and blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine linen, and goats' hair, and rams' skins dyed red, and badgers' skins, and shittim wood, and oil for the light, and spices for anointing oil, and for the sweet incense, and onyx stones, and stones to be set for the ephod, and for the breastplate. Reading this list, we discover that it coincides almost item for item with the list in chapter 25, verses 2 to 7. Chapter 25 was the command God gave Moses on the mountain; at that time the list was still within the instructions, and the people had not yet heard it. Now, in chapter 35, the same list, read by Moses to the whole congregation, changes from a list of command into a list of response. This is a structural observation, and its levels must be distinguished: at the textual level, it shows that the building that follows is not a fresh human invention, but a faithful execution of the earlier instructions; at the theological level, it shows that God's word has not fallen empty — the command once heard only on the mountain is now, in the camp, to become a reality in which the people take part with their own hands. One list, two positions, with the fall of the golden calf and the grace of the renewed covenant between them, shows precisely that God's word withstands human failure and still moves forward.

In the list there is precious gold, silver, and gems, and also ordinary goats' hair and wood. There are treasures brought out of Egypt, and materials obtainable in wilderness life. God placed them on the same list, showing that the value of an offering cannot be measured by price alone. Gold is indeed precious, but goats' hair, if it comes from a willing heart, likewise enters the tabernacle. One person can offer gems, another only wood; one has spices, another oil; each has its place in God's plan. The beauty of the tabernacle was not piled up out of one material; it was the whole congregation, each bringing what he had and setting it together, that became complete worship.

The word for the gift here is, in Hebrew, terumah. Discussion of this word in the notes focuses on its relation to the action of lifting up and presenting. Traditionally it is understood as a gift lifted up and presented to God: a person lifts up a portion of what belongs to him and places it in God's hand. This word reminds us that offering is not throwing away what is surplus, but solemnly presenting upward what was once held in one's own hand. In presenting it, a person confesses that these materials were never created by himself in the first place, and that even his ability to offer was received from God. So the first beneficiary of an offering is not God, as if God lacked something, but the one who offers: as he opens his hand, he learns who the true Master is.

When the church speaks of offering today, it most easily falls into two errors. One error is to speak only of amounts and not of the heart, as if what God cared most about were the total. The other error is to speak only of intention and not of action, as if willingness stayed at the level of feeling and never needed to produce concrete time, money, and skill. Chapter 35 puts the two together: there is willingness, and there is a list; there is inward moving, and there is real gold, silver, fine linen, and wood. True willingness will always be carried out in action, and true action must always begin with willingness. What God delights in is neither a gift driven out by pressure, nor an intention that remains mere words, but an offering willing in heart and actually brought in hand.

We should also note that Moses did not rank those who offered. The list sets gold beside goats' hair, and gems beside shittim wood, and that in itself is a declaration: in God's project, the price of the material cannot decide the rank of the giver. If the man who offers gold does so out of pride, his gold is no weightier before God than goats' hair offered willingly; if the man who offers goats' hair earnestly brings the little he has, his portion is fully accepted. It is the same in the church today: one can give a large sum of money, another only a limited income; one gives an afternoon of a weekend, another the professional experience of many years. Moses' appeal left a place for everyone, and left everyone his dignity, because what he called for was not an amount, but willing-hearted people. Amounts can be counted; willingness must first be discerned before God.

## III. Whoever Is Wise-Hearted, Let Him Come and Make (24:00-42:00)

Moses went on to say, And every wise hearted among you shall come, and make all that the LORD hath commanded. This sentence carries the offering forward from giving materials to giving skill. The tabernacle project needed not only people to provide things, but people to work with their hands, their wisdom, and crafts practiced over many years. The wisdom here is not abstract cleverness, nor degrees and titles, but the ability, in concrete work, to discern, to design, and to finish. Scripture calls this ability being wise-hearted, meaning that wisdom is not only in the head but also in the heart, where a person's will, judgment, and craft are joined as one. If a craftsman has only technique and no reverence, what he makes may be only a product; if he has only enthusiasm and no craft, his good intention cannot land anywhere real. God calls those who are wise-hearted and who are willing to come before Him.

Moses then listed the main line of the project and its furnishings: the tabernacle and its tent, its covering, its taches and boards, its bars, pillars, and sockets; the ark and its staves, the mercy seat, and the vail of the covering; the table and its vessels and the shewbread; the candlestick and its vessels, its lamps, and the oil for the light; the incense altar and its staves, the anointing oil and the sweet incense; the hanging for the door of the tabernacle; the altar of burnt offering and its brasen grate, its staves and vessels; the laver and its foot; the hangings of the court, its pillars and sockets, and the hanging for the door of the court; the pins of the tabernacle and the pins of the court and their cords; and finally the cloths of service, the holy garments for Aaron the priest, and the garments of his sons to minister in the priest's office. With this long list we take only the main line; there is no need to unfold it item by item here. It corresponds to the instructions of chapters 25 to 28 and to the call in chapter 31, showing that chapter 35 is the beginning of the execution section: what was heard before is here to be made, piece by piece. We touch on it and stop, because the centre of this chapter is not a renewed explanation of the symbolism of each vessel, but how the people responded. The details of the vessels will correspond one by one in the building record that follows; if a sermon unfolded every vessel here, it would cover Moses' narrative focus, which is that the whole congregation moved together.

Note that the call of the craftsmen was public. Moses did not speak only to a few specialists, but to the whole congregation: whoever is wise-hearted, let him come. This means that in God's house, skill is not private capital, but a gift to be called out, seen by all, and set into the common project. Carpenters, metalworkers, weavers, embroiderers — in the eyes of the world perhaps only artisans — in the tabernacle project had work directly joined to worship. Every stitch, every hook, every board in the sanctuary needed faithful hands. God did not separate the spiritual from craftsmanship, as if only preaching and prayer were spiritual and carpentry and spinning were not. Chapter 35 says the opposite: it lets us see that craftsmanship guided by God's word is itself part of worship.

This is an important correction for the church today. We often exalt the service that is visible on the platform and belittle the concrete work behind the scenes. One can speak, one can organise, one can repair, one can design, one can mend, one can cook, one can care for children; if all these abilities are offered because hearts are moved, the church is no longer a place where a few perform and the many watch, but a household building together. Moses' call is still valid today: whoever is wise-hearted, let him come and make. The question is not whether you have a portion, but whether you are willing to give the portion you know how to do for use in God's house.

Looked at more deeply, the phrase wise-hearted also corrects our misunderstanding of where gifts come from. A craftsman's proficiency has in it years of practice, the teaching of masters, and accumulated materials and experience; Scripture does not deny any of this. But when these abilities are brought before the tabernacle project, the narrative understands them within the frame of God's call: hands trained by men can still be used by God and become part of worship. Today there are those who have been cooks, engineers, teachers, accountants, and medical workers for many years; their professions sound unrelated to the sanctuary, yet when the church must care for people, teach, handle accounts, or repair a building, these are exactly the wisdoms needed. The problem was never that a profession is too far from worship, but whether we bring our profession before God and ask Him, How may this portion of mine be used in Your house? Moses' words opened the door: whoever is wise-hearted, let him come — not a few, while the rest watch.

## IV. The Response of the Whole Congregation: Men and Women Came Together (42:00-56:00)

From verse 20 onward comes the true centre of the chapter. When Moses had finished speaking, all the congregation of the children of Israel departed from the presence of Moses. The narrative pauses briefly here, as if the camera moved away from the leader and turned to every tent in the camp. What happened next was not Moses urging them again, but the people moving themselves. The text says, And they came, every one whose heart stirred him up, and every one whom his spirit made willing, and they brought the LORD's offering to the work of the tabernacle of the congregation, and for all his service, and for the holy garments. In this sentence, a heart stirred up and a spirit made willing are set one upon the other, showing that their action had both inward impetus and a choice of the will. A stirring that does not become willingness soon dissipates; willingness without inward stirring becomes a burden of duty. Set together, they are mature offering: they were touched by God's word, and they also soberly decided to bring the gift.

Verse 22 says, And they came, both men and women, as many as were willing hearted, and brought bracelets, and earrings, and rings, and tablets, all jewels of gold. The narrative deliberately sets men and women side by side; men came together with women — this is a fact plainly stated in the text, and we present it as it stands. Still more worth noting is what they offered: the gold articles were not surplus stock in a storehouse, but breast ornaments, nose rings, signets, and bracelets worn on the body — precious things carried on the person, marks of identity and dignity. To offer these things on the spot meant that they did not wait to go home and sort out what was spare; having heard the appeal, they at once handed over what was closest to them and hardest to part with. Scripture does not exaggerate their feelings, nor write bold declarations for them; it simply records, with the words that they brought them, the action itself. True willingness sometimes needs few words: an opened hand is the clearest answer.

The participation of the women is written with particular concreteness in this chapter. Verse 25 says, And all the women that were wise hearted did spin with their hands, and brought that which they had spun, both of blue, and of purple, and of scarlet, and of fine linen. Verse 26 adds, And all the women whose heart stirred them up in wisdom spun goats' hair. The verbs here are solid: they did not stand by watching, nor merely hand over raw material; they spun with their own hands. Spinning is time-consuming, repetitive work requiring patience, and its product is not as dazzling as gold, yet the curtains of the tabernacle and the priestly garments needed precisely these strands of thread. The text writes their skill directly into the tabernacle project. This is a presentation at the factual level; we neither inflate it nor bury it. Their names were not recorded, but their hands were. In God's remembrance, nameless faithfulness is never weightless.

The leaders brought onyx stones and other stones to be set for the ephod and the breastplate, and spice and oil for the light, and for the anointing oil, and for the sweet incense. Those in high position were not absent because of their status, nor did they substitute status for offering. What they offered corresponded exactly to the resources they could bring. The people offered gold articles, the women spun thread, the leaders offered gems and spices, the craftsmen offered skill — different people, different gifts, all toward the same direction. The text concludes: The children of Israel brought a willing offering unto the LORD, every man and woman, whose heart made them willing to bring for all manner of work, which the LORD had commanded to be made by the hand of Moses. This summary gathers in everyone in the chapter; no one is left out. The tabernacle was not yet built, but a more beautiful picture had already appeared in the camp: a people who had once worshipped the golden calf now, with one heart, offered all they had to the true God. Gold had once been used to cast an idol; now it was used to build the sanctuary. It was the same gold in the people's hands, but its direction had changed, and so its meaning changed completely. The deepest work of grace is exactly this: to turn a person back from the wrong direction, so that what he has is no longer used for an idol, but for God.

We read this passage today not merely as the record of an ancient fund-raising. It shows us that in a healthy church, offering is certainly the response of the whole congregation, not the burden of a few. One offers money, one offers time, one offers expertise, one does the unseen fine work behind the scenes, and no portion is dispensable. If in a church only a few are willing and most are mere spectators, that church's tabernacle will be hard to build. The picture chapter 35 gives us is that, both men and women, they came. Those words are the most powerful place in this chapter.

This response has another detail easily read past: its timing. The congregation departed from Moses' presence and did not delay until their zeal had cooled. They returned to the camp, brought the gifts, spun the thread, and handed over the gems; the pace of the narrative is compact. If willingness always waits for next time — when I have time, when I have sorted things out, when others have begun — it will most likely go cold in the delay. The people's example is not impulsiveness, but responding once they have heard, giving their stirring a concrete date and their willingness a concrete action. We need the same pace today: having heard God's word, do not merely say Amen in your heart; take out that portion of time this week, send out that offering, and finish with your own hands that one thing you can do. Willingness delayed often ends as only regret; a timely response is what makes the picture of the whole congregation possible.

## V. Those Filled with the Spirit Can Also Teach Others (58:00-60:00)

Finally, Moses presented Bezalel and Oholiab to the congregation again. Moses said, See, the LORD hath called by name Bezaleel the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah; And he hath filled him with the spirit of God, in wisdom, in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship; And to devise curious works, to work in gold, and in silver, and in brass, And in the cutting of stones, to set them, and in carving of wood, to make any manner of cunning work. Moses also said that the LORD had put wisdom in the hearts of Bezalel and Oholiab, the son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan, and had made them able to teach others. Set against the call in chapter 31, most of this passage is a restatement, but within the restatement two strokes of the pen are crucially new. The first, in verse 34, is that God made Bezalel and Oholiab able to teach. The second is that Oholiab's skills are written in finer detail: he could engrave, design, embroider in blue, purple, and scarlet and in fine linen, and weave. These two additions make plain the transmissibility of the gifts — this is an observation at the level of sermon application: a person filled with the Spirit not only works himself, but can teach what he has received to others, so that more people enter service.

This point is supremely important for the church. A person who can only work himself, however capable, can finish only a limited project. A person willing and able to teach can multiply a gift. Bezalel's greatness lay not only in his skilful hands, but in that his skill could be passed on. Nor was Oholiab a solitary genius: he was named together with Bezalel and received together with him the portion of teaching. God's work has never rested on one or two stars; it rests on people filled with the Spirit passing wisdom on from generation to generation and from person to person. If a church only admires the capable but does not train teachers, the next generation can only start again from zero. True spiritual maturity is to do one's own work well, and also to enable others to do theirs.

The chapter ends by saying that Bezalel and Oholiab received a wise heart to do all the work, that they would do all the holy work, and that whatever the LORD commanded, they would do accordingly. Here the chapter closes; the actual building unfolds in the next chapter. A structural note is also needed here: by chapter 36, the people's offering was so abundant that the craftsmen reported to Moses that the material was enough, and more than enough, and only then did Moses give commandment and restrain the people from bringing more. That is the content of chapter 36; this chapter touches it in one stroke and does not unfold it ahead of time. But the note helps us see the ending toward which chapter 35 moves: if willingness is real, it will in the end be more than enough. God never treats the willing shabbily; the more hearts He moves, the less lack there is in His house.

Brothers and sisters, this chapter leaves us with three questions today. First, are you willing to rest first, to confess that God is God, rather than proving yourself by busyness? Second, what is in your hand — are you willing to bring it out willingly, whether it is a gold article, goats' hair, or the one strand of thread you can spin with your own hands? Third, the gift you have received — are you willing to teach it to others, so that they too can rise up and serve? The tabernacle was built by one generation, but teaching lets worship continue from generation to generation. May we be such people: hearing the call in rest, offering all we have in willingness, and learning to teach within our gifts. The willing-hearted — God Himself uses them to build His dwelling.

## Endnotes

1. Rest, Sabbath, and Sabbath of solemn rest: שַׁבַּת שַׁבָּתוֹן (shabbat shabbaton), H7676 + H7677. שַׁבַּת (shabbat) denotes the Sabbath, the ceasing of the seventh day; שַׁבָּתוֹן (shabbaton) is an intensive form, often rendered a sabbath of rest, a holy rest. According to BDB, the root is connected with ceasing and resting; HALOT likewise glosses it as rest and Sabbath observance. The doubled expression in Exodus 35:2 shows the set-apartness of this day and that it is not to be treated lightly; it is no ordinary holiday.

2. Work, labour: מְלָאכָה (melakhah), H4399. It denotes work, occupation, the work of a craftsman, business. According to BDB, the word most often denotes purposeful, skilled, productive labour, as distinct from general toil; HALOT likewise glosses it as work, task, and handiwork. What the Sabbath forbids is precisely this kind of creative, productive work, and the tabernacle work done by the craftsmen later in this chapter belongs to the same word-field, so that the contrast between rest and work gains its tension.

3. Offering, contribution: תְּרוּמָה (terumah), H8641. It denotes an offering, a contribution, a heave offering. According to BDB, the word denotes a contribution presented to the LORD and is often connected with the image of lifting up and presenting; HALOT glosses it as contribution and offering, and discusses its etymological association with the act of lifting. Whether its derivation comes directly from the sense of lifting is still debated in scholarship; the sermon text takes only its traditional sense of presenting, and draws no etymological conclusion. Exodus 25:2 and 35:5 both use this word, and the correspondence of the lists before and after also stands on this word.

4. Willing, willing-hearted, generous: נָדִיב (nadiv), H5081. It denotes willing and generous, and can also denote a noble person or a leader. According to BDB, the word carries both the sense of generosity and willingness and that of a person of noble standing; HALOT likewise lists both the willing and the noble senses. The willing heart of 35:5 and 35:22 is related to this word-group, while the leaders of 35:27 are referred to in the narrative by another term for rulers; the two senses appear side by side in the same chapter, showing that those who offer willingly are of no single status. The sermon takes the sense of willingness and generosity as primary.

5. Wise-hearted: חֲכַם־לֵב (chakam-lev), H2450. חָכָם (chakam) denotes wise, skilled; לֵב (lev) denotes the heart. According to BDB, chakam can denote a craftsman skilled in his craft, not only abstract thought; HALOT also includes its senses of skilled and wise. Exodus 35:10, 35:25, 35:35 and other places repeatedly use this expression for craftsmen and women, showing that the biblical view of wisdom includes the unity of craft, heart, and judgment.

6. To spin, spinning: טָוָה (tavah), H2901. It denotes to spin yarn, to spin thread. According to BDB, this word occurs only in Exodus 35:25-26, denoting the women's action of spinning thread and spinning goats' hair with their own hands; HALOT likewise glosses it as to spin. The rarity and concreteness of the verb show precisely that the narrative commemorates the women's actual labour; it is not participation in general terms, but a factual record of entering the tabernacle project by skill.

7. On the scope of the prohibition of kindling fire in 35:3: This sentence does not appear in the parallel formulations of other Sabbath regulations. The transmission of the Samaritan Pentateuch, the rabbinic literature's delimiting interpretation of the relation between kindling and work, and the Karaite reading that insists on a literal comprehensive prohibition each have their supporters in the history of interpretation. Both Childs and Durham note the uniqueness of this rule and the divergence of interpretation; the sermon text sets the views side by side and adopts none as a final conclusion.

8. On Bezalel and Oholiab being made able to teach: The word about teaching in Exodus 35:34 does not appear in the parallel call passage of 31:1-11; it is a new point added in the restatement of chapter 35. Durham notes that this verse extends the craftsmen's office from making to teaching, and Childs likewise observes that the restatement passage is not a simple repetition, but a reconfirmation and unfolding of the call in the context of execution. The sermon's argument about transmissibility in Part V is built on this textual observation; what stands upon it belongs to the level of sermon application, and is marked as such here.

## Devotional Excerpt

Stop first, then offer. When Moses assembled the people, his first word was not about the project, but about the Sabbath. God wants us to confess that when we stop, He still works. We do not prove our worth by ceaseless busyness, nor our love for Him by exhausting ourselves. Only when the heart rests is it free, and only a free heart can offer willingly. Only after rest comes offering. Men and women came together; some brought the gold they wore, the precious things closest to them; some spun thread with their own hands, strand by strand, in slow and careful work; and the leaders brought gems and spices, the best they could bring from their place. No gift was too small: gold was precious, goat hair also entered the tabernacle, and ordinary wood also became part of the sanctuary. The thread spun by unnamed women was remembered, and the gifts given to the craftsmen could be taught to others, so that more people could rise up and work. What is in your hand does not need to be compared with anyone else's; bring it willingly, and God will use it. A heart moved by Him will, in the end, bring more than enough. Rest before Him today, offer willingly the part you know how to do, and pass it on to the next person, so that His house may be built up by the willing hearts of all, and the glory given to Him.

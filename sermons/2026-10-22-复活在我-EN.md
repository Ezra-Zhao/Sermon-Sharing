## Summary (about 100 words)

At the tomb of Lazarus, Jesus said to the weeping Martha: I am the resurrection and the life; he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live. Today, as we bid farewell to our loved one, the tears are real, and the hope is real too. Those who die in Christ have fallen asleep; they have not perished. Our tears today have a place to go, because the Lord Himself has promised that whoever lives and believes in Him shall never die.

---

## (00:00 - 04:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Dear family members, dear friends, as we gather here today, none of our hearts is at ease. However beautiful the flowers may be, they cannot cover our reluctance to part; however kind the words may be, they cannot fill the empty place in our hearts. So today, I do not intend to use many words to persuade you not to grieve — the Bible never teaches us that. What the Bible teaches us is this: in grief, there may be tears; but our tears fall into the hands of a Lord who hears us, stays with us, and has overcome death.

Please listen to the words the Lord Jesus spoke with His own lips, from the eleventh chapter of the Gospel of John, when Lazarus had already been in the tomb four days and He met Martha, who had come out to meet Him on the way to Bethany:

"Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?" (John 11:25-26)

And read again the words of 1 Thessalonians: "For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him." (1 Thess. 4:14)

Let us pray: Lord, we come before You today with tears. We do not say that we are not in pain; we hand our pain to You. Comfort this family with Your own words, and let us hear the sound of the resurrection in the midst of sorrow. You weep with those who weep, and You have also promised to wipe away every tear. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (04:00 - 12:00) Part One: The Lord on the Way to the Tomb — He Declared the Promise First, Then Wept with the Weeping, Then Called (John 11:20-44, selections)

The eleventh chapter of the Gospel of John is one of the most human chapters in the Bible. Lazarus was sick, and his sisters sent word to Jesus. Yet Jesus delayed for two days. By the time He reached Bethany, Lazarus had already been in the tomb for four days. Martha came out to meet Him, and her first words carried both complaint and faith: Lord, if You had been here, my brother would not have died.

It was in answer to that mingled complaint and faith that He said to Martha those words for the ages: I am the resurrection and the life. Resurrection — in the original, anastasis [1] — is to stand up again, to rise from the dead. This is not an abstract doctrine, not a future institution; this is a Person — “I am the resurrection,” He said; the resurrection is the person of Jesus Himself. When He said this, the tomb was still sealed, the stone had not yet been rolled away, and Martha's tears had not yet dried. Hope has never been something we have only after circumstances improve; hope is the Lord speaking with His own lips in the midst of grief.

Martha's answer in that moment is also worth learning from. She said: Yes, Lord, I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who was to come into the world. Her brother had not yet risen, her tears had not yet been wiped away, and yet she spoke her faith first. Dear family, faith is not something we have only after everything has been explained; faith is laying hold of the Lord Himself while we have not yet seen the ending. Today you may not be able to say many things, and that is all right. Martha, too, said only one central thing: I believe that You are the Christ. If today you can say only one sentence — Lord, I believe — that is already enough. The Lord does not require those who weep to sort out their emotions and organize their words first; He asks only that we give Him our hearts. When He asks, "Believest thou this?", He is not giving an examination; He is placing His promise, with His own hand, into yours. If you receive it, these words will become words you can turn over again and again on the nights ahead when you cannot sleep: I am the resurrection and the life; the one I love is in His hands, and I myself am in His hands too.

Friends, there are some here today who carry words like these in their hearts as well. Why was there not a little more time? Why, though we prayed, did he still leave us? Today I will not try to explain on God's behalf every "why" — there are many "whys" that we will only understand on that day, face to face. But John tells us how Jesus responded in that moment, and it is worth remembering for a lifetime: He saw Mary weeping, and He saw the Jews who had come with her weeping, and He groaned in His spirit and was troubled; and then, Jesus wept.

Having declared the promise, He did not stand back from the grief; He stepped into it Himself. The Lord did not stand at a distance and lecture. The Jews watched Him weep and said: See how He loved him. Today I want to hand these words to the family first: the Lord understands your tears; the Lord knows the weight your loved one carries in your hearts. Before you have heard any word of comfort, know this one thing first — you are not weeping alone. There is a Lord who weeps with you, and His tears came earlier than ours.

After He had spoken, He went to the tomb and called Lazarus out. That coming out, on that day, was a foretaste, not the final end — a foretaste of the day when He will call all who sleep in Him to hear His voice and come out.

## (12:00 - 20:00) Part Two: Asleep in Jesus — Death, Renamed by the Lord (1 Thess. 4:13-18)

Paul begins the fourth chapter of 1 Thessalonians by saying: Concerning those who sleep, we do not want you to be uninformed, lest you sorrow as others who have no hope.

Notice: Paul did not say that believers do not sorrow. He said: do not sorrow as those who have no hope. The sorrow remains, and the hope remains too — these two can be held in one heart at the same time. The family's tears today are not a sign of unbelief; they are a sign of love. The deeper the love, the more painful the farewell. God never looks lightly on this pain.

Paul used a very tender word for the passing of those who believe in the Lord: sleep. In the original, koimaomai [2] — to fall asleep, to sleep peacefully. The New Testament uses this word for those who have died in Christ. To have fallen asleep and to have perished are two different things. One who sleeps will wake again; one who sleeps is at rest and suffers no more; one who sleeps has a Lord who watches over him and will call him to rise in the morning. If the loved one we bid farewell to today trusted in the Lord, we may say of him according to the words of Scripture: he has not vanished; he sleeps in Jesus.

Paul then describes for us what that day will look like: The Lord Himself will descend from heaven, with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trumpet of God; and the dead in Christ will rise first. Then we who are alive and remain will be caught up together with them in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air. And so we shall always be with the Lord.

Friends, this is the firmest foundation in a Christian's heart at a farewell: the reunion has a date. It is not a pleasant parting after which we never meet again; it is a temporary separation, and a future reunion. The goodbye we say today, however reluctant, is only the goodbye of this stretch of the journey. For those who are in the Lord, there is a next stretch — meeting in the air, being with the Lord forever, when there will be no more tears, no more sickness and pain, and no more death to separate those who love one another.

So at the end of this passage Paul says: Comfort one another with these words. Comfort — in the original, parakaleō [3] — is to call alongside, to accompany, to encourage. This comfort is not shouting "be strong" from a height; it is drawing near, sitting down beside someone, and speaking the words of the resurrection, one by one, to the one who weeps. Today's service is exactly such an act of comfort: we sit with the family for a while, and gently place the Lord's words in their hearts.

This way of speaking of sleep also leaves the family a very practical comfort. When we bid a loved one farewell, what we fear most is that we will never see him again, never hear him again, and cannot hold on to him even in our dreams. The Bible does not ask us to pretend this fear does not exist; the Bible sets a greater fact before us: for the one who sleeps, the day of waking has already been fixed; for those who are separated, the place of reunion has already been fixed. When you return home, there will be one voice missing in the house, one set of bowl and chopsticks missing at the table, one place missing in the holiday photograph. These empty places are real, and there is no need to avoid them. Whenever you remember, speak your longing to the Lord in prayer, and shed your tears before the Lord. As time passes, you will find that the longing does not disappear, but that it slowly changes from a sharp pain into gratitude: gratitude that he came to this family, gratitude that you loved one another, gratitude that, in Christ, this love will not be confiscated by death, but is only kept for a while in heaven. Such hope does not cancel grief, but it gives grief a direction and a home.

## (20:00 - 26:00) Part Three: O Death, Where Is Your Victory — The Victory Is Given by the Lord (1 Cor. 15:51-57)

If we had only the hope of reunion, our hearts might still ask: death is so terrible — will it not be the winner in the end? In the fifteenth chapter of 1 Corinthians, Paul gives the answer with utter decisiveness.

He says: We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed — in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. For the trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed. For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality.

The one we love today — he grew old, he fell ill, his frail body lies here today — but Scripture says this body is not the final version. One day, what is corruptible will put on incorruption, and what is mortal will put on immortality. At that time, Paul, quoting the prophets, issues a challenge to death: O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?

Victory — in the original, nikos [4] — triumph, conquest. Paul says the sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law; but thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ. — The victory is not something we grit our teeth and endure our way into; it is what the Lord Jesus has already won back at the cross and the empty tomb, and given to us.

So a Christian funeral is never the closing scene of a defeat. We weep because we love; we sing, read Scripture, and pray because the outcome has already been decided. The grave is not the terminal station; it is a sleeping chamber. Death is not the master; it is an enemy already defeated by the Lord. Our loved one's name is written in the Book of Life, not in the book of death. Today we return him to the Lord — not to darkness, but into the hands of the One who said, I am the resurrection and the life — hands in which the nail marks are still there, and love is there, and safety is there too.

This victory also reminds us how to remember the one we love. To remember is not simply to leave a person in a photograph frame; to remember is to go on living out the best of what he was while he lived. If he loved his family, then love one another all the more; if he feared God, then follow all the more earnestly; if he gladly helped others, then take up that warm heart of his. The most lasting thing a person leaves on earth is not property; it is the influence he planted in the lives of others. Those here today — if you received his kindness, heard his counsel, or were helped by him — please take that gratitude home with you and live it out before your family. In this way, what death takes away is his body; it cannot take away the love he left behind, and still less can it take away the life the Lord has already accomplished in him. The best way for us to remember him is to carry his goodness and the Lord's hope with us, and to live every day ahead earnestly, until the day of reunion, when we can slowly tell him these stories again.

## (26:00 - 29:00) Application: For Those Present, Each Hear a Word

To the family. You have worked hard these past days; your bodies are tired, and your hearts are more tired still. I ask you only to remember three small things. First, allow yourselves to weep. Even Jesus wept; you do not need to pretend to be strong. When you want to weep, weep; the shoulders of the brothers and sisters of the church are lent to you today. Second, allow yourselves to go slowly. Grief has no timetable. The festivals, birthdays, and empty chairs ahead will stir up longing again, and all of that is normal; there is no need to blame yourselves. Third, speak your longing aloud. Speak it to your family, speak it to God, and you are welcome to come and talk with us and pray. This family will not be forgotten by the church after the service ends; we will continue to pray for you and walk with you for a stretch of the road.

To believing relatives and friends. Our duty today is not to come as teachers of doctrine, but to come as companions. The best seven days of Job's friends were the days when they sat on the ground with him and said not a word (Job 2:13; it was Job himself who sat in the ashes, Job 2:8). In the time ahead, please let your comfort land in concrete places: a meal, a visit, a sentence — "I remember him" — a stretch of time weeping together. Also watch for the quiet one in the family — the one who weeps least sometimes carries the heaviest heart.

To relatives and friends who do not yet believe. Some friends here today do not ordinarily come to church; you have come today out of family affection and friendship, and we thank you. I will not press you to believe anything today; I will only leave these heartfelt words here: the Jesus we believe in is not a distant figure of myth. He wept at the tomb of His friend; He knows the taste of loss. If you too carry a "why" in your heart, and something you cannot let go of, you are welcome after the service to stay and talk with any of us, or simply to sit quietly for a while. We will not rush you; we are willing to stay with you, and to pray for you. May you take at least one sentence home today: I am the resurrection and the life — the Lord who spoke these words is not far from you.

At the end, Scripture also lets us glimpse what the final end will look like. Revelation says that God will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and there will be no more death, nor sorrow, nor crying, nor pain, for the former things have passed away. Friends, this is not an empty word of comfort; this is the future God Himself has written down. Today, when we wipe away tears, it is people wiping the tears of people, and after they are wiped, they will flow again; but the tears God will wipe away on that day will never flow again. If our loved one sleeps in the Lord, he has already gone one step ahead of us, free from sickness and frailty, waiting for a body that will never decay and a feast that will never end. We who are still on the road walk forward with tears, and with this vision: walk a little more steadily, live a little more earnestly, love one another a little more, because we know that separation is only temporary, and that the end of the story is not the grave, but the new heaven and the new earth, and the hand of the Lord Himself wiping away our tears. After today's service ends, may the family go home carrying the hope of that hand, and on waking each morning, say softly to themselves: he sleeps; I must live well; and on that day, we will meet again.

## (29:00 - 30:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Dear friends, today we have heard three things the Lord Himself said: I am the resurrection and the life; those who are in Jesus sleep, they do not perish; and thanks be to God, through the Lord Jesus Christ, we have the victory. The tears will flow for a while yet, and the days must still be lived slowly, but the way we bid farewell can be different: bidding farewell with hope, bidding farewell with the covenant of reunion.

Let us pray together, and pray especially for the family: Lord of the resurrection, Lord of life, we thank You. You shed tears at the tomb of Lazarus, and today You have compassion on the tears of this family too. We place our beloved one in Your hands, firmly believing that he sleeps in You, awaiting the morning of the resurrection. Comfort the family Yourself, and be with them on every night of longing; let the hope of reunion become their strength in the days ahead. You asked Martha: Believest thou this? Lord, today we answer You with tears: we believe. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

Please stand. After we sing, we will view in order, and shake hands with the family in sympathy.

## Endnotes

[1] anastasis: Greek ἀνάστασις (G386), to stand up again, resurrection, rising from the dead; John 11:25, "I am the resurrection"; the meaning follows BDAG. This manuscript stresses that the resurrection rests on the person of Jesus rather than on an abstract concept, a homiletical exposition based on the Lord's self-declaration ἐγώ εἰμι, "I am" (John 11:25); the KJV reads "I am the resurrection," and the exposition rests on that wording, not on any phrase "in me," which the Greek and the KJV do not contain.

[2] koimaomai: Greek κοιμάω (G2837), to fall asleep, to sleep peacefully; the New Testament uses it as a gentle term for those who have died in Christ; 1 Thess. 4:13-15, "those who sleep"; the meaning follows BDAG. On the basis of this word, this manuscript explains that passing away is peaceful sleep rather than annihilation, and notes this expressly.

[3] parakaleō: Greek παρακαλέω (G3870), to call alongside, to comfort, to encourage, to accompany; 1 Thess. 4:18, "Comfort one another with these words"; the meaning follows BDAG. This manuscript's claim that "comfort is sitting alongside, not lecturing" is a homiletical exposition based on the literal structure of the word.

[4] nikos: Greek νῖκος (G3534), victory, triumph; 1 Cor. 15:57, "thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ"; the meaning follows BDAG. This manuscript makes clear that the victory is accomplished by Christ and given to believers, not obtained by human effort.

## Devotional Excerpt

After a farewell, the hardest moments are often not the day of the service, but the quiet moments that follow: an empty chair, an old coat, a sentence no one answers anymore, a place left empty at a family feast. Today, lay the Lord's words gently on your heart: I am the resurrection and the life. Jesus spoke them not after everything was well, but on the way to a tomb, in the midst of tears. He declared the promise first, and then He wept with those who wept. Hope, it turns out, is not the absence of pain; hope is knowing, while it hurts, in whose hands your loved one rests, believing that those who sleep in Christ will wake again and meet again, and allowing yourself both to grieve slowly and to be lifted, little by little, by His word. If you are missing someone today, take one minute to give thanks for them, to count the graces you shared, and to pray for your own grieving heart: Lord, I give You my reluctance to let go, I give You my tears, I entrust to You the promise of reunion, and I ask You to walk with me through every night of missing them. He hears, He is near, He will wipe away every tear, and He will wake those who sleep, on the morning of resurrection.

# Therefore Thou Shalt Surely Rejoice — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 16

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries

Title verification: Five candidates were considered — (i) taken from the sentence at v. 15, "Thou Shalt Surely Rejoice"; (ii) taken from the sentence at v. 11, "Rejoice Before the LORD"; (iii) taken from the sentence at v. 16, "They Shall Not Appear Before the LORD Empty"; (iv) taken from the sentence at v. 16, "Three Times in a Year"; (v) taken from the sentence at v. 20, "That Which Is Altogether Just." Candidate (i) is adopted: "rejoice" is the rhythm-word of the whole chapter — among the three great feasts, the Feast of Weeks and the Feast of Tabernacles are each expressly commanded to rejoice (vv. 11, 14), and the Tabernacles section closes with the intensified form, "thou shalt surely rejoice" (v. 15; in the original wəhāyîṯā ʾaḵ śāmēaḥ, "thou shalt be wholly joyful"); the Passover section, though it has no word of rejoicing, has the root of it, the remembrance of the Exodus. The chapter's three bodies of material — the three feasts, the judges, and the prohibitions beside the altar — are gathered up by the two words rejoicing and justice: the feast sections close on rejoicing, and the judicial section on "that which is altogether just." The English title takes the original words of KJV 16:15, "therefore thou shalt surely rejoice" (checked directly, verse by verse, at wd.bible in the Chinese round). The English title is fixed as: Therefore Thou Shalt Surely Rejoice.
Form: The sermon is divided into five parts (Deut 16:1–8; 9–12; 13–17; 18–22; and a part setting the four feast lists side by side), the time codes running continuously over 60 minutes. Within each part, paragraphs are marked [Fact] / [Argument] / [Summary] / [New Testament Reading] / [Scholarly and Historical Discussion] to keep the layers distinct: wherever the Argument layer draws an inference it is marked "this is an inference / argument"; wherever the New Testament Reading layer registers an echo it is marked "registered as a report"; and wherever the feast lists of Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers that parallel this chapter differ from it, the differences are set out side by side only — not adjudicated, and no stemma is arranged. This manuscript has three hard points of adjudicative discipline: first, the differences between the three great feasts of this chapter and the lists at Exodus 23, Leviticus 23, and Numbers 28–29 are set out side by side only; second, the relation of 16:21–22 to 17:1 across the chapter boundary is registered and checked only, and no verse is reassigned on the strength of the boundary; third, the surface tension between the "roast / boil" of v. 7 and the "not sodden at all with water" of Exodus 12:9 is set out side by side only. Deuteronomy 16 treats three matters: the three great feasts (vv. 1–17), judges and justice (vv. 18–20), and the prohibitions beside the altar (vv. 21–22). The feasts are in this book a third sounding: chapter 12 laid down the general rule of worship at "the place which the LORD shall choose," and this chapter turns that rule into the calendar of a year — the Passover remembering the Exodus (vv. 1–8), the Feast of Weeks counting the harvest (vv. 9–12), the Feast of Tabernacles celebrating the ingathering (vv. 13–15), and the whole gathered up by the general rule of appearing three times a year (vv. 16–17). Then the pen turns: the people who keep the feasts must go back to their several cities to live, and so there are judges (vv. 18–20); and the altar of the feasts must be kept clean, and so there are the two prohibitions beside the altar (vv. 21–22). This sermon first reads the three feast sections through verse by verse (Parts One to Three), then reads the general rule, the judiciary, and the prohibitions (Part Four), and last sets this chapter side by side with the three feast lists of Exodus 23, Leviticus 23, and Numbers 28–29 for comparison (Part Five) — the differences among the four lists are set out side by side through the whole sermon and not adjudicated. ## Main Text

Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Fact] The place of this chapter in the book is registered first: Deuteronomy 12–26 is the body of the law — chapter 12 treats of the place of worship, chapter 13 of loyalty in worship, chapter 14 of the food and the tithe that mark a people set apart, chapter 15 of release and manumission, and chapter 16, of the time of worship — how a year is to be lived. Its structure is "feasts (vv. 1–17) + judiciary (vv. 18–20) + prohibitions at the altar (vv. 21–22)": the calendar takes up more than three quarters of the chapter (17 of 22 verses), and the judiciary and the altar prohibitions bring up the rear with three verses and two verses respectively. The sacred name "the LORD" occurs in this chapter, in the Chinese base text, 21 times, distributed over 15 verses; the forms are of two kinds — "the LORD your God" (with the fuller possessive) 8 times (twice at v. 1, and at vv. 2, 8, 10, 11, 15, 21), "the LORD thy God" (with the shorter possessive) 11 times (vv. 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20, 22), and the form without a possessive suffix twice (the "place which the LORD shall choose" at vv. 2 and 15). Recounted on the KJV's own letter, "the LORD" occurs 23 times over the same 15 verses: "the LORD thy God" 20 times, and the form without the possessive 3 times (the "place which the LORD shall choose" at vv. 2 and 15, and "before the LORD empty" at v. 16) — the KJV carries the name twice more than the Chinese account, once at v. 10, where it reads "unto the LORD thy God" a third time in the verse, and once at v. 16, where the appearing is expressly "before the LORD"; the totals differ, and the two accounts are registered separately and not harmonized (see Endnote 2). What is counted is not merely a word; it is the belonging of every arrangement in this chapter: the feasts are kept unto Him, the officers are set in the cities He gives, and the altar is made for Him. "(The place) which (the LORD) shall choose" occurs 6 times in the chapter (vv. 2, 6, 7, 11, 15, 16; in the KJV five times "shall choose" and once, at v. 11, "hath chosen") and is the spatial anchor of the feast sections: however the three great feasts differ in content, their place is one only.

[Argument] This is an argument in summary form: chapter 12 asks "where shall men worship?", and chapter 16 asks "when shall men worship, and with whom?" Deuteronomy's view of time shows itself here: time is not a neutral container — in the month of Abib that night is to be remembered, after the harvest seven sevens are to be counted, and after the ingathering men are to keep the feast of booths for seven days; the three turning-points of the year are all marked by the story of the Exodus and by the produce of the land. This is an inference (drawn from the order of the reason-sentences in the three sections, not a doctrine self-evident in any single verse).

[Summary] One sentence to grasp the whole chapter: the three feasts of the year bring the whole people to one place to rejoice, and the days outside the feasts are upheld by the justice of the several cities — rejoicing has its feasts, justice has no off-season. In the five parts that follow: first the Passover is read (vv. 1–8), then the Feast of Weeks (vv. 9–12), then the Feast of Tabernacles with the general rule of the three appearings (vv. 13–17), then the judges and the two prohibitions beside the altar (vv. 18–22), and last the four feast lists are set side by side for comparison.

### Part One: The Passover in the Month of Abib (Deut 16:1–8; 05:00–17:00)

[Fact] Verse 1 gives the month and the root: "Observe the month of Abib, and keep the passover unto the LORD thy God: for in the month of Abib the LORD thy God brought thee forth out of Egypt by night." The verb "observe" is in the original šāmar (H8104) — this book's familiar word for keeping a commandment, keep, observe, set here upon a month: a month, too, is to be kept. The month of Abib (ʾāḇîḇ H24) is the month of the green ears. This chapter gives the month only and no day-number — the day-number stands at Leviticus 23:5, "In the fourteenth day of the first month at even is the LORD's passover," and at Numbers 28:16, "And in the fourteenth day of the first month is the passover of the LORD"; registered for the record, and set side by side in Part Five. The word "by night" is a detail of the root: the Exodus happened by night, and the feast is kept at night (v. 6) — the moment remembered and the moment of the event are aligned. Verse 2 gives the sacrificial animal and the place: "Thou shalt therefore sacrifice the passover unto the LORD thy God, of the flock and the herd, in the place which the LORD shall choose to place his name there." The range of the animal is "the flock and the herd" (ṣōʾn H6629, the flock; bāqār H1241, the herd) — broader than the lamb of Exodus 12, and registered as standing so; the verb is zāḇaḥ (H2076), to slaughter in sacrifice. Verse 3 gives the bread and the remembrance: "Thou shalt eat no leavened bread with it; seven days shalt thou eat unleavened bread therewith, even the bread of affliction; for thou camest forth out of the land of Egypt in haste: that thou mayest remember the day when thou camest forth out of the land of Egypt all the days of thy life." The unleavened bread (maṣṣâ H4682) receives here a name: "the bread of affliction" (leḥem ʿŏnî; ʿŏnî H6040, affliction) — the other lists say only "unleavened bread"; this chapter names the bread, and the name carries the taste of Egypt in it. The parenthesis, "for thou camest forth out of the land of Egypt in haste" (ḥipāzôn H2649, haste), is the bread's provenance. The term of the "remembering" (zākar H2142) is "all the days of thy life." Verse 4 gives the scope of the prohibition: "And there shall be no leavened bread seen with thee in all thy coast seven days; neither shall there anything of the flesh, which thou sacrificedst the first day at even, remain all night until the morning." The leaven (śəʾōr H7603) is to be seen nowhere within "all thy coast" (gəḇûl H1366, border, territory) — not in a cupboard at home, but in the whole territory. That the sacrificial flesh is not to be left overnight is in the same key as Exodus 12:10, 23:18, and 34:25; the intertext is registered. Verses 5–6 are the double sentence of the place-prohibition: "Thou mayest not sacrifice the passover within any of thy gates, which the LORD thy God giveth thee: But at the place which the LORD thy God shall choose to place his name in, there thou shalt sacrifice the passover at even, at the going down of the sun, at the season that thou camest forth out of Egypt." First the several cities are forbidden (šāʿar H8179, gate; the Chinese base text reads "the several cities"), and then the one place is established; the moment is nailed down at "at even, at the going down of the sun" (the going down of the sun, šemeš H8121), and that moment is itself the remembrance — "at the season that thou camest forth out of Egypt" (môʿēḏ H4150, the appointed season). Verse 7 gives the manner of eating and the dispersal: "And thou shalt roast and eat it in the place which the LORD thy God shall choose: and thou shalt turn in the morning, and go unto thy tents." The verb bāšal (H1310) in its root sense is to boil, to cook; the Chinese base text's main line reads "roast," and its translator's note keeps "boil" — both readings stand registered on the page of the base text itself (the tension with Exodus 12:9 is set out side by side at the Discussion layer). "Thou shalt turn in the morning, and go unto thy tents" — the word "tents" (ʾōhel H168) is worth registering: men had by now come to dwell in cities and houses (compare chapters 8 and 12), yet this verse still calls the dwelling a tent; it is the vocabulary of the wilderness preserved within the feast, the pilgrims dispersing back to their "tents" like the fathers sojourning. Verse 8 closes the section: "Six days thou shalt eat unleavened bread: and on the seventh day shall be a solemn assembly to the LORD thy God: thou shalt do no work therein." Six days of eating the bread, and on the seventh day a solemn assembly (ʿăṣeret H6116, a solemn gathering); the "seven days" of v. 3 and the "six days + the seventh day" of v. 8 are two ways of counting one and the same cycle (v. 3 counts seven in total, v. 8 counts six plus one separately), entered side by side without adjudication — Leviticus 23:6 and Numbers 28:17 both read unleavened bread for seven days, and whether this chapter's separate counting points to something else is set side by side in Part Five.

[Argument] This is an inference: the weightiest change in this section is the place. The Passover of Exodus 12 was a household rite, eaten inside the door of each house; this chapter expressly forbids sacrificing it within any of the gates and permits it only at the chosen place — the Passover has been turned from a household rite into a pilgrim feast. The text does not state the reason for the change; it gives only the repetition of the spatial anchor (the "shall choose" of vv. 2, 6, 7, three times), but its effect can be registered: on one and the same night, in one and the same place, the whole people eat one and the same meal of remembrance, and the memory of the nation has from now on a common scene. This is an argument, an inference from vv. 5–6. A further inference concerns the name "the bread of affliction": remembrance is not an abstract recollection, it is a taste — affliction made into a shape that can be eaten, once a year, passing from mouth to mouth down the generations; a memory that does not land in the body evaporates within one generation. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) Of the New Testament's Passover echoes, one line can be checked in the register: the four Gospels' correspondence between Jesus' last supper and the Passover meal, and in 1 Corinthians, "For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us" (1 Cor 5:7) and "therefore let us keep the feast … but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (1 Cor 5:7–8) — all of them echoes of Passover vocabulary in the New Testament, registered only, and not used to read this chapter back. The unleavened bread of this chapter, in this chapter's own context, is the bread of affliction remembering the Exodus; the sense of "sincerity" in the New Testament reading is a later layer of reading; the two layers are set side by side and not confused.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Two registers are set side by side. First, the surface tension between the bāšal of v. 7 and Exodus 12:9, "Eat not of it raw, nor sodden at all with water, but roast with fire": the readings are set side by side — (a) bāšal here takes the broad sense "to cook, to prepare," not specifically to boil in water, and the Chinese base text's main line "roast" is this reading; (b) the two ordinances belong to different traditions and settings (Exodus 12 is the rule of a household rite, this chapter the rule of a sanctuary pilgrimage), and the difference in the manner of cooking is a difference of rule. The translator's note in the base text, "or, boil," keeps both readings on the page; this sermon follows the base text, registers only, and does not adjudicate. Second, the difference between the sacrificial animal "of the flock and the herd" and the lamb to which Exodus 12 restricts it: the readings are (a) that the sacrifice of the pilgrim feast also carries the character of the festal meal of a peace offering, and so cattle and flock are used together; (b) that "the passover" of this chapter denotes the offerings of the feast as a whole and not the Passover lamb alone, strictly so called. The two readings are set side by side. At the level of historical practice one item only is registered: 2 Chronicles 30 and 35 record Hezekiah and Josiah keeping the Passover at Jerusalem, the record in the historical books of the centralized Passover being carried out; this sermon registers its existence and does not expand the historical examination.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: the Passover enters the sanctuary, and the bread is named affliction — in the month of Abib, by night, the whole people come back to one place, eat the Exodus into their bodies, and in the morning each returns to his tent.

### Part Two: Numbering the Seven Weeks (Deut 16:9–12; 17:00–26:00)

[Fact] Verse 9 gives the reckoning: "Seven weeks shalt thou number unto thee: begin to number the seven weeks from such time as thou beginnest to put the sickle to the corn." "Number" is in the original sāpar (H5608), to count, to number; seven weeks are seven sevens of days, forty-nine days; the starting-point is not a fixed date on the calendar but an act of husbandry — "put the sickle to the corn": the sickle (ḥermēš H2770) falls on the standing corn (qāmâ H7054), and the counting begins. This chapter gives no month and no day-number; the feast is triggered by the ripeness in the field. Verse 10 gives the manner of offering: "And thou shalt keep the feast of weeks unto the LORD thy God with a tribute of a freewill offering of thine hand, which thou shalt give unto the LORD thy God, according as the LORD thy God hath blessed thee:" The name of the feast is in the original ḥag šāḇuʿôt (šāḇûaʿ H7620, week); the character of the offering is a freewill offering (nidbâ H5071, a willing, voluntary offering), and the measure of its quantity is the proportion of the blessing received — "according as the LORD thy God hath blessed thee" — of the same construction as the measure of the send-off bundle in chapter 15 (according to the measure of God's blessing); the intertext is registered. Verse 11 is the chapter's first list of rejoicing: "And thou shalt rejoice before the LORD thy God, thou, and thy son, and thy daughter, and thy manservant, and thy maidservant, and the Levite that is within thy gates, and the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow, that are among you, in the place which the LORD thy God hath chosen to place his name there." The list is counted item by item: thou, son and daughter, manservant and maidservant, the Levite within thy gates, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow — seven classes in all; the place of the rejoicing is still the chosen place, and its posture is "before the LORD thy God." The order of the list is registered as it stands: from within the house (son and daughter, manservant and maidservant) to within the city (the Levite) to those in the midst (the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow), the circles moving outward from the inside; the Tabernacles list at v. 14 is in the main the same as this, and the small differences of detail are entered in the endnotes. Verse 12 closes the whole section with remembrance: "And thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman in Egypt: and thou shalt observe and do these statutes." The root of the Feast of Weeks is not the harvest itself — every nation has a harvest; the root is the record of the bondman: once a bondman who had not even a field of his own, now with corn to count and a sickle to put to it. "These statutes" (ḥuqqîm H2706) is the only self-reference of its kind in the section: of the three great feasts, only the Weeks section carries this closing sentence of record and statutes; the Passover section closes on the solemn assembly (v. 8), and the Tabernacles section has no such sentence — the sections close in different ways, entered side by side for the record.

[Argument] This is an inference: the counting of the Feast of Weeks is a counting of gratitude within time. From the putting of the sickle, forty-nine days are counted; through those forty-nine days the corn ripens day by day, and the counting is itself a kind of watching — watching the gift take shape, and then coming with the freewill offering. The measure of this chapter is registered once more: the freewill offering is according to the proportion of the blessing, and no fixed amount is set; Deuteronomy, in dealing with giving, twice refuses a fixed figure (15:14 according to the measure of the blessing, 16:10 according as He hath blessed thee, and 16:17 each as he is able, according to the blessing) and gives only the principle of proportion — incomes differ, but the proportion of the heart can be the same. This is an inference from the measures at the three verses. A further inference concerns the list: rejoicing in this chapter is not a private feeling, it works by a list — the persons named and called, not one of them may be left out: the Levite who has no inheritance, the stranger who has no land, the fatherless who has no father, the widow who has no husband, all stand within the list; if the master's rejoicing does not include them, then on this chapter's account the feast has not been kept. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) Acts 2 records the descent of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost — "Pentecost" being the Greek name for the fiftieth day after the Feast of Weeks, the feast receiving in the New Testament the content of a new event; in registering this intertext a distinction must be kept: the New Testament's record is a register of an event, and this chapter's Feast of Weeks is a feast of husbandry and remembrance; their relation belongs to the history of reading, and this sermon does not, on the strength of the New Testament event, rewrite the original sense of this chapter's feast.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] The starting-day of the Feast of Weeks is a famous divergence in historical practice, registered side by side: the starting-point at Leviticus 23:15 is "from the morrow after the sabbath, from the day that ye brought the sheaf of the wave offering" — whether "the sabbath" means the sabbath within the Passover season (the first day of the feast) or the weekly sabbath was already read in two ways in antiquity (the divergence between the Pharisaic tradition and the Sadducean, and later Karaite, tradition lies precisely here), and with a different starting-day the day on which the Feast of Weeks falls differs too. The starting-point written in this chapter is "from such time as thou beginnest to put the sickle to the corn," a mark of husbandry; whether it is the same day as the wave-sheaf mark of Leviticus 23, each text states in its own words, and this sermon sets the three starting-points side by side only (this chapter = the day the sickle is put in; Lev 23 = the morrow after the sabbath of the wave sheaf; Num 28:26 speaks only of "the day of the firstfruits, when ye bring a new meat offering unto the LORD") and does not adjudicate. Further: the Feast of Weeks in this chapter has neither the words "holy convocation" nor "ye shall do no servile work," whereas Leviticus 23:21 and Numbers 28:26 both have them — presence and absence are set side by side in Part Five, and one place is not used to supply another.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: the Feast of Weeks begins its count from the falling of the sickle, and counts on to the freewill offering and the rejoicing of seven classes of persons — the harvest is one's own, but the root is that sentence, "thou wast a bondman in Egypt."

### Part Three: The Feast of Tabernacles after the Ingathering, and the General Rule of the Three Appearings (Deut 16:13–17; 26:00–37:00)

[Fact] Verse 13 gives the season: "Thou shalt observe the feast of tabernacles seven days, after that thou hast gathered in thy corn and thy wine:" The season is still a mark of husbandry: after the gathering in (ʾāsap H622, to gather in) is finished — the threshing floor (gōren H1637) and the winepress (yeqeḇ H3342), two of the three sources of the send-off bundle at 15:14 (the flock, the floor, and the winepress) reappearing here; registered as standing so. This chapter gives no month and no day-number; the day-number at Leviticus 23:34 and Numbers 29:12 is the fifteenth day of the seventh month, set side by side in Part Five. The name of the feast is in the original ḥag hassukkôt (sukkôt H5521, booths) — but a fact of the whole chapter must be registered: this chapter gives the name of the feast only, and does not give the command to dwell in booths; the command to dwell in booths stands at Leviticus 23:42, "Ye shall dwell in booths seven days," and this chapter does not have it. Verse 14 is the second list of rejoicing: "And thou shalt rejoice in thy feast, thou, and thy son, and thy daughter, and thy manservant, and thy maidservant, and the Levite, the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow, that are within thy gates." The seven classes are the same as at v. 11, with a small difference of wording: v. 11 reads "the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow, that are among you," and v. 14 "the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow, that are within thy gates" — the list is the same and the phrasing differs slightly; both are entered word for word side by side, and no uniformity is forced. Verse 15 closes the Tabernacles section and gives the chapter's strongest sentence of rejoicing: "Seven days shalt thou keep a solemn feast unto the LORD thy God in the place which the LORD shall choose: because the LORD thy God shall bless thee in all thine increase, and in all the works of thine hands, therefore thou shalt surely rejoice." The blessing has two faces — the increase (təḇûʾâ H8393) and the works of thine hands — in intertext with the blessing-face at 15:10 ("in all that thou puttest thine hand unto"); "thou shalt surely rejoice" is in the original wəhāyîṯā ʾaḵ śāmēaḥ (śāmaḥ H8055), ʾaḵ being a particle of restriction, "wholly, only": rejoicing here is not the decoration of the feast, it is the feast's whole state. Verse 16 is the general rule: "Three times in a year shall all thy males appear before the LORD thy God in the place which he shall choose; in the feast of unleavened bread, and in the feast of weeks, and in the feast of tabernacles: and they shall not appear before the LORD empty:" The subject of the obligation is "all thy males" (zākar H2145, male); and at this point the name of the first feast is changed: it stands first as "the feast of unleavened bread" and not "the passover" — one feast under two names running side by side (the Passover is the name from the victim, the Feast of Unleavened Bread the name from the bread), and this chapter has both names (the Passover at v. 1, the Feast of Unleavened Bread at v. 16); Exodus 23:15 and 34:18 likewise use the Feast of Unleavened Bread; entered side by side for the record. "They shall not appear before the LORD empty" — "empty" is in the original rēqām (H7387), the same word as at 15:13, where the man released is not to be let go empty: a man must neither send his brother away empty nor come before God empty — one word governing both ends; the intertext is registered. Verse 17 gives the measure of the general rule: "Every man shall give as he is able, according to the blessing of the LORD thy God which he hath given thee." The gift (mattənat H4979) follows a double proportion: as he himself is able, and according to the blessing given — the same measure as the freewill offering at v. 10, appearing three times in the chapter as a whole (v. 10, the blessing at v. 15, and v. 17), the closing sentence of the sections on giving.

[Argument] This is an inference: the season of the Feast of Tabernacles is chosen after the ingathering, and it is no accident of the farming slack: it is precisely when the barns are full that a man is most apt to enter the increase under his own name, and the feast at just this moment brings the whole people to the chosen place and, with "shall bless thee," enters the account back where it belongs — rejoicing is the way the account is kept, and only when the account is kept right can the rejoicing be "surely," wholly. This is an inference from the reason-sentence at v. 15. A further inference lies between the "males" of the general rule and the "seven classes" of the lists: the obligation of appearing is entered under the name of the males, while the list of rejoicing is opened to the whole household and to the stranger — the register in which the obligation is entered and the actual composition of the rejoicing are not the same, and the text writes both exactly as they stand; the one who preaches reads both exactly as they stand and does not let one swallow the other. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) John 7 records Jesus going up to Jerusalem at the Feast of Tabernacles ("the Jews' feast of tabernacles was at hand"), the pilgrim crowds of the feast forming the background of that narrative; and the scene of the branches at John 12, when the Lord is welcomed into the city, is also often mentioned in discussions of the festal customs of Tabernacles and Passover. Both registers concern the presence of the feasts within the New Testament narrative; neither asserts that the New Testament re-established the feasts. Further: Hebrews speaks of earthly life as sojourning and as a tent (Heb 11:9, 13), which has an echo in the history of reading with the vocabulary of v. 7 of this chapter, "go unto thy tents" — registered only.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] That the "booths" of the Feast of Tabernacles have in this chapter neither command nor explanation, and stand in the name of the feast only, has always been a point of discussion; the readings are set side by side: (a) this chapter presupposes that its hearers already know the festal custom (the Tabernacles command of Leviticus 23 standing earlier), and therefore names the feast and its face of celebration only; (b) this chapter's interest lies in pilgrimage and rejoicing, and the details of the rite give way to the framework of centralized worship. The division of labour among the three texts (Lev 23 giving the details of the rite, Num 29 the list of sacrifices, and this chapter the pilgrimage and the rejoicing) is set side by side at the level of textual fact; which reading explains that division, this sermon does not adjudicate. Further: the ʾaḵ śāmēaḥ of v. 15 was used in rabbinic tradition as one of the textual bases for deriving the intensity of the rejoicing at the Feast of Tabernacles ("wholly joyful" being extended to mean that the whole course of the feast must be joyful) — a register of the history of reading; this sermon registers the existence of that reading only and does not adopt its derivation.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: after the ingathering the Feast of Tabernacles is kept, and the rejoicing must be whole; three times a year, not empty, each giving as he is able — the general rule gathers the three feasts into one rule that runs through the whole year.

### Part Four: Judges in the Several Cities, and the Two Prohibitions beside the Altar (Deut 16:18–22; 37:00–47:00)

[Fact] Verse 18 changes the scene: "Judges and officers shalt thou make thee in all thy gates, which the LORD thy God giveth thee, throughout thy tribes: and they shall judge the people with just judgment." The place moves back from "the place which he shall choose" to "in all thy gates" — the feasts are centralized, the judiciary is distributed. The offices are of two kinds: judges (šōpəṭîm, from šāpaṭ H8199, to judge, to govern) and officers (šōṭərîm H7860, scribes, officers); they are set "throughout thy tribes" (šēḇeṭ H7626); and the standard is one sentence: "with just judgment" (mišpāṭ-ṣeḏeq; ṣeḏeq H6664, righteousness, justice). Verse 19 is three prohibitions: "Thou shalt not wrest judgment; thou shalt not respect persons, neither take a gift: for a gift doth blind the eyes of the wise, and pervert the words of the righteous." To wrest judgment is in the original nāṭâ (H5186) mišpāṭ, to bend judgment aside; to respect persons is nākar (H5234) pānîm, to recognize a face, that is, to show partiality; the gift — in the original šōḥaḏ (H7810), a bribe, which the KJV renders "a gift" — occurs twice, and its consequences are two sentences: it blinds the eyes of the wise (ʿiwwēr H5786, of the same root as "blind") and perverts the words of the righteous (sālap H5557, to twist) — what a bribe wounds is not only the poor man's case, but the wise man's eyes and the righteous man's tongue. Verse 20 is the keynote sentence of the chapter's judicial section: "That which is altogether just shalt thou follow, that thou mayest live, and inherit the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee." "That which is altogether just" is in the original ṣeḏeq ṣeḏeq, the word doubled for weight; "follow" is rāḏap (H7291), a verb of chasing and hunting — justice is not waited for, it is pursued; and the effect is life and the inheriting of the land (yāraš H3423) — judicial justice is here tied directly to living and possessing the land, of the same family as the "live and possess" sentences at 4:1 and 8:1; the intertext is registered. Verses 21–22 are the two prohibitions at the altar: "Thou shalt not plant thee a grove of any trees near unto the altar of the LORD thy God, which thou shalt make thee. Neither shalt thou set thee up any image; which the LORD thy God hateth." The grove is in the original ʾăšērâ (H842), which the Chinese base text here renders "a wooden idol" (the register at 12:3 and 7:5 being the same; the 2010 edition reads "Asherah"); the verb of planting is nāṭaʿ (H5193), and the thing forbidden is "any trees" (kol-ʿēṣ; ʿēṣ H6086) — planting a tree is in itself no sin; planted beside the altar as an idol, it is forbidden. For the image (maṣṣēḇâ H4676) the verb is to set up (qûm H6965, in the causative stem); and the closing sentence is "which the LORD thy God hateth" (śānēʾ H8130). The connection of the two prohibitions with 17:1 has been registered in the Base texts paragraph (ii): the three verses each carry a samekh and form their own closed section, and the chapter boundary falls between v. 22 and 17:1; the word of abhorrence at 17:1 is "an abomination" (tôʿăḇâ H8441), entered separately from the "hateth" of this verse — one subject (the purity of the altar), two words of abhorrence, both Chinese renderings standing side by side; the main text does not cross the boundary to expound chapter 17.

[Argument] This is an inference: the position of this section is the hinge of the whole chapter. The feasts gather men from the several cities to one place; when the feasts are over, men must scatter back to the several cities — the judges of v. 18 are set for the days of the scattering: rejoicing has its feasts, justice has no off-season. If centralized worship is not undergirded by distributed justice, the feasts are left as a bustle three times a year; this chapter writes the judges into the feast chapter, and the order itself is the sermon. This is an inference from the arrangement of the chapter. A further inference concerns the "near unto the altar" of v. 21: the nearer the forbidden tree stands to the altar, the greater the problem — the pollution of worship never begins with a whole grove of idols; it begins with one tree that stands "near unto the altar": near at hand, familiar to the eye, added to year by year, until it has become part of the worship. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) Of the echoes of the pursuit of "righteousness" in the New Testament, one line is registered: the Sermon on the Mount's "seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness" (Matt 6:33) and "Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness" (Matt 5:6) belong to the same semantic field as the pursuit of ṣeḏeq in this section — the intertext is registered only. For the appointment of judges the New Testament has no corresponding institution, and at the level of reading this section registers echoes of vocabulary only and makes no institutional analogy.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Two registers side by side. First, the appointment of local judges in v. 18, set against the counsel of Moses' father-in-law to appoint officers in Exodus 18 and Moses' own account of appointing officers in Deuteronomy 1:9–18: the accounts of the three places (the proposer, the lists of ranks, the place of appointment) differ among themselves, a standing topic in the comparison of the Pentateuch's institutional narratives; this chapter gives only the fixed rule, "in all thy gates, throughout thy tribes," and does not trace the history of the appointment back; the three are set side by side, and no editorial relation is arranged. Second, the actual fortunes of groves and images in the history of Israel (the Books of Kings repeatedly recording their destruction and rebuilding, and the pillar-shaped remains known to archaeology) are much discussed; this sermon registers only the textual existence of the two prohibitions and the existence of their echo in the Books of Kings (the command of destruction at Exodus 34:13, "But ye shall destroy their altars, break their images, and cut down their groves," checked directly; see the cross-references of Part Five) and does not expand a historical reconstruction.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: when the feast breaks up, the judges of the several cities take over — justice is to be pursued, the gift refused, and beside the altar no tree is to be planted and no image set up; the word of abhorrence is spoken in the open.

### Part Five: The Four Feast Lists Set Side by Side (47:00–57:00)

[Fact] This part only sets things side by side and makes no adjudication. The four lists are: this chapter (Deut 16:1–17), Exodus 23:14–19, Leviticus 23, and Numbers 28:16–29:38 (the festal-sacrifice part). They are set side by side item by item as follows.

**Names and number of the feasts.** This chapter: the Passover (with the Feast of Unleavened Bread included, the list at v. 16 naming the Feast of Unleavened Bread), the Feast of Weeks, the Feast of Tabernacles — three pilgrim feasts. Exodus 23: the Feast of Unleavened Bread, the Feast of Harvest, and the Feast of Ingathering — likewise three feasts, with two names differing: the "feast of harvest" answering to this chapter's "feast of weeks," and the "feast of ingathering" to this chapter's "feast of tabernacles," the feasts named from the work and not from the number or the booths. Leviticus 23: the Passover, the Feast of Unleavened Bread, the Firstfruits (the wave sheaf), the Feast of Weeks (told in that chapter by way of the counting and the new meat offering), the Feast of Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and the Feast of Tabernacles — all seven festal seasons of the year complete, this chapter's three feasts being only the three among them that carry the character of pilgrimage. Numbers 28–29: the whole year's daily, sabbath, new-moon, and festal sacrifices arranged by month and day, the names of the feasts the same as in Leviticus 23, and the content throughout a list of sacrifices.

**The dating.** This chapter: the Passover is given the month only (the month of Abib), the Feast of Weeks the reckoning only (counting the seven weeks from the putting of the sickle), and the Feast of Tabernacles the farming season only (after the ingathering) — not one of the three feasts is given a number of month and day. Leviticus 23: the Passover on the fourteenth day of the first month (23:5, "In the fourteenth day of the first month at even is the LORD's passover"), the Feast of Unleavened Bread from the fifteenth day for seven days (23:6), the Feast of Tabernacles from the fifteenth day of the seventh month for seven days (23:34), and a solemn assembly on the eighth day (23:36). Numbers 28:16–17: the Passover on the fourteenth day of the first month, the feast on the fifteenth; Numbers 29:12: the Feast of Tabernacles on the fifteenth day of the seventh month. The difference between light and shade in the dating is registered here: this chapter fixes the feasts by month, by reckoning, and by farming season; Leviticus and Numbers fix them by month and day; the two sets of reckoning stand side by side.

**The starting-point of the reckoning (the Feast of Weeks).** This chapter, v. 9: from such time as thou beginnest to put the sickle to the corn. Leviticus 23:15–16: "And ye shall count unto you from the morrow after the sabbath, from the day that ye brought the sheaf of the wave offering; seven sabbaths shall be complete: Even unto the morrow after the seventh sabbath shall ye number fifty days." Numbers 28:26: "Also in the day of the firstfruits, when ye bring a new meat offering unto the LORD, after your weeks be out" — the day of the firstfruits, when a new meat offering is brought, only. The three starting-marks — the putting of the sickle, the morrow after the sabbath of the wave sheaf, the day of the firstfruits offering — are set side by side; whether they are the same day is not adjudicated.

**The centre of the content.** This chapter: the place (the chosen place), two lists of rejoicing, two roots of remembrance (v. 3, remember all the days of thy life; v. 12, remember that thou wast a bondman), and giving as each is able — with no details of sacrifice and no words of holy convocation (the solemn assembly appearing only at v. 8, of the seventh day of Unleavened Bread). Exodus 23: feasts and laws intermingled — appearing empty forbidden (23:15, "and none shall appear before me empty:"), the blood of the sacrifice not to be offered with leavened bread and the fat not to remain until the morning (23:18), the first of the firstfruits to be brought into the house and a kid not to be seethed in its mother's milk (23:19); the details of sacrifice are likewise brief. Leviticus 23: holy convocation and abstention from work marked verse by verse (23:7, 21, 36, and elsewhere), the command of the booths and their materials (23:40, 42: "the boughs of goodly trees, branches of palm trees, and the boughs of thick trees, and willows of the brook"; "Ye shall dwell in booths seven days"), and the reason of remembrance for the feast given in the Tabernacles section (23:43, "That your generations may know that I made the children of Israel to dwell in booths, when I brought them out of the land of Egypt") — the Tabernacles section of this chapter has no such reason-sentence; the distribution of the reason-sentences is set side by side. Numbers 28–29: the kinds and numbers of the sacrifices day by day (as with the diminishing bullocks over the seven days of Tabernacles, checked in this series' sermon on Numbers 29), with no list of rejoicing and no sentence of remembrance.

**The place of the solemn assembly.** This chapter, v. 8: the seventh day of Unleavened Bread. Leviticus 23:36 and Numbers 29:35: the eighth day of the Feast of Tabernacles ("On the eighth day ye shall have a solemn assembly: ye shall do no servile work therein," Num 29:35). One name, two places — registered side by side, and not harmonized.

**The Passover victim and the manner of eating.** This chapter, v. 2: of the flock and the herd; v. 7, the verb bāšal (the Chinese base text "roast," with the note "or, boil"), and in the morning a return to the tents after eating. Exodus 12:8–9: the lamb, roast with fire, "Eat not of it raw, nor sodden at all with water," eaten with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, and finished that night (12:10, "And ye shall let nothing of it remain until the morning"). The two places are set side by side; the differences (the range of the victim, the word for the manner of cooking, and where men go after eating) stand out plainly, and this sermon does not take one place as the base text with which to rewrite the other.

**The eighth day of Tabernacles and the booths command.** Leviticus 23 has both the eighth day and the command to dwell in booths; this chapter has neither. This chapter has the lists of rejoicing; the Tabernacles section of Leviticus 23 has no list (its list-like texts stand in other books). Presence and absence are registered only, and one place is not used to supply another.

[Argument] This is an inference: the four lists are not four calendars fighting one another, but four kinds of writing writing the same year in four ways — Exodus 23 is the festal clauses within a book of the covenant, Leviticus 23 the priests' festal calendar, Numbers 28–29 the ledger of the sacrifices, and this chapter the feasts within a sermon address: spoken to men standing east of the Jordan, so that only three things remain weightiest — go to one place, bring all the people, and tell the root. Genre determines fullness and brevity; fullness and brevity do not determine truth and falsehood. To lay the four side by side is reverence; to take one and delete or rewrite another by it is to cross the boundary. This is an argument.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) The presence of the feasts in the New Testament narrative corresponds for the most part to this chapter's pilgrim framework, and is registered accordingly: Jesus going up to Jerusalem with His parents as a child to keep the Passover (Luke 2:41–42), His going up to the feasts repeatedly as a man (John 2:13; 7:2–10; 11:55), and the gathering of the apostles at Pentecost (Acts 2:1) — all of them narrative registers of the feasts fixed by the four lists being observed in the late Second Temple period; this sermon registers only the existence of their correspondence with this chapter's framework of pilgrim feasts and does not expand it.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] The sources and editorial relations of the four lists (which stands first, and how each was supplemented) are one of the central topics of Pentateuchal study, and the conclusions of the schools differ among themselves; the observance of the festal calendar in the Second Temple period and its divergences (such as the dispute over the starting-day and the dispute over calendars) likewise have their own monographs. The position of this sermon registers only the question and the textual facts: the differences among the four lists in their present form are as set out above, and the existence of a difference does not mean that any one list can cancel another; the details of the history of observance lie outside the scope of this sermon's adjudication.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: four lists, four kinds of writing — let the ledger keep the dates, and the priests' calendar keep the rites; this chapter's business is only to bring the people to one place, to call the whole list by name, and to tell the root plainly.

## Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Fact] The chapter closes at v. 22: "which the LORD thy God hateth." Look back at the chapter's chain of closing sentences: v. 8 closes the Passover section on the solemn assembly, v. 12 closes the Weeks section on remembrance and the statutes, v. 15 closes the Tabernacles section on "thou shalt surely rejoice," v. 17 closes the general rule on giving as each is able, v. 20 closes the judicial section on "that which is altogether just … and inherit the land," and v. 22 closes the whole chapter on what God hateth — rejoicing and hatred answering one another front and back: the feasts in which God would have men wholly rejoice, and the thing beside the altar that God hateth, are spoken out in full within one and the same chapter.

[Argument] This is an inference: the final definition Deuteronomy 16 gives to the calendar lies not in the dates but in the people: at the year's three turning-points the whole people eat the bread of affliction in one place, count the days of the harvest, and keep the feast of booths in celebration; and on the days outside the turning-points there are judges in the several cities pursuing justice, and beside the altar no man plants a tree. Time is filled with remembrance, space is upheld by justice, and worship is kept clean — that is this chapter's year. This is an argument.

[Summary] One sentence to gather the whole: "therefore thou shalt surely rejoice" — this command stands a little past the middle of the chapter (v. 15), with the bitterness of the Exodus to be remembered before it and the justice of the several cities to be pursued after it; rejoicing set between remembrance and justice — that is Deuteronomy's rejoicing.

## Devotional Extract

Deuteronomy 16 will not let three things be empty. The hand must not be empty: "they shall not appear before the LORD empty" — each man gives as he is able, according to the blessing of the LORD his God which He hath given him. The table must not be empty: the rejoicing has a list — son and daughter, manservant and maidservant, the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow — and not one name on it may be dropped; a rejoicing that is left with only one's own household does not count in Scripture as having kept the feast. And the heart must not be empty: before the feast has even begun, the heart is first to be filled with remembrance — in the month of Abib, remembrance of the night of the leading out; at the Feast of Weeks, remembrance of "thou wast a bondman in Egypt." And the last command is the one that sounds least like a command: "therefore thou shalt surely rejoice." It turns out that in God's sight rejoicing is not a mood but a duty — the increase is His blessing, and the works of our hands are His blessing, and all that a man has to do is not to let the gratitude leak away. The man who has kept the feast goes back to his tent, and the days still have to be lived in the several cities; but the ground of that whole rejoicing does not lie in the feast — it lies in the hand of the One who blesses.

"Seven days shalt thou keep a solemn feast unto the LORD thy God in the place which the LORD shall choose: because the LORD thy God shall bless thee in all thine increase, and in all the works of thine hands, therefore thou shalt surely rejoice." (Scripture quoted in the devotional extract: Deut 16:15, KJV, verbatim.)

## Translation Note

This English manuscript is a first translation, made paragraph by paragraph in full from the finalized Chinese manuscript on Deuteronomy 16, "Therefore Thou Shalt Surely Rejoice," which has passed strong re-verification in Chinese (the cross-model review leg and the Chinese strong re-verification round of the sixth Deuteronomy batch are recorded in the Chinese manuscript itself; that review record is not reproduced here, this note standing in its place). All Scripture quotations in English, including those in the cross-references to Exodus 12, Exodus 23, Exodus 34, Leviticus 23, Numbers 28–29, Deuteronomy 15, Deuteronomy 17, and 1 Corinthians 5 and in the devotional extract, are embedded verbatim from the King James Version as held in the anchor text of this chapter (申16-KJV.txt, fetched in full on 2026-10-25) and in the KJV texts fetched on the same occasion; no quotation has been back-translated from the Chinese. One discipline of the source is preserved exactly: this chapter gives the Feast of Tabernacles its name only and no command to dwell in booths — that command stands at Leviticus 23:42 — and nothing of it has been supplied into the translation beyond what the Chinese manuscript itself registers. The correspondence of numbering between the CUNPSS base text of the Chinese manuscript (22 verses) and the KJV (22 verses, all with text) is verse by verse, with no empty number and no fusion, and is registered in the Base texts paragraph and Endnote 1. The counts were recounted on the KJV's own letter and registered in Endnote 2; where they differ from the Chinese account — the divine name 23 times rather than 21 (the two Chinese possessive forms falling together into the KJV's single "the LORD thy God," 20 times, with 3 occurrences without the possessive), the phrase "seven days" 4 times rather than 7, the sacrifice family 4 times rather than 6, "altar" once rather than twice, and "gates" 4 times on its own lexical basis — they are registered there and not harmonized; the feast-name account (passover 4, feast of weeks 2, feast of tabernacles 2, feast of unleavened bread 1), "rejoice" 3, "remember" 2, "Egypt" 5, the blessing family 3, and the list of seven classes twice agree with the Chinese account in full. The Strong's numbers are carried over as they stand from the Chinese manuscript; the one pending item of that manuscript, the etymology and number of missat at v. 10, is carried over as pending and no number has been asserted for it in translation. This English version is a first translation pending proofreading; it has not been published, archived to a repository, or pushed.

# The Number of the Names — A Sermon on Numbers 1

- Text: Numbers 1:1-54
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending cross-review (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Numbers 1; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations; verses not checked word for word are given in summary only, never as quotations.
- Note on the title: The tentative title "reckoned by the number of the names" was checked against the base text of Numbers 1:2. In the Chinese text the verb phrase does not stand in one unbroken sequence with "the number of the names," so the title was corrected to the base text's own unbroken wording, "the number of the names" — in the KJV, "with the number of their names" (Num 1:2). See Endnote 2.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Leviticus closes at Mount Sinai, and Numbers opens at the same mountain: on the first day of the second month of the second year after the exodus, the LORD commands Moses in the tabernacle to number Israel. The chapter's fifty-four verses appear to contain a single act — a census: first the time and place of the command (vv. 1-19), then the results, tribe by tribe, with the total of 603,550 (vv. 20-46), and finally the exception of the tribe of Levi and its charge (vv. 47-54). This sermon's sections follow that structure given by the text itself: the time and place; the command and its scope; the results read item by item; the Levitical exception and the foreshadowing of the camp's order; and the close, in which the people do as they were commanded. What the sermon traces is not a table of population but a theological thread: those numbered here are numbered not by heads but by names — by families, by clans, by the number of the names, one by one; those numbered are not labourers but a people arrayed by their armies; and the one tribe deliberately not numbered among them is appointed to stand round about the tabernacle. In this chapter a number is never cold statistics; it is belonging, order, and guard duty.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 Time and place: the second year, the second month, the first day — in the wilderness of Sinai, in the tabernacle

(Fact) The chapter's first sentence fixes time, place, and speaker all at once: "And the LORD spake unto Moses in the wilderness of Sinai, in the tabernacle of the congregation, on the first day of the second month, in the second year after they were come out of the land of Egypt, saying," (Num 1:1, KJV) Set this date back onto the Pentateuch's timeline. The exodus belongs to the first year; the tabernacle was set up on the first day of the first month of the second year (Exodus 40, Summary); the whole of Leviticus was delivered at Sinai after the tabernacle was raised (Lev 27:34, Summary); and here, on the first day of the second month, the people have not yet left Sinai. From the exodus to this chapter, the narrative has scarcely moved: the place is still Sinai, the camp still the same camp. But the people's identity has changed: at the exodus they were slaves led out; now, in the same place, they are to be formally numbered and arrayed as a host. The place has not changed; the way they are addressed has — and that is the first fact of the chapter to hold on to.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) The English name "Numbers," like the Greek title Arithmoi ("numbers") in the Septuagint, is taken from the two great censuses of this chapter and of chapter 26; the Hebrew title, Bəmidbar, "in the wilderness," is taken from this chapter's opening words, "in the wilderness of Sinai." This sermon simply sets the two titles side by side without resting any conclusion about composition on them; yet together they name the book's two faces: on one side, numbers — counting, arraying, registering; on the other, the wilderness — the host counted here must walk forty years in it. First the census, then the wilderness; the array fixed in the census is the array of the march.

(Fact) Note also where the speech is given: not on the mountaintop, not in the cloud, but "in the tabernacle of the congregation." Leviticus opens with the LORD calling to Moses from the tabernacle (Lev 1:1, Summary); Numbers opens with the LORD speaking to Moses in the tabernacle. The words of the book of sacrifices and holiness, and the words of the book of camps and marches, issue from the same place. (Application) That place is itself a sermon: God numbers His people not from a reviewing stand or a battlefield but from the tent in which He dwells. From its first verse, the census in this chapter is not the staff work of an army but the work of a worshipping community — first the tabernacle, first God dwelling in the midst, and only then the people round about being numbered, arrayed, and appointed. Reverse that order and a census is mere conscription; keep the text's order and the census becomes the re-placing of a redeemed people according to their distance from, and relation to, the tabernacle.

(Fact) One more reckoning of time: this chapter falls on the first day of the second month, and the Passover fell on the fourteenth day of the first month. On the text's own timeline, before leaving Sinai the people have just kept the Passover of the second year (Numbers 9, Summary), and immediately afterwards are numbered as a host. First the exodus is remembered; then the fighting men are counted. (Application) That order matters for today's hearers too. When we count what we have — people, resources, strength — without first remembering that we are a people led out, counting easily turns into boasting or panic: more, and we grow proud; less, and we grow afraid. This chapter sets the census after the Passover and inside the tabernacle: the counters are first settled inside the story of redemption, and only then is the register opened. Every figure on the register belongs first to that story, and only second to the army.

(Fact) Twice in its fifty-four verses the chapter writes "as the LORD commanded" as a small conclusion: verse 19 records the numbering itself — "As the LORD commanded Moses, so he numbered them in the wilderness of Sinai." (Num 1:19, KJV) — and verse 54 closes the chapter on the same note (Summary: the children of Israel did according to all that the LORD commanded Moses). The command was given on the first day of the second month, and on that same day the congregation was assembled and the numbering carried out (v. 18; see the next section). Within one chapter, from command to completion, head and tail are clasped by "commanded" and "so did they." (Application) A sermon on this chapter need not romanticise the people's enthusiasm; the text records no tears. It records two things only: God spoke, and the people did as He said. The beauty of this chapter is a quiet beauty — more than six hundred thousand people, each in his place in one register, without clamour, only order. And that order begins with the unadorned time and place of verse 1: the second year, the second month, the first day; the wilderness of Sinai; the tabernacle.

### II. 08:00-24:00 The command and its scope: by the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all that are able to go forth to war

(Fact) The command proper begins at verse 2: "Take ye the sum of all the congregation of the children of Israel, after their families, by the house of their fathers, with the number of their names, every male by their polls;" (Num 1:2, KJV) The sentence works at three levels, and a sermon must unfold them one by one. The first is scope: "all the congregation of the children of Israel" — the object of the numbering is the whole congregation, not an elite. The second is unit: "after their families, by the house of their fathers" — the count does not scatter individuals; it proceeds through the genealogy in layers: house, clan, tribe. The third, and most surprising, is method: "with the number of their names" — what is taken is the number of the names. The register records not merely a total but names: every man numbered is entered under his own name. Verse 18 records the execution in exact agreement with the command: "And they assembled all the congregation together on the first day of the second month, and they declared their pedigrees after their families, by the house of their fathers, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, by their polls." (Num 1:18, KJV) "They declared their pedigrees" is the operative clause: those numbered are not passively ticked off; each man speaks, declaring from which house and clan he comes. The census is thus not the reduction of persons to figures, but every person's own acknowledgement of his belonging.

(Fact) Verse 3 then draws the boundary of the numbered: "From twenty years old and upward, all that are able to go forth to war in Israel: thou and Aaron shall number them by their armies." (Num 1:3, KJV) The boundary has two edges: age — from twenty years old and upward — and capacity — all that are able to go forth to war. The word rendered "armies" is ṣāḇāʾ (Strong's H6635), whose basic sense is army or host, used also of the host of heaven and of those who stand in service; its use here shows the registration to be a military arraying, not a tax census. And the verb that runs through the whole chapter, "to number," is pāqad (Strong's H6485), whose semantic field is far wider than "to count": it can mean to muster, to inspect, to visit with care, and also to appoint and entrust. In this chapter the same verb numbers the people (vv. 3, 19) and, at verse 50, appoints — appoints the Levites over the tabernacle (see section IV). Numbering and appointing stand side by side in one verb: a man who is pāqad is not only a man who has been counted, but a man who has been entrusted with a place. (Application) That lexical fact governs the reading of the whole chapter. If numbering were inventory-taking, the bigger and faster the better; but the word the text uses, and the method it records, forbid that reading: registered name by name, each declaring his pedigree, arrayed by armies — this is an inspection conducted with honour. God counts His people as a king reviews his host, and as a father calls his children's names one by one: the count must be exact because every name counts.

(Fact) Moses does not execute the command alone. Verse 4 provides that a man of every tribe, head of the house of his fathers, shall stand with Moses and Aaron (Summary); verses 5-15 then list the twelve helpers by name (Summary presentation; names as in the KJV): of Reuben, Elizur the son of Shedeur; of Simeon, Shelumiel the son of Zurishaddai; of Judah, Nahshon the son of Amminadab; of Issachar, Nethaneel the son of Zuar; of Zebulun, Eliab the son of Helon; of the children of Joseph, of Ephraim, Elishama the son of Ammihud, and of Manasseh, Gamaliel the son of Pedahzur; of Benjamin, Abidan the son of Gideoni; of Dan, Ahiezer the son of Ammishaddai; of Asher, Pagiel the son of Ocran; of Gad, Eliasaph the son of Deuel; of Naphtali, Ahira the son of Enan. Verse 16 sums up the twelve (Summary): these were the renowned of the congregation, princes of the tribes of their fathers, heads of thousands in Israel. Verse 44 looks back and records that the numbering was done by Moses and Aaron and these twelve princes together (Summary). (Application) This list deserves to be read slowly, name by name, because it demonstrates the chapter's central action: those who bear responsibility stand at the head of the register under their own names first, and only then number others. The names of the twelve princes are recorded in full, with their fathers' names — in God's register, authority is not anonymous; those who lead are named first, recorded first, inspected first. In any community today, a leader who is willing to count others but unwilling to give his own name first has already departed from this chapter's order.

(Fact) The list also carries a structural fact that must not be read past: the twelve tribes do not coincide exactly with the twelve sons of Jacob. The tribe of Levi is not among the numbered (see section IV); and Joseph counts as two, Ephraim and Manasseh, represented by Elishama and Gamaliel respectively (v. 10, Summary). One tribe out, one tribe doubled — the total remains twelve. Verse 20 calls Reuben Israel's eldest son (Summary), and the numbering begins with the firstborn's descendants. (Scholarly and historical discussion) Commentators regularly read this arrangement together with the close of Genesis: Jacob adopts Joseph's two sons, counting them with their uncles (Genesis 48, Summary), and lays his hands on Ephraim and Manasseh (Genesis 48, Summary); the tribal constitution of this chapter is that adoption worked out on the register. This sermon sets the inner-biblical comparison side by side and builds nothing further on it. As for the father of Gad's prince: verse 14 of this chapter reads "Deuel," while Numbers 2:14 reads "Reuel" — the KJV itself carries both spellings, the underlying Hebrew letters being easily confused, and commentators discuss the point at length; this sermon's list follows this chapter's text and does not merge the two.

### III. 24:00-42:00 The results: the twelve tribes item by item, and the total of 603,550

(Fact) Verses 20-43 are the list of results. Every tribe's entry follows the same form: first the tribe's descendants — the Hebrew heading word is tôlədôt (Strong's H8435), generations, descendants, genealogies — by their families, by the house of their fathers, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, every male able to go forth to war, registered one by one; then the tribe's numbered total. The form is regular to the point of seeming rigid; but it is precisely inside that regularity that a sermon must read the figures out item by item, because the text wrote them down item by item (the following are statements of fact; the figures follow the text, and the verse cited is the verse of each tribe's total): Reuben, 46,500 (v. 21); Simeon, 59,300 (v. 23); Gad, 45,650 (v. 25); Judah, 74,600 (v. 27); Issachar, 54,400 (v. 29); Zebulun, 57,400 (v. 31); Ephraim, 40,500 (v. 33); Manasseh, 32,200 (v. 35); Benjamin, 35,400 (v. 37); Dan, 62,700 (v. 39); Asher, 41,500 (v. 41); Naphtali, 53,400 (v. 43).

(Fact) Now settle the account. In preparing this sermon the twelve items above were added one by one by hand: 46,500 plus 59,300 makes 105,800; plus 45,650, 151,450; plus 74,600, 226,050; plus 54,400, 280,450; plus 57,400, 337,850; plus 40,500, 378,350; plus 32,200, 410,550; plus 35,400, 445,950; plus 62,700, 508,650; plus 41,500, 550,150; plus 53,400, 603,550 — in exact agreement with the total recorded at verse 46: "Even all they that were numbered were six hundred thousand and three thousand and five hundred and fifty." (Num 1:46, KJV) The sum of the parts and the stated total agree to the last man — not a tidiness manufactured by the preacher, but the text's own internally consistent account. (Application) Mark the weight of that consistency. The total of 603,550 is famous — so famous that hearers easily remember the total and skip the items. But the text refuses to give the total alone: it counts first tribe by tribe, house by house, name by name, and only then closes the sum. The total is credible, and preachable, because beneath it lie twelve lines of items, and beneath the twelve lines lie more than six hundred thousand names. A sermon must keep that order here: God never looks at His people as a total only; in His hand the total is names, added one by one.

(Fact) The items also hold contrasts worth pointing out. The largest tribe is Judah, 74,600; next is Dan, 62,700; the smallest is Manasseh, 32,200 — less than half of Judah. And in the order of proceeding, Reuben, the firstborn's tribe, is numbered first, while Gad stands third in the results — though Gad's prince stands eleventh in the list of helpers (v. 14), Gad follows Reuben and Simeon in the numbering (vv. 24-25, Summary), in agreement with the order of Reuben's camp in chapter 2 (Numbers 2, Summary). (Application) The text records these differences of size and order exactly as they stand, without cosmetic adjustment: the large tribe is not inflated, the small tribe is not passed over, the firstborn's tribe is not in fact the largest, and the smallest tribe still has its own prince, its own camp position, and its own line in the register, written in the same words as every other line. In God's register, being numbered has never been conditional on size; Manasseh's 32,200 and Judah's 74,600 are written down in one identical form. For people today who are used to measuring worth by numbers, that is a quiet correction: in this chapter a number is the credential of belonging, not a league table.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) The total of 603,550, and the total population it implies, have long been discussed in scholarship: how, barely a year after the exodus and in the wilderness, could there be a population and an army of this scale? Commentators and historians have worked the question repeatedly. One well-known line of discussion is lexical: the word rendered "thousand," ʾelep (Strong's H505), can in some contexts denote a family unit or a contingent, and on that basis some commentators have proposed that the chapter's figures should be understood in another way; other commentators maintain the literal reading and defend the total from the consistency of the book's narrative. This sermon honestly sets out the existence and the outline of that discussion and decides nothing here: its argument does not rest on any historical conversion of the figure, but on the structure the text itself has written out — numbered name by name, totalled item by item, the total agreeing with the parts. Whatever position a reader takes on the historical question, the sentence this chapter addresses to Israel does not change: you were numbered, name by name, without a single blur.

(Fact) The results section closes at verse 44 (Summary): these are those that were numbered, which Moses and Aaron numbered, and the princes of Israel, being twelve men, each one for the house of his fathers. With that, the five steps are complete, none missing: command (vv. 2-4), list of names (vv. 5-16), execution (vv. 17-19), results (vv. 20-43), total (vv. 44-46). (Application) Read back now to the command of verse 2 — "with the number of their names" — and see that across more than forty verses it has not fallen to the ground once: not by estimate, not by tribal approximation, but by names, tribe by tribe, to the very end. In the least ingratiating of literary forms — a list — this chapter has written its least ambiguous declaration: this people is known, remembered, and set in order. The list is not literary laziness here; it is theological method: love that is real can survive being itemised; belonging that is real lands on every single name.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The Levitical exception: not numbered among them, yet standing round about the tabernacle

(Fact) No sooner is the total reported than the text adds the exception: "But the Levites after the tribe of their fathers were not numbered among them." (Num 1:47, KJV) Verses 48-49 give the ground: "Only thou shalt not number the tribe of Levi, neither take the sum of them among the children of Israel:" (Num 1:49, KJV) Mark the placement of this note: it is not announced in advance, before the numbering, but appended after the total has been reported. The narrative first lets the reader see a complete total — 603,550 — and only then tells him: in this total, one tribe is deliberately missing. (Application) That "missing" is the loudest sentence in the chapter. The twelve tribes are numbered into the army; Levi is held back entire, and entered in no army register. By human reckoning that is wasted manpower; by the text's reckoning it is another appointment altogether: not every company serves in the same way, and a tribe not numbered into the army is not thereby unnumbered — the Levites are entered in another register (chapter 3 numbers the Levites separately, Summary) and bear another charge. Being set apart sometimes looks exactly like "not being on this list."

(Fact) The Levites' charge is stated completely and concretely at verse 50: "But thou shalt appoint the Levites over the tabernacle of testimony, and over all the vessels thereof, and over all things that belong to it: they shall bear the tabernacle, and all the vessels thereof; and they shall minister unto it, and shall encamp round about the tabernacle." (Num 1:50, KJV) The verb "appoint" is pāqad itself (H6485) — the same verb that numbers the people (see section II). Numbering and appointing in one word is no accident here: as Moses pāqad the people into a host, so God pāqad the Levites into office. The office has three layers: charge — over the tabernacle of testimony and all its vessels; bearing — they carry the tabernacle and its vessels and minister to it; and encamping — they dwell round about the tabernacle. Verse 51 continues with the marching orders (Summary): when the tabernacle sets forward, the Levites take it down; when it is to be pitched, the Levites set it up; and the stranger that comes nigh shall be put to death. The "stranger" here is zār (Strong's H2114): in this chapter's context, not the foreigner by nation but the unauthorised person by office — one who draws near across a boundary not his. The danger at the sanctuary lies not in the number of people but in the approach of the unappointed.

(Fact) Verse 52 draws the general map of the camp in advance (Summary): the children of Israel shall pitch their tents, every man by his own camp and by his own standard, throughout their hosts. The verb "pitch/encamp" is ḥānāh (Strong's H2583), to set up camp and dwell; it will run through the camp arrangement of chapter 2. Verse 53 then states the purpose of the Levites' position: "But the Levites shall pitch round about the tabernacle of testimony, that there be no wrath upon the congregation of the children of Israel: and the Levites shall keep the charge of the tabernacle of testimony." (Num 1:53, KJV) The "charge" they keep is mišmereṯ (Strong's H4931) — the duty, the guard, the thing entrusted to be kept. (Application) Read verses 50 and 53 together and the Levites' position stands clear: they stand between the tabernacle and the congregation. That ring of tents faces two ways: toward the tabernacle, they are the people who minister, carry, and keep; toward the congregation, they are a living boundary, so that the people do not draw near out of order and bring wrath on themselves. Keeping guard here is not exclusion but protection — protection of the sanctuary from offence, and protection of the people from being harmed by the sanctuary's holiness. God gives the innermost ring of positions, nearest to Himself, not to the most numerous tribe but to the one tribe not numbered into the army: nearness and manpower, in this chapter, are never the same thing.

(Fact) One further foreshadowing at the chapter's end must be marked. Verse 52's single sentence — every man by his own camp, every man by his own standard — is expanded by chapter 2 into the complete arrangement of the camp: four camps set on the four sides of the tabernacle, the Levites in the centre (Numbers 2, Summary). The figures numbered in chapter 1 become the establishments of the camps in chapter 2; the twelve princes named in chapter 1 become the captains of chapter 2; the Levites appointed in chapter 1 stand in chapter 2 in the very midst of the camps, round about the tabernacle. (Application) Chapter 1, therefore, is never a free-standing population table; it is the draft of the whole book's marching order: who belongs to which camp, who marches under which standard, who travels in which rank — all of it grows out of this census. The wilderness becomes a road not because the people are many, but because every man is in his place. The chapter's final picture is accordingly not the figure of six hundred thousand but a camp plan not yet unfolded: the tabernacle at the centre, the Levites around it, the twelve tribes arrayed by their standards outside — the order already written down, waiting only for the order to march.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: according to all that the LORD commanded Moses, so did they

(Fact) The chapter closes at verse 54: "And the children of Israel did according to all that the LORD commanded Moses, so did they." (Num 1:54, KJV) Across fifty-four verses, the chapter runs from "the LORD spake unto Moses" (v. 1) to "so did they" (v. 54): between them stand command, list of names, census, total, and exception; at head and tail stand speaking and obeying. The chapter records no speech of response from the people — their response is the completed register and the closed total. (Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, God numbers His people by the number of their names: beneath the total of 603,550 stand men who declared their pedigrees house by house, clan by clan, name by name; in God's hand a number is never a tool for flattening persons, but the way He claims them one by one. Second, everyone numbered has his place: those able to go forth to war are arrayed in the armies, and the Levites stand round about the tabernacle — to be absent from the army register is not to be absent from the register; those set apart to keep the charge and those arrayed to march belong to one and the same book. Third, the chapter's end is not a figure but obedience: the most complete establishment, if the people do not act on it, is only paper — and the text deliberately seals the chapter with one verse: so did they. The story of the census ends; the story of the wilderness is only beginning: the generation numbered here into a host will face its real testing in the chapters that follow (Summary) — but that lies ahead; this chapter nails down the starting point.

(New Testament reading) Two New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. The first is Revelation 7: John first hears the number of those who are sealed (Revelation 7, Summary), and then sees a multitude so great that no man could number it, standing before the throne and before the Lamb (Rev 7:9, Summary). From "the number of those numbered" to "a multitude no man could number," the register of this chapter, in the New Testament reading, points at last to a people too many to count, yet standing one by one before God. The second is Luke 2: the enrolment under Caesar Augustus (Luke 2, Summary) brings Joseph and Mary back to Bethlehem, the city of David — the kings of this world number their people for taxation and rule; in this chapter God numbers His people for arraying and for guarding. Two censuses, two kings: in the contrast the church reads this chapter's depth. (Application) But the layers must be kept straight: Numbers 1 is first of all itself — an actual census at Sinai in the second year after the exodus: command, register, total, and the Levitical exception. The New Testament reading is the light in which the church, in Christ, looks back on it; it must not be used to dissolve this chapter's accounts. The more solid this chapter's accounting — items stated, sums agreeing, names all recorded — the more weight the New Testament's "no man could number" carries by contrast: a people first numbered name by name ends too many to number at all — the two ends of one road of numbering, from Sinai to the throne.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's own hands. Most of us stand on no conspicuous register: not at the front, not at the head of any list — like the Levites, not even on the most noticed table. To such people this chapter speaks with particular clarity: God's book is more than one book. Those numbered into the army are His people; those set apart to stand round about the tabernacle are His people too; Manasseh, small at 32,200, and Judah, great at 74,600, stand in one register, entered in one identical form. What matters is never which list you are on or in which place, but whether your name is in the book of the One who numbers by names, and whether you are standing in the place to which He appointed you. May we not flinch when we declare our own "pedigrees," and not compare ourselves when we take our camp positions — for the God who spoke in the tabernacle, when He numbers us, numbers never heads, but names.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 1 first showed me only figures: forty and six thousand and five hundred, fifty and nine thousand and three hundred, six hundred thousand and three thousand and five hundred and fifty. Reading again, I saw what lies beneath the figures — they declared their pedigrees, name by name, and so were numbered. With God I am never a remainder in a total; He takes the sum of His people with the number of their names, and my name, too, is spoken there. This chapter comforts me in another way as well: the Levites were not numbered among the armies, yet they were appointed round about the tabernacle. I have envied those who stand in the front lines of the host, as if not being on that list meant not counting at all. But the text says that those set apart to keep the charge are appointed no less. So I pray today: Lord, let me rest in the place where Thou hast appointed me, neither envying another man's camp nor despising my own portion. Whether I am numbered among the many or set round about in a quiet place, remember my name, appoint my work, and make me do according to all that Thou commandest.

The father of Eliasaph is "Deuel" at 1:14 in this chapter and "Reuel" at Num 2:14; the underlying Hebrew letters are similar and easily confused in transmission, and commentators discuss the point; this sermon follows this chapter's text and does not merge the two.
5. pāqad (Strong's H6485): the chapter's verb of numbering, as at 1:3 and in the report at 1:19 (checked occurrence by occurrence against the Hebrew text); its semantic field includes mustering, inspecting, visiting with care, appointing, and entrusting. At 1:50 the appointment of the Levites over the tabernacle uses this same verb in its appointing sense; sections II and IV rest on this lexical fact alone.
6. tôlədôt (Strong's H8435), "generations, descendants, genealogies": the heading word of each tribal entry in this chapter (vv. 20, 22, 24 and following), the same word that heads the "generations" sections of Genesis. Section III notes the form and builds nothing further on it.
7. ṣāḇāʾ (Strong's H6635), "army, host": used at 1:3 ("by their armies") and 1:52 ("throughout their hosts"), checked against the Hebrew text; the KJV renderings are followed.
8. zār (Strong's H2114): the "stranger" of 1:51 is this word; in this chapter's context it denotes the unauthorised person who draws near across an office boundary. Section IV states the text's usage and does not interpret it as nationality.
9. ḥānāh (Strong's H2583), "to encamp, to pitch": used at 1:50 ("encamp round about the tabernacle"), 1:52, and 1:53, checked against the Hebrew text; it is also the running verb of the camp arrangement in chapter 2.
10. mišmereṯ (Strong's H4931), "charge, guard-duty, that which is entrusted to be kept": at 1:53 the Levites "keep the charge" of the tabernacle of testimony, and the charge kept is this word, checked against the Hebrew text. The sermon states the lexical fact and the text's usage only.
11. ʾelep (Strong's H505) and the discussion of the large numbers: the chapter's totals are counted in "thousands," and ʾelep in some contexts can denote a family or contingent unit; scholarship accordingly reads the total in different ways. The sermon sets this out at the (Scholarly and historical discussion) level with the outline of both positions and decides nothing. That the twelve items sum exactly to the stated total of 603,550 is an internal fact of the text, independent of the historical-conversion debate.
12. The two titles of the book: the Hebrew title Bəmidbar is taken from 1:1, "in the wilderness" (of Sinai); the Greek title Arithmoi ("numbers") and the English "Numbers" alike derive from the two great censuses of this chapter and of chapter 26. Common scholarly ground, noted at the level of form only.
13. The relations of Revelation 7 and Luke 2 to this chapter are intertextual and thematic relations checkable in the New Testament text itself; this sermon places them at the (New Testament reading) level and does not read them back as the original sense of Numbers.

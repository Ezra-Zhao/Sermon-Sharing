# Be Not Afraid of Them — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 20

> Status: First translation, pending proofreading. This is the first English translation of the finalized Chinese sermon on Deuteronomy 20, "Be Not Afraid of Them." All Scripture quotations in this English text are taken verbatim, including punctuation, from the King James Version (KJV), which serves as the base text for quotations in this translation. Quotations are never re-translated from the Chinese. Hebrew transliterations, Strong's numbers, counts, and unresolved registrations follow the Chinese source, except that word counts are re-counted from the English base text (KJV) and registered as they stand in English; where the English count differs from the Chinese count, both are stated side by side and neither is smoothed over.

> Verification of the title: Five candidates were considered. (1) "He Will Fight for You," from the closing words of the priest's declaration in verse 4 — forceful, but it collides in subject with the Deuteronomy 3 sermon, "The LORD Your God Will Fight for You," and it captures only the declaration on the eve of battle, not the three exemptions and the limits on siege warfare. (2) "Proclaim Peace to It First," from verse 10 — the hinge of the chapter's second half, but it misses the fear of the whole army in the first half. (3) "Lest His Brethren's Heart Faint," from verse 8 — it states the truth that fear is contagious, but cannot carry the legal weight of the whole chapter. (4) "Are the Trees of the Field Men?" from the rhetorical question in verse 19 — the most surprising sentence in the chapter, but only one command in the closing section. (5) "Be Not Afraid of Them," from the opening proposition of verse 1 — everything in this chapter grows out of these words: the priest's declaration exists to remove fear (vv. 1–4), the officers' three exemptions and the release of the fearful exist to deal with fear (vv. 5–9), proclaiming peace first keeps fear from turning into indiscriminate killing (vv. 10–15), and the destruction command and the fruit-tree command draw a boundary around war itself, so that neither hatred nor force may be unbounded (vv. 16–20). Candidate (5) is adopted: "Be Not Afraid of Them." The English title is taken from the original wording of KJV 20:1: "be not afraid of them: for the LORD thy God is with thee, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt."

> Note on the base text and collation: In the Chinese source, quotations follow the Chinese Union Version with New Punctuation (CUNPSS, simplified-character "God" edition) as the fixed text, collated verse by verse against the Hebrew BHS and the English KJV; all twenty verses of the chapter were checked first-hand, verse by verse. In this English translation, quotations follow the KJV as the fixed text (chapter anchor: BibleGateway KJV, Deuteronomy 20, checked against the Bolls KJV full-chapter text). The numbering agrees in all three: Deuteronomy 20 has twenty verses, and CUNPSS, BHS, and KJV align verse by verse, with no empty numbers, no fusions, and no offsets (a regular chapter, like chapters 14, 16, 17, 18, and 19; within this book, the final verse of chapter 12 is displaced, chapter 13 has two empty numbers and two fusions, and chapter 15 has one empty number and one fusion — each chapter takes its own form, and nothing about this chapter may be inferred from another; this chapter was verified verse by verse in this round). The BHS paragraph markers, verified verse by verse, are: samekh in two places, at the end of verse 9 and at the end of verse 18; petuhah in one place, at the end of verse 20 (the close of the whole chapter); the remaining seventeen verses end with sof pasuq only. The Masoretic division is therefore threefold: verses 1–9 (on the eve of battle, and the exemptions), verses 10–18 (the two forms of siege), and verses 19–20 (the fruit-tree command). This sermon is divided into four sections (1–4 / 5–9 / 10–15 / 16–20), which is an interpretive, homiletic division; its correspondence with the three Masoretic sections is as follows: the sermon's first and second sections both belong to the first Masoretic section; the sermon's third section and the first half of its fourth section (vv. 16–18) both belong to the second Masoretic section, with no paragraph marker between them; and the second half of the sermon's fourth section (vv. 19–20) is the third Masoretic section. The two divisions are stated side by side here; this sermon does not pass off its homiletic division as the textual division. Six fixed-text entries for the KJV text: (1) In 20:3 the KJV joins the four prohibitions of fear in a single sentence, separated by commas and semicolons and closed by a semicolon, with a colon after "against your enemies"; there is no exclamation mark (the Chinese fixed text reads the corresponding clause with commas, where a modern-punctuation edition divides it with an exclamation mark). (2) In 20:10 the KJV has no punctuation break corresponding to the Chinese pause marker after "city"; the sentence runs on to "then proclaim peace unto it." (3) The forms of the divine name: the Chinese fixed text has three forms — "the LORD thy God" in the singular without the possessive suffix written separately (vv. 1, 14, 16, 17), "the LORD thy God" with the suffix (v. 13), and "the LORD your God" in the plural (vv. 4, 18). In the KJV these collapse into two forms: "the LORD thy God" (vv. 1, 13, 14, 16, 17) and "the LORD your God" (vv. 4, 18). The difference is registered here and not smoothed over; quotations in this translation follow the KJV verse by verse. (4) In 20:16 the Chinese fixed text closes with a semicolon, running on into verse 17; the KJV closes verse 16 with a colon, and verse 17 then begins the list of names. (5) In 20:19 the KJV sets a colon after "by forcing an axe against them," places the clause "for the tree of the field is man's life" in parentheses, and closes the verse with a colon; the corresponding Chinese punctuation (pause marker after "besiege," semicolon after "destroy the trees," full stop after "thou shalt not cut them down") is registered in the Chinese source and is not reproduced in English. (6) In 20:11 the KJV renders mas (H4522) as a noun of status, "shall be tributaries unto thee," followed by "and they shall serve thee"; this corresponds to the Chinese fixed text's verbal rendering and to the note in the Chinese source that mas is a noun denoting the status of one bound to forced labour. Account of quotation marks: in the Chinese fixed text, the discourse of this chapter carries double quotation marks, with an opening mark three times — at the head of verses 1, 10, and 19, corresponding one-to-one with the three BHS units (1–9 / 10–18 / 19–20) — and a closing mark only once, at the end of verse 20, where the legal discourse of the whole chapter is at last gathered up; verses 9 and 18 end with a BHS paragraph marker (samekh) but the Chinese fixed text has no closing quotation mark there — it marks a division by reopening quotation marks at the head of a new unit, not by a closing mark at a unit's end (settled in the revision round by checking the verse pages for vv. 9, 10, 18, and 19). The KJV text carries no quotation marks at all in this chapter; direct speech is introduced by verbs of speaking and by colons. This formal difference between the two fixed texts is registered, not harmonized. Embedded single quotation marks in the Chinese text form three groups: one group for the priest's declaration (vv. 3–4), one for the officers' first declaration (vv. 5–7), and one for the officers' second declaration (v. 8). Quotations in this translation reproduce the KJV verbatim, including its punctuation.

Deuteronomy 20 is the only chapter in the legal core (chapters 12–26) devoted entirely to war, yet from beginning to end it never teaches anyone how to fight. It deals first with the heart of the army — before the battle, the priest must declare, and the officers must send men home; then it deals with the enemy city — on approaching a city, words of peace are proclaimed first, and distant cities and nearby cities are treated differently; and finally it deals with the force of a siege — not even the fruit trees outside a city may be cut down at will. The logic of the whole chapter is limit upon limit: for fear, a declaration is set (you may first hear the word of God's presence); for the ranks, exemptions are set (the unfinished great affairs of life, and fear that would spread, are both released first); for killing, a procedure is set (peace first, siege afterwards; distant cities keep survivors, and for nearby cities the destruction command states its objects and its reason in plain words); for destruction, a boundary is set (a tree is not a man, and the axe may not vent itself on it). In this chapter "battle" is a force drawn in, step by step, by law; and the first sentence of the chapter is not tactics but comfort — "be not afraid of them: for the LORD thy God is with thee, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt" (20:1).

## Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Factual layer] The chapter opens with no command to advance, only with a candid look at what the eyes report: "When thou goest out to battle against thine enemies, and seest horses, and chariots, and a people more than thou, be not afraid of them: for the LORD thy God is with thee, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt." (20:1) — The law first admits a fact: the comparison of forces is visible. Horses, chariots, and numbers are all greater than yours. Fear is not treated in advance as a failure of faith to be condemned; it is a situation the law has seen beforehand and provided for beforehand. The order of command in this chapter is also worth noting at the outset: the first to speak is not the commander but the priest (vv. 2–4), and after him the officers (vv. 5–9); captains are not appointed until verse 9 — the order is declaration first, organization afterwards; the heart is dealt with first, and the ranks are formed afterwards.

[Theological layer] One general inference is set down first (this is an inference): this chapter speaks of war inside the cage of the law — war is not a law-free zone, an exception to the law; it is precisely the place where the law most needs to be present. Going out to battle has its declaration, killing the enemy has its limits, and besieging a city has its boundary; even the composition of the army first passes through the screening question of who may go home. The greater the force, the clearer the boundary. The four sections that follow take up these four arrangements one by one.

[Summary layer] The chapter in one sentence: God does not permit His people to go into battle with unsettled hearts, does not permit the sword to be unsheathed before peace has been proclaimed, and does not permit the axe to vent itself on a tree that has wronged no one.

## I. The Priest's Declaration on the Eve of Battle (Deuteronomy 20:1–4; 05:00–17:00)

[Factual layer] These four verses form a complete address on the eve of battle, together with its introduction. Verse 1 sets the situation (already seen in the Introduction): going out to battle, seeing, the enemy strong and Israel weak. Verse 2 fixes the moment and the speaker: "when ye are come nigh unto the battle, that the priest shall approach and speak unto the people," (20:2) — the moment is "when ye are come nigh unto the battle," before contact is made; the speaker is the priest, not a captain; and the action is to approach and speak to the people, standing before the whole army. Verse 3 is the body of the address: "And shall say unto them, Hear, O Israel, ye approach this day unto battle against your enemies: let not your hearts faint, fear not, and do not tremble, neither be ye terrified because of them;" (20:3) — first the summons by name, "Hear, O Israel," then the word "this day"; then come three groups of negations covering four kinds of fear: let not your hearts faint (rākak, the heart going soft), fear not and do not tremble (yārēʾ with ḥāpaz, fearing in panic and haste), and neither be ye terrified because of them (ʿāraṣ) — the whole spectrum of fear, from inward softness to bodily trembling, spoken out in one breath. Verse 4 gives the reason, and there is only one: "For the LORD your God is he that goeth with you, to fight for you against your enemies, to save you." (20:4) — "goeth with you" is presence, "to fight for you" is fighting in your place, and "to save you" is the outcome; the priest reports no intelligence about the enemy, stirs up no morale, and promises no victory of Israel's own making — he declares these three things only. The divine name occurs twice in this section: in verse 1 in the singular form, "the LORD thy God," and in verse 4 in the plural form, "the LORD your God" (in the KJV wording; for the three Chinese forms see the headnote), and the count is given in the notes.

[Theological layer] Three inferences, each marked as such. First, the first lesson on the eve of battle is for the ears, not for the hands and feet (this is an inference): the first verb of the priest's address is "Hear" — before every action, first listen. Fear mostly enters through the eyes ("and seest horses, and chariots," 20:1); faith enters through the ears. By arranging for a priest to speak before the battle line, the law lets the whole army, after seeing the enemy's array and before drawing the sword, first hear another order of fact. Second, under the four "nots" there lies one "for" (this is an inference): the prohibitions of verse 3 are not a command to suppress one's feelings; the reason in verse 4 is the medicine — God goes with you, God fights for you, God saves you. The priest does not say "you are in fact very strong," nor does he say "the enemy is in fact very weak"; the ground of victory is moved entirely out of human hands: it is not that you can fight, but that He goes with you. Third, this address is written into the law; it is not an improvisation on the spot (this is an inference): the moment, the speaker, and the wording are all written into the law in advance. Fear is something every generation will meet, so the law makes comfort a standing provision of the system as well — comfort is not a favour dispensed by a commander, but a regular institution.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two registrations. First, the priestly declaration on the eve of battle has a traceable echo in later narrative: in the days of Jehoshaphat, when Judah faced a great host, there was the declaration "Be not afraid nor dismayed by reason of this great multitude; for the battle is not your's, but God's." (2 Chr 20:15; the Chinese source originally registered the gist only; in the checking round the wording was verified first-hand and the quotation was set in the fixed text's verbatim form, and the KJV form is used here) — scholarship commonly reads that narrative together with verses 1–4 of this chapter; this is registered only, and neither text is adjudicated by the other. Second, in the ancient Near East it was also customary for a priest or prophet to speak before a war in other nations; sacrifice before battle, divination, and the proclamation of an oracle were the general practice. What is distinctive in this chapter is not that there is a declaration, but the content of the declaration — it asks after no omens, reads no signs, and only repeats the deliverance of the exodus and God's presence.

[New Testament reading layer] No direct New Testament quotation of verses 1–4 is registered for this section. Only one distant echo is recorded: Romans 8:31 takes God's help as the ground of the question who can be against us (the verse reference only is registered; the fixed text of that verse was not checked word for word in the Chinese source, and it is not quoted verbatim here) — its direction agrees with this section: first the declaration of presence, then the conclusion of not fearing. This is registered only, and the original meaning is not rewritten by it.

[Summary layer] Section One: before going into battle, a priest steps forward. He does not count the enemy's array and report the numbers; he says one thing only — do not faint today, because the LORD your God goes with you, to fight for you against your enemies, to save you. The whole army's first lesson is to hear.

## II. The Officers' Declaration: The Three Exemptions, the Fearful, and the Appointment of Captains (Deuteronomy 20:5–9; 17:00–30:00)

[Factual layer] These five verses are the officers' (šōṭēr, H7860) two rounds of declaration, plus a conclusion. The first round asks three questions, all in the same form: "And the officers shall speak unto the people, saying, What man is there that hath built a new house, and hath not dedicated it? let him go and return to his house, lest he die in the battle, and another man dedicate it." (20:5) "And what man is he that hath planted a vineyard, and hath not yet eaten of it? let him also go and return unto his house, lest he die in the battle, and another man eat of it." (20:6) "And what man is there that hath betrothed a wife, and hath not taken her? let him go and return unto his house, lest he die in the battle, and another man take her." (20:7) — The objects of the three exemptions are three great affairs of life that are "not yet" completed: a house has been built, but its dedication has not yet been held (ḥānaḵ, H2596, to put formally into use; the word occurs twice in v. 5); a vineyard has been planted, but its first fruit has not yet been opened for use (ḥālal, H2490); a wife has been betrothed (ʾāraś, H781), but not yet taken. All three sentences close with the same words, "lest he die in the battle, and another man …": lest he die in the battle, and another man dedicate it; lest he die in the battle, and another man eat of it; lest he die in the battle, and another man take her. The second round asks one question: "And the officers shall speak further unto the people, and they shall say, What man is there that is fearful and fainthearted? let him go and return unto his house, lest his brethren's heart faint as well as his heart." (20:8) — The fearful and fainthearted man (yārēʾ with rak, fearing with a soft heart) may also go home, and the reason lies not in himself but in the others: "lest his brethren's heart faint" (māsas, H4549, the heart melting like wax), spreading through the whole army as his does. Verse 9 concludes: "And it shall be, when the officers have made an end of speaking unto the people that they shall make captains of the armies to lead the people." (20:9) — Only when the declarations are finished are captains appointed (pāqad, H6485, to appoint). At the end of this verse the BHS has a samekh, and the first great Masoretic section (vv. 1–9) closes here. Counts: in this section the return home occurs four times (vv. 5, 6, 7, 8), "lest" occurs four times, and the "What man" questions occur four times; see the notes for details.

[Theological layer] Three inferences, each marked as such. First, the law asks first "whose life is not yet settled," and only then "who can go into battle" (this is an inference): what the three exemptions have in common is "not yet" — not yet dedicated, not yet eaten of, not yet taken. In the law's eyes, war is not an exceptional state that swallows everything; where a man's life has a beginning that is still unfinished, the law first permits him to finish that beginning, and only then speaks of going out to battle. The first lesson in what the nation may demand of the individual is the admission that some "not yets" come before a battle. Second, "lest he die in the battle, and another man …" is a rare plainness in the law (this is an inference): it does not cover the reality of death in battle with glorious words — the house you built will be dedicated by another, the vineyard you planted will be enjoyed by another, the wife you betrothed will be taken by another. These three sentences are not meant to frighten; they let those who remain first see the worst outcome clearly. Having seen it clearly and still remaining, going into battle becomes a voluntary bearing of the burden, not blind compliance swept along by morale. Third, the release of the fearful protects the whole army; it is not a humiliation of the individual (this is an inference): verse 8 attaches no term of contempt to the fearful man — no punishment, no record, no expulsion from the camp; it says only "let him go and return unto his house," the same treatment as the three exemptions. The reason lies entirely on the side of the others: fear is contagious, and hearts melt into one another. The law's way of dealing with fear is not to force a man to be brave, but to let him leave, with dignity, the position from which the infection spreads. This forms exactly two sides with the priest's words in Section One: the priest declares to the whole army, "fear not"; the officers open the door to the individual: if you fear, you may go home.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three items registered side by side, with no adjudication between them. First, the juxtaposition with Deuteronomy 24:5: the man released in verse 7 of this chapter is one who "hath betrothed a wife, and hath not taken her," and the reason is the danger of dying in battle; Deuteronomy 24:5 treats a man who has already taken a wife: "When a man hath taken a new wife, he shall not go out to war, neither shall he be charged with any business: but he shall be free at home one year, and shall cheer up his wife which he hath taken." (Deut 24:5, KJV, grabbed first-hand in this translation round; see the revision record at the end) The differences, stated side by side: the objects differ, one betrothed and the other married; the terms differ, one a release on the eve of battle and the other a full exemption for one year, together with exemption from public duties; the reasons differ, one the fear that another man will take her and the other the gladdening of the wife he has taken. The exemptions of verses 5 and 6 of this chapter have no corresponding provision in chapter 24. Whether the three sets of regulations are stages, supplements, or provisions for different occasions, scholarly opinion differs; this is stated side by side only, with no adjudication. Second, the juxtaposition with the camp regulations of Deuteronomy 23: Deuteronomy 23:9–14 contains separate regulations for cleanness within and outside the camp when going out to war (this sermon registers the chapter and verse references only and does not quote them word for word); they share the war theme with this chapter but differ in focus — there the cleanness of the camp, here the coming and going of persons. The two chapters stand side by side within Deuteronomy's war vocabulary; this is registered only, with no adjudication between them. Third, the mirror juxtaposition with Deuteronomy 28:30: the three exemptions of this chapter have a mirror in the curse section of Deuteronomy 28 — "Thou shalt betroth a wife, and another man shall lie with her: thou shalt build an house, and thou shalt not dwell therein: thou shalt plant a vineyard, and shalt not gather the grapes thereof." (Deut 28:30, KJV, grabbed first-hand in this translation round) The differences, stated side by side: the order differs (here house, vineyard, wife; there wife, house, vineyard); the sentence form differs (here the ground of an exemption, "lest he die in the battle, and another man …"; there the curse of a broken covenant, "another man shall …"); the tone differs (the "another man" here is a possibility the law means to block, there a certainty after the covenant is broken). Which of the two came first, and which cites which, is not adjudicated.

[New Testament reading layer] This section first records an instance of the regulation being carried out in the Old Testament: in Judges 7, Gideon is commanded to proclaim: "Now therefore go to, proclaim in the ears of the people, saying, Whosoever is fearful and afraid, let him return and depart early from mount Gilead. And there returned of the people twenty and two thousand; and there remained ten thousand." (Judg 7:3, KJV, grabbed first-hand in this translation round) — The regulation of verse 8 was actually invoked in the days of the judges; twenty-two thousand men went home under it, and the text records not one man as blamed. This is registered only. On the New Testament side, no direct quotation of verses 5–9 is registered for this section. In Luke 14, when the cost is counted, a king going out to war is used as an illustration (Luke 14:31–32); it shares with this chapter the setting of "before the war begins, first reckon, first ask," and its sentence about sending an ambassage to desire conditions of peace has a further echo with the proclaiming of peace in Section Three of this chapter, registered in the reading layer of Section Three and not developed here.

[Summary layer] Section Two: before any captain appears, the officers first ask four questions. Three questions are for men whose lives are "not yet" complete, and one question is for the man whose heart has gone soft; all four kinds of men may go home, without shame. Only when the declarations are finished are captains appointed to lead them — an army that has passed through this release is an army under the law.

## III. Proclaim Peace First: The Treatment of Distant Cities (Deuteronomy 20:10–15; 30:00–44:00)

[Factual layer] These six verses form a complete siege procedure, and they open with the hinge sentence of the whole chapter: "When thou comest nigh unto a city to fight against it, then proclaim peace unto it." (20:10) — The word of sequence, "then," at this point is a hard rule (in the Chinese text the order word is "first"): the first thing on approaching a city is not to set ladders, nor to besiege, but to proclaim peace to the people of the city (šālôm, H7965). Verse 11 treats the case of acceptance: "And it shall be, if it make thee answer of peace, and open unto thee, then it shall be, that all the people that is found therein shall be tributaries unto thee, and they shall serve thee." (20:11) — If the people of the city answer with peace and open the city, the treatment is that the whole city keeps its life and is bound to forced labour (mas, H4522, rendered "tributaries" in the KJV; the Chinese fixed text renders it verbally, "shall serve"): not one person is killed, and the city becomes a serving city. Verse 12 treats the case of refusal: "And if it will make no peace with thee, but will make war against thee, then thou shalt besiege it:" (20:12) — Only when peace is refused and war is chosen does the siege follow (ṣûr, H6696). Verse 13 treats the disposition when the city falls: "And when the LORD thy God hath delivered it into thine hands, thou shalt smite every male thereof with the edge of the sword:" (20:13) — The fall of the city is ascribed to "the LORD thy God hath delivered it into thine hands," and the execution is limited to "every male" (zāḵār, H2145). Verse 14 treats what is kept and what is taken as spoil: "But the women, and the little ones, and the cattle, and all that is in the city, even all the spoil thereof, shalt thou take unto thyself; and thou shalt eat the spoil of thine enemies, which the LORD thy God hath given thee." (20:14) — Women, little ones, and cattle are all preserved; and the spoil (šālāl, H7998, twice in this verse; the Chinese fixed text renders the second occurrence as "goods, property") is characterized as "given thee" by God, and its treatment is "thou shalt eat." Verse 15 delimits the scope of this section's procedure: "Thus shalt thou do unto all the cities which are very far off from thee, which are not of the cities of these nations." (20:15) — Peace first, then, when a city falls, the males are smitten, and all the rest are preserved: this procedure applies only to "all the cities which are very far off from thee"; "the cities of these nations" are not in this class, and what follows treats them separately.

[Theological layer] Three inferences, each marked as such. First, proclaiming peace first puts peace in the first place in the procedure; it is not rhetoric (this is an inference): before the sword is unsheathed, the law first sends out a sentence — are you willing to make peace? However strong the attacking side may be, it must ask this question first; and the defending side, if it only answers with peace and opens the city, sees everyone in the city live. In this section peace is not a favour granted after victory; it is a compulsory step before war begins. A war that skips this question is in breach from its first step. Second, this procedure narrows killing stage by stage (this is an inference): if war can be avoided, it is avoided; if war comes, then even when the city falls, it reaches only the males; women, little ones, and cattle are all preserved. Within this chapter's own logic, the law's pen keeps subtracting — it does not beautify war; it sets a "thus far" under every step. In reading this section, look first at what it blocks, and only then discuss what it permits. Third, the characterization of the spoil is this section's ethical anchor (this is an inference): verse 14 does not say "you may plunder"; it says "the spoil of thine enemies, which the LORD thy God hath given thee," and "thou shalt eat" — taking goods is characterized as receiving a gift, not seizing by force; and "eat" fixes the use of the spoil: it is provisions, not a trophy for display. What force obtains first passes through God's hand, and only then reaches man's mouth.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three registrations. First, the distinction between distant and nearby cities is the key to this chapter's structure: verse 15 draws the line by the double standard of distance and people — "all the cities which are very far off from thee" follow the peace procedure (vv. 10–15), and "the cities of these nations" follow another treatment (vv. 16–18). Scholarship generally points out that this chapter's war regulations are in essence two sets, not one universal law of conquest: for the distant, surrender and service are the norm; for the peoples within the land of Canaan there is a separate, specific command. Disputes over the scope of the two sets (by what standard "very far off" is measured, and whether the list of nearby cities is exhaustive) differ; this is registered only. Second, the proclamation of peace also had a "summons to surrender" stage in the war customs of the ancient Near East; a besieging side first calling on a city to yield was the general practice, and this chapter shares it. The difference is that the summons is written as a compulsory order in the law ("then proclaim," with "first" in the Chinese text), and that after the city opens, the preservation of every life in the city is stated in plain words and does not depend on a commander's momentary mercy. This is the standard scholarly description; it is registered only. Third, "tributaries" in verse 11 (mas) and māsas in verse 8 (the heart fainting, melting) are said in the lexicons to be cognate; this word-origin discussion is registered only, and no theological inference is built on it.

[New Testament reading layer] Two registrations. First, Luke 14:31–32 uses a king going out to war as an illustration: "Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand? Or else, while the other is yet a great way off, he sendeth an ambassage, and desireth conditions of peace." (Luke 14:31–32, KJV, grabbed first-hand in this translation round) — "he sendeth an ambassage, and desireth conditions of peace" and this chapter's "then proclaim peace unto it" answer one another in vocabulary and in sequence: both take the step of peace first, before the armies close and while the other is still far away. Jesus' purpose in this illustration concerns the cost of discipleship; this sermon registers the echo only and does not rewrite this chapter by means of it. Second, this chapter's "proclaim peace" has only a distant echo in the New Testament's vocabulary of peace proclamation (as in Eph 2:17, concerning the one who preached peace; this sermon registers the verse reference only, did not check that verse's fixed text word for word, and does not quote it verbatim); it does not amount to a quotation, and this sermon makes no leap of application from it.

[Summary layer] Section Three: on approaching a city, the first thing is to open the mouth, not to open fire. Those willing to make peace keep the whole city alive, in service; for those who refuse and fight, even after siege and fall, the sword is permitted to reach only the males; women and little ones are all preserved, and the spoil is reckoned as given by God and permitted only to be eaten. The procedure of words before weapons is promulgated by the law first to the farthest cities.

## IV. The Destruction Command for Nearby Cities and the Fruit-Tree Command in a Siege (Deuteronomy 20:16–20; 44:00–57:00)

[Factual layer] These five verses contain two commands of entirely different character. The first is the severest command in the whole chapter — "But of the cities of these people, which the LORD thy God doth give thee for an inheritance, thou shalt save alive nothing that breatheth:" (20:16) "But thou shalt utterly destroy them; namely, the Hittites, and the Amorites, the Canaanites, and the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites; as the LORD thy God hath commanded thee:" (20:17) "That they teach you not to do after all their abominations, which they have done unto their gods; so should ye sin against the LORD your God." (20:18) — The objects are delimited by "for an inheritance" (naḥălâ, H5159) in connection with "doth give thee," and by "nothing that breatheth" (nəšāmâ, H5397): not every enemy city, only "the cities of these people"; the list names six peoples expressly (the Hittites, the Amorites, the Canaanites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites); the command is "utterly destroy" (ḥāram, H2763, in the intensive form); and the reason is written plainly within the Scripture itself (v. 18): lest they teach you to do after all their abominations (tôʿēḇâ, H8441), which they have done unto their gods. At the end of verse 18 the BHS has a samekh, and the second great Masoretic section (vv. 10–18) closes here. The second command, at the head of verse 19 in the Chinese fixed text, reopens the quotation marks and turns to the everyday conduct of a siege: "When thou shalt besiege a city a long time, in making war against it to take it, thou shalt not destroy the trees thereof by forcing an axe against them: for thou mayest eat of them, and thou shalt not cut them down (for the tree of the field is man's life) to employ them in the siege:" (20:19) "Only the trees which thou knowest that they be not trees for meat, thou shalt destroy and cut them down; and thou shalt build bulwarks against the city that maketh war with thee, until it be subdued." (20:20) — The siege drags on ("a long time"), and military provisions are at hand, yet the law forbids forcing an axe (garzen, H1631, the same word as in 19:5) against the fruit trees to destroy them, for two reasons: the fruit is something you may eat (to destroy the tree is to destroy provisions); and, in the Chinese fixed text's interrogative reading, "are the trees of the field men, that you should mistreat them?" — a tree is not an enemy soldier and does not stand among the objects of war (for the KJV's parenthetical statement form of this clause, and the three readings registered side by side, see Note 5). The exception is left only for trees that are not trees for food, and their use is limited to building bulwarks (māṣôr, H4692) against the city, until it is subdued. The petuhah at the end of verse 20 closes the whole chapter.

[Theological layer] Three inferences, each marked as such, the third being this sermon's general conclusion on this section. First, the destruction command carries a threefold limitation within the text itself (this is an inference about the text's structure): a limitation of object — it reaches only "the cities of these people," kept separate from the distant-city procedure of verse 15, and the two must not be crossed; a limitation of list — six peoples are named expressly, and it does not refer in general to every foreign people (it has one name fewer than the seven peoples of Deut 7:1, the Girgashites; the difference is registered in the notes, with no adjudication as to which list is fuller and which is shorter); and a limitation of reason — verse 18 locks the reason to one thing, the contagion of faith: what is feared is not their soldiers, but their teaching. Within the Scripture this command is never a blank licence to kill: it has an address, a list, and a reason, and all three are written on the page. Second, the fruit-tree command is the law's ecological boundary for war (this is an inference): at the very moment when a siege consumes the most provisions and most easily vents anger, the law takes up the matter of the axe. Its ground is not a romantic love of nature, but a double sobriety — the fruit tree is a source of provisions, and to cut the tree is to cut off one's own supply line; and a tree is not a man, so the hatred of war has no right to spread to an innocent created thing. This chapter's limits run from the heart of the army, to the ranks, to killing, and all the way to destruction. Third, the two commands stand side by side not to cancel each other out, but each in its own place (this is an inference): verses 16–18 treat a specific command for that land, that time, and those six peoples; verses 19–20 treat a general rule for sieges; and the text separates the two with a paragraph marker (the samekh at the end of v. 18). The former may not be drawn out as a template for war in any age or against any object — its threefold limitation is its seal; the latter remains in the form of a general rule and becomes the source of the later principle of "do not destroy wantonly." In reading this section, hold this boundary first, and only then speak of application.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three registrations, stated side by side only, with no adjudication. First, the ethical discussion of the destruction command (ḥērem) has never ceased in scholarship and in the history of the church; the approaches are stated side by side: (1) The ancient-context reading — ḥērem is the ancient Near Eastern idea of sacrificial warfare (comparable practice is seen, for example, on the Moabite Stone), not unique to Israel, and this chapter is its form as taken up and bounded within the law. (2) The judgment reading — the command is placed within the frame of "iniquity full" (Gen 15:16 registered), as judgment rather than expansion by conquest. (3) The implementation-gap reading — the narratives of Judges and Samuel show that the peoples of Canaan in fact remained, in large part, for centuries afterwards, and the command and the gap stand side by side in the text. (4) The church-tradition reading — the mainstream tradition generally regards this command as a one-time command for a specific time and place, not transferable as a norm for any later group. The four approaches above are registered only, not decided. Second, the difference between the six-peoples list here and the seven peoples of Deuteronomy 7:1 (including the Girgashites): scholarship either supposes that lists were expanded or reduced in different textual traditions, or that this chapter's list is given according to this chapter's setting; this sermon registers the difference only and does not adjudicate which is fuller and which is shorter (see the cross-reference index and the unresolved registrations in the notes). Third, the development of the fruit-tree command in Jewish tradition: tradition extended this verse into the principle of bal tashchit ("do not destroy"), its scope expanding from fruit trees in wartime to all wanton destruction — destroying clothing, destroying vessels, and wasting water are all included. This is the standard registration of that tradition's history; this sermon records its source and course only and does not develop its details.

[New Testament reading layer] Two registrations, strictly without reading backwards. First, this section's fruit-tree imagery has one clear echo registered in the New Testament (in the cross-reference tradition for Deut 20:19 this verse is listed): "And now also the axe is laid unto the root of the trees: therefore every tree which bringeth not forth good fruit is hewn down, and cast into the fire." (Matt 3:10, KJV, grabbed first-hand in this translation round) — The same axe and the same trees, in opposite directions: here, in a siege, fruit-bearing trees may not be cut down; in John the Baptist's preaching, in judgment, the tree that bears no good fruit is hewn down. This sermon registers this contrast only: the imagery corresponds, the settings are distinct; and Matthew 3's judgment setting must never be read back into this chapter — the original meaning of verse 19, protecting the trees, is a wartime general rule, and it is not turned into a prophecy of judgment by the New Testament's fruit-tree judgment imagery. There are further fruit-tree passages — John 15:2, Matthew 7:19, and Luke 13:7–9 — listed together with Matthew 3:10 in this chapter's cross-reference tradition; only the verse references are registered, and they are not developed one by one. Second, for the destruction command there is no positive New Testament registration of succession: the New Testament never directs believers, by ḥērem logic, in their treatment of any group; where it treats the Gentiles, its direction is mission and reception (Acts 10 and 15 registered). This fact of "no succession" is a boundary note for the church's reading of this section; it is registered only and not developed.

[Summary layer] Section Four: the severest words in this chapter and its gentlest words are separated by only one paragraph marker. The destruction command is sealed threefold — by object, by list, and by reason — and locked to that land, that time, and those six peoples; the fruit-tree command, by contrast, is a general rule: however long the siege, the axe may not vent itself on a tree, because a tree is not a man and its fruit is provisions. Where force reaches this point, its boundary reaches this point.

## Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Factual layer] Return to the two pictures at the chapter's head and tail. At the opening stands a man going out to battle, seeing horses, chariots, and a people more than himself (20:1); at the close stands a man in a siege, under a fruit tree, his axe raised and then held back by the law (20:19–20). Between them stand the priest, the officers, captains appointed only late, and a city that first hears words of peace (20:2–15). Nowhere does the chapter teach anyone how to win; everywhere it teaches how not to be carried away by fear, and not to be carried away by one's own strength — if you fear, first hear the declaration; if your life has a "not yet," first be permitted to complete it; if the sword is to be unsheathed, first open the mouth; if force is to be vented, first ask whether the object is a man.

[Theological layer] The general conclusion gathers into one sentence (this is an inference): this chapter's war is placed, from beginning to end, within an order of words — first the priest's words, then the officers' questions; first words of peace, then the order to besiege; first the declaration "be not afraid of them," and only then the captains' leading. Force is never the forerunner; words are. The presence in "be not afraid of them: for the LORD thy God is with thee, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt" (20:1) is the same hand behind the fourfold limits — He holds back fear, holds back exaction, holds back indiscriminate killing, and holds back the axe. A people held back in this way is then permitted to go out to battle.

[Summary layer] The whole sermon in one sentence: Deuteronomy 20 does not teach anyone how to fight; it teaches a man to remain under restraint in the place where restraint is most easily lost — the God who brought them out of Egypt and goes with them first settles fear, first releases lives, first asks after peace, and first holds back the axe, and only then says: be not afraid of them.

## Devotional Extract

There is a kind of fear that enters through the eyes. When you go out to battle, what you see first is the horses, the chariots, and a people more than you — the numbers arrive first, and the heart softens first. God does not rebuke this fear, nor does He tell you to pretend you have not seen; He sets a priest before the whole army, and the first words are: Hear. Fear enters through the eyes; peace enters through the ears. So the law sets a declaration before the drawn sword, words of peace before the approach to a city, and a command that stays the axe before the axe is raised. If the situation you face today also outnumbers you at every point, as an enemy array does, do not be in a hurry to act — listen first. Listen to the God who once brought you out of Egypt, and who goes with you still today. This chapter also settles those whose lives are still stuck at "not yet": a house built but not yet dedicated, a vineyard planted but its fruit not yet tasted, a wife betrothed but not yet taken — God permits them to go home first and finish living out the unfinished days; even the man whose heart has gone soft may withdraw with dignity, because He does not want a melted heart to go on and melt a brother's heart. Then you will find that even the strength in your hand has been given its boundaries: what should be released, release it; what should be asked first, ask it first; and what must not be destroyed — not even one tree — do not touch at will. Be not afraid of them — not because they have become fewer, but because He is here.

"When thou goest out to battle against thine enemies, and seest horses, and chariots, and a people more than thou, be not afraid of them: for the LORD thy God is with thee, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt." (Deuteronomy 20:1, KJV)

"When thou shalt besiege a city a long time, in making war against it to take it, thou shalt not destroy the trees thereof by forcing an axe against them: for thou mayest eat of them, and thou shalt not cut them down (for the tree of the field is man's life) to employ them in the siege:" (Deuteronomy 20:19, KJV)

## Summary

Moses has just set up his successor (Numbers 27, preceding-chapter layer) when God hands down a calendar of offerings — beginning tomorrow morning, and not one day may be broken. The opening rule is a single sentence: "My offering, and my bread for my sacrifices made by fire, for a sweet savour unto me, shall ye observe to offer unto me in their due season." (28:2) Everything below is the unfolding of that one sentence, in five tiers. The first tier is the foundation: two lambs of the first year every day, without spot, one in the morning and one at even, for a continual burnt offering, each with a meat offering of a tenth part of an ephah of flour mingled with the fourth part of an hin of beaten oil, and a drink offering of the fourth part of an hin — ordained in mount Sinai, a sacrifice made by fire unto the LORD for a sweet savour (28:3-8). The second tier is laid on top: on the Sabbath, two more lambs and two tenth deals of meat offering, beside the continual offering (28:9-10); at the beginnings of months, two young bullocks, one ram, and seven lambs more, the meat offering descending by tier — three tenth deals for a bullock, two for a ram, one for a lamb — the drink offering descending likewise — half an hin for a bullock, the third part of an hin for a ram, a fourth part for a lamb — and one kid of the goats for a sin offering, every month throughout the months of the year (28:11-15). The third tier is the feasts: the fourteenth day of the first month is the passover of the LORD; from the fifteenth day unleavened bread is eaten seven days, the first day and the seventh each with an holy convocation and no servile work; and on each of the seven days the same complement as at the beginnings of months — bullocks, ram, lambs, and a goat for a sin offering — is offered beside the burnt offering in the morning (28:16-25). The last tier is the day of the firstfruits, after the weeks are out, the day of the new meat offering: an holy convocation likewise, no servile work likewise, and two bullocks, one ram, seven lambs, and one kid of the goats for a sin offering, all without blemish (28:26-31). The whole chapter has no narrative and no human speech; there is one Speaker only, arranging a timetable from beginning to end: day, week, month, feast — layer laid upon layer, and the layer underneath is always offered first and never cancelled. What God's people must learn first, He teaches them, is not the great thing but the due season — to live the days into an offering, and the ordinary into a sweet savour.

## Main Text

### Part One: The General Rule — Offer unto Me in Their Due Season (Numbers 28:1-2) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) Verse 1, the introduction to the speech, quoted verbatim from the base text: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying," (28:1) Two items registered: first, the introduction's verb is "spake," the same speech verb that introduces the verdict of Numbers 27 (27:6, preceding-chapter layer, in key points); second, from here to the end of the chapter all thirty verses (28:2-31) are the content of this one speech — the chapter has only one Speaker, and Moses has not one sentence of speech in it; he is only the man commanded, and the command of verse 2 is precisely what he is to pass on: "Command the children of Israel, and say unto them." Verse 2, the general rule quoted in full: "Command the children of Israel, and say unto them, My offering, and my bread for my sacrifices made by fire, for a sweet savour unto me, shall ye observe to offer unto me in their due season." (28:2) The items registered one by one: first, the thing offered carries a threefold naming — "my offering," and "my bread for my sacrifices made by fire" — the thing is an offering, it is a sacrifice made by fire, it is bread; three names pointing to one thing; second, the general requirement of the manner of offering is one clause only — "shall ye observe to offer unto me in their due season": the emphasis does not yet lie on what is offered (that is the business of the movements below) but first on the three words "in their due season"; third, this verse has no quantity, no kind, and no detail of time — all the details stand in the five movements below; this verse lays down the rule only.

(Argument) First, the place where this calendar appears. The preceding chapter has just settled two pieces of unfinished business: a father with no son has had his name preserved, and a leader about to depart has had his successor set up. By our reckoning, once the personnel are settled, the next thing to discuss should be the deployment for entering Canaan. God does otherwise: after the personnel, He first arranges the timetable — what is to be offered tomorrow morning, what at even, what is added on the Sabbath, what at the beginnings of months, what day the fourteenth of the first month is. It turns out that in God's order a community's future does not rest first on who succeeds, but first on how the days are lived: leaders will change, the days must not fall into disorder; Moses will die, the offering must not break. This is a reminder to today's church and today's family alike: we always suppose that the great things decide destiny, when what in fact decides a community's savour is the small, unannounced constancy of morning and evening every day. If revival comes, it will most likely not begin from one great event, but from one calendar kept — prayer with its season, worship with its season, rest with its season, tier upon tier, offered as appointed, not on impulse.

(Argument) Next, this expression "my bread." God calls the offering "my bread for my sacrifices made by fire" — He lacks no food; He lacks nothing at all; He speaks this way to present the offering as a table set before Him: what the people send up to the altar morning and evening is the portion He delights in and remembers. This rescues the offering from mere "duty discharged": it is not God standing overhead inspecting, counting, and fining; it is God waiting for a table His children have prepared. When children bring food to their parents, what they bring is not nutrition but remembrance; brought daily, brought on time, what the parents savour is not the smell of the food but "he has me in his heart." So too that small prayer of yours each day, that passage read, that one bowing down, is this "bread" before God: the portion need not be large; its worth lies in the due season — His table has a portion laid on it by you every day. And the question returns on us: has our worship kept only the feasts' great banquets, while the daily ordinary meal has been broken off? The chapter's order is set down very clearly: however solemn the feast, it is added beside the continual offering (28:24, 31); if the ordinary meal is broken, no banquet can hold up a household's relationship with God.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The original behind "in their due season" is môʿēd (H4150), occurring once in this chapter (28:2; KJV "in their due season"), denoting the appointed time, the feast; the number was verified for the Chinese manuscript against the BibleHub lexicon page, which expressly lists Numbers 28:2. The word for "offering" is qorbān; its number is marked unverified in this sermon — see Endnote 5. "Bread" corresponds to laḥmî ("my bread / my food"), whose root is leḥem (H3899, number verified; see Endnote 5). The speech verb of verse 1 is registered in the base text's rendering, occurring once in this chapter (28:1).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) How the phrase "my bread for my sacrifices made by fire" is to be understood is set out here in two accounts, without adjudication. On one account it is registered literally as the language of sanctuary provision — the offering is burned on the altar, the smoke rises, and the text itself calls this God's "bread," a sweet savour (compare Leviticus 21:6, the bread of their God, cross-reference layer, in key points). On the other account it is read as anthropopathic covenant language — God uses the wording of "being fed" to express His delight in, and remembrance of, the people's constant worship, without thereby asserting any lack in God. Both are readings; this chapter's text gives the wording itself only, and the argument of this sermon's main text builds only on what the text states of itself — "sweet savour" and "in their due season" — and rests no conclusion on either account.

(Fact) This movement closes with a register of how the general rule corresponds to what follows: this section lays down the rule in one clause (in their due season), and the four movements below unfold it tier by tier according to the unit of time — the day (28:3-8), the Sabbath and the month (28:9-15), the seven days of the feast (28:16-25), and the one day of the feast (28:26-31). The units of time run from dense to sparse: the day denser than the week, the week than the month, the month than the feasts of the year; yet the tiers of offering are stacked from sparse to dense — the greater the day, the more is added, but no tier ever replaces the daily continual offering. This structure is the text's own statement; this sermon registers it movement by movement and draws no conclusions in advance.

### Part Two: The Continual Burnt Offering — One Lamb in the Morning, One at Even (Numbers 28:3-8) (10:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 3, the main item of the continual offering, quoted verbatim from the base text: "And thou shalt say unto them, This is the offering made by fire which ye shall offer unto the LORD; two lambs of the first year without spot day by day, for a continual burnt offering." (28:3) The items registered: the kind — lambs; the quality — without spot; the age — of the first year; the quantity — two day by day; the name — a continual burnt offering. Verse 4, the distribution of the two, in the base text's wording: "The one lamb shalt thou offer in the morning, and the other lamb shalt thou offer at even;" (28:4) Verse 5, the morning meat offering, in the base text's wording: "And a tenth part of an ephah of flour for a meat offering, mingled with the fourth part of an hin of beaten oil." (28:5) The proportion registered: a tenth part of an ephah of flour, the fourth part of an hin of oil, mingled as a meat offering — this is the quota for one lamb. Verse 6, the origin and character of the continual offering, in the base text's wording: "It is a continual burnt offering, which was ordained in mount Sinai for a sweet savour, a sacrifice made by fire unto the LORD." (28:6) Registered: this rule is no new institution — the text itself calls it an old rule ordained in mount Sinai (cross-reference layer: Exodus 29:38-42 carries the rule of two lambs daily, in key points; this sermon registers this chapter's own claim only). Verse 7, the drink offering, in the base text's wording: "And the drink offering thereof shall be the fourth part of an hin for the one lamb: in the holy place shalt thou cause the strong wine to be poured unto the LORD for a drink offering." (28:7) Two items registered: the quantity of wine is the same as the oil of the meat offering — the fourth part of an hin; and the place of the drink offering is expressly "in the holy place." Verse 8, the second lamb at even, in the base text's wording: "And the other lamb shalt thou offer at even: as the meat offering of the morning, and as the drink offering thereof, thou shalt offer it, a sacrifice made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD." (28:8) Registered: the evening offering has no separate specification — the words "as the meat offering of the morning" carry the morning's whole set (lamb, meat offering, drink offering) over to the evening unchanged; the time-word of this verse is "at even," the same wording as verse 4 in the KJV (the Chinese base text differs between its two places; see the base-text notes at the head of this sermon).

(Argument) The whole point of the continual offering lies in the word "continual." It is not offered when a great occasion arises, nor when trouble comes, nor when feeling moves — it is two lambs every day, one in the morning and one at even, every day like this. The hardest worship in the Bible has never been the great kind, but the continual kind: a great feast comes once a year and can be managed by an effort of the teeth; but morning and evening daily, wind and rain notwithstanding, unbroken for decades — that is where a community's true weight shows. Our spiritual lives today usually fail at exactly this place: we do not lack peaks — special meetings, camps, nights of being moved, we lack none of them; what we lack is the plain, ordinary lamb of the next morning. We offer when the mood is good and break off when it is bad; we break off when busy, and do not necessarily make it up when free. The rule of the continual offering says: the relationship with God is not kept alive by peaks, but by mornings and evenings — in the morning the day is offered up first, at even the day is handed back, and so the days, one by one, are ringed round by offering. If you feel yourself grown distant from God, do not first go looking for some great thing to do; first restore the two lambs of morning and evening: a quiet time on rising, an accounting before sleep; the portion need not be large — "without spot" is enough: whole-hearted, unmixed, and that is a sweet savour.

(Argument) The words "without spot" must also be spoken to everyone who would fob God off with second-best. Two lambs a day, more than seven hundred in a year (reckoned from the daily number the text itself states; this chapter gives no yearly total — see Endnote 8) — a quantity that large, yet the quality does not drop by a fraction: every single one must be without spot. Quantity and quality do not quarrel here — it is not that because much is offered it may be offered roughly; precisely because it is offered continually, whether each instance is earnest shows itself truly, true or false. For an occasional great occasion a man can grit his teeth and bring out his best; day after day like this, still spotless lamb after lamb — that is no longer gritting the teeth, that is character. The fault we fall into most easily is precisely using "every day like this" as an excuse for lowering the quality: since there is prayer every day anyway, murmur a couple of sentences and have done; since there is a meeting every week anyway, arriving late and leaving early does no harm. The continual offering's account is not reckoned that way: God can count the days clearly, and He can also see every lamb clearly. Treat every ordinary instance as if it were the only one — that is the second lesson the continual offering leaves us.

(Argument) One more word on verse 8, "as the meat offering of the morning." The evening offering is issued no separate specification; Scripture says only: as the morning's. That means the quality of worship is not permitted to differ between morning and evening: in the morning the spirit is fresh and the offering is made neatly; at even the man is weary and the day's matters are many — the specification is not permitted to weary with him. How many men's days are a tiger's head and a snake's tail: resolutions in the morning, makeshifts at night; covenants at the year's opening, slackness at its close. God nails the evening offering to the morning's specification precisely to forbid worship being discounted with strength and mood. And there is a further layer: the evening offering following the morning's also gives the day a complete closing — what was offered in the morning is offered once more at even, the two ends of the day are both sealed by offering, and whatever bumps lie in the middle, the two ends are fixed. Our way of closing a day today is mostly to fall asleep scrolling a phone; Scripture's way of closing a day is to offer one more lamb: to lay this day's successes, failures, gains, and losses once more upon the altar — and only then to sleep.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Burnt offering" is ʿōlâ (H5930); "continual" is tāmîd (H8548), occurring in this chapter within the phrase "continual burnt offering" at 28:3, 6, 10, 15, 23, 24, 31 (for the count see Endnote 4), denoting constancy, unbrokenness; both numbers were verified for the Chinese manuscript against the BibleHub verse-by-verse comparison pages (the comparison page for 28:6 expressly lists ʿōlâ H5930 and tāmîd H8548). "Sweet savour" is the fixed pairing rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ: rêaḥ (H7381) plus nîḥōaḥ (H5207), verified on the same page. "Sacrifice made by fire" is ʾiššeh (H801), verified on the same page. "Drink offering" is nesek (H5262), verified against the comparison page for 28:10. "Meat offering" is minḥâ (H4503), verified against the comparison page for 28:5. "Lamb" is kebeś (H3532), verified against the comparison page for 28:7. "Ephah" is ʾêpâ (H374) and "hin" is hîn (H1969), verified against the comparison pages. (Fact) This movement closes with a register of the continual tier's complete inventory: in one day — two lambs (of the first year, without spot), two meat offerings (each a tenth part of an ephah of flour and the fourth part of an hin of oil), two drink offerings (each the fourth part of an hin of wine, poured in the holy place); two times — in the morning and at even (28:4, 8); one line of origin — ordained in mount Sinai (28:6). This tier is the foundation of the whole chapter: the closing sentence of every tier below returns to this tier to say "beside" it — the foundation is never demolished because the upper storeys are built higher.

### Part Three: The Sabbath and the Beginnings of Months — Beside the Continual Offering (Numbers 28:9-15) (24:00-36:00)

(Fact) Verse 9, the Sabbath addition, quoted verbatim from the base text: "And on the sabbath day two lambs of the first year without spot, and two tenth deals of flour for a meat offering, mingled with oil, and the drink offering thereof:" (28:9) Three items registered: the added lambs are still two, of the first year, without spot — of the same quality as the continual offering; the meat offering is two tenth deals of an ephah, entered as one total (the continual offering allows a tenth deal per lamb, and two lambs make exactly two tenth deals; this sermon registers the text's way of writing the total only); the drink offering is offered with it as usual. Verse 10, the closing of the Sabbath tier, in the base text's wording: "This is the burnt offering of every sabbath, beside the continual burnt offering, and his drink offering." (28:10) Here stands the chapter's first stacking sentence: the Sabbath offering does not replace the continual offering — on that day there are first the continual offering's two lambs of morning and evening, and then the Sabbath's two lambs in addition. Verse 11, the main item of the beginnings of months, in the base text's wording: "And in the beginnings of your months ye shall offer a burnt offering unto the LORD; two young bullocks, and one ram, seven lambs of the first year without spot;" (28:11) The kinds and numbers registered: two young bullocks, one ram, seven lambs — from here on, every feast tier uses this same combination (see the counts in Parts Two, Four, and Five). Verses 12-13, the meat-offering proportions of the beginnings of months, in the base text's wording: "And three tenth deals of flour for a meat offering, mingled with oil, for one bullock; and two tenth deals of flour for a meat offering, mingled with oil, for one ram;" (28:12) "And a several tenth deal of flour mingled with oil for a meat offering unto one lamb; for a burnt offering of a sweet savour, a sacrifice made by fire unto the LORD." (28:13) The descending gradient registered: three tenth deals for a bullock, two for a ram, one for a lamb — the quantity of the meat offering is set by the size of the animal. Verse 14, the drink-offering proportions and the standing rule of the beginnings of months, in the base text's wording: "And their drink offerings shall be half an hin of wine unto a bullock, and the third part of an hin unto a ram, and a fourth part of an hin unto a lamb: this is the burnt offering of every month throughout the months of the year." (28:14) Registered: the gradient of wine is half an hin, the third part of an hin, a fourth part of an hin; and the tier's rhythmic definition in one clause — "throughout the months of the year." Verse 15, the sin offering of the beginnings of months and its closing, in the base text's wording: "And one kid of the goats for a sin offering unto the LORD shall be offered, beside the continual burnt offering, and his drink offering." (28:15) Registered: at every beginning of a month, besides the burnt offering there is one more kid of the goats for a sin offering; and the closing sentence presents the stacking construction again — "beside the continual burnt offering, and his drink offering."

(Argument) The keyword of this movement is "beside" — the Sabbath beside the continual offering, the beginnings of months beside the continual offering. Increase in worship, in Scripture, is never replacement; it is stacking: it is not that, the Sabbath having come, the daily lesson is excused; nor that, the feast having passed, the ordinary offering may be saved. Our fault is exactly the reverse — we always want to offset the small with the great: having been to church once on the Lord's Day, we think a week's prayer is excused; having given one large offering, we think ordinary faithfulness is optional; having read through the night once, we think the daily devotion need not be kept. God's abacus does not reckon that way: however solemn the Sabbath's two lambs, they cannot move the morning lamb from its place; however many the bullocks and rams of the beginnings of months, they are offered beside the continual offering. Growth in the spiritual life is stacking upward, not changing patterns: keep the daily lesson, then add the weekly Sabbath; keep the weekly Sabbath, then add the monthly self-examination; every layer is an addition, and no layer is there to pay off a debt. If you want to go deeper in faith, do not first think of new patterns — first ask: is the layer I now stand on stacked firm?

(Argument) The sin offering of the beginnings of months must be spoken of separately. On the first day of every month, besides the burnt offering of bullocks, ram, and lambs, one kid of the goats must be added for a sin offering (28:15) — that is to say, whenever time turns a new page, deal with sin first, and only then speak of anything else. The transgressions, negligences, and debts a man accumulates unawares in a month are not permitted to be dragged rolling into the next month: settled monthly, begun afresh monthly. What consideration this is: God knows a man cannot bear the long stretch of one atonement a year, and He also knows the two daily lambs of the burnt offering are worship, not a sin offering — so on the head day of every month He has prepared a kid of the goats for a sin offering, waiting for the people to clear the preceding month. The commonest backlog in our lives today is not money but sin: having wronged someone, no apology; having failed God, no dealing with it; resentment piled up in the heart, left to mildew. The rule of the beginnings of months says: give your conscience a first-of-the-month — once a month, settle the account clearly: confess what must be confessed, repay what must be repaid, be reconciled where reconciliation is due, and do not let last month's ash fall into this month's offering.

(Argument) The gradient of the meat offering and the drink offering is also worth a word: a bullock is matched with three tenth deals, a ram with two, a lamb with one; the wine likewise — half an hin, the third part of an hin, a fourth part of an hin — tier by tier, without the slightest disorder (28:12-14). That God fixes even the quantities of the accompanying offerings this carefully shows that in His eyes worship has no "roughly": the larger animal is matched with the larger meat offering, the smaller with the smaller, each according to its measure; a fraction more for show, or a fraction less in grudging, is alike out of rule. What we offer God should likewise be according to measure, according to rule, according to heart: it is not a contest of spectacle — the bullock's three tenth deals are not there to crush the lamb's one tenth deal; the lamb offering its one tenth deal and the bullock offering its three are alike a sweet savour before God (28:13). Let each offer according to what he has — no comparing, no self-deprecation, no makeshift: if your one tenth deal is offered earnestly, it is a duly qualified entry on this calendar.

### Part Four: The Fourteenth Day of the First Month — The Passover and the Seven Days of Unleavened Bread (Numbers 28:16-25) (36:00-48:00)

(Fact) Verse 16, the date of the Passover, quoted verbatim from the base text: "And in the fourteenth day of the first month is the passover of the LORD." (28:16) Registered: this verse has date and name only — which day, and whose feast; this chapter records no detail at all of the slaying, the blood, or the eating for the Passover itself (those details stand in Exodus 12, preceding-text layer); this chapter's force falls on the seven days that immediately follow. Verse 17, the opening of the feast, in the base text's wording: "And in the fifteenth day of this month is the feast: seven days shall unleavened bread be eaten." (28:17) Registered: the fifteenth day is the beginning of the feast, and the feast's mark is unleavened bread eaten seven days. Verse 18, the rule of the first day, in the base text's wording: "In the first day shall be an holy convocation; ye shall do no manner of servile work therein:" (28:18) Verse 19, the burnt offering of each of the seven days, in the base text's wording: "But ye shall offer a sacrifice made by fire for a burnt offering unto the LORD; two young bullocks, and one ram, and seven lambs of the first year: they shall be unto you without blemish:" (28:19) Registered: the kinds and numbers are the same combination as at the beginnings of months (two bullocks, one ram, seven lambs), and the requirement of quality is stated again — "they shall be unto you without blemish." Verses 20-21, the meat-offering proportions, in the base text's wording: "And their meat offering shall be of flour mingled with oil: three tenth deals shall ye offer for a bullock, and two tenth deals for a ram;" (28:20) "A several tenth deal shalt thou offer for every lamb, throughout the seven lambs:" (28:21) Registered: the proportions are the same gradient as at the beginnings of months (28:12-13, earlier movement of this chapter). Verse 22, the sin offering, in the base text's wording: "And one goat for a sin offering, to make an atonement for you." (28:22) Verse 23, the relation to the continual offering, in the base text's wording: "Ye shall offer these beside the burnt offering in the morning, which is for a continual burnt offering." (28:23) One close-reading item registered: here the text does not speak of the continual offering in general but expressly of "the burnt offering in the morning" — the feast additions are reckoned from the morning onward: first the morning offering, then the feast offering added. Verse 24, the general rule of the seven days, in the base text's wording: "After this manner ye shall offer daily, throughout the seven days, the meat of the sacrifice made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD: it shall be offered beside the continual burnt offering, and his drink offering." (28:24) Registered: the whole set of verses 19-22 is not a one-time thing — throughout seven days it is offered daily after this manner; and the word "meat" (the Chinese base text: "food") appears here again, answering to the general rule (28:2). Verse 25, the rule of the last day, in the base text's wording: "And on the seventh day ye shall have an holy convocation; ye shall do no servile work." (28:25) Registered: the first and last days of the seven each have an holy convocation and each rest from servile work (28:18, 25); for the five days between, the text records offerings and the eating of unleavened bread only, and records no convocation.

(Argument) First, the "lightness" of the Passover in this chapter and the "weight" of the Feast of Unleavened Bread. The story of the exodus night is passed over in this chapter with a single verse giving the date (28:16); the offerings of the seven days take ten verses, quantities arranged in detail. This is not a slighting of the Passover; it is that this chapter's charge is different: this chapter does not tell the story, it arranges the roster — it presupposes that the people already know what happened that night, and it concerns itself only with turning the seven days that remember that night into seven days with offerings, with convocations, and with rest from work. There is a lesson here worth our learning: if remembrance is left as story only, three years of telling will wear it thin; remembrance must land on the calendar, on every day's offering, on the rest from work of the first and last days, if it is to be handed down. When families and churches today remember the Lord's grace, they should likewise have this kind of landing arrangement: not only telling the old story once from the platform, but arranging thanksgiving into the days — which days to stop, which days to gather together, what to offer each day, all fixed. Grace arranged into the calendar will not be crowded out of life by busyness.

(Argument) "Ye shall do no servile work" is said twice within the seven days (28:18, 25), and once more in the closing movement at the Feast of Weeks — three times in the whole chapter. The weight of this sentence must be weighed in the wilderness setting: a people living hand-to-mouth from heaven, their census just taken and Canaan ahead of them, and God precisely commands them to stop their hands during the feast — stop on the first day, stop on the seventh, the offerings continuing as usual in between. Rest from work is not laziness; it is a declaration: our living does not rest wholly on our hands; the produce of the ground and the offerings on the altar are not things our labour has earned — they are God's gift. Only when the hands stop does the heart have room made in it for remembrance. What we are least willing to stop today is precisely the hand: messages must be answered in seconds, work must be rushed through the night, even the feast days are spent rushing from venue to venue. The rule of Unleavened Bread stands against us and says: the feast's first lesson is not offering, but stopping — do no servile work; first bring the hands back, then bring the offering up. A man whose hands do not stop offers everything with a flavour of hurry on it; once the hurry rises, the sweet savour is gone.

(Argument) That the seven days are daily after the same manner carries one more layer. Verse 24 says "ye shall offer daily, throughout the seven days" after this manner — seven days, the same bullocks, the same ram, the same lambs, the same goat for a sin offering; not one day may be reduced, not one day may change its pattern. By the third day of a feast a man easily tires: the freshness of the first day has passed, the hope of the seventh day is still far off, and the middle is where the discount most easily creeps in. God nails the seven days into one manner precisely as a guard against this middle weariness: the depth of remembrance lies not in the solemnity of the first day, but in the seventh day's still following the first day's manner. So it is with marriage, so with service, so with devotion — the beginnings are all solemn; the hard thing is the word "throughout": once after the manner is not hard; seven times, seventy times after the manner — there the true heart shows. If your worship has the look of an holy convocation on the first day only and is wound up carelessly by the seventh, these ten verses were written for you: daily after this manner — live the last day as earnestly as the first.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The "passover" is pesaḥ (H6453), verified in this series' Exodus sermons. The "unleavened" of "unleavened bread" belongs to the maṣṣâ family (H4682), verified in the series. "Holy convocation" is the fixed pairing miqrāʾ-qōdeš: the number of miqrāʾ is marked unverified in this sermon — see Endnote 5; qōdeš (H6944) has been verified (see the Summary of Part Two). How the word rendered "feast" at 28:17 corresponds to the môʿēd (H4150) of the general rule this sermon does not determine here; the base text's renderings only are registered.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) How this chapter's list is to be read together with the feast list of Leviticus 23 is set out here without adjudication. The two places both record the Passover and Unleavened Bread: Leviticus 23 takes as its frame the proclamation of the feasts, the convocations, and the commemorative meaning of each feast, and for Unleavened Bread records in addition the details of the sheaf and the eating (cross-reference layer, in key points); this chapter gives only the dates and the quantities of the public offerings, and for the Passover itself records the date in one sentence only (28:16). The difference in fullness has more than one reading — it may be taken that the two places divide the labour complementarily (one governing the feast's meaning, the other the offering's quantity), or that they arise from different layers of editing. This sermon registers only what each of the two texts carries, and makes no determination of source or editing at this chapter's fact layer. Further, this chapter does not record the manner of slaying and eating the Passover lamb; its reading together with the original rule of Exodus 12 and the place provisions of Deuteronomy 16 belongs to cross-book synthetic judgment; this sermon registers the three places as standing side by side and adjudicates nothing here.

(Fact) This movement closes with a register of the seven days of Unleavened Bread in total: two days of holy convocation (the first day and the seventh, 28:18, 25), two days of rest from work (the same two verses); seven sets of additions (one set daily: two bullocks, one ram, seven lambs, one goat, with the meat-offering gradient, 28:19-22, 24), all seven days beside the continual offering (28:23, 24). And one whole-chapter contrast is registered: this movement is the longest in the chapter (ten verses) and the only tier spanning seven days — its rhythm the sparsest (once a year), its arrangement the densest (seven days in a row): the farther apart the days, the more strictly they are kept.

### Part Five: The Feast of Weeks and the Firstfruits — The Day of the New Meat Offering (Numbers 28:26-31, with the Whole Chapter) (48:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 26, the date and naming of the last tier, quoted verbatim from the base text: "Also in the day of the firstfruits, when ye bring a new meat offering unto the LORD, after your weeks be out, ye shall have an holy convocation; ye shall do no servile work:" (28:26) The items registered one by one: first, the threefold naming of this day — the day of the firstfruits, after the weeks are out, the day when a new meat offering is brought unto the LORD; second, this day has an holy convocation and rest from servile work, on the same rule as the first and last days of Unleavened Bread (28:18, 25, earlier movement); third, this verse gives neither a calendar-style date for the day nor a formula for reckoning from what day — the reckoning stands in Leviticus 23:15-16 (cross-reference layer, in key points); this chapter records this day's naming and manner of offering only. Verse 27, this day's burnt offering, in the base text's wording: "But ye shall offer the burnt offering for a sweet savour unto the LORD; two young bullocks, one ram, seven lambs of the first year;" (28:27) Registered: the combination is the same as at the beginnings of months and at Unleavened Bread (two bullocks, one ram, seven lambs); its character is "the burnt offering for a sweet savour" — the word "sweet savour" here reaches its sixth occurrence in the chapter (for the count see Endnote 4). Verses 28-29, the meat-offering proportions, in the base text's wording: "And their meat offering of flour mingled with oil, three tenth deals unto one bullock, two tenth deals unto one ram," (28:28) "A several tenth deal unto one lamb, throughout the seven lambs;" (28:29) Registered: the gradient appears for the third time (previously at 28:12-13 and 20-21) — three rounds of proportions in the chapter, not a letter changed. Verse 30, the atonement, in the base text's wording: "And one kid of the goats, to make an atonement for you." (28:30) A difference of wording registered: this verse reads "to make an atonement for you," not the full name "for a sin offering" of 28:15 and 22; recorded as the base text stands, separately. Verse 31, the closing of the whole chapter, in the base text's wording: "Ye shall offer them beside the continual burnt offering, and his meat offering, (they shall be unto you without blemish) and their drink offerings." (28:31) Two items registered: first, the closing sentence returns to the continual offering for the fifth time — and this time names the continual offering's meat offering and drink offerings along with it; second, the whole chapter ends with "they shall be unto you without blemish" — the general rule began with "in their due season," and the chapter ends with "without blemish": from head to tail, the one governs time, the other quality.

(Argument) The last tier offers what is "new": the firstfruits of the harvest, a new meat offering (28:26). That a calendar which speaks of constancy should land at last on the word "new" is deeply significant. What the continual offering keeps is the old rule — ordained in mount Sinai (28:6); what the Feast of Weeks offers is the first of the new grain from the ground: the old rule unbroken, and new grain come again. The healthiest shape of a life of faith is exactly these two together: a constancy that cannot be shaken — morning and evening daily, the Sabbath weekly, settlement monthly; and also firstfruits fresh every year — the grace God has newly given this year, the fruit newly grown, the doors newly opened, to be offered as the first portion, not remembered for God only when they have grown old and are left over. Many men have constancy without newness: the days are kept in order, but the man has dried up. Others have newness without constancy: freshness everywhere, but the root shallow. This chapter arranges the two in one calendar: the new meat offering of the firstfruits is in its turn offered beside the continual offering (28:31) — newness always grows on the root of constancy.

(Argument) The word "firstfruits" must also be spoken to everyone who cannot bear to offer the first portion. The first of the ground's grain is the most precious: a famine year just past, the old grain and the new not yet joined, the first portion of grain is the whole family's life. And this is precisely the portion God asks for — not because God lacks this portion, but because the people must learn a lesson: the man who offers the first portion first is a man who believes that all the grain in the ground is God's gift; to offer the remainder after eating one's fill — that is not offering, that is dispensing leftovers. What we offer God is so often the leftover time, the leftover strength, the leftover money: when the busyness is done, the tiredness done, the pleasure done, a few minutes left for reading Scripture; when the spending is done, the saving done, the enjoying done, a little left for an offering. The rule of the firstfruits stands against us: reverse the order — the first portion of time, the first portion of income, the first portion of strength, offered first. In the heart of the man who offers first there is one sentence settled: God first; my needs — He is responsible for them. That sentence is the sweet savour of the firstfruits.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Firstfruits" is the word bikkûrîm, and "weeks" (rendered in the Chinese base text as the Feast of Weeks, literally "seven sevens") is šābûʿôt (weeks / sevens of days); the original numbers of both words are marked unverified in this sermon — see Endnote 5; the word-forms and the base text's renderings only are recorded. A recount of personal names and place-names in this chapter: no member of the people is named by personal name in the whole chapter; place-names occur twice — mount Sinai (28:6) and the holy place (28:7, a place-designation); there is one Speaker only, God (28:2-31), with the introduction occurring once (28:1). A recount of the offering names, each recounted on the KJV letter for this translation: "burnt offering" is the chapter's most frequent offering name; "meat offering" stands in nine verses and "drink offering" in eight verses (the verse lists are in Endnote 4 — the drink-offering verse count differs from the Chinese manuscript's register, for the reason registered there).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two matters are set out here without adjudication. First: this chapter's feast list stops at the Feast of Weeks — the Feast of Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and the Feast of Tabernacles are not in this chapter; their quantities stand in the next chapter (Numbers 29, following-chapter layer). Numbers 28 and 29 together make one complete annual calendar of offerings, and this chapter is the first half; where the boundary between the two chapters falls, and why it is so divided, has more than one reading, and this sermon registers only this chapter's beginning and end (beginning with the daily, ending with the Feast of Weeks) and makes no determination of the intent of the division. Second: this chapter says nothing of who provides for, or who handles, the offerings of its several tiers: the text commands only "ye shall offer"; by whom they are prepared and through whose hands they pass, this chapter has not one word. Later texts carry records of kings providing for the public offerings (2 Chronicles 31:3, cross-reference layer, in key points); how that is to be read together with this chapter's silence belongs to cross-book synthetic judgment, and this sermon registers this chapter's blank only and fills nothing in by extrapolation.

(New Testament Reading) When the apostle Paul speaks of the resurrection he calls Christ, in key points, the firstfruits of them that slept, and says, in key points, that every man rises in his own order — Christ the firstfruits, afterward they that are Christ's at his coming (1 Corinthians 15:20, 23, cross-reference reading layer, in key points). One contrast is registered at the reading layer: the firstfruits of the Feast of Weeks in this chapter (28:26) are the presentation of the first of the ground's new grain; the New Testament uses this same "firstfruits" image for Christ's resurrection as the first portion of the resurrection of all. The reading layer registers only how the New Testament reads Christ by "firstfruits"; it does not fill the doctrine of the resurrection back in as this chapter's sacrificial fact. What this chapter offers remains the new grain of the harvest and the burnt offering of bullocks, ram, and lambs, its verses and its proportions all following the original text.

"Without spot / without blemish": five occurrences in all (28:3, 9, 11, 19, 31) — the same places as the Chinese register's five, but the KJV splits the wording, "without spot" at 28:3, 9, 11 and "without blemish" at 28:19, 31. The stacking construction ("beside"): five occurrences (28:10, 15, 23, 24, 31) — the same count, and uniform in the KJV where the Chinese base text splits its closing word between two forms. "Holy convocation": three occurrences; the rest-from-work clause: three occurrences (all at 28:18, 25, 26; the KJV wording at verse 18 is the fuller "no manner of servile work"). The joins with the chapters before and after: at the end of the preceding chapter the man is set up (Joshua is installed, 27:23, in key points); at this chapter's opening the days are arranged (in their due season, 28:2) — first a man to succeed, then a calendar to keep; the next chapter continues with the feasts of the second half of the year (Numbers 29, following-chapter layer), and the two chapters together make the book, so that the wilderness's new generation has its year's worship settled complete.

(Argument) The whole chapter closes with one sentence left for each of three kinds of people. First, for those whose worship has only peaks and no daily life: restore your continual offering first — one lamb in the morning, one at even; a small portion does no harm; spotless and unbroken outweighs everything; the sweet savour God delights in is first of all there every day, and only then the great feasts'. Second, for those who would offset the small with the great: the Sabbath does not offset the continual offering, the feasts do not offset the beginnings of months, an offering does not offset prayer — on God's account every tier has its own place, and an addition is always "beside"; no tier can stand in for another; stack every layer firm, and your days will have thickness. Third, for those who cannot bear to offer the first portion: learn from the Feast of Weeks — the new grain offered first, the first portion offered first; once the order is right, the heart is at rest; what God has always wanted is not our leftovers, but the order of our faith. The days are God's, the offerings are in their due season; we have only to offer every day, every tier, every portion after the manner — constant without slackening, new without forgetting: that is the whole lesson this calendar leaves us.

## Devotional Excerpt

This chapter has no story, only a calendar. Yet the calendar's first sentence drives the root of worship firm: "My offering, and my bread for my sacrifices made by fire, for a sweet savour unto me, shall ye observe to offer unto me in their due season." (28:2) What God wants is not grandeur on impulse, but constancy in due season: a lamb every morning and a lamb every evening — "It is a continual burnt offering, which was ordained in mount Sinai for a sweet savour, a sacrifice made by fire unto the LORD." (28:6) On the Sabbath more is added, at the beginnings of months more is added, at the feasts more still — and however much is added, it is all beside the continual offering; those two lambs of morning and evening may not be broken for a single day. Our lives of faith fail in just this way so often: we do not lack being moved, we lack constancy; we do not lack great things, we lack the daily. We always wait until we have the feeling before we pray, until we have the time before we read — and the fire on the altar goes out in fits and starts like this. Today God sets this calendar before us: first offer up the two small lambs of morning and evening — a quiet time on rising, an accounting at even; they need not be large; their worth lies in the due season, unbroken. Keep the continual offering, and the additions of Sabbath, beginnings of months, and feasts have a root; if the root is broken, no feast however great can hold the rest up. Day by day the days are ringed round by offering, and day by day the life gains its sweet savour — and that savour is no gust of wind; it is gathered day by day.

English (KJV-based):

"My offering, and my bread for my sacrifices made by fire, for a sweet savour unto me, shall ye observe to offer unto me in their due season." (Num 28:2, KJV) "It is a continual burnt offering, which was ordained in mount Sinai for a sweet savour, a sacrifice made by fire unto the LORD." (Num 28:6, KJV) God did not ask Israel for occasional grandeur; He asked for offerings in their due season — a lamb every morning and a lamb every evening, without fail, and every feast added beside the continual offering, never instead of it. Bring back the daily lambs: a quiet morning offering, an honest evening one. Constancy, offered without blemish, is the sweet savour God delights in.

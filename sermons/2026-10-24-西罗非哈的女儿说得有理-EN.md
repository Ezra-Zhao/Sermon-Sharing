# The Daughters of Zelophehad Speak Right — A Sermon on Numbers 27

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: First translation, pending proofreading
Text: Numbers chapter 27 (23 verses in full, numbered alike in Chinese and English; the Hebrew canonical chapter division was checked and is the same chapter and verses, see Endnote 1)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), taken from the anchor text fetched from bible.com, version 1 (KJV), for Numbers 27, and checked verse by verse against that anchor; the companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (Simplified, "Shen" edition; hereafter "the Chinese base text"). The English devotional excerpt embeds the KJV wording directly and is not a back-translation from the Chinese.
Title verification: The title is taken from the opening words of God's verdict to Moses in verse 7, read continuously in the base text's own words — "The daughters of Zelophehad speak right". The chapter has two movements — the five daughters of Zelophehad contend for an inheritance, God gives the verdict and establishes the order of inheritance (27:1-11), and the announcement of Moses' end with the setting apart of Joshua (27:12-23) — and one line runs through both: in each case a cause is brought before God, and God Himself gives the verdict and Himself establishes the outcome: the daughters' presentation of their case receives the one sentence, "speak right", and Moses' presentation of his case (asking for a man to govern the congregation) receives Joshua, a man in whom is the spirit. The candidates were checked verse by verse: "Give unto us therefore a possession among the brethren of our father." (27:4) covers only the five daughters' request; "that the congregation of the LORD be not as sheep which have no shepherd." (27:17) covers only Moses' prayer; "And thou shalt put some of thine honour upon him," (27:20) covers only one link in the setting apart. Verse 7 alone is the chapter's pivotal verdict: it is both God's pattern of response to the case presented by the weak, and it prefigures the way God answers Moses' presented case in the second half of the chapter — God hears a case presented, God gives the verdict — and it is therefore the title. The English title follows the KJV wording of the same verse: "The Daughters of Zelophehad Speak Right".
Base-text notes (wherever another version or a common paraphrase differs, the base text is recorded as it stands): At 27:3 the KJV reads "but died in his own sin, and had no sons." — he was not in the company of Korah; the Chinese base text likewise reads that he did not gather with Korah's company against the LORD and died in his own sin, and it does not follow the Revised Chinese Union Version's wording of the kind "did not belong to Korah's party". At 27:4 the verse begins "Why should the name of our father be done away" — the first word is "Why"; the request reads "Give unto us therefore a possession among the brethren of our father." At 27:6 the introduction reads "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying," — in the Chinese base text the verb is its word for a formal speaking-forth to Moses. At 27:7 the command reads "thou shalt surely give them a possession of an inheritance among their father's brethren; and thou shalt cause the inheritance of their father to pass unto them." — the Chinese base text reads, in full, that land is to be given to them as a possession among their father's brethren, and is not to be passed over with a bare "give them an inheritance". At 27:8 the rule reads "If a man die, and have no son, then ye shall cause his inheritance to pass unto his daughter." At 27:11 the successor "he shall possess it", and the conclusion reads "and it shall be unto the children of Israel a statute of judgment", without a break in that phrase. At 27:12 the place is "this mount Abarim" — Mount Abarim, not "the Abarim range". At 27:13 Moses is to be "gathered unto thy people", in the Chinese base text "gathered to your ancestors". At 27:14 the charge is "ye rebelled against my commandment", in the Chinese base text "rebelled against my command" ("command", not the fuller word for "commandment" as a code), and "to sanctify me at the water before their eyes", with the base text's parenthetical note carried in full: "that is the water of Meribah in Kadesh in the wilderness of Zin." At 27:16 the Chinese "Shen" edition reads "the God of the spirits of all flesh" with its word for God, not the alternative word for God. At 27:17 the flock is "as sheep which have no shepherd" — "sheep", in the Chinese base text "like a flock of sheep without a shepherd", not "like sheep" in the looser paraphrase. At 27:18 the verse is broken by a semicolon in the KJV: "a man in whom is the spirit, and lay thine hand upon him;" — the Chinese base text likewise breaks the sentence at the semicolon. At 27:20 the KJV reads "And thou shalt put some of thine honour upon him, that all the congregation of the children of Israel may be obedient." At 27:21 the KJV reads "at his word shall they go out, and at his word they shall come in" — the Chinese base text reads that he and all the congregation shall go out and come in according to Eleazar's word; the difference in the pronoun's reference is registered in the Scholarly and Historical Discussion of Part Five and is not harmonized. At 27:22 the KJV reads "And Moses did as the LORD commanded him:" — there is no "his" in the verse. At 27:23 the KJV reads "And he laid his hands upon him, and gave him a charge, as the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses." — the verse has no expressed subject at its head, carrying Moses over from verse 22, as the Chinese base text likewise does.
Form: The main text is divided into five parts, marked with their Scripture ranges and time codes (00:00-10:00 / 10:00-24:00 / 24:00-36:00 / 36:00-48:00 / 48:00-60:00); paragraphs are marked (Fact) / (Argument) / (Summary) / (New Testament Reading Layer) / (Scholarly & Historical Discussion Layer) to keep the layers distinct. Original-language words appear only in the (Summary) paragraphs as philological statements; material from other chapters enters only as cross-reference, with its layer marked, and is never read back into this chapter's fact layer.

## Summary

The numbering of chapter 26 has only just ended when the five daughters of Zelophehad stand at the door of the tabernacle. Their father died in the wilderness and had no son; under the rule for dividing the land as it then stood, this family's name was finished in the register, yet the land had no share for them, and their father's name was to be done away from among his family. The five sisters state their case before Moses, Eleazar the priest, the princes, and all the congregation: their father was not one of Korah's company, he died in his own sin; why should his name be done away because he had no son? They ask that a possession be given to them among their father's brethren (27:1-4). Moses does not decide on his own authority; he brings their cause before the LORD, and God's verdict gives its characterization in its very first sentence: "The daughters of Zelophehad speak right:" — and He then commands that land be given to them as a possession, and turns the single case into a general rule: if a man dies and has no son, his inheritance passes to his daughter; if he has no daughter, to his brethren; if he has no brethren, to his father's brethren; if there are none of those, to the kinsman next to him in his family (27:5-11). The words have scarcely fallen when God turns to Moses and speaks of Moses' own end: go up Mount Abarim and see the land of promise, and after seeing it be gathered to your people, as your brother Aaron was, because at the water of Meribah you did not sanctify God (27:12-14). Moses hears the date of his own death, and his first sentence is not a plea for himself but a plea for the congregation: may the LORD, the God of the spirits of all flesh, set a man over the congregation, who may go out and go in before them and lead them, so that the congregation will not be as sheep which have no shepherd (27:15-17). God then names Joshua the son of Nun — a man in whom is the spirit — and commands Moses to lay his hand on him, to set him before Eleazar and all the congregation and give him a charge, and to put some of Moses' honour upon him; thereafter Joshua is to stand before the priest, who shall ask counsel for him after the judgment of Urim, and they are to go out and come in at that word (27:18-21). Moses does it all, one thing after another: he takes him, sets him, lays his hands on him, and gives him a charge (27:22-23). Within one chapter, the name of a sonless household is preserved, and a leader's place is handed over — inheritance and office alike are transferred by God's verdict.

## Main Text

### Part One: Five Daughters Stand at the Door of the Tabernacle (27:1-4) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) Verse 1, those who come forward and the genealogy, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "Then came the daughters of Zelophehad, the son of Hepher, the son of Gilead, the son of Machir, the son of Manasseh, of the families of Manasseh the son of Joseph: and these are the names of his daughters; Mahlah, Noah, and Hoglah, and Milcah, and Tirzah." (27:1) The items registered one by one: First, the genealogy chain is recorded with its five links — Manasseh, Machir, Gilead, Hepher, Zelophehad: the five sisters' plea is not shouted out of nowhere; they first fasten their own line under the name of Manasseh the son of Joseph. Second, the names of the five are recorded one by one — Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, Tirzah — the same as the brief register note of their names in the preceding chapter (26:33, preceding-text layer): the preceding chapter records only the names; in this chapter the owners of the names step forward and speak for themselves. Third, the narrative verb of the verse is gathered into the one movement, they came forward — to whom they came and what they said is unfolded only in verse 2. Verse 2, the place where they stood and those before whom they stood, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And they stood before Moses, and before Eleazar the priest, and before the princes and all the congregation, by the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, saying," (27:2) Registered for the record: the place is the door of the tabernacle of the congregation; those who hear the case are in four layers — Moses, Eleazar the priest, the princes, and all the congregation; there is no private occasion anywhere. Verse 3, the first half of their statement, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "Our father died in the wilderness, and he was not in the company of them that gathered themselves together against the LORD in the company of Korah; but died in his own sin, and had no sons." (27:3) The three facts recorded as they stand: their father died in the wilderness; he was not among those who gathered themselves together against the LORD in the company of Korah; he died in his own sin, and he had no sons. Verse 4, the second half of their statement — first a question, then a request — quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "Why should the name of our father be done away from among his family, because he hath no son? Give unto us therefore a possession among the brethren of our father." (27:4) "the name of our father be done away from among his family" is the central phrase of the question: what they contend for first is their father's name, and only second is the land.

(Argument) Consider first the manner in which the five sisters stand forward. They do not canvass support through the camp, they do not make a scene, they do not bypass the process and seek Moses out privately to plead; they walk to the door of the tabernacle, stand before Moses, the priest, the princes, and all the congregation, and say their words plainly in public. This is the strongest form that the defence of the rights of the weak takes in Scripture: the occasion is public, those addressed are all present, and the statement is orderly — first the genealogy (whose family we are), then the facts (how our father died, and whether he had a son), then the question (why should his name be done away), and finally the request (give us a possession among the brethren). Four steps, not one word too many and not one word too few. Many people today who have suffered a wrong know only two things to do: endure, or make an uproar. To endure is to swallow the question that ought to be asked, and not dare to make a sound even when one's name really is done away; to make an uproar is to wreck the scene beyond repair, until even the right that was in one's hand has been talked away. The five sisters give a third way: bring your cause, in due order, to the place where it ought to be brought. If your standing, your inheritance, your grievance is real, stand forward as they did — stand in the open light of day, and tell the facts, one by one, to those who hold authority. God is pleased with a case presented in this way; the next part of this chapter is the clear proof.

(Argument) Consider next for whom they contend. They do not open their mouths with "we"; they say first "Our father": our father died in the wilderness, he was not a rebel, he simply had no son — why should his name be done away? To contend for an inheritance is the substance, to contend for the name is the root; in the world of this chapter, for a name to be done away from among his family is for a man to be deleted, line by line, from the history of God's people, and for the mark of his house in the land of promise to be wiped away with it. The filial devotion of the five sisters is not to build their father a handsome tomb; it is not to let their father's name disappear from the register. What people today contend for on behalf of their parents is mostly face; what these sisters contended for was their father's place in God's promise. If you truly honour your forebears, do not merely put on a show after they die; stand forward and speak, as these five sisters did, over the standing, the charge, and the unfinished matters they have left behind. There is a further word for everyone who says "my father was no great man": Zelophehad was not a prince, not a priest, not a famous name like Korah; he was only an ordinary man who died in the wilderness, who died in his own sin — and even for his name God was willing to stop the pen of the law. If your father was also a man of no name, read first the question of verse 4: his name, in God's sight, was never a thing to be done away at will.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Zelophehad" is Ṣəlopḥād (H6765) and "Manasseh" is Mənaššeh (H4519), both already checked in the sermon on Numbers 26; "Korah" is Qōraḥ (H7141), its number likewise already checked in the sermon on Numbers 26. "Inheritance/possession" in the underlying word is naḥălâ (H5159, its number already checked in the sermon on Numbers 26), the portion of land received as an inheritance; it is a word of high frequency in this chapter. "Congregation" is ʿēdâ (H5712). The original-language numbers for each of the five daughters' names are marked in this manuscript as pending verification; they are not filled in from memory — see Endnote 5. "Tabernacle of the congregation" is registered in the wording of the base text.

(Scholarly & Historical Discussion Layer) What in particular Zelophehad's dying "in his own sin" refers to, this chapter does not say: he died in the wilderness and was not of Korah's company, and beyond that the text gives no detail. The commentarial tradition has one line of speculation: some have held that he was the man put to death for gathering sticks on the Sabbath in Numbers 15 (preceding-text layer; 15:32-36 itself likewise does not record his name), an identification found in the rabbinic tradition; others have held that it is only a general reference to each man of the older generation dying in the wilderness in his own sin (set beside the verdict of 26:64-65, preceding-text layer). Both lines are inferences by later readers; the text of this chapter gives only the two qualifications, "his own sin" and "not in the company of Korah". This manuscript states them without adjudicating, and the argument of the main text adopts neither account.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of speech in it: the narrator's introduction occupies two verses (27:1-2), and the five sisters' speech is one passage of two verses (27:3-4), the first speech of man to man in the whole chapter. The structure of the speech has all four steps: the line of descent (27:1, stated on their behalf by the narrator), the character of their father's death (27:3, first half), the fact that he had no son (27:3, second half), and the question about the doing away of the name with the request for a possession (27:4). They receive no answer within this part — the case hangs at the door of the tabernacle, waiting to be brought before God.

### Part Two: Brought Before the LORD — Speak Right, and the Order of Inheritance (27:5-11) (10:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 5, Moses' action, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And Moses brought their cause before the LORD." (27:5) One item registered: in this verse Moses does not speak and does not decide; he does one action only — he brings the cause. Moses, who stands first among the four layers who heard the case (27:2), brings the case upward in public. Verse 6, the introduction to the speech, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying," (27:6) Verse 7, the verdict, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "The daughters of Zelophehad speak right: thou shalt surely give them a possession of an inheritance among their father's brethren; and thou shalt cause the inheritance of their father to pass unto them." (27:7) The two commands recorded as they stand: give them a possession of an inheritance among their father's brethren, and cause the inheritance of their father to pass unto them. Verses 8 to 11, the single case is established as a general rule — the first level, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And thou shalt speak unto the children of Israel, saying, If a man die, and have no son, then ye shall cause his inheritance to pass unto his daughter." (27:8) The second level, from the base text: "And if he have no daughter, then ye shall give his inheritance unto his brethren." (27:9) The third level, from the base text: "And if he have no brethren, then ye shall give his inheritance unto his father's brethren." (27:10) The fourth level and the closing sentence, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And if his father have no brethren, then ye shall give his inheritance unto his kinsman that is next to him of his family, and he shall possess it: and it shall be unto the children of Israel a statute of judgment, as the LORD commanded Moses." (27:11) The order registered: son (27:8, the reverse condition) → daughter → the man's own brethren → his father's brethren → the kinsman that is next to him of his family, five levels in all, with four transfers; and one sentence on the character of the general rule closes it — it shall be a statute of judgment unto the children of Israel.

(Argument) Moses' bringing of the case in this verse must be spoken to everyone who holds authority in his hand. No one in the camp was more qualified than Moses to give a decision on the spot: the law had been promulgated through his hand, the register had just been numbered by him, and whose family the five sisters' father belonged to he could know at one inquiry. Yet he pointedly does not decide — he brings their cause before the LORD. It is not evasion; it is reverence: in the provisions as they stood there was no such case (daughters contending for an inheritance had no express text before this), and where the provisions do not reach, he does not make up the gap with his own cleverness; he brings it up and waits for the verdict. The sickness to which those who make decisions today are most prone is to take their own experience as law: a thing never seen before is dismissed out of hand with "it has always been so", and a thing the provisions do not write is treated wholesale as not permitted. Moses' pattern stands in verse 5: to have no precedent to follow is not the same as to have no way to go — bring it up. In the church, in a company, in a family, in everything on which you have the deciding word, when you meet a new situation that neither provision nor custom can answer, learn this verse first: do not reject it out of hand, do not approve it out of hand; bring the case honestly before God. God's verdict is often wider than our custom: if the five sisters had run into the custom that "only a son has ever received an inheritance", their father's name really would have been done away; brought before God, God says they speak right.

(Argument) In God's verdict, characterization comes first and disposition after — the order must be spoken through. "The daughters of Zelophehad speak right:" — these words come first; the command to divide the land comes after. God first affirms that those who speak are in the right, and only then does what they asked for. Our order in dealing with people is often the reverse: we mostly ask first, "Does what you ask accord with the rules?" and, if it cannot be found in the rules, we dismiss the person together with his words; God first hears their reasoning through to the end, gives the verdict "right", and then lets what they asked for stand. What deserves still more attention is that this verdict is at once raised in rank: the case of a single household God writes, on the spot, into a statute of judgment for all Israel (27:11) — from that time every father who has no son, his daughter has express protection in writing because these five sisters stood forward once. When the weak present a case once according to the rule, those who are blessed are often not their household alone: they have walked a road through, and countless people after them in the same situation walk by it. If today, in your place, you stand forward, bring up, and state plainly an injustice that "no one has ever dealt with", and God judges that you speak right, what you win back may not be your share alone — you may have won, for a whole company of people behind you, a road written down in express text.

(Argument) The order of the general rule needs a further layer to be spoken: the four transfers grow one step more distant at each step, yet not one step may be skipped. The inheritance passes first to the daughter, then to the brethren, then to the father's brethren, and only last to the kinsman that is next in the family — when God establishes the rule, He does not allow men to disturb the order by favour or dislike, nor does He allow the strong to seize across levels: every level is a wall to the next. What this order protects is those who stand later in it and whose voice is smallest: without an order written in express text, a powerful house in the family could pass over the daughter, pass over the brethren, seize the inheritance in one grasp, and then say, "Family matters are settled within the family." God writes the order down immovably precisely to stand behind the weaker level. In handling family property, church resources, and the rights of an institution today, what is most to be feared is that under the four words "internal coordination" there is no order at all: whoever has the loudest voice gets it, whoever is nearest takes it. The pattern of 27:8-11 says: the order must be in express text, must be public, must be level by level — that the daughter's level stands before the brethren is written by God's own hand, and no one may move it back. What the five sisters won was not only a piece of land; it was a rule that the order must not be disturbed.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Statute of judgment" corresponds to the fixed pairing of the ḥuqqat mišpāṭ kind: "judgment" is mišpāṭ (H4941); the number for ḥuqqâ (statute) is marked in this manuscript as pending verification — see Endnote 5. The verb in the speech introduction at 27:6 is registered in the wording of the Chinese base text; it occurs once in this chapter (27:6); the other introductions to God's speaking to Moses all read, in the KJV, "said unto Moses" (27:12, 18). "Cause" is registered in the wording of the base text (27:5).

(New Testament Reading Layer) When the Lord Jesus teaches on prayer, He takes as His example a widow presenting her plea to an unjust judge, and calls His disciples to learn to cry out day and night (Luke 18:1-8, cross-reference at the reading layer). One comparison is registered at the reading layer: what the widow asked of the unjust judge was to be avenged; what the five daughters asked of Moses was an inheritance — both cases are presented by those who have the least weight in the system, and in both the text's emphasis falls on the presenters stating the facts plainly and stating their request in full. The reading layer registers only how the New Testament takes up this chapter's theme of presenting a case in its teaching on prayer; it does not read the New Testament's teaching on prayer back into this chapter as its legal facts. That the five daughters in this chapter obtained the verdict rested not on importunity but on this: what they said was true, what they asked was right, and God Himself gave the verdict plainly (27:7).

(Fact) This part closes by registering the relation between the verdict and the general rule: the disposition of the single case has two commands (give them a possession of an inheritance, and cause their father's inheritance to pass to them, 27:7); the general rule has four transfers (27:8-11); in both places God speaks directly, and not one sentence comes from Moses. From this point the five sisters speak no more in this chapter — their plea has already passed from spoken statement into words written in the statute (later-text layer: Numbers 36 contains a further supplementary ruling on this very rule; this manuscript registers its existence only).

### Part Three: Up Mount Abarim — After Seeing It, Be Gathered to Your People (27:12-14) (24:00-36:00)

(Fact) Verse 12, God's announcement to Moses, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Get thee up into this mount Abarim, and see the land which I have given unto the children of Israel." (27:12) Verse 13, what follows the seeing, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And when thou hast seen it, thou also shalt be gathered unto thy people, as Aaron thy brother was gathered." (27:13) Two items registered: what is granted to Moses is to "see" — to go up the mount and see the land; the wording of his end is to be "gathered unto thy people", with an express comparison to his brother Aaron (Aaron's death, preceding-text layer, Numbers 20). Verse 14, the reason, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "For ye rebelled against my commandment in the desert of Zin, in the strife of the congregation, to sanctify me at the water before their eyes: that is the water of Meribah in Kadesh in the wilderness of Zin." (27:14) The items registered one by one: the place — the desert of Zin, Kadesh, the water of Meribah; the ground — in the strife of the congregation they rebelled against God's commandment and did not sanctify God at the water before the eyes of the congregation; the subject reads "ye", in the plural — the verdict of this verse refers to Moses and Aaron together (Aaron had already been gathered first, chapter 20, preceding-text layer), and this chapter restates it once over Moses. The parenthetical sentence is the narrator's geographical footnote, recorded with its parenthesis as the base text gives it: in the KJV it runs on after the colon, "that is the water of Meribah in Kadesh in the wilderness of Zin."

(Argument) The place of this passage must be spoken first: God has only just finished judging the five sisters' case when He speaks at once to Moses of Moses' own death. The arrangement is not heartlessness; it is pattern: God is righteous — He will not, because Moses has just handled one fine case, cancel the old account of Meribah; nor will He, because Moses has the old account of Meribah, refuse to let him finish the five sisters' case before his end. The two accounts are each kept as its own, and neither is mixed into the other. In dealing with people today we often commit exactly this mixing: when a man has made one mistake, we wipe out a whole lifetime of his faithfulness; when a man has won one merit, we pardon all his faults. God's ledger is not kept that way: Moses' disobedience is still, after many years, to be spoken once more face to face before he goes up the mount (27:14); and Moses' faithfulness is still remembered — he may still go up the mount and see the land, and the successor he asks for God gives on the spot. If you are a leader, read this passage to yourself first: do not hope to offset a fault with merit, and do not, because of one fault, think that God has rejected you as a whole man. The man who can stand before God is the man who lets Him settle both accounts face to face.

(Argument) Between the "see" and the "be gathered", there is a word for everyone approaching his end. God's final arrangement for Moses is most measured: the land he may see; his life is numbered to its end. The place to which he has led the people all his life, he himself may not enter, yet he is led up the mount to see it first. That is not humiliation; it is grace: he can see that the promise is real; only, those who receive it are the next generation. Many faithful pioneers, in old age, must pass this very gate: the tree they planted themselves gives shade to others; the road they built themselves is walked on by sons and grandsons. The man who cannot pass this gate will be bitter at his end — I gave my whole life, why may I not receive it? Moses' answer in the second half of this chapter sets the bearing for this gate: having heard the date of his death, he does not plead, he does not bargain; his first sentence is to ask for a shepherd to succeed him for the people (27:15-17). The man who truly treats the work as God's work, when he leaves, is concerned not most with "I have not yet received", but with "the flock must not be left without anyone to care for it". If one day you too must hand over the field of your whole life's work, read this passage first: go up the mount and take one look, hand the staff over well to the people — that is a pioneer's most honourable departure.

(Argument) The seven words "as Aaron thy brother was gathered" also deserve attention. When God speaks of Moses' end, He uses no other comparison; He uses the end of his own elder brother as the comparison: Aaron on Mount Hor took off his garments and was gathered to his people (Numbers 20, preceding-text layer), and Moses is the man who with his own eyes saw his brother off. Now it is his turn, and God sets the same sentence quietly before him: thou also shalt be gathered unto thy people, as Aaron thy brother was gathered. There is no frightening in this sentence, only a place to rest — the road the elder brother walked, the younger brother will walk too; the place to which the elder brother was gathered, the younger brother will be gathered too. Those who have seen off their nearest kin fear most that one day, when it is their own turn, there will be nothing to say; the word God gives Moses is to make him remember that his brother's end was at rest, and that his own end will also be at rest. As we today see off our loved ones, and prepare for our own day, what is to be learned is just this quietness: to be gathered to one's people is not to disappear; it is to rejoin the ranks.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Abarim" is registered in the wording of the base text; its original-language number is marked in this manuscript as pending verification — see Endnote 5. The wording "be gathered unto thy people" is registered as the base text gives it, and differs in wording from the verdict of 26:65 on the older generation, that they should die in the wilderness; this manuscript registers the textual difference in wording only and develops no theology on it in this section. "Meribah", "Kadesh", and "Zin" are all registered in the wording of the base text; the phrase "to sanctify me" forms an intertext with the title given, in its own base text, to the finalized sermon on Numbers 20 (preceding-text layer); this manuscript registers its recurrence only.

(Scholarly & Historical Discussion Layer) The relation of this passage to Numbers 20 is set out side by side without harmonizing. Numbers 20 records the event of Meribah itself and the announcement of Aaron's end (20:12, 24, preceding-text layer); 27:14 in this chapter is a restatement of the reason for that event, in the wording "ye rebelled against my commandment" and "to sanctify me at the water before their eyes" not done. Deuteronomy has further retrospectives (Deut 1:37; 3:26; 32:48-52, later-text layer), with their own emphases of wording and attribution. How the several texts interpret one another belongs to cross-volume synthetic judgment; this manuscript registers only the wording of this chapter and the existence of the several cross-reference layers, and makes no expansion of harmonization or attribution at this chapter's fact layer. Further, how Mount Abarim corresponds to Moses' ascent of Mount Nebo and the top of Pisgah to see the land as recorded in Deuteronomy (Deut 34:1, later-text layer), this chapter does not say; it registers only that the two records stand side by side.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the skeleton of speech in the chapter up to this point: God has now spoken directly in three passages — the verdict on the five daughters' case (27:7-11) and the announcement of Moses' end (27:12-14) among them — and Moses' speech is still only the action of bringing the case (27:5), without one sentence in his own defence. Verse 14 is the only verse in the whole chapter carrying a parenthetical note, and what the footnote notes is the source of "the water": in restating a case many years old, God fastens even the place-names immovably — the desert of Zin, Kadesh, Meribah. An old account, in God's keeping, is never blurred.

### Part Four: Set a Man over the Congregation — Joshua Is Set Apart (27:15-21) (36:00-48:00)

(Fact) Verse 15, Moses opens his mouth, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And Moses spake unto the LORD, saying," (27:15) Verses 16 to 17, Moses' request in full: "Let the LORD, the God of the spirits of all flesh, set a man over the congregation, Which may go out before them, and which may go in before them, and which may lead them out, and which may bring them in; that the congregation of the LORD be not as sheep which have no shepherd." (27:16-17) The three items of its content recorded as they stand: what is asked for is a man to be set over the congregation; his office is to go out and go in before the people, and to lead them out and bring them in; its reason is that the congregation be not as sheep which have no shepherd. Moses in this passage does not ask one sentence for himself — he does not ask for his life to be lengthened, does not ask for the verdict to be changed, does not ask to enter the land. Verse 18, God's naming, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Take thee Joshua the son of Nun, a man in whom is the spirit, and lay thine hand upon him;" (27:18) The man is named by God: Joshua the son of Nun, characterized in one sentence, "a man in whom is the spirit"; the first thing Moses is to do is to take him and lay his hand on him. Verse 19, the public charge, read continuously in the base text: "And set him before Eleazar the priest, and before all the congregation; and give him a charge in their sight." (27:19) Verse 20, the transfer of honour, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And thou shalt put some of thine honour upon him, that all the congregation of the children of Israel may be obedient." (27:20) The wording registered: what is given is "some" of the honour — not all of it; the purpose is expressly stated: that all the congregation of the children of Israel may be obedient to him. Verse 21, the pattern by which Joshua is thereafter to ask, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And he shall stand before Eleazar the priest, who shall ask counsel for him after the judgment of Urim before the LORD: at his word shall they go out, and at his word they shall come in, both he, and all the children of Israel with him, even all the congregation." (27:21) Two items registered: Joshua does not receive oracles directly as Moses did; he is to stand before the priest, and the priest is to ask counsel for him after the judgment of Urim; the going out and the coming in are at that word, which comes through this asking.

(Argument) Moses' prayer must be spoken sentence by sentence. "Let the LORD, the God of the spirits of all flesh" — he calls God the God of the spirits of all flesh: every man's breath is in God's hand, and therefore who succeeds is also in God's hand; Moses does not designate anyone himself, does not arrange an appointment from within; he asks God to set the man. The man who has led more than a million people for forty years hands over authority at the end by prayer, not by arranging his own trusted men. "set a man over the congregation" — what he asks is that the place be occupied by someone, not that his own place endure forever; how many leaders cannot hand over because they have treated the place as a part of themselves, and when the man goes and the place is empty they feel that they themselves have been negated. Not so Moses: he asks only that the flock have a shepherd; who the shepherd is, God decides. "that the congregation of the LORD be not as sheep which have no shepherd" — note that he says throughout "the congregation of the LORD", not "my congregation": the flock is God's; he is only a man who has shepherded it. To ask at the point of death nothing for himself and only a shepherd for the flock is the quietest and also the weightiest prayer of Moses' whole life. In choosing a successor in church and institution today, what is to be learned first is not procedure but this heart: the man who hands over must first truly believe that these people are God's, not his own; he must first pray for his successor, rather than prolong the life of his own influence.

(Argument) "And thou shalt put some of thine honour upon him" must be spoken to everyone who passes on the staff. God commands Moses to divide some of his own honour to Joshua — honour is a thing which, divided out, means oneself receives a little less; Moses divided it. Many handovers fail not because the successor is incapable, but because the predecessor cannot bear to divide: the standing is given, but the dignity is not given; the place is yielded, but he undermines him in public. God's pattern runs the other way: the predecessor is himself to lay his own honour on his successor with his own hand, and to do it before all the congregation, so that the people are obedient to him (27:20). That it is "some" is also honest: Joshua is not a second Moses, God does not require him to act the part of Moses, and the people need not measure him with Moses' measure — the honour he receives is enough for the people to be obedient to him, and that is enough. Those who are predecessors today must learn this lesson: if what you hand over is a real place, make your successor complete in public, divide some of your credit and your dignity to him, and let the people be obedient to him because of you; if you keep a hand in reserve, the people will despise him, and the flock will scatter. And those who are successors should also receive this "some" with a quiet heart: you need not live as your predecessor's shadow; with the spirit in your heart (27:18), standing before the priest and asking plainly (27:21), your "some" is enough for you to stand.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Spirit" is rûaḥ (H7307); the "spirits of all flesh" of 27:16 and the "spirit" in "a man in whom is the spirit" of 27:18 both use this same word, and the two wordings are recorded separately as the base text gives them. "Honour" is hôd (H1935). "Urim" is ʾÛrîm (H224). "Congregation" is ʿēdâ (H5712, occurring a number of times in this chapter). The root of the original verb behind "give him a charge" is of the same family as the word for "command"; its number is marked in this manuscript as pending verification — see Endnote 5. All the numbers filled in above were checked one by one before writing (for the method of checking see Endnote 5).

(New Testament Reading Layer) When the Lord Jesus was on earth and saw the multitudes fainting and scattered abroad, "as sheep having no shepherd", He was moved with compassion on them (Matt 9:36; Mark 6:34, cross-reference at the reading layer). Registered at the reading layer: Moses' wording, "that the congregation of the LORD be not as sheep which have no shepherd." (27:17), and the wording with which the Gospel narrators describe the multitudes answer one another, before and after — Moses asks for a shepherd for the congregation; the Lord Himself became that Shepherd and laid down His life for the sheep (John 10:11, reading layer). The reading layer registers only how the New Testament takes up this chapter's shepherd theme; it does not read John's shepherd self-description back into this chapter as the fact of the appointment. What this chapter sets apart is Joshua the son of Nun, and his office and its limits are all as the original text of 27:18-21 gives them.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the list of commands for the procedure of setting apart: take him (27:18), lay the hand on him (27:18), set him before the priest and all the congregation (27:19), give him a charge (27:19), and put some of the honour on him (27:20) — all five are God's commands to Moses, and their execution is in the next part (27:22-23). One comparison is also registered: Eleazar the priest stands in the foreground in this chapter on three occasions — hearing the daughters' case (27:2), Joshua standing before him to be set apart (27:19), and his asking counsel for Joshua after the judgment of Urim (27:21) — the government of the new generation is a pattern in which leader and priest stand side by side, distinct from the old pattern in which Moses alone received oracles directly; this difference is stated by the text itself, and this manuscript registers it without developing it.

### Part Five: Lay His Hands on Him — Moses Does as Commanded, and the Whole Chapter (27:22-23, and the Whole Chapter) (48:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 22, the opening sentence of the execution, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And Moses did as the LORD commanded him: and he took Joshua, and set him before Eleazar the priest, and before all the congregation:" (27:22) Verse 23, the close of the execution, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "And he laid his hands upon him, and gave him a charge, as the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses." (27:23) The items registered one by one: First, verse 22 opens, "And Moses did as the LORD commanded him:" — answering, at head and tail, to the closing sentence of the general rule in 27:11, "as the LORD commanded Moses": both halves of this chapter close within the words "as commanded". Second, of the five items commanded, this passage expressly records the execution of four actions — he took him, he set him, he laid his hands on him, he gave him a charge; the putting of "some of thine honour upon him" (27:20) has no separate verse in the narrative recording its execution, and the narrative gathers it under the one act of laying on of hands (the laying on of hands is precisely the vehicle of the transfer of honour in the command of 27:18; this manuscript registers the text's fullness and brevity only, and does not supplement the narrative). Third, there is one close reading on the singular and plural of the hand: the command of verse 18 reads "lay thine hand upon him", in the singular form; the execution of verse 23 reads "he laid his hands upon him" — the Chinese base text reads the same wording at both places; the KJV has the singular "hand" at verse 18 and the plural "hands" at verse 23 (English comparison layer; only the difference of presentation between the versions is registered). Fourth, the chapter closes here: Joshua, from beginning to end in this chapter, does not say one sentence — he is only taken, laid hands on, and given a charge; the five sisters spoke two verses, Moses spoke three verses, God spoke fourteen verses, and the man who is to lead the people into the land is silent in this chapter.

(Argument) Speak last of Moses' "doing as commanded". Of the five things God commanded, he reduces none and adds none; he handles them all, in public, to the end. The hardest to handle is precisely the most honourable one: before all the congregation, to lay his hand on the head of the man who replaces himself. That is not a formality — it amounts to declaring in public: from now on, obey him as you obeyed me. The hardest obedience of a man's whole life may not be suffering; it is to make complete a man who is to take his place, and to make him complete so that all can see it and are convinced in heart and mouth. Many people leave one flattering sentence behind them as they go, and that is already reckoned magnanimous; Moses lays his hand on him, divides his honour out, and speaks the charge to the end. In church, family, and company, wherever there is a handover, this chapter's ending is to be learned: the man who hands over hands over as commanded, the man who receives receives before the people, and no private account is left in between. Having handled this chapter, Moses may go up the mount with a quiet heart — because before he leaves, the flock already has a shepherd, the statute already stands with its verdict, and the name of the weak has also been preserved. The best three things a leader can leave behind him as he departs are just these three: a rule that can be followed, a name that is remembered, and a man to succeed him.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The word rendered "by" in "by the hand of Moses" (27:23) is registered in the wording of the base text. A recount of the personal names in this chapter: "Moses" is the most frequent personal name in the whole chapter; "Eleazar" is involved in the foreground scenes of four verses (27:2, 19, 21, 22); "Joshua" is named twice in this chapter (27:18, 22), and is carried on by a pronoun at verse 23; "Zelophehad" is named twice (27:1, in the narrative, and 27:7, in the verdict), and the five daughters are named once (27:1), with a further occurrence at 26:33 in the cross-reference layer.

(Scholarly & Historical Discussion Layer) Two matters are set out side by side without adjudication. First, verse 21 in the KJV reads "at his word shall they go out, and at his word they shall come in": the Chinese base text attaches the word of going out and coming in to Eleazar (the word obtained through asking after the judgment of Urim); in the KJV the reference of the pronoun in "at his word" has been discussed at the level of readings — it may refer to Joshua's issuing of the order as the fruit of the priest's asking, or to the priest's word. The Chinese base text's reading is a road of its own, and scholarly readings are not one road only; the main text of this manuscript registers the quotations and its argument on the base texts as stated at the head of this sermon, and does not alter a quotation or an argument by a reading from the discussion layer. Second, "put some of thine honour upon him" in verse 20: that the transfer of honour is partial and not whole is often read together with the irreplaceability of Moses' office (Deut 34:10, "there arose not a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses", quoted here in key points at the later-text layer); the relative standing of Joshua's office after his setting apart and of Moses' office belongs to cross-volume synthetic judgment; this manuscript registers only the textual wording "some" and the existence of the later text, and gives no verdict on it in this chapter.

(Fact) The data of the whole chapter close here, every item already counted (for the details see Endnote 4): the whole chapter has 23 verses; the verses of the five parts are, in order, 4 (27:1-4), 7 (27:5-11), 3 (27:12-14), 7 (27:15-21), and 2 (27:22-23), 23 in all. The account of speech: the five daughters speak in one passage (27:3-4); Moses speaks in one passage (27:16-17, with the further action of bringing the case at 27:5 and the introduction to his prayer at 27:15); God speaks in three passages — the verdict (27:7-11, its introduction at 27:6 using the distinctive verb of the Chinese base text), the announcement of the end (27:12-14), and the naming with the commands for the setting apart (27:18-21). The word of the chapter's pivot is naḥălâ: on the Chinese base text it stands in the six verses 27:4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, once in each verse; on the KJV letter the word "inheritance" itself occurs six times in all — twice in 27:7 and once each in 27:8, 9, 10, 11, five verses in all — while 27:4 reads "a possession" for the same underlying word (the KJV recount is registered here as it stands, and the difference from the Chinese register is recorded in Endnote 4 and not harmonized). The order of inheritance has five levels (son, daughter, brethren, father's brethren, the kinsman that is next of the family). The going-out-and-coming-in wording occurs at two verse-places (27:17, 27:21), and at 27:17 the wording "which may lead them out, and which may bring them in" also stands beside it. The close of this chapter set beside the close of the preceding chapter: the preceding chapter ends with the settling of accounts for the older generation (not so much as one was left of them, 26:65, in key points), and this chapter ends with the settling of the new generation (hands laid on and a charge given, 27:23) — the first thing after the settling of accounts is to contend for a name for those who have no name, and to set up a shepherd for those who have no shepherd; before Israel crosses the river, these two final matters are first handled on the plains of Moab.

(Argument) The whole chapter closes by leaving one sentence for each of three kinds of people. First, for those who feel that their case is too small and that no one is willing to hear it: five daughters with neither father nor brother brought their cause before God, and the first sentence God said was "speak right" — that your case is small does not mean it has no right in it; state the facts plainly, state the request suitably, stand in the place where you ought to stand, and God hears a case presented. Second, for those who hold authority and meet a matter for which there is no precedent to follow: learn Moses in verse 5 — do not reject it out of hand, do not approve it out of hand; bring the cause before the LORD; the end of the provisions is not the end of the road, and God's verdict often opens a general rule for those who come after. Third, for everyone who must pass on the staff and everyone who must receive it: the man who hands over is to lay on hands in public, divide his honour, and leave no private account; the man who receives is to have the spirit in his heart, stand before the priest and ask plainly, and need not live as his predecessor's shadow. The flock is the congregation of the LORD, not any man's inheritance — the names, God remembers them; the shepherd, God sets him up; we need only handle our own portion as commanded, to the end.

## Devotional Excerpt

Moses heard the date of his own death. God said to him: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Get thee up into this mount Abarim, and see the land which I have given unto the children of Israel." "And when thou hast seen it, thou also shalt be gathered unto thy people, as Aaron thy brother was gathered." (27:12-13) If it were we who heard such words, our first sentence would mostly be a plea for ourselves: give me a few more years, let me go in and take a look at least. Moses' first sentence, however, was a plea for the people: "Let the LORD, the God of the spirits of all flesh, set a man over the congregation, Which may go out before them, and which may go in before them, and which may lead them out, and which may bring them in; that the congregation of the LORD be not as sheep which have no shepherd." (27:16-17) He did not ask that his own life be lengthened; he asked only that the flock not be cut off from its shepherd. For forty years, what he could never lay down was never his own end; it was God's congregation. God then pointed Joshua out to him, and commanded him to lay hands on him in public, divide his honour to him, and give him a charge. Today God reminds us too: those we shepherd in this world — children, students, fellow workers, the flock — are all the congregation of the LORD, not our inheritance. When the time comes, we must all be "gathered to our people"; before that, what matters most is not to hold on and not let go, but to pray for them, to ask God to prepare the man who will receive the staff, and to lay our hand gladly on his head.

English (KJV-based):

"Let the LORD, the God of the spirits of all flesh, set a man over the congregation, Which may go out before them, and which may go in before them, and which may lead them out, and which may bring them in; that the congregation of the LORD be not as sheep which have no shepherd." (Num 27:16-17, KJV) Told that he would soon be gathered to his people, Moses asked for nothing for himself — only that God's flock would not be left shepherdless. And of the daughters who brought their case before the LORD, God said: "The daughters of Zelophehad speak right: thou shalt surely give them a possession of an inheritance among their father's brethren; and thou shalt cause the inheritance of their father to pass unto them." (Num 27:7, KJV) Bring your case honestly before God, pray for those who will come after you, and lay your hand gladly on the one who will carry on.

Form: The sermon is divided into five parts (Deut 15:1–6; 7–11; 12–18; 19–23; and a part setting the three systems side by side), the time codes running continuously over 60 minutes. Within each part, paragraphs are marked [Fact] / [Argument] / [Summary] / [New Testament Reading] / [Scholarly and Historical Discussion] to keep the layers distinct: wherever the Argument layer draws an inference it is marked "this is an inference / argument"; wherever the New Testament Reading layer registers an echo it is marked "registered as a report"; and wherever the ordinances of Exodus and Leviticus that parallel this chapter differ from it, the differences are set out side by side only — not adjudicated, and no stemma is arranged. This manuscript has three hard points of adjudicative discipline: first, the tension between 15:4, "there shall be no poor," and 15:11, "the poor shall never cease," is set out side by side only; second, the relation of the year of release to the sabbatical year and the jubilee is set out only as the three systems' own respective accounts, side by side; third, the differences among the three servant ordinances of Exodus 21, Deuteronomy 15, and Leviticus 25 are set out side by side only. Deuteronomy 15 treats three matters: the release of debts (vv. 1–11), the release of servants (vv. 12–18), and the setting apart of the firstling (vv. 19–23). On the surface the three groups of ordinances each govern their own affair, but what runs through the whole chapter is the same pair of hands in three postures: toward a debt, the hand must release; toward a poor brother, the hand must be opened wide; toward the man being released, he must not be let go empty; and toward the firstling beast, the hand must in turn stop, and must not first take it to plough the field or shear the wool. This sermon first reads the three groups of ordinances through verse by verse (Parts One to Four), and then sets the three calendars — the year of release, the sabbatical year, and the jubilee — and the two servant ordinances of Exodus 21 and Leviticus 25 side by side for comparison (Part Five); the tension between v. 4, "there shall be no poor among you," and v. 11, "the poor shall never cease," is set out side by side through the whole sermon and not adjudicated. ## Main Text

Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Fact] At the end of Deuteronomy 14 the yearly rule of the tithe has just been finished: once in three years the tithe is brought out and given to the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow "within thy gates" (Deut 14:28–29). In that chapter the order of money and grain is a dividing outward; in chapter 15 the order goes deeper: not a tenth divided out, but the whole sum struck off — when the seventh year comes, the debt a brother owes is released, the brother who has sold himself as a servant goes out free, and even the firstling of the herd and flock the owner must not first lay his own hand on to use. The manner of speaking in this chapter has a marked rhythm: each section first gives the institution, and only then the heart — the release first has its fixed rule (v. 2), and only then deals with the hand of the creditor who conceives an evil thought (vv. 7–9); the manumission first has its term of six years (v. 12), and only then deals with the bundle for the send-off (vv. 13–14). Of the three groups of ordinances, two are headed by a case introduced with "if" (v. 7, "If there be among you a poor man"; v. 12, "if thy brother … be sold unto thee") — the tone of case law: first a concrete person is set standing before the eyes, and only then is the rule given; the rules of this chapter never hang in the air, they are always spoken facing a concrete face. The sacred name "the LORD" occurs in this chapter, in the Chinese base text, 15 times, distributed over 12 verses, of which the full form "the LORD your God" (with the fuller possessive) occurs 7 times (vv. 6, 10, 14, 15, 18, 19, 21), the shortened form "the LORD thy God" 4 times (twice at v. 4, and at vv. 7 and 20), and the form without a possessive suffix 4 times (the year of release at v. 2, the great blessing at v. 4, the crying unto Him at v. 9, and the place the LORD shall choose at v. 20). Recounted on the KJV's own letter, the name "Lord" likewise occurs 15 times, but distributed over 13 verses, because the KJV carries the name also at v. 5, which in the CUNPSS is an empty number fused into v. 4; the form "Lord thy God" occurs 11 times in the KJV (vv. 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 14, 15, 18, 19, 20, 21), and the form without the possessive 4 times (vv. 2, 4, 9, 20) — the totals agree, the verse distribution differs, and the two are registered separately and not harmonized (see Endnote 2). What is counted is not merely a word; it is a line: every arrangement in this chapter is tied under the name of this God.

[Argument] This is an argument in summary form: read chapters 14 and 15 together, and Deuteronomy's order in handling wealth can be seen to be worship first, and men's affairs afterwards — first the feast at the chosen place and the tithe (ch. 14), and only then release and manumission (ch. 15). The order is not an accident of arrangement: how a man renders his account to God determines how he opens his hand to his brother; the man who has first learned to open his storehouse before God is the man who can learn to open his hand before his brother. This is an inference (drawn from the sequence of the two chapters, not a doctrine self-evident in any single verse).

[Summary] One sentence to grasp the whole chapter: the year of release is mercy written into the calendar — it does not wait for the rich man's heart to soften; when the term arrives, the account is cleared, the man is let go, and the firstling belongs to God. In the five parts that follow: first the fixed rule of the release is read (vv. 1–6), then the command to open the hand wide (vv. 7–11), then the rule for releasing servants (vv. 12–18), then the setting apart of the firstling (vv. 19–23), and last the three calendars and the three servant ordinances are set side by side for comparison.

### Part One: At the End of Every Seven Years Thou Shalt Make a Release (Deut 15:1–6; 05:00–15:00)

[Fact] Verse 1 first gives the season: "At the end of every seven years thou shalt make a release." In the original, miqqēṣ šeḇaʿ-šānîm, "at the end of seven years" (šānâ H8141, year). "Release," in the original šəmiṭṭâ (H8102), comes from the root šāmaṭ (H8058), whose root sense is to let fall, to release the grip, to let a thing drop — this same root is used of the land at Exodus 23:11: in the seventh year the land is to "rest"; the original there is precisely a verbal form of this word (tišməṭennâ) — the land is to release its grip, and man is to release his grip, one and the same word. Verse 2 gives the rule in definitional form: "And this is the manner of the release: Every creditor that lendeth ought unto his neighbour shall release it; he shall not exact it of his neighbour, or of his brother; because it is called the Lord's release." In the original the sentence opens with the intensified form šāmôṭ, "release utterly"; the subject is baʿal maššēh, "the master of the claim," that is, the party holding the claim (nāšâ H5383, to lend, a claim); the objects are the "neighbour" and the "brother"; and the reason is that this year has already been proclaimed in the LORD's name — the release is not the creditor's private favour, it is a proclaimed year-rule. Verse 3 draws the boundary: "Of a foreigner thou mayest exact it again: but that which is thine with thy brother thine hand shall release;" Two lines, inward and outward: toward the foreigner, the claim stands as before; toward the brother, "thine hand shall release" — in the original tašmēṭ yāḏekā, thy hand shall release; the "hand" makes its first appearance, already paired with the verb of release. Verse 4 is the fused verse (see (ii) of the Base texts paragraph), read here in the KJV's own two-verse form, the correspondence being made plain: first the promise — "Save when there shall be no poor among you; for the Lord shall greatly bless thee in the land which the Lord thy God giveth thee for an inheritance to possess it:" (naḥălâ H5159, inheritance; bāraḵ H1288, to bless — the original bārēḵ yəḇārəkəḵā is the intensified form, so that "shall greatly bless thee" is a rendering with a ground) — and then the condition as its own verse, v. 5: "Only if thou carefully hearken unto the voice of the Lord thy God, to observe to do all these commandments which I command thee this day." (šāmaʿ H8085, to hearken; šāmar H8104, to keep; miṣwâ H4687, commandment). In the CUNPSS order the condition is read first, then the promise, then the parenthetical reason; this exposition states the correspondence of condition and promise plainly, and does not alter the quotations to follow the MT order. On the empty number: at this point the Chinese Bible passes directly to v. 6; no text is to be supplied for the empty number, and the condition of v. 4 in the CUNPSS is not to be taken apart and put back into v. 5 — the arrangement of the CUNPSS is the way that text is read. Verse 6 turns the promise toward the nation's face: "For the Lord thy God blesseth thee, as he promised thee: and thou shalt lend unto many nations, but thou shalt not borrow; and thou shalt reign over many nations, but they shall not reign over thee." "Lend" and "borrow" come from one and the same word, lāwâ (H3867); within a single verse the directions are reversed: to lend out and not to borrow in is the picture of the economic sovereignty of a blessed community. The picture of this verse stands in exact contrast with the blessing and the curse of chapter 28: the blessing at Deut 28:12 reads, in its latter half, "and thou shalt lend unto many nations, and thou shalt not borrow" (the whole verse: "The Lord shall open unto thee his good treasure, the heaven to give the rain unto thy land in his season, and to bless all the work of thine hand: and thou shalt lend unto many nations, and thou shalt not borrow."), while the curse (Deut 28:44) reverses it — he shall lend to thee, and thou shalt not lend to him (registered as a cross-reference in sense; the intertext is registered, and this sermon does not expand it).

[Argument] This is an inference: the heart of the year of release is that it sets a sacred due date for claims. In any society lending rolls naturally into long-term dominion: debt rolls into debt, and a man is permanently occupied by the ledger. This chapter's answer is not to abolish lending — the next part will command men to go on lending to the poor brother (v. 8) — but to fit every inward claim with a seven-year zeroing point: a brother may owe a debt, but he may not be a debtor for ever; a ledger may have its years, but its years are seven at the longest. This is an argument (inferred from the term and the objects in vv. 1–3; the text does not itself use the word "zeroing" — it says that in the year of release a release is made, and does not itemize the accounting treatment of old debts carried across the year; that discussion is registered at the Discussion layer). Nor are the two lines, inward and outward, a double morality: the foreigner is not within the covenant community and does not share its land, its calendar, and its mutual sustenance; the release is the family account inside the covenant, and the word "brother," which occurs in this chapter 7 times in the Chinese base text (at vv. 2, 3, twice at v. 7, and at vv. 9, 11, 12) — in the KJV, "brother" 6 times and "brethren" once, 7 tokens in all over 6 verses (vv. 2, 3, 7, 9, 11, 12) — is precisely the marker of that boundary. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) The Lord's Prayer, "forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors" (Matt 6:12), speaks of forgiveness in the vocabulary of debt, and is the place most often registered in New Testament reading as intertext with this chapter; the teaching in Luke that counsels lending to men, hoping for nothing again (Luke 6:35), is also often read alongside the ethic of the year of release. What both places register is an echo of vocabulary and of ethic; neither asserts that the New Testament re-established the institution of the year of release.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Whether what the year of release remits is a full cancellation or a suspension of collection has been discussed in scholarship for a long time: one reading holds that the debt is wholly cancelled in the year of release; another reading, on the ground of the root sense of šāmaṭ, "to let fall, to release the grip," and of its parallel in the land's "letting fall" at Exodus 23:11, holds that the effect of the year of release is a suspension of collection for that year, the debt itself being extended. The lexical material on which the two readings rest is the same; this sermon registers only this unresolved question and does not adjudicate it — the Chinese word for "release" itself leans toward full cancellation, which is the colouring of the translation, and that too is registered. The historical background has a further register: in the ancient Near East there was a tradition of kings proclaiming a remission of debts on their accession or in a crisis (generally called in scholarship edicts of the mīsharum type), whose character was a king's one-off act of grace; the release of this chapter, set against it, differs in being made periodic and made law — it does not depend on a king's mood, but is written into the year-rule and runs of itself. This is registered as background at the level of the general scholarly account. Further: from the Second Temple period onward, the side-effect of credit freezing as the year of release approached left in rabbinic tradition the prosbul (a document by which, through deposit with a court, a claim was kept from lapsing in the year of release, traditionally associated with Hillel) — this is registered as a later response, and this sermon does not read this chapter back through it.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: the fixed rule of the release is that the creditor's hand must let go in the seventh year — "it is called the Lord's release": the proclamation comes first, and the letting go after it.

### Part Two: Harden Not Thine Heart, Nor Shut Thine Hand (Deut 15:7–11; 15:00–26:00)

[Fact] Verse 7 draws the scene from the year-rule to the city gate: "If there be among you a poor man of one of thy brethren within any of thy gates in thy land which the Lord thy God giveth thee, thou shalt not harden thine heart, nor shut thine hand from thy poor brother:" In the original it is "within any of thy gates" (šāʿar H8179) — not the whole land in the abstract, but the city you live in. "A poor man," in the original ʾeḇyôn (H34), one who lacks; the "poor" of "thy poor brother" in the second half of the same verse is the same word. The two prohibitions fall on two organs: "harden thine heart," in the original ʾāmēṣ ləḇāḇ (ʾāmēṣ H553, to make the heart hard); "shut thine hand," in the original qāpaṣ yāḏ (qāpaṣ H7092, to clench, to draw together) — the heart hardens first, and only then does the hand clench; the order is given by the original. Verse 8 is the positive command, with two intensified forms in a row in the original: "But thou shalt open thine hand wide unto him, and shalt surely lend him sufficient for his need, in that which he wanteth." — "open wide," pāṯōaḥ tip̄taḥ (pāṯaḥ H6605, to open); "shalt surely lend him," haʿăḇēṭ taʿăḇîṭennû (ʿāḇaṭ H5670, to lend) — both the giving and the lending are in the intensified form, and the tone leaves no room for negotiation; and the measure is dê maḥsōrô, "sufficient for his need, in that which he wanteth" (maḥsôr H4270, lack): not according to what I have left over, but according to what he lacks. Verse 9 deals with the calculation made at a point in time: "Beware that there be not a thought in thy wicked heart, saying, The seventh year, the year of release, is at hand; and thine eye be evil against thy poor brother, and thou givest him nought; and he cry unto the Lord against thee, and it be sin unto thee." The "thought in thy wicked heart," in the original dəḇar bəlîyaʿal (bəlîyaʿal H1100, worthless, a scoundrel) — the sentence in the heart, "The year of release is nearly here; to give now is to give for nothing," is given a name by the text: "a worthless word." The evil eye is the third organ: heart hard, hand clenched, eye evil — the three parts are blocked off one by one. The outcome of not giving is not a moral appraisal but a determination of attribution: the poor man cries unto the LORD, and "it be sin unto thee" — in this chapter not giving is entered in the account as sin. Verse 10 pushes the command one layer further: "Thou shalt surely give him, and thine heart shall not be grieved when thou givest unto him: because that for this thing the Lord thy God shall bless thee in all thy works, and in all that thou puttest thine hand unto." — "shalt surely give," in the original nāṯôn tittēn (nāṯan H5414), another intensified form; and the command reaches to the feelings: "thine heart shall not be grieved" — in the original lōʾ-yēraʿ ləḇāḇekā, thy heart shall not turn sour, shall not do ill (rāʿaʿ H7489); and the reason is blessing: the hand appears again, this time a blessed hand. Verse 11 closes the whole part: "For the poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy, in thy land." In the original two words are used together: ʿānî (H6041, the afflicted, the oppressed) and ʾeḇyôn (the needy poor); the Chinese base text's "afflicted and needy" follows the order of the original, while the KJV's order is the reverse — "to thy poor, and to thy needy" (see Endnote 1). The command sentence occurs again in the form of a quotation, and the intensified form pāṯōaḥ tip̄taḥ appears for the third time. Verses 4 and 11 are registered here side by side: within one and the same chapter, v. 4 says that in obedience there shall be no poor, and v. 11 says the poor shall never cease — the two sentences stand seven verses apart (in the KJV reckoning, counting the fused CUNPSS verse as the MT's two) and come from one and the same mouth; this sermon's way of reading is first to read each accurately on its own and to set them side by side; where the tension is housed is left to Part Five and the endnotes, the full account being in Endnote 5, and the sermon as a whole does not adjudicate it.

[Argument] This is an inference: this part moves "giving" out of the list of good deeds and into the list of "thou shalt not sin." Not to give to a poor brother counts in many ethics at most as stinginess; in the account of this part, not giving produces "sin," and for the sin to be established the poor man need not go and lodge a complaint — the moment he cries unto the LORD, the attribution is completed of itself. A further inference concerns timing: the nearer the year of release, the less "worth it" lending becomes — this is the institution's known side-effect; the text does not on that account relax the command, but at the very point where it is least worth it speaks the charge at its heaviest — the intensified forms occur densely in this part (twice at v. 8, once at v. 10, and once more within the quotation at v. 11). The institutional zeroing governs the account; the charge governs every day before the account is zeroed. This is an argument.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) The intertext between "Ye have the poor with you always" (Matt 26:11; Mark 14:7; John 12:8) and v. 11's "the poor shall never cease" is the most registered of all in Old–New Testament comparison; in registering it the contexts must be kept distinct: in the New Testament the saying occurs in the anointing incident, and treats of the contrast between worship and timing, while in this chapter the saying is the ground for the command to open the hand wide — the two places use one and the same statement of fact, but the function of the statement differs; the intertext is registered only, and the two are not read together as one and the same lesson. Further: 1 John's "shutteth up his bowels of compassion" (1 John 3:17), in the Chinese base text "blocketh up a heart of compassion," is often registered alongside the imagery of heart and hand in this part; and the correspondence between the narrative wording of Acts 4, that no one among them lacked (Acts 4:34), and v. 4's "no poor," is likewise a common register in the history of reading.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] The division of labour between the two words for the poor is registered at the lexical level: ʾeḇyôn leans toward objective lack (one who has nothing), ʿānî toward affliction of circumstance (the oppressed, the lowly); this chapter uses ʾeḇyôn as its main word (vv. 7, 9, 11), and at v. 11 uses the two words together to close the whole part. (Recounted on the KJV's own letter: "poor" occurs 6 times — once at v. 4, twice at v. 7, once at v. 9, twice at v. 11 — and "needy" once, at v. 11, 7 tokens in all over 4 verses; in the Chinese base text "poor" occurs 3 times and the word for needy lack 3 times, 6 in all over 4 verses; the totals differ because the KJV renders both Hebrew words in part by "poor" — the difference is registered and not harmonized, see Endnote 2.) As to genre, vv. 7–11 are typical Deuteronomic exhortation: first the scene is set ("If there be among you a poor man"), then the excuses are blocked off (the wicked thought, the evil eye), then the command is given (the intensified forms), then the promise is attached (blessing), and at the last the text returns to the fact (the poor shall never cease) — the order of the exhortation's structure is registered as it stands, and no judgment on sources is extended from it. bəlîyaʿal occurs elsewhere in Deuteronomy at chapter 13 (CUNPSS 13:13, where the Chinese base text renders it of persons, "children of Belial"): the same word at chapter 13 points to the men who entice to idolatry, and in this chapter to the heart-thought that will not give to the poor — the word's pedigree is registered to this point and not expanded.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: the year of release comes once in seven years, but the opening of the hand is daily — the heart must not harden, the hand must not clench, the eye must not be evil; because the poor shall never cease, the command never expires.

### Part Three: Let Him Go Free from Thee, and Let Him Not Go Away Empty (Deut 15:12–18; 26:00–38:00)

[Fact] Verse 12: "And if thy brother, an Hebrew man, or an Hebrew woman, be sold unto thee, and serve thee six years; then in the seventh year thou shalt let him go free from thee." The object is expressly named in both sexes — "an Hebrew man, or an Hebrew woman"; "free," in the original ḥopšî (H2668), the word for the status of a free man; the formula of six years' service and release in the seventh year is of the same construction as Exodus 21:2, "If thou buy an Hebrew servant, six years he shall serve: and in the seventh he shall go out free for nothing." Verse 13 is the first article of the rite of release: "And when thou sendest him out free from thee, thou shalt not let him go away empty:" — "empty," in the original rêqām (H7387), with nothing at all. Verse 14 gives the list for the bundle and its measure: "Thou shalt furnish him liberally out of thy flock, and out of thy floor, and out of thy winepress: of that wherewith the Lord thy God hath blessed thee thou shalt give unto him." Three sources — the flock, the floor (gōren H1637, the threshing floor), and the winepress (yeqeḇ H3342) — husbandry, farming, and horticulture: a portion is taken from every branch of the releaser's livelihood; the verb "furnish him liberally," in the original haʿănêq taʿănîq (ʿānaq H6059, at root a necklace, to hang about the neck, by extension to give lavishly), is in the intensified form; and the measure sets no ceiling, but sets a reference: according to the measure with which God has blessed you. Verse 15 gives the root: "And thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman in the land of Egypt, and the Lord thy God redeemed thee: therefore I command thee this thing to day." "Remember," zāḵar (H2142), is in the imperative; "redeemed," pāḏâ (H6299), bought out — the ground for releasing the servant is not sympathy but the household's own record: your whole clan is itself a people that was released. Verses 16–17 deal with the man who stays willingly: "And it shall be, if he say unto thee, I will not go away from thee; because he loveth thee and thine house, because he is well with thee; Then thou shalt take an aul, and thrust it through his ear unto the door, and he shall be thy servant for ever. And also unto thy maidservant thou shalt do likewise." The content of the saying is the servant's own quoted words, "I will not go away from thee"; the reasons are given by the narrative — that he loves thee and thine house, with the footnote "because he is well with thee"; the rite has only one step: the awl (marṣēaʿ H4836), the ear, the door (delet H1817). The rule applies to the maidservant in the same way, expressly written at the end of the verse. Verse 18 does the master's accounts for him: "It shall not seem hard unto thee, when thou sendest him away free from thee; for he hath been worth a double hired servant to thee, in serving thee six years: and the Lord thy God shall bless thee in all that thou doest." "Hired servant," ŝāḵîr (H7916); "hire/wages," ŝāḵār (H7939); in the original mišneh ŝəḵar ŝāḵîr, double the hire of a hired servant — the text does its own pricing: the value of six years' service is a double above that of a hired servant paid by the day; to count the release as a hardship is to have done the accounts wrong. The blessing sentence occurs here for the third time in the giving-and-release group (vv. 10, 14, 18), each link fastened to the next (for the whole chapter's blessing account — 5 occurrences — see Endnote 2).

[Argument] This is an inference: the most important step in this part is that it changes release from "letting the man go" into "sending the man off." To let go without sending off leaves the man, once out, still in his former lack, and before long he will have to sell himself again; the substance of the lavish gift is capital for a fresh start — the produce of the flock and of the threshing floor and the fruit of the winepress are a bundle with which a man can live independently. This is an argument and inference from vv. 13–14. A further inference concerns the "he shall be thy servant for ever" of v. 17: the institution gives the servant himself the right to open his mouth — the declaration of staying comes from his own mouth (the quotation at v. 16), and only then does the rite begin; compelled service has a ceiling of six years, and only voluntary service is spoken of with the word "for ever." Love and circumstance ("because he is well with thee") are the two footnotes the text gives to the voluntary staying; without those two footnotes, the boring of the ear is mere possession. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) Philemon's calling Onesimus "not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved" (Philem 16) is often registered alongside this part's designation "brother"; so likewise is Colossians' charge to masters to render unto their servants that which is just and equal (Col 4:1). Further: the mutual reference between Deuteronomy's "remember that thou wast a bondman in Egypt" and the structure of remembrance in the Passover meal, and indeed in the Lord's Supper, is a common register in liturgical discussion; this sermon registers only the structural echo and does not expand a doctrine of the liturgy.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Three registers are set side by side: first, whether "Hebrew" (ʿiḇrî) refers to the same population as the Ḥabiru / ʿApiru of ancient Near Eastern documents has long been discussed in scholarship, and the mainstream for the most part does not advocate a simple equation; this sermon registers the question itself and adopts neither side. Second, the textual relation between the servant ordinances of Exodus 21 and Deuteronomy 15 (which is earlier, and how each was edited) is a standard topic in discussion of the law codes' sources; this sermon sets out only the differences side by side (see Part Five) and arranges no stemma. Third, what the "double" at v. 18 refers to is read in scholarship in two ways — by value (six years' service is worth double a hired servant's hire) and by hours worked (a servant is on call day and night without distinction, and is therefore reckoned at double) — both readings are registered side by side and not adjudicated.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: the complete act of releasing a servant has three steps — let the man go when the term expires, send him off according to the measure of your own blessing, and remember that you too were ransomed out; leave out any one step, and the freedom is empty-handed.

### Part Four: All the Firstling Males Thou Shalt Sanctify (Deut 15:19–23; 38:00–49:00)

[Fact] Verse 19: "All the firstling males that come of thy herd and of thy flock thou shalt sanctify unto the Lord thy God: thou shalt do no work with the firstling of thy bullock, nor shear the firstling of thy sheep." "Firstling," in the original bəkôr (H1060); "sanctify unto," in the original the causative form of the verb qādaš (H6942), ləhaqdîš — to set it apart as holy, to make it belong to God. Both prohibitions aim at production: it must not be used to work the ground, nor its wool shorn for profit (gāzaz H1494, to shear) — before the firstling beast is offered up, it must not first be entered into its owner's production account. (On the KJV's own letter "firstling" occurs 3 times, all at v. 19; at vv. 20 and 21 the firstling is referred to by the pronoun "it" — in the Chinese base text the word for "firstling" occurs 5 times, 3 at v. 19 and once each at vv. 20 and 21; the two accounts are registered separately, see Endnote 2.) Verse 20 gives the rule for the eating: "Thou shalt eat it before the Lord thy God year by year in the place which the Lord shall choose, thou and thy household." Note who eats: not the priest alone, but "thou and thy household"; the place is the place the Lord shall choose; the rhythm is year by year — the firstling is not an occasional offering but a year-rule repeated annually: the herd and flock produce year by year, and the setting apart is therefore also performed again year by year. Verse 21 gives the blemish clause: "And if there be any blemish therein, as if it be lame, or blind, or have any ill blemish, thou shalt not sacrifice it unto the Lord thy God." "Blemish," in the original mûm (H3971); the list gives examples: lame, pissēaḥ (H6455), and blind, ʿiwwēr (H5787), and then gathers all under "any ill blemish" — for the blemished, the gate of sacrifice is shut. Verse 22 gives the downgraded way out: "Thou shalt eat it within thy gates: the unclean and the clean person shall eat it alike, as the roebuck, and as the hart." "Within thy gates," in the original bišʿāreyḵā, "within thy gates," another use of the same place-word as at v. 7; clean and unclean at one table is a latitude that the rules for sacrificial flesh do not have; the comparisons, the roebuck (ṣəḇî H6643) and the hart (ʾayyāl H354), are precisely the game in the list of clean animals at 14:5 — what has not been set apart as holy is eaten as ordinary food. Verse 23 closes with the prohibition of the blood: "Only thou shalt not eat the blood thereof; thou shalt pour it upon the ground as water." "Pour," šāpaḵ (H8210); the prohibition of the blood is in the same key as 12:16 and 12:23–24: even when downgraded to ordinary food, the blood still belongs singly to the ground and not to man. The sacrificial verb zāḇaḥ (H2076) and "blood," dām (H1818), are registered together here: the word for sacrifice occurs in this section only at v. 21, and there in the negative form ("thou shalt not sacrifice") — the belonging of the setting apart comes first, and the use in sacrifice only after it.

[Argument] This is an inference: what the firstling ordinance rewrites is the ownership of "the first." The first portion of the produce — the firstling beast, the firstfruits — in Deuteronomy's account does not at the first instance belong to the producer. What the two prohibitions block off is precisely the thought of converting what has been set apart as holy into productive power: to keep it for ploughing is self-use in disguise, to shear its wool for profit is realization in instalments; the text permits neither. This is an inference from the two prohibitions at v. 19. The blemish clause in turn shows the other face of reverence: an unqualified offering is neither forced onto the altar nor thrown away — it is downgraded to food within the city, eaten by clean and unclean together. In this chapter holy and common are not mixed; the boundary is clear and each has its own destination: what meets the standard is eaten at the chosen place, before God, with the household together; what does not meet the standard is eaten in the city, its blood poured on the ground, and it does not go up to the altar. This is an inference from vv. 21–23.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) The echo of the firstborn theme in the New Testament has a line that can be checked in the register: Hebrews' "church of the firstborn" (Heb 12:23) and Romans' calling Christ "the firstborn among many brethren" (Rom 8:29) are both echoes of the "firstborn" vocabulary in the doctrine of redemption — registered only, and the animal offerings of this chapter are not read back through them. Further: Malachi's rebuke of men who offer the blind and the lame in sacrifice (Mal 1:8) and the blemish clause of this verse reflect each other in reverse: this chapter is the rule given beforehand, the prophetic book the rebuke given afterwards — the comparison is registered, and the two are not read as one.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] Two registers side by side: first, the firstling ordinance occurs in several places in the Pentateuch, with accounts that differ from one another — at Exodus 13 the firstling includes man and beast, and a system of redemption is set up; Numbers 18:15–17 assigns the blood and the fat of the firstling beast to the altar and the flesh to the priest; this chapter says "thou and thy household" shall eat it at the chosen place. The three are set side by side; their differences lie in who eats (priest / householder's household) and in whether there is redemption or not; this sermon does not harmonize them and does not arrange them in order. Second, the firstling account of chapter 12 and of this chapter: 12:6 and 12:17 treat of bringing the firstling beast to the chosen place and forbid eating it within the gates; v. 22 of this chapter expressly says that the blemished firstling may be eaten within the gates. On the surface the directions are opposite; this sermon's register is: the objects of the two places are different — chapter 12 treats of the complete, set-apart firstling, and v. 22 of this chapter of the firstling with an ill blemish, which may not be sacrificed; the downgrading has its ground (v. 21). This is registered as a reading according to the text's own boundary; the source relation of the two ordinances is not adjudicated.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: with the firstling the hand must first stop — it must not first be taken to plough, nor its wool first be shorn off; with what is set apart as holy, first acknowledge to whom it belongs, and only then comes food.

### Part Five: The Three Calendars Side by Side — the Year of Release, the Sabbatical Year, and the Jubilee (49:00–57:00)

[Fact] This part only sets things side by side and makes no adjudication. First the three calendars are set side by side. The first is the year of release (vv. 1–11 of this chapter): its cycle is seven years; its object is debts and debtors — the debt a brother owes undergoes a release (vv. 1–3); its keyword is šəmiṭṭâ (H8102); and the point where its effect lands is the account between man and man. The second is the sabbatical year (Lev 25:2–7; cf. Exod 23:10–11): its cycle is likewise seven years; its object is the land — "When ye come into the land which I give you, then shall the land keep a sabbath unto the Lord" (Lev 25:2; "sabbath," šabbāṯ H7676); Exodus 23:11 gives the social landing-point of the land's rest: "But the seventh year thou shalt let it rest and lie still; that the poor of thy people may eat: and what they leave the beasts of the field shall eat. In like manner thou shalt deal with thy vineyard, and with thy oliveyard." — and the verb used in that verse is precisely a form of šāmaṭ: the land too is to "let fall." The third is the jubilee (Lev 25:8–55): its cycle is fifty years (after seven sabbatical years); the content of Lev 25:10 is: "And ye shall hallow the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof: it shall be a jubile unto you; and ye shall return every man unto his possession, and ye shall return every man unto his family." ("liberty," dərôr H1865; "jubile," yôḇēl H3104) — its object is possessions and the family: the possession that was sold returns, and the member who went out returns to his own family. The distinctions among the three, set side by side, are registered as follows: the rhythm — 7 years, 7 years, 50 years; the object — debts, land, possessions and men; the keywords — šəmiṭṭâ, šabbāṯ, dərôr / yôḇēl. The year of release and the sabbatical year are both on a seven-year cycle but govern different things — one governs the ledger, the other the land — and the text never confuses the two names; the jubilee's fifty years join onto the seven-year cycle (after seven sevens), but the narrative of Leviticus 25 runs on its own track, and this sermon does not merge the three calendars' accounts on their behalf.

Next the three servant ordinances are set side by side. Exodus 21:2–6: the object is the Hebrew servant who is "bought"; the term is six years, and in the seventh "he shall go out free for nothing" — "If thou buy an Hebrew servant, six years he shall serve: and in the seventh he shall go out free for nothing." (in the original ḥinnām, for nothing, without payment); there is no article of a gift; the procedure for the man who stays willingly is that his master "shall bring him unto the judges" — "Then his master shall bring him unto the judges; he shall also bring him to the door, or unto the door post; and his master shall bore his ear through with an aul; and he shall serve him for ever." (in the original hāʾĕlōhîm H430, for which the Chinese base text's note reads "or, God"); beside the door or the doorpost (məzûzâ H4201) his ear is bored; and the content of the voluntary declaration includes his wife and children: "And if the servant shall plainly say, I love my master, my wife, and my children; I will not go out free:" (Exod 21:5). Verses 12–18 of this chapter: the object is the Hebrew man or woman who is sold (both sexes expressly named); the term is likewise six years, with freedom in the seventh; there is an article of a lavish gift (vv. 13–14); there is an article of remembering Egypt (v. 15); the boring of the ear is performed only at the man's own door, with no procedure before judges; the voluntary declaration is "I will not go away from thee," and the reasons are given by the text's narrative — that he loveth thee and thine house, with the footnote "because he is well with thee." Leviticus 25:39–42: the object is the brother who "be waxen poor, and be sold unto thee" — "And if thy brother that dwelleth by thee be waxen poor, and be sold unto thee; thou shalt not compel him to serve as a bondservant: But as an hired servant, and as a sojourner, he shall be with thee, and shall serve thee unto the year of jubile." (ʿeḇeḏ H5650, servant); the term is bounded by the jubilee and is not reckoned by the individual's six years; and the reason is: "For they are my servants, which I brought forth out of the land of Egypt: they shall not be sold as bondmen." The three set side by side, their differences are plain: whether there is a judicial procedure or not, whether there is a gift or not, how broad or narrow the content of the declaration is, whether the term is reckoned individually or by the jubilee, and the wording of the reason sentence — this sermon registers them one by one, does not select any one place as the base text with which to rewrite the other two, arranges no order of editing, and does not adjudicate the relation of the three.

Last, the tension within this chapter itself is set side by side: vv. 4 and 11. At v. 4 (in the KJV's own two verses, 4–5): the promise stands in v. 4 — "Save when there shall be no poor among you" — and the condition in v. 5 — "Only if thou carefully hearken unto the voice of the Lord thy God, to observe to do all these commandments which I command thee this day." (in the CUNPSS fused reading the condition stands first and the promise after it). At v. 11 the statement of fact stands first — "For the poor shall never cease out of the land" — and the command after it — "therefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy, in thy land." The sentence patterns in which the two stand are different (condition–promise / fact–command), but the action they drive is in the same direction: both push a man toward opening his hand wide. The housings given to these two sentences in reading are not uniform: some read v. 4 as the promise under the condition of obedience and v. 11 as the statement of the constant reality; some read v. 4 as the covenant ideal and v. 11 as the historical actuality; and some, following the older reading of the KJV's opening words at v. 4, "Save when," read v. 4 as an exception clause. This sermon registers only these three housings side by side and adopts none of them as its conclusion — when these two verses are quoted, both sentences must be quoted together, each noted against the other, exactly as this sermon has done.

[Argument] This is an inference: setting side by side is not muddling through. With three calendars and three servant ordinances the text guards one and the same bottom line — a brother's future must not be bought out for good by a single sale or a single loan: debts have their seven years, the land has its sabbath, men have their jubilee, layer upon layer of sluice gates. Which reach of water each gate governs, the text distinguishes clearly; for the preacher to lay the accounts side by side is itself reverence; to merge the accounts on the text's behalf looks accommodating, but in fact wears away the edge that each of the three systems has in its own right. This is an argument. Further: when the promise of v. 4 and the fact of v. 11 are read at the same time, the hearer's position is also fixed: the promise keeps a man from losing heart — the opening of the hand is not a bottomless pit, for the blessing is in God's hand; the fact keeps a man from idling — the poor shall never cease, so the command never expires. Quote both sentences together, and a man can stand steady. This is an inference.

[New Testament Reading] (Registered as a report; the original wording has not been checked word for word.) Luke's record of Jesus reading in the synagogue at Nazareth from Isaiah of "the acceptable year of the Lord" (Luke 4:18–19), and its intertext with the jubilee of Leviticus 25 and with Isaiah 61, is the jubilee echo most registered in scholarship and from the pulpit; this sermon registers the existence of this intertext and does not, on the ground of the New Testament's reading, alter the side-by-side setting of the three calendars — the original sense of the jubilee in Leviticus 25 still rests on Leviticus 25.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion] The degree to which the three calendars were historically carried out is discussed in scholarship in various ways; it is registered as follows, without adjudication: the sabbatical year has left sporadic records of its observance in the literature and historical accounts of the Second Temple period (the relevant narratives in the books of the Maccabees are commonly used material); the observance of the year of release and the prosbul response have already been registered in Part One; whether the jubilee was ever actually observed has no clear record in the literature, and scholarship for the most part has its reservations. This sermon's position is to register the question: the existence of an ordinance in the text is not equal to a clear history of its observance, and an unclear history of observance does not cancel the ordinance's standing in the text — the two levels are kept apart and neither is set off against the other.

[Summary] This part in one sentence: debts must be released, the land must rest, men must return home — the three calendars each govern one account; the promise of v. 4 and the fact of v. 11 stand side by side in one and the same chapter, and this sermon sets them side by side to this point and does not adjudicate.

## Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Fact] The whole chapter closes at v. 23: the blood is poured upon the ground as water. Looking back, the chain of actions in the whole chapter runs: at v. 1 a release is made, at v. 3 the hand releases, at v. 8 the hand is opened wide, at v. 10 there is all that the hand takes up, at v. 12 the man is let go free, at v. 13 he must not go away empty, at v. 19 the hand that would work the beast is stopped — one pair of hands is schooled verse by verse through this chapter: first it learns to release the claim, then to open in giving, then to send off not empty, and last to stop before what has been set apart as holy.

[Argument] This is an inference: Deuteronomy 15's final definition of wealth is not in the storehouse but in the hand. Storehouses empty, accounts are zeroed, calendars run out; what remains is the gesture: is it clenched, or is it opened wide? Not one of this chapter's commands requires a man to be rich first: the man who releases his grip in the year of release is the man holding an IOU in his hand; the man who opens his hand wide to the poor brother gives according to what the brother lacks; the man who sends the servant out gives according to the measure of his own blessing — every lesson is learned starting from what is already held in the hand. This is an argument.

[Summary] One sentence to gather the whole: Thou shalt open thine hand wide — this command occurs twice in this chapter, once in the charge (v. 8) and once within the quotation (v. 11); it is also the key the chapter's three groups of ordinances share: debts are to be released, the poor are to be given to, men are to be sent off, and with the firstling the hand is to stop — the opened hand is the mark the year of release leaves for every ordinary day.

## Devotional Extract

Deuteronomy 15 says one sentence twice: first in the charge, "Thou shalt open thine hand wide" (v. 8); and three verses later it is set inside quotation marks and said all over again (v. 11). Between the two stands the account we are best at reckoning: the year of release is nearly here; to give now is to give for nothing. The text drags that heart-sentence straight out into the open — it is not called cleverness, it is called a wicked thought; to look with an evil eye on a brother and give him nothing lays the sin at one's own door. And when one does give, the heart must not be grieved while giving: the hand has opened and the heart is still clenched — before God that does not count as giving. So after the giving there is still a question to ask: when I gave, was my heart loose or tight? The last sentence is the heaviest: "For the poor shall never cease out of the land." God did not wait for the world to become good before commanding us to open the hand; He wrote the perpetual presence of the poor as the reason for the command — precisely because a brother's lack will not cease, the opened hand cannot be a single stirring of feeling, but must become the settled norm: every time he stands at the gate, open it once more.

"Thou shalt surely give him, and thine heart shall not be grieved when thou givest unto him: because that for this thing the Lord thy God shall bless thee in all thy works, and in all that thou puttest thine hand unto. For the poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy, in thy land." (Scripture quoted in the devotional extract: Deut 15:10–11, KJV, verbatim.)

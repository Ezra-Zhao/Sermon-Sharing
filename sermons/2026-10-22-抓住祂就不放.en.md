## Summary (about 100 words)

After making a covenant and parting from Laban, Jacob set out on the road home, and ahead of him were his brother Esau and his four hundred men. At Mahanaim he met the angels of God; then he sent gifts ahead and divided his company for safety; at night he crossed the ford of Jabbok, and alone he wrestled with the Man until daybreak. His thigh was put out of joint, yet he held on and would not let go. He came out with a new name, Israel, limping into the sunlight, finally able to face his brother.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

After making a covenant and parting from Laban, Jacob set out on the road home. Twenty years earlier he had fled from home with only a staff in his hand; twenty years later he was walking back with two wives, a crowd of children, and large flocks and herds behind him. But the road home was no easier than the road of escape, because waiting ahead of him was not Laban, but his brother Esau. Twenty years earlier he had cheated his brother out of the birthright and stolen his father's blessing, and from then on the brothers were enemies. His brother's words, "I will slay my brother Jacob," he could never forget for the rest of his life. Now the news arrived: Esau was coming to meet him, with four hundred men behind him.

In this life, there are always a few roads a person cannot go around. You can run to Haran for twenty years, build a family and a fortune in your father-in-law's house, but that matter that was never settled, that person you never faced, that account you never admitted — none of it disappears by itself just because a long time has passed. It stands on the road home waiting for you. This chapter tells how Jacob walked that road. Before he set out he first met the angels of God; on the way he sent company after company of gifts ahead; after crossing the river, in the dark of night, alone, he wrestled with a mysterious opponent until dawn — his leg was crippled, but his hand did not let go, and in the end even his name was changed.

There is also a moving feature in this chapter: its timing. From sending gifts at dusk, to crossing the river and wrestling at night, to walking limping as the sun rose — it is one whole night. The Bible places the most important transformation of Jacob's life in a night with no audience. No wives or children beside him, no servants present, and his brother not yet even met. When God remakes a person, He often does not use a great spectacle; He uses a long night. He does not use a crowd watching; He uses a solitary facing. As we read this chapter today, let us also place ourselves in this night: you are the one standing by the river, an unavoidable tomorrow ahead of you, and no one at your side. Where you go at such a moment, and what you hold on to, will decide who you are after daybreak.

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several key passages:

"And Jacob went on his way, and the angels of God met him. And when Jacob saw them, he said, This is God's host: and he called the name of that place Mahanaim." (Genesis 32:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob sent messengers before him to Esau his brother unto the land of Seir, the country of Edom. ... And the messengers returned to Jacob, saying, We came to thy brother Esau, and also he cometh to meet thee, and four hundred men with him." (Genesis 32:3, 6, excerpt, KJV)

"So went the present over before him: and himself lodged that night in the company. And he rose up that night, and took his two wives, and his two womenservants, and his eleven sons, and passed over the ford Jabbok. ... And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day." (Genesis 32:21-24, excerpt, KJV)

"And he said, Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel: for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed." (Genesis 32:28, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob called the name of the place Peniel: for I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved. And as he passed over Penuel the sun rose upon him, and he halted upon his thigh." (Genesis 32:30-31, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, each of us has an Esau in his heart, and a tomorrow he dares not face. Through Jacob's night, teach us today: fear is not shame, and avoidance is not a way out; let us learn from him to hold on to You and not let go, until Your word comes to us, until You give us a new name. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Mahanaim and the Four Hundred Men — The Afraid Man Prays First (32:1-21)

First look at the beginning of the road home. Jacob had just left Laban's territory when the Bible writes: "the angels of God met him." It was not that Jacob found God; the angels of God came up to meet him first. Jacob saw them and said, "This is God's host," and called the name of that place Mahanaim. Mahanaim, in the original machaneh (מַחֲנֶה, H4264), a camp, an encampment, a host [1]. The name is in a dual form: two camps. Which two camps? One camp was Jacob's own company, traveling with his whole household; the other camp was the company of God's angels, silent and unseen. Brothers and sisters, this opening is deeply comforting: before Jacob saw Esau, he first saw God's camp; before he had opened his mouth to pray, God had already set His guard before him. Very often we think God comes only when we reach a dead end. In fact God often stands on the road before we have even seen the danger. That we cannot see Him does not mean He is not there. In Jacob's life, Bethel was God waiting for him on the road of his flight, and Mahanaim was God meeting him on the road home. When he went, God did not abandon him; when he returned, God did not abandon him either.

But seeing God's camp does not mean the heart is no longer afraid. Jacob's next actions are very true to life: he sent messengers ahead to see Esau, and his words were extremely humble — "my lord Esau," "thy servant Jacob" — saying that he had sojourned with Laban and stayed until now, that he had oxen and donkeys, flocks and servants, and that he had sent to tell his lord, "that I may find grace in thy sight." The whole speech does not mention the old account of those years in a single word; there is only one posture: I have come back, I bow my head, I seek reconciliation. The messengers returned with one sentence; the Bible does not record Esau's attitude, only the number: "he cometh to meet thee, and four hundred men with him." Four hundred men at that time was not a wedding procession; it was a force that could fight a battle. When Jacob heard it, the text writes it plainly: "Then Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed."

Note that the Bible does not rebuke him for being afraid. Fear is an honest reaction. For a man traveling with women, children, and flocks, hearing that four hundred men are coming toward him, not to be afraid would be abnormal. Faith has never meant having no fear; faith is about where you go when you are afraid. Jacob went in three directions, and these three steps are still useful to us today.

The first step: he made the preparations a man can make. He divided the people, the flocks, the herds, and the camels into two bands, reasoning in his heart: "If Esau come to the one company, and smite it, then the other company which is left shall escape." This was dividing for self-protection, a plan for the worst case. Some people think that a person who trusts God should not make preparations, but should lie back and wait for a miracle. Jacob was not like that. He had just seen God's camp at Mahanaim, and he turned around and divided his company all the same. Reverence and preparation do not conflict: arrange what can be arranged, and hand back to God what cannot be controlled — that is doing one's duty, not unbelief.

The second step, and the most important step in the whole chapter: he prayed. Of Jacob's prayers in Genesis, this is the longest one recorded. His prayer had roots and grounding: "O God of my father Abraham, and God of my father Isaac, the LORD which saidst unto me, Return unto thy country, and to thy kindred, and I will deal well with thee: I am not worthy of the least of all the mercies, and of all the truth, which thou hast shewed unto thy servant; for with my staff I passed over this Jordan; and now I am become two bands." First he laid hold of what God had said, then he counted up the grace God had already given, and only then did he open his mouth to ask: "Deliver me, I pray thee, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau: for I fear him, lest he will come and smite me, and the mother with the children." He even said out loud how far his fear went, without pretending. Then he set God's word back before Him again: "And thou saidst, I will surely do thee good, and make thy seed as the sand of the sea, which cannot be numbered for multitude." Brothers and sisters, this is what prayer looks like: not reporting to God how calm we are, but spreading our fear out as it is, and laying hold of His promise as it is. In the hand, God's word; in the heart, real fear — neither of the two is hidden. Such prayer, God hears.

The third step: he sent gifts ahead. From his livestock he picked out large numbers of goats, sheep, camels, cattle, and donkeys, divided them drove by drove, and had his servants drive them ahead one company after another, with space left between each drove, instructing the servants that when they met Esau they should all say the same thing: these are thy servant Jacob's; it is a present sent unto my lord Esau; and behold, he himself is behind us. The Bible even writes out the thought in Jacob's heart: "I will pacify him with the present that goeth before me, and afterward I will see his face; peradventure he will accept of me." A man who once cheated his brother now sent his own twenty years of accumulated household wealth ahead, drove by drove, asking only that his brother's anger might be cooled a little. This was not a bribe; it was the posture of making amends: what I owe you, I acknowledge; what I can repay, I repay first. A person who truly wants reconciliation does not only move his mouth; he pays a cost.

One more detail is worth pausing over: when Jacob arranged the gifts, he deliberately left space between each drove. One drove arrived, and his brother's anger cooled a little; after an interval, a second drove arrived, and it cooled a little more. He understood that anger needs time, and that making amends cannot be squeezed into a single sentence. Brothers and sisters, repairing a relationship with someone is often not settled by one deep conversation; it is company after company of sincerity, slowly bringing the fire in the other person's heart down. When Jacob was young, he grabbed things all in one clutch; now he sent things company by company — twenty years of sojourning had ground patience into this man. But we must also be honest: gifts can only do this much. Gifts can soften an atmosphere; they cannot change a heart. Dividing the company can reduce losses; it cannot guarantee safety. After finishing everything a man can do, Jacob knew clearly in his own heart that the remaining, larger half could only be handed to God. That is exactly why, after praying that night, he sent his family across the river — it was not that finishing his preparations put him at ease; it was that after finishing his preparations he discovered that the one he most needed to face was not Esau, but God, and himself. When a person has arranged everything outside, the battle in his heart has only just begun.

The preparations were finished, the prayer was prayed, the gifts were sent, and night had fallen. Jacob sent his wives and children across the ford Jabbok and stayed on this side of the river himself. The text leaves only one sentence: "And Jacob was left alone." All the busyness before — messengers, gifts, companies, wives and children — at this moment all left the stage. The deepest lesson of a life is never taught when crowds are present; it is taught when you are the only one left. Jacob thought the hardest thing that night would be waiting for dawn, waiting for Esau. But He had arranged for Another to wait for him first, in the dark of night.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Night at the Ford of Jabbok — Alone, Wrestling Until Daybreak (32:22-26)

The ford of Jabbok is on the east bank of the Jordan; it was the last pass on Jacob's way back to Canaan. That night he first sent his family across the river and fell behind himself. The text says: "there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day." Notice this initiative: it was not Jacob who went looking for someone to wrestle; the Man came looking for him. Jacob had actively grabbed many things in his life — in the womb he grabbed his brother's heel; grown up, he grabbed the birthright, grabbed his father's blessing, grabbed flocks in Laban's house. He grabbed all his life. This night, it was an Opponent's turn to come and grab him first.

Who was that Man? The text only calls him "a man" and does not give him a name. Yet Jacob said afterward, "I have seen God face to face." The prophet Hosea, looking back on this night, says that Jacob "had power over the angel," and also that he wept and made supplication, and that at Bethel he found the LORD [see Hosea 12:3-4]. The Bible deliberately writes this One as both real and mysterious: He appears in the shape of a man, can be held, can wrestle with a man, and yet He can change a man's name and give a man a blessing, and Jacob was certain that he had seen God face to face and that his life was preserved. We need not hurry to state the whole answer; we only need to hold on to the facts the text gives in full: the One who wrestled with Jacob that night was not a robber, not a dream, but God Himself coming to him in a way that could be touched. When God dealt with Jacob, it was not in a great daytime scene before people; it was at night, by the river, when he was the only one left.

Then look at this word "wrestled." In the original, ye'aveq (יֵאָבֵק, H79), to wrestle, to grapple [2]. In the exegetical tradition it has often been pointed out that its sound is close to the word for "dust" — men grappling on the ground stir up dust, and Jacob rolled in the dust that night; this wordplay has been handed down widely, and this manuscript records it only as a traditional view, not as a settled conclusion. What can be confirmed is the resonance of place and name: Jacob (Ya'aqov), Jabbok (Yabboq), and wrestled (ye'aveq) — three words whose sounds are close in the original, as if the biblical author intended the reader to hear it: this man whose name means grabbing, at the river Jabbok, went through the most important grabbing of his life.

This bout lasted extremely long — "until the breaking of the day." It was not a single move that subdued an enemy, nor a push that knocked him down; it was a whole night of entanglement. Please think about Jacob's situation at that moment: tomorrow he had to meet Esau, whose four hundred men were right across from him; this night should have been for storing up strength, yet he was dragged into an unending struggle. The life of prayer for many people is just like this: on the very night before the night when they most need sleep, it turns out to be the longest night. In the corridor outside the ward, in the small hours before surgery, on the night before an interview or a verdict, you toss and turn in the dark, and the bout in your heart is wrestled from midnight until dawn. Jacob's night tells us that God does not always shorten the night; sometimes He personally stays in the night and wrestles it through with us to the end.

The turning point came in one action: "And when he saw that he prevailed not against him, he touched the hollow of his thigh; and the hollow of Jacob's thigh was out of joint, as he wrestled with him." Look carefully: it was not a strike, not a kick, only a touch. With one light touch, the hollow of the thigh was put out of joint. "Thigh," in the original yarekh (יָרֵךְ, H3409), the thigh, the side of the loins; "the hollow of the thigh," in the original kaph (כַּף, H3709), whose basic meaning is the palm of the hand, extended to mean a hollow or socket [3]. The strongest part of a person's body is the thigh; standing, walking, and exerting force all depend on it. The One who had wrestled with Jacob all night and seemingly could not prevail against him needed only one touch to remove the strongest fulcrum of his whole body. Brothers and sisters, what does this show? It shows that all night long, that One was not unable to win; He was unwilling to win. He wrestled with Jacob all night not in order to defeat him, but in order to exhaust him — to exhaust that strength Jacob had accumulated over twenty years by grabbing, cheating, and scheming. With a man like Jacob, if God only crushed him with power, he would submit with his mouth but not in his heart. God's way was this: wrestle with him, wrestle until his strong point was disabled by a single touch, wrestle until he could no longer stand steady and could only hang onto his Opponent. Many people's transformation does not happen at the moment God defeats them; it happens at the moment God exhausts them: their own methods are used up, their own strength is gone with one touch, and all that remains is to hold on and not let go.

With the thigh out of joint, the bout could in fact no longer continue. But the scene that follows is the heart of the whole chapter. The Man said, "Let me go, for the day breaketh." Jacob's answer has been handed down for thousands of years: "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me." A man with a crippled leg should have let go and begged for mercy; instead he hung the whole weight of his body on his Opponent and made a request that was almost unreasonable: no blessing, no letting go. This is the most precious, and also the most real, thing about Jacob as a person. All his life he grabbed the wrong things: he grabbed the birthright, grabbed the portion of blessing prepared for his brother, grabbed wealth. But this night, he finally grabbed the right object, and grabbed the right thing — he held on to God Himself, and what he asked for with his mouth was not flocks, not a safe escape, but a blessing. This man who once stole a blessing now clung to the Source of blessing and would not release his grip; whatever happened, he was not willing to steal again. Brothers and sisters, the essence of prayer is right here: not treating God as a tool for satisfying wishes, but holding on to God Himself and not letting go — even if the leg is already crippled, even if the day is almost breaking, even if He says, Let me go. Carrying a wound, carrying fear, carrying a whole night of weariness, still saying: unless You bless me, I will not let go. Such prayer, the Bible calls prevailing.

Hosea, looking back on this night, adds one detail that Genesis does not state explicitly: Jacob wept and made supplication. So this night held not only the strength of wrestling, but also tears. Strong people wrestle by strength; exhausted people wrestle by tears and by a hand that refuses to let go. Many of us, praying to the very end, can no longer even speak; all that remains is weeping, all that remains is clinging and not releasing. Do not look down on such prayer. Whether heaven responds often does not depend on how beautiful our words are; it depends on whether, at the moment we have the least strength left, we let go, or are still hanging onto Him. That night Jacob changed from a man who grabbed heels into a man who grabbed hold of God; what he grabbed changed, and so this man began to change from the root. What God wants has never been for us to switch to a more respectable set of phrases; what God wants is for us to turn the hand that grabbed the wrong things all our life around, and grab the right place.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: A New Name and a Limp — Carrying a Wound into the Sunlight (32:27-32)

The Man did not bless him at once; He first asked a question: "What is thy name?" He said, "Jacob." Those words had never been so hard for Jacob to say in his whole life. The name Jacob, whose basic sense is connected with the heel, had long carried the verdict people gave him: a man who grabs the heel, who grasps, supplants, and deceives. Esau had once said through clenched teeth, "Is not he rightly named Jacob? for he hath supplanted me these two times." Now, asked his name before God, Jacob did not defend himself and did not beautify it; he spoke that stained name out exactly as it was. Brothers and sisters, before a person receives a new name, he must first admit the old name. Many people are stuck here all their lives: we are willing to ask God for blessing, but unwilling to admit before God who we actually are. Jacob's turning point began right here, with this honest sentence: "Jacob."

The Man then declared: "Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel: for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed." Israel, in the original yisra'el (יִשְׂרָאֵל, H3478); the exact etymology of this name is explained differently in scholarship, and the text's own explanation connects it with "power / striving" — possibly related to sarah (שָׂרָה, H8280, to strive, to contend, to have power) [5]. We take the text's own explanation as our basis: Israel is a person who has striven with God and whom God counts as having prevailed. Note: it was not that he threw God down — his leg was still out of joint. It was God Himself who credited him with prevailing: a limping man who held on and would not let go is called, in God's account, one who prevailed. From then on, this name no longer belonged to him alone. A nation would bear it as its name, and the whole Old Testament would bear it as its name; to this day, what we read is still the story of Israel. A new name God gives to one person can be large enough to hold His whole plan of salvation. Your past may be called Jacob; your future can be called Israel — provided that in the dark of night you admit the old name and hold on and do not let go.

Jacob wanted to ask the other party's name as well; the other party did not answer, but only asked in return, "Wherefore is it that thou dost ask after my name?" and right there He blessed him. Some things God does not explain; He only blesses. Jacob rose and called the name of that place Peniel, in the original peni'el (פְּנִיאֵל, H6439), composed of panim (face, H6440) and 'el (God), meaning "the face of God" [4]. His own footnote was: "for I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved." By the reverence of that time, to see the face of God was to die; yet Jacob came out alive. What astonished him was not that he had won, but that he was actually still alive. This is the shape of grace: having wrestled with God all night, coming out carrying a wound — life still there, blessing received, name changed.

One more thing should be noticed: before this night, Jacob had already prayed once — the rooted and grounded prayer in Part One. Why did God not, right after he finished praying, simply send an angel to soften Esau's heart and let him cross over in peace? Because Jacob's greatest problem, on the surface, was Esau, but in his heart it was himself. Esau's four hundred men were tomorrow's danger; Jacob's heart, which only knew how to grab, to scheme, and to run, was a danger that had followed him all his life. If God only removed Esau, Jacob, having arrived in Canaan, would still be the Jacob who grabs, cheats, and flees whenever something happens. Because God loved him, He would not only save him out of his circumstances; He would also save him out of his old self. So the wrestling of this night was not an episode outside the prayer; it was precisely the answer to that prayer — only the way it was answered was unexpected: God first deals with the person who prays, and then deals with the matters mentioned in the prayer. We ask God to change our circumstances, and God often first changes us; we ask God to remove the four hundred men, and God first touches the hollow of our thigh. This is not God not hearing prayer; this is God hearing more deeply than we asked.

The final scene — please look at it slowly: "And as he passed over Penuel the sun rose upon him, and he halted upon his thigh." The sun had risen. Esau's four hundred men were still waiting ahead, but the man who walked into the sunlight was no longer the same as the man who crossed the river last night. The man of last night had many methods, fast legs, and a quick hand for grabbing anything; the man of this morning walked with one limp after another, his pace slower, but he had a new name, had received a blessing, and knew that he had seen the face of God. The text also specially records: the children of Israel eat not of the sinew which shrank to this day, because He touched the hollow of Jacob's thigh in the sinew that shrank. One man's wound became a memorial for a whole nation, generation after generation. Brothers and sisters, do not belittle the wound on your body. God did not set Jacob's leg right on the spot before letting him go; He let him limp off to meet Esau. Blessing and wound are often two things received on the same night. The new name did not cancel the limp, and the limp did not cancel the new name. From then on, every step Jacob took would remind him of this night; and precisely because he walked with a limp, he could never again be the man of former days who relied only on his own fast legs to flee for his life.

An honest word must also be said here: a change of name does not mean a change of character completed in one night. Jacob would still show favoritism later, and would still make mistakes; in the next chapter, when he met Esau, he first ran ahead of the whole family and bowed himself to the ground seven times — and that was indeed a fruit of this night: the man who once hid at the back scheming now walked to the very front himself, shielding the others. God's work in him was the project of a lifetime; this night was the turning point, not the finish line. We are the same: do not expect one prayer to solve every problem, and do not, because you are still weak after praying, conclude that that night was false. A seed needs time to sprout; for a man walking with a limp, if the direction is right, slow is still forward.

Applying this chapter to ourselves, I will speak of only four kinds of people. The first kind: people who tomorrow must face their "Esau." There is a conversation you have avoided for a long time, an account, an apology, a decision that can be delayed no longer — and it is tomorrow. Learn from Jacob and walk these steps through: first see Mahanaim — God is already on the road; prepare what can be prepared, and send the amends-gift ahead where amends are owed; then bring your fear into prayer exactly as it is, and hold God's word in your hand exactly as it is. The second kind: people in the middle of a long night. Your bout has already been wrestled for half the night, the problem is not solved, and your strength is nearly gone. Please hear Jacob's sentence: I will not let thee go, except thou bless me. Prayer at this point is not technique; it is not letting go. The leg may limp, but the hand must not loosen; the circumstances may not change, but the One you hold on to must not be exchanged. The third kind: people living under an old name. Your past has been labeled by others: failure, trouble, unloved, good-for-nothing. First admit your old name before God, without defending yourself; then let God give you a new name. A name given by people binds you for a time; a name given by God defines your whole life.

The fourth kind: people whose leg is already limping and who think their prayer has failed. You have asked for a long time; the illness is not fully healed, the relationship is not fully restored, the job has not immediately returned, and you are beginning to doubt whether that night was wrestled in vain. Please read the last verse again: after Jacob received the blessing, his leg was still limping. God did not prove that He had heard the prayer by "restoring everything to how it was"; He proved it with a new name and with sunlight. Some answers to prayer do not take the wound away; they let you keep walking even with the wound, and walking in the right direction. That limp on your body may be exactly the mark of your meeting with God; there is no need to hide it, and no need to be ashamed of it. The Israelites remembered it for generations by not eating the sinew of the hollow of the thigh, which shows that among God's people a scar has never been something that cannot be seen in public; it is evidence that grace has been here.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we walked one night with Jacob: at Mahanaim he first saw God's camp; at the ford of Jabbok he wrestled alone with God until daybreak; his thigh was put out of joint, and he held on and would not let go; at dawn he received a new name, Israel, and limping, he walked into the sunlight. Prayer that wrestles with God is just like this: the person who holds on in fear and will not let go receives a new name while carrying a wound. God is not afraid of your holding on to Him; what He fears is that you let go in the night and give up before daybreak.

Let us pray together: Lord, tonight we will not pretend to be calm before You. You know what we fear, what we avoid, and what is hidden in our old name. Deal with us as You dealt with Jacob: wrestle this bout with us, touch away the fulcrum we thought was our strongest, and teach us to set our hands on the right place — to hold on to You Yourself. Give us a new name, and make us willing to walk forward even limping, into the sunlight, and up to the person we must face. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

[2] ye'aveq: to wrestle, Hebrew יֵאָבֵק (H79, the verb אָבַק), to wrestle, to grapple, to become entangled; Genesis 32:24, "there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Its closeness in sound to "dust" ('avaq, אָבָק) and the idea of dust stirred up by grappling are a traditional exegetical view handed down for a long time; in Part Two this manuscript notes that it records it only as a traditional view, not as an etymological conclusion. The resonance of sound among the place Jabbok (Yabboq) and the name Jacob (Ya'aqov) is likewise a literary association of the narrative, not an etymological assertion.

[3] yarekh and kaph: yarekh, Hebrew יָרֵךְ (H3409), the thigh, the side of the loins, the hip; Genesis 32:25, 31-32 use this word for "thigh" and "the hollow of the thigh." kaph, Hebrew כַּף (H3709), the palm of the hand, extended to mean a palm-shaped hollow or socket; Genesis 32:25, 32, "the hollow of the thigh," is "the hollow/socket of the thigh" (kaph-hayyarekh); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The memorial in Genesis 32:32, that the children of Israel do not eat the sinew of the hollow of the thigh, is related to this.

[4] peni'el: Peniel, Hebrew פְּנִיאֵל (H6439), composed of panim (פָּנִים, H6440, face, faces) and 'el (אֵל, God), meaning "the face of God"; Genesis 32:30, Jacob called the name and gave his own note, "for I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Genesis 32:31 writes Penuel, a variant spelling of the same place name.

[5] yisra'el: Israel, Hebrew יִשְׂרָאֵל (H3478); Genesis 32:28, "Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel: for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed." Its etymology is explained differently in scholarship: one view relates it to the verb sarah (שָׂרָה, H8280, to strive, to contend, to have power), meaning "one who strives with God"; the text itself gives "power with God and with men" as the explanation, and this manuscript argues from the text's own explanation, recording the differing etymological views alongside; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day." (Genesis 32:24) "And he said, Let me go, for the day breaketh. And he said, I will not let thee go, except thou bless me." (Genesis 32:26) "And as he passed over Penuel the sun rose upon him, and he halted upon his thigh." (Genesis 32:31)

The night Jacob feared most became the most important night of his life. Tomorrow he would meet Esau, with four hundred men waiting ahead; he sent his family over the ford and remained alone in the dark. God did not first settle Esau for him; He first wrestled with him all night by the river, and touched the hollow of his thigh out of joint. His leg was crippled, yet he held on and said: I will not let thee go, except thou bless me. Our prayers often stall at midnight: the problem is not solved, and our strength runs out first. Jacob teaches us that victory in prayer is not about whether the leg is healed, but whether the hand lets go. You may hold on with fear, hold on with tears, hold on with a wound. Do not let go first today. The one who holds on to Him may walk limping at daybreak, but he walks away blessed and renamed. Your old name cannot hold you, and neither can the long night.

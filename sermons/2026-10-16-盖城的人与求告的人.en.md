## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you all, brothers and sisters!

Yesterday we concluded our look at Cain and Abel. Cain killed his brother and, carrying the mark God gave him, departed from the presence of the Lord, journeyed east, and lived in the land of Nod. Nod means wandering.

Ordinarily, for a wandering man, the story should slowly fade out. Yet the second half of Genesis chapter 4 does not halt with Cain alone; it traces his descendants downward, through seven generations in a single breath. Then, with a turn of the pen, it returns to the household of Adam and Eve, recording that they bore yet another son.

The second half of this chapter actually records only two things: how man built a city, and how man began to pray.

The line that built the city had everything: they had a city, tents and livestock, the harp and flute, bronze and iron implements. Civilization emerged piece by piece, yet there was not a single word of prayer. The line that called on the Lord received pathetically little in the biblical text: only a replacement son, a frail grandson, and one sentence—then men began to call on the name of the Lord.

Today, let us follow these two lines. Please turn in your Bibles to Genesis 4:17–26. I will read several essential passages:

"And Cain knew his wife, and she conceived and bore Enoch. And he built a city, and called the name of the city after the name of his son—Enoch." (Gen. 4:17)

"Then Lamech said to his wives: 'Adah and Zillah, hear my voice; wives of Lamech, listen to my speech! For I have killed a man for wounding me, even a young man for hurting me. If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, then Lamech seventy-sevenfold.'" (Gen. 4:23-24)

"And Adam knew his wife again, and she bore a son and named him Seth, 'For God has appointed another seed for me instead of Abel, whom Cain killed.' And as for Seth, to him also a son was born; and he named him Enosh. Then men began to call on the name of the LORD." (Gen. 4:25-26)

Ten verses, three acts:

First, 4:17-22: Cain's descendants build a city and forge tools—man walks away from the presence of God, yet his hands become ever more capable; civilization charges forward while the heart drifts eastward.  
Second, 4:23-24: The song of Lamech—by the seventh generation, killing is no longer something concealed in the field, but has become something boasted of in song; vengeance escalates from sevenfold to seventy-sevenfold.  
Third, 4:25-26: The line of Seth—God appoints another son, a child named mortal man is born, people begin to call on the name of the Lord, and another path is opened.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we give You thanks. While man built cities along the road eastward, You prepared on another narrow path those who would call upon Your name. We pray that today You would grant us clarity to see which line we are walking upon, and grant us the courage in this generation to return to the path of calling upon Your name. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Hands Growing More Capable, Hearts Drifting Further East—The Civilization of Cain’s Descendants (4:17-22)

### 1.1 The Wandering Man Built a City

First, let us observe a paradoxical detail in verse 17.

God's sentence upon Cain was: "A fugitive and a vagabond you shall be on the earth." (Gen. 4:12) Yet in verse 17, the very first major endeavor Cain undertook was to build a city.

What does it mean for a wanderer to build a city? In antiquity, a city stood first and foremost for walls, settlement, and a sense of security. When a person is most terrified of wandering in his heart, he longs most intensely to erect a wall for himself. The stability he cannot obtain before God, he attempts to manufacture for himself with bricks.

This is not to say that building a city is intrinsically sinful. There is nothing inherently wrong with a city; the Bible's ultimate consummation is also a city—the New Jerusalem. The problem lies in two places.

One is the name. He named the city after his son, Enoch. Enoch, in Hebrew *Chanokh*, carries the meaning of dedication or inauguration. A father naming a city after his son desires his own bloodline to stand firmly and endure upon the earth. After departing from the presence of God, the first hope man grasps for is to prevent his name from being cut off.

The other is the order of priorities. For Cain, the city came first, followed only then by this long lineage of descendants; his security was built from the outside in. First the walls, then the heart. Millennia have passed, yet man retains this exact order: gather all the external conditions first—houses, careers, titles, savings—and then hope the inner heart will follow in finding peace. But while walls can keep out the wind, they cannot keep out the sense of wandering when one wakes in the middle of the night. The first sentence of Psalm 127 articulates this with absolute clarity:

"Unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain who build it; unless the LORD guards the city, the watchman stays awake in vain." (Ps. 127:1)

Notice that the psalmist does not forbid building or watching. Building continues as building, watching continues as watching. What the psalmist says is: unless the Lord is in it, the higher man builds, the more it is in vain. What Cain's city lacked was not craftsmanship; what it lacked was the Lord in its midst.

### 1.2 The Genealogy of Seven Generations: Having Everything, Except God

From Enoch downward, Scripture lists a succession of names: Irad, Mehujael, Methushael, and Lamech. Arriving at the generation of Lamech, civilization suddenly bursts with richness through three sons, bringing three pioneering breakthroughs:

"Adah bore Jabal. He was the father of those who dwell in tents and have livestock." (Gen. 4:20) This was the path of livelihood: tents and livestock. Humanity learned to sustain life on the move and learned to manage enterprises.

"His brother’s name was Jubal. He was the father of all those who play the harp and flute." (Gen. 4:21) This was the path of art: the harp and flute; music emerged. Brothers and sisters, please take note: music itself is God's gift. Later, David used the harp in worship, and the Psalms are filled with harps and lyres. There is nothing wrong with instruments.

"And as for Zillah, she also bore Tubal-Cain, an instructor of every craftsman in bronze and iron." (Gen. 4:22) This was the path of technology: bronze and iron. Farm implements are made of bronze and iron, and weapons of war are also made of bronze and iron. The very same craft—which direction it serves—depends not on the craft, but on the human heart.

Reading through this genealogy reveals a chillingly silent reality: from Cain to Lamech, across seven generations, Scripture does not record a single word spoken to God by any of them. There was no sacrifice, no prayer, no calling upon Him—not even the sullen, sullen-faced sacrifice that Cain had offered. In chapter 3, man was still hiding from God; in the first half of chapter 4, man was still talking back to God; but by the second half, even talking back was abandoned. The state of being furthest from God is not active hatred toward God, but feeling no further need to speak to Him at all.

Proverbs 10:7 states: "The memory of the righteous is blessed, but the name of the wicked will rot." That this string of names remains preserved in Scripture today is not for a memorial, but for a mirror: a family, a generation, can possess enterprise, possess art, possess technology, lacking nothing whatsoever, except for one thing—lacking the practice of opening their mouths to God. And when this one thing remains missing, trouble erupts in the very next verse.

### 1.3 Talents Are Gifts; Direction Is a Choice

In this section, we must speak a fair word on behalf of talent, lest anyone misunderstand.

Scripture never despises human talent. In Exodus, Bezalel was filled with the Spirit of God, given wisdom and understanding, capable of all kinds of artistic workmanship; even the engraving of gold, silver, and bronze was accounted as part of God's provision (cf. Ex. 35:30-35). The enterprise, music, and metallurgy of Cain's descendants were themselves expressions of the creative capacity given to man through being created in God's image. The problem has never been that man is too capable; the problem is that the capable man no longer turns back to acknowledge the God who bestowed those talents.

In his commentary on Genesis, when expounding on Cain's genealogy, Warren W. Wiersbe reminds readers of a striking contrast, the essence of which is: Cain's descendants continually advanced in culture and craftsmanship, yet continually regressed in worship; external achievements cannot mask a spiritual void. (Source: General teaching summarized from Warren W. Wiersbe's *Bible Commentary* on Genesis 4; paraphrased, not a direct quote)

Let us close this first section with one sentence: **Cain's descendants constructed everything, only leaving prayer entirely off their blueprint. The more our talents grow, the more urgently we must ask: In the midst of all this, is there still room for God?**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Song of Seventy-Sevenfold—Lamech and the Escalation of Vengeance (4:23-24)

### 2.1 The Second Song in the Bible Sings of Murder

In this passage about Lamech, a marital issue arises first: "Then Lamech took for himself two wives: the name of one was Adah, and the name of the second was Zillah." (Gen. 4:19)

This is the first time the Bible records polygamy. The order established in Genesis chapter 2 was still right there: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh." (Gen. 2:24) Two people, one flesh. When it came to Lamech, "one flesh" was breached first. When the Bible records this matter, there is not a single word of praise; it merely records it plainly—this is how many deviations make their entrance: quietly, first becoming a fact, and then becoming a trend.

Then Lamech opened his mouth and sang. The first song in the Bible was Adam's words when he welcomed Eve; the second is this very one. Listen to what he sings:

"For I have killed a man for wounding me,
Even a young man for hurting me." (Gen. 4:23)

To what degree others wounded him, the Bible does not state; taken literally, for a single wound or a slight hurt, his response was killing and harm. An excessive retaliation was turned into song lyrics by him, sung aloud right before his two wives. Brothers and sisters, when Cain killed, he hid in the field where no one could see; when Lamech killed, he stood at home and sang of it to his wives. Cain was still afraid afterward and begged for a mark; Lamech was no longer afraid—he treated it as a badge of honor.

The progression of sin is hidden within this contrast: the first generation does evil and still knows to hide; after a few generations doing the exact same thing, they begin to boast. The sense of shame is worn thin, generation after generation. If there is something in a family that the first generation did behind closed doors, but by the children's generation it is brought out as a joke to tell and a skill to boast about, that household needs to be on high alert—it is not that the children went bad first, but that shame was worn away first, generation after generation, at the dining table.

### 2.2 Sevenfold and Seventy-Sevenfold: The Mark Subverted into an Amulet

The final line of Lamech's song is the most crucial line of the entire section:

"If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold,
Then Lamech seventy-sevenfold." (Gen. 4:24)

These words are aimed directly at Genesis 4:15. God set a mark on Cain, saying that whoever kills Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him sevenfold. That was God's declaration of sovereignty: "Vengeance is Mine; man is not permitted to take the law into his own hands." That was meant to restrain retaliation.

Yet in Lamech's mouth, the very same words took on a completely different flavor. God spoke of sevenfold to restrain others from killing Cain; Lamech spoke of seventy-sevenfold to embolden himself in killing others. He took the mark of God's mercy and secretly swapped it into an amulet for his own violence. God's word was quoted by him without a single word changed, yet the usage was entirely inverted.

This tactic appears repeatedly in the Bible later on. When the Lord Jesus was tempted in the wilderness, the devil also quoted words from Psalm 91 (cf. Matt. 4:6). Quoting Scripture does not equate to obeying Scripture. Being able to memorize it, quote it, or preach it is nothing extraordinary; what matters is in which direction you apply this word: is it to restrain your hand, or to embolden your hand?

Proverbs says: "Fools mock at sin, but among the upright there is favor." (Prov. 14:9) Lamech's song turned sinning into a mockery and even set it to music—do not forget, his son was the father of all those who play the harp and flute. This family had the finest musical instruments, yet what they sang was a song of vengeance. When instruments meant for worship fall into a heart that does not worship, even their melodies will be corrupted.

### 2.3 The Lord Jesus Inverted the Seventy-Sevenfold

If this passage were to stop at Lamech, it would be far too heavy. In the New Testament, the Lord Jesus overturned this number with His own hands.

One day Peter came to Jesus and asked: "Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Up to seven times?" Jesus said to him, "I do not say to you, up to seven times, but up to seventy times seven." (Matt. 18:21-22)

The renderings of this verse in various English translations are worth comparing:
- KJV: "until seventy times seven."
- NIV: "not seven times, but seventy-seven times."
- NRSV: "not seven times, but, I tell you, seventy-seven times."

The NIV and NRSV translate it as seventy-seven times, directly corresponding to Lamech's seventy-sevenfold. The same number, two different lines. Lamech said: "If someone wounds me once, I will retaliate seventy-sevenfold." Jesus said: "If someone sins against you once, you forgive them seventy-seven times." Lamech multiplied vengeance by seventy-seven; Jesus multiplied forgiveness by seventy-seven. When Cain's line reached the seventh generation, what it produced was the all-time record for vengeance; the Son of God came, took that exact record upon Himself, and turned it into the baseline standard for forgiveness.

The words of Romans 12 fit right in here: "Beloved, do not avenge yourselves, but rather give place to wrath; for it is written, 'Vengeance is Mine, I will repay,' says the Lord." (Rom. 12:19) What God foreshadowed when He set the mark upon Cain, Paul fully articulated here. Vengeance belongs to the Lord; it is not in my hands, nor is it in my song.

Let us bring this home with some fire.

Brothers and sisters, Lamech is not seven generations away from us.

Who does not have a song of Lamech in their heart? "He treated me like that, why should I let him off easy? Every loss I suffered, I keep track of line by line; whenever I get the chance, I will pay it back twofold." This song needs no instrumental accompaniment; it can stay on single-track repeat in your heart for many years.

Yet listen clearly: every time you sing it, your heart hardens a little more; every year you sing it, the people in your home grow colder with you for another year. The most deceptive thing about retaliation is that it promises you an outlet for your anger, but in the end what it delivers is bondage—the other person has long forgotten it, while you remain tethered to the spot by that very song.

It must be today, not tomorrow. Hand over your seventy-sevenfold. The Lord Jesus has already taken this number upon Himself; when He was nailed to the cross and said, "Father, forgive them," He was settling this very ledger. For the person you refuse to forgive, He has already paid the bill; if you try to collect it again yourself, what you forfeit is your own peace.

To sum up the second part: **Lamech took God's words that restrain vengeance and sang them as a song to carry out vengeance. Jesus came, seized the seventy-sevenfold back from the hands of vengeance, and restored it to forgiveness. Today, change the lyrics of that song of yours.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The Appointed Son, and Those Who Began to Call—The Line of Seth (4:25-26)

### 3.1 "Appointed Another for Me": Eve's Second Declaration of Faith

The lens pans back to Adam's home.

"And Adam knew his wife again, and she bore a son and named him Seth, 'For God has appointed another seed for me instead of Abel, whom Cain killed.'" (Gen. 4:25)

The one speaking was still Eve. The Chinese Union Version brings her meaning directly into the text: God has appointed another son for me. For this mother, one son was dead, and the murderer was her other son. The first words out of her mouth were not a curse, but the recognition of God's hand: "appointed another"—it was God who appointed him.

Seth, Hebrew *Sheth* (שֵׁת), means to appoint, place, or grant. From his birth, this child's name carried a substitution: Abel's place was empty, but God did not let that place remain empty forever. In the Bible, this thread of substitution begins right here: the ram substituted for Isaac as a burnt offering, the Passover lamb substituted for the firstborn, and ultimately the Lord Jesus on the cross substituted for sinners. Seth is the first station of this thread within the family: loss is not the final word; God appointing another is.

The words of Psalm 30 fit Eve remarkably well: "For His anger is but for a moment, His favor is for life; weeping may endure for a night, but joy comes in the morning." (Ps. 30:5) This family wept through an entire night—not just one night, but many years. Yet they did not stop at weeping; in their grief, they still dared to bear children, dared to name them, and dared to acknowledge God's hand. Brothers and sisters, to continue believing in the midst of loss that God has another provision—this is not numbness; this is the sturdiest faith in Genesis chapter 4.

### 3.2 Enosh: A Child Named "Mortal Man"

"And as for Seth, to him also a son was born; and he named him Enosh." (Gen. 4:26)

Enosh, Hebrew *Enosh* (אֱנוֹשׁ), means man, specifically man in his fragile, mortal, and finite nature. When Psalm 103 says, "For He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust," it draws on this very foundation of human frailty. Naming him Enosh was equivalent to his parents writing a hard truth right onto the child's identity card: "Child, you are mortal, you are finite, and you cannot stand on your own."

When Cain named his son, he also named the city Enoch, thinking of establishing a name, passing down a name, and seeking immortality. When Seth named his son Enosh, his thought was to recognize what man truly is. One named toward loftiness; the other named toward reality. The wonder is that this very family who named toward reality went on to do something that benefited all of humanity.

### 3.3 "Then Men Began to Call on the Name of the Lord"

The final sentence of chapter 4 is the light of the entire chapter:

"Then men began to call on the name of the Lord." (Gen. 4:26)

"To call upon," in Hebrew *qara* (*qara*), means to call out, to proclaim; to call upon the name of the Lord means to cry out to Him, acknowledge Him, and rely on Him publicly and in worship. This phrase later became a standard expression for worship: when Abraham came to Bethel, he built an altar and called on the name of the Lord (cf. Gen. 12:8); the prophet Joel said that whoever calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved (cf. Joel 2:32); and Paul brought it into the New Testament era in Romans 10:13: "For 'whoever calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved.'"

Notice where this calling upon the Lord appears. It is not found in the line of Cain building cities; it is in the line of Seth. It was not recorded when man was at the peak of his capability; it was recorded only after a family clearly recognized that they were mortal, finite, and unable to replace the dead. When does a person begin to call upon the Lord? When they admit that the city they built cannot save them.

Proverbs 18:10 declares the result of this calling: "The name of the Lord is a strong tower; the righteous run to it and are safe." Cain built a city seeking security, but the descendants of Seth ran into a Name to find security. A city is piled up by man, brick by brick; the Name is established by God Himself. Inside the city built by man, Lamech sang a song of vengeance; but into God’s Name, when man runs into it, he finds safety. As the two lines reach the end of the chapter, the contrast between them is already unmistakable.

Hebrews 11:10 says that Abraham "waited for the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God." Tracing down through Seth's line—Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, David, all the way to the Lord Jesus—the genealogy in Luke 3 ultimately traces back through Seth to Adam (cf. Luke 3:38). The names of city builders gradually decay, but the line of those who call upon the Lord leads straight to the Savior.

To conclude this third section: **Cain’s line proved over seven generations that man can build everything except his own security. Seth’s line needed only one verse to show that the moment man opens his mouth to call upon the name of the Lord, the history of salvation begins moving again.**

## (52:00 - 60:00) Applications for Six Groups of People · Call to Response · Closing Prayer

### Application 1: For Seekers Who Have Not Yet Believed in the Lord

My friend, between these two lines today, you are inevitably walking on one of them.

One line is building by your own strength: accumulating qualifications, accumulating credentials, accumulating a sense of security—accumulating until your hands hold everything, while your heart remains adrift. The other line is opening your mouth to call upon the Lord: first admitting that you are finite and mortal, and then running into the Name of God.

Calling upon the Lord is not superstition; it is turning around. If you are willing to give it a try today, you don't need to understand all the doctrines first. Just begin with a simple prayer: "God, I admit that I cannot find security on my own, and I want to know You." Seth's family switched lines starting from a single honest sentence like that.

### Application 2: For Marriages and Families—Spouses, Parents, and Children

Look first at Lamech's home. The song of vengeance was sung at home, and his wives were the very first audience. Words spoken around the family dinner table become the lyrics in a child's heart. How you talk at home about those who offend you is how your children learn to treat those who offend them.

Now look at Seth's home. In their deepest grief, what they did was bear children, give names, acknowledge God’s hand, and open their mouths to call upon the Lord. Establishing a habit of calling upon the Lord in your home is far more crucial than leaving a city to your children: pray together whenever something happens, give thanks together before going to sleep, and extend forgiveness in front of your children when you have been wronged. Your children may forget what you have built, but they will never forget whom you called upon in times of trouble.

### Application 3: For Those in the Workplace and Business

Cain’s descendants are the most familiar kind of people in the workplace: skilled in enterprise, appreciative of the arts, proficient in technology, with an impressive track record. This lesson is not telling you to abandon your skills; it is calling you to inspect the blueprints: in your planning, have you left room for God?

Here is a practical exercise: every week before work begins, spend ten minutes calling upon the Lord before you open your planner. Once the order is reversed, the flavor of everything changes—your talents remain the same, but your anxiety has already been surrendered. The vanity spoken of in Psalm 127 does not mean your hard work is useless; it means that hard work apart from God ultimately feeds a deeper sense of wandering.

### Application 4: For New Believers

To our new believers, learn the starting point of prayer from Enosh: prayer does not begin with "I am very spiritual"; it begins with admitting "I am fragile, finite, and in need of God."

You do not need to prepare eloquent words. Calling upon the name of the Lord means placing His Name, His character, and His promises in front of your difficulties: "Lord, You are faithful, You watch over me, You forgive sins—I run into Your Name." Open your mouth to call upon Him once every morning, and give thanks once every evening. This is how this line has been walked, generation after generation.

### Application 5: For Leaders, Parents, and Those Who Serve

Leaders must choose a side between these two lines, and help others choose as well.

Lamech-style leadership relies on amplifying volume, escalating retaliation, and making people afraid. He turned Cain's mark into a threat. Seth-style leadership relies on God appointing another and on calling upon the Lord: when someone stumbles, you help them see that God can appoint another; when the team hits a deadlock, you lead everyone to open their mouths and call upon the Lord, rather than holding meetings to point fingers at one another.

A special reminder to parents: your children’s names, how you address each other at home, and the songs at the dinner table are all laying the tracks for the next generation. Whether you sing Lamech's song or Seth's prayer at home today will be made plain after seven generations—in fact, you don’t need seven generations; seven years will make it plain.

### Application 6: For the Whole Church

Where the church stands is crystal clear: we belong to the line of Seth; we are the people who call on the name of the Lord. In Acts and 1 Corinthians, one of the earliest designations for the church was those who call on the name of the Lord (cf. 1 Cor. 1:2).

Yet we must beware of a danger: the church can also slide unconsciously into Cain's line—the programs are there, the equipment is there, the events are there, the roster is long, but the calling upon the Lord is gone. The history of revival is always written in reverse: it does not begin with resources and then lead to prayer; it begins when a group of people reopen their mouths to pray, and only then do heaven’s resources begin to flow again. Let our generation not merely build the church bigger, but restore our calling upon the Lord more deeply.

### Igniting Moment

*(Pause, slowing down the pace)*

Brothers and sisters, as Genesis 4 comes to a close, two structures stand before humanity.

One is Cain’s city: high walls, a great name, and the seventy-sevenfold song echoing within. The other is invisible, heard only as a sound: a family gathered together, calling upon the name of the Lord.

Thousands of years have passed. Where is Cain’s city? It has long vanished. And what about the voice of calling upon the Lord? We who sit here today are the echo of that very voice.

*(Pause, speaking with added weight)*

Therefore, I ask you today: In your life, which one are you actually building?

What are you leaving behind for your children—only a city, or calling upon the Lord as well? When you are wounded, what do you sing—the song of Lamech, or the prayer of Seth's household? Today, not tomorrow, switch the line. Cities will fall, and names will rot, but those who call on the name of the Lord will endure with His Name forever.

### Call to Response

I want to make two calls:

**First call**: If you carry a song of Lamech in your heart, keeping record of someone's wrong against you and thinking about paying them back double one day—if today you are willing to surrender that seventy-sevenfold vengeance into the Lord’s hands and learn to forgive seventy-seven times, please stand up, and we will pray for you together. Laying down revenge does not let the other person off easy; it sets your own heart free.

**Second call**: If you have been busy for many years, building so many things, but it has been a long time since you earnestly opened your mouth to call upon God; or if you are a friend who has not yet believed in the Lord, and you are willing to begin running into the Lord's Name and calling upon Him today—please raise your hand. I want to pray for you, so that our generation may once again become a people who call on the name of the Lord.

*(Wait for response, pray for those standing and raising their hands)*

### Concluding Prayer of Response

Let us bow our heads together in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. Genesis 4 did not end with murder and vengeance; it ended with calling upon Your Name. While man wandered far away on the road eastward, You appointed another son in a grieving home, causing the sound of calling upon Your Name to resound once again on the earth.

Lord, we confess that we, too, often live on the line of Cain: busy building cities, busy making names for ourselves, our hands becoming more and more capable while our prayers grow fewer and fewer; when wounded, our hearts sing the song of Lamech, taking Your words of mercy and turning them into an excuse for our own unforgiving spirit. Today, we lay both before You: we ask You to tear down that song of retaliation in our hearts, and awaken within us that prayer that has long been silent.

Teach us to be people of Seth’s line: daring to believe in our loss that You have prepared another; admitting in our limitation that we are mortal and fragile; and then running into Your Name to find security. Let our homes first become homes of calling upon the Lord, and let our church become a church of calling upon the Lord.

We also pray for the next generation: may what they remember be not what we built, but whom we called upon in times of trouble. May the sound of calling upon the name of the Lord be passed down from our generation to the next, until the day of the Lord's return.

In the Name of the One upon whom we call, our Lord Jesus Christ, we pray. Amen."

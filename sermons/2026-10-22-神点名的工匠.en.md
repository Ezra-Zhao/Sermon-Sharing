# The Craftsman Whom God Called by Name

**Preacher:** Composed by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries

**Scripture:** Exodus 31:1-18

## Synopsis

The pattern for the tabernacle has been given, yet God does not let the work begin at once. First He calls craftsmen by name, then He reaffirms the Sabbath in the very middle of the project, and only at the end does He place the tablets written by His own finger into the hand of Moses. This chapter shows us that God cares for the workers more than for the project itself: He equips ordinary hands by His Spirit and marks out His covenant people by rest, so that all building rests on His sovereignty and glory.

## Sermon

### I. After the Pattern, First the Person — Introduction (00:00-08:00)

Brothers and sisters, when we read Exodus chapters 25 to 30, we can easily feel overwhelmed by the long succession of measurements, materials, and patterns. How long and how wide the ark must be, how many talents of pure gold the lampstand will require, how the priests' holy garments are to be woven with blue, purple, scarlet thread and fine linen — the ark, the lampstand, the altar of incense, the laver, the anointing oil, one item after another. Then, in chapter 31, the pace of the text suddenly slows. God no longer speaks about objects. He speaks about a person: "See, I have called by name Bezaleel the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah" (31:2, KJV).

This turn deserves our attention. In God's order, the blueprint matters, but so does the worker — and in the arrangement of the narrative, the calling of the worker is placed before the work formally begins. Exodus 25-30 records the instructions God gave Moses on the mountain; Exodus 35-40 records the execution of those instructions below the mountain; chapter 31 stands between instruction and execution, like a threshold. It tells us that God's will does not become reality automatically. He wills to carry out His word in the midst of the camp through specific people, with specific hands, in a specific way. And these people did not step forward and volunteer, saying, "I can do it." God saw them first, knew them first, and called them by name first.

Those of us who serve the Lord and take part in building the church today often ask first: How shall the work be done? Where are the resources? Is there enough time? The text brings us back to an earlier question: Who called the worker? By what does he work? And where are the limits of his work? Chapter 31 answers these three questions in three movements: verses 1-5 tell of Bezalel's calling and equipment, verses 6-11 tell of Oholiab and the company of craftsmen working together, verses 12-17 tell of the sign of the Sabbath, and verse 18 tells of the delivery of the two tablets. These are not unrelated appendices. They lock together: the called person works within a community, the working community is bounded by rest, and all of it must finally bow before the words God wrote with His own hand.

Let me also explain how we shall read this chapter. A sermon must not read modern ideas back into an ancient text. We must first honestly observe what the text says in its own setting — this is the level of fact and text. Then we observe how it echoes what comes before and after in the structure of the book and of the whole Bible — this is the level of structural observation. Only then do we speak of how the New Testament takes it up and how it applies to the church today — this is the level of homiletical use and New Testament reading. When these three levels are kept distinct, our devotion has roots and our application does not become forced. This chapter especially requires such care: it speaks of being filled with the Spirit, of gifts of wisdom, of the Sabbath, and also of the punishment for violating it. Each of these can easily be seized upon in a single sentence and developed far beyond what the text says. We would rather move slowly and read it verse by verse.

The tabernacle project itself reminds us of something else. Israel had only just left Egypt. In Egypt they were slaves who made bricks for Pharaoh and built store cities for another man; their skills had been trained under the whip. Now the same hands are to build a dwelling place for God, to do fine work in gold, precious stones, and engraving. The object of the work has changed, and so its meaning has changed entirely. The person is the same person, the hands are the same hands, but when God calls him, equips him, and places him within the covenant community, his work is transformed from forced labour into called service. This is a theme that runs through Exodus: God brings people out not only so that they leave the house of bondage, but so that they enter a new order of worship and service. Chapter 31 shows what that new order looks like in the concrete matter of work.

So today let us not read this chapter as an inspiring story in praise of craftsmen, nor as an ancient engineering file that has nothing to do with us. Let us read it as God's word to everyone who works: He calls people by name, He equips them by His Spirit, He appoints fellow workers, He establishes rest, and He writes His words with His own hand. May we leave this chapter not merely knowing a few more names, but rethinking the work in our own hands — at our jobs, in our families, in the church — under whose name it is done, by whose power, and to whom we shall give account.

### II. Called by Name, Filled with the Spirit of God — The Calling of Bezalel (08:00-24:00)

Let us read verses 1-5 closely. "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, See, I have called by name Bezaleel the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah: And I have filled him with the spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship, To devise cunning works, to work in gold, and in silver, and in brass, And in cutting of stones, to set them, and in carving of timber, to work in all manner of workmanship" (31:1-5, KJV).

The opening word, "See," is often in Hebrew narrative a marker that draws attention, as if God were pointing to a person and saying, "Mark this man." Bezalel's identity is given in detail: of the tribe of Judah, grandson of Hur, son of Uri. Judah is the tribe from which kings would later come, and now a craftsman is raised up from this tribe first — a fact itself worth pondering. The name Hur has already appeared in chapter 17: when Amalek came to fight, Hur, together with Aaron, held up the hands of Moses until the going down of the sun (17:10-12). Bezalel of chapter 31 comes from this very family that shared in intercession. Of course, the text does not say plainly that family heritage is the reason for his calling; we can only note it structurally and must not preach it as a settled conclusion. What is certain is that God's calling is never abstract. He calls a person who has a tribe, a genealogy, and a name.

"I have called by name." Kings in the ancient Near East summoned craftsmen by name for royal and temple work; that in itself was not unusual, and the archives contain many records of skilled artisans called up by kings. But here it is not Pharaoh who names him, nor Moses — it is the LORD Himself. In Israel's faith, to be known by God by name is a profound grace: God says to Moses, "I know thee by name" (33:12, 17), and Isaiah later says that God calls Jacob by name (Isa. 43:1). Now this grace of being named falls upon a worker in gold and wood. It reminds us that God's calling is not limited to offices that stand before the congregation; it also reaches those who work with their hands behind the scenes.

The name Bezalel is traditionally explained as "in the shadow of God" or "under the protection of God," composed of the preposition be ("in / under"), tsel ("shadow, shade"), and El ("God"). This explanation has been widely handed down and is often cited in sermons and commentaries. But honesty requires us to add: scholars still discuss the formation and precise meaning of the name, some divide the roots differently, and the ancient versions leave no explicit interpretation. "In the shadow of God" may therefore be introduced as the traditional explanation — and it does fit his calling beautifully — but it should not be made the foundation of doctrine as if it were beyond dispute. The pulpit respects tradition, and it also respects the places where scholarship remains undecided. The two are not in conflict.

Then comes the most striking sentence in the chapter: "I have filled him with the spirit of God." In the Old Testament, being filled with the Spirit of God is most often connected with prophets prophesying, judges strengthened for battle, and kings anointed to rule — as when Pharaoh discerned that the Spirit of God was in Joseph (Gen. 41:38), or Joshua was full of the spirit of wisdom because Moses had laid his hands on him (Deut. 34:9). But in Exodus 31, being filled with the Spirit is for the first time connected so explicitly with manual skill — not preaching, not warfare, not government, but casting, engraving, inlaying, and carpentry. This is an observation at the factual level, and it must be labelled as such: the text itself here makes the effect of being filled with the Spirit concrete as wisdom and ability for work, not as some exciting religious experience. If we skip this level and apply the verse directly to any display of talent today, we have not first listened to what the text itself is saying.

Together with "the Spirit" appear three related words: wisdom, understanding, and knowledge. Wisdom enables a person to know how a thing ought to be done, with proportion and judgment; understanding enables him to discern and to grasp the meaning behind the pattern; knowledge includes skilled mastery and practical experience. These three words often appear together in Proverbs, and they describe what God Himself used in creating the world: "The LORD by wisdom hath founded the earth; by understanding hath he established the heavens. By his knowledge the depths are broken up, and the clouds drop down the dew" (Prov. 3:19-20). Commentators have noted this echo, and it is not accidental: the wisdom that builds the tabernacle and the wisdom that built heaven and earth come from the same source, because the tabernacle is precisely the place God appointed to manifest His presence on earth. In other words, what the craftsman's hammer and chisel carry is not human cleverness, but a sharing in the wisdom of the Creator Himself.

Verses 4 and 5 list the scope of his work: he is "to devise cunning works" — the original carries the sense of designing and planning, which shows that Bezalel was not merely a workman who followed drawings; he also had the capacity to conceive and plan. His materials include gold, silver, and brass; his crafts include cutting stones, setting them, and carving timber; and the phrase "to work in all manner of workmanship" appears at the end of both verse 4 and verse 5, like a refrain. The setting of stones relates directly to the breastplate and ephod of the priest; the carpentry relates to the ark, the table, and the altar of incense, all made of shittim wood. God's equipment is concrete. It comes down to materials and processes, with nothing vague about it. When we speak of gifts today, we often speak very abstractly, as if a gift were only a feeling. Bezalel's example tells us that the ability given by the Spirit of God must finally be tested in concrete results — whether the pattern is right, the measurements true, and the workmanship fine. These too are spiritual matters.

One balance must be noted here: being filled with the Spirit did not free Bezalel from the pattern to invent his own. The pattern given in chapters 25-30 remained the norm the craftsmen had to follow, and Moses later inspected their work and found that "they had done it as the LORD had commanded" (39:43). The equipment of the Spirit and the norm of the Word never exclude each other in God's building. The more a person is equipped by God, the more he respects what God has already said. This principle matters equally for the church today: we welcome the work of the Holy Spirit, and we insist that all work return to the test of Scripture. If either is missing, the building will be distorted.

Finally, observe the order of Bezalel's calling: first God calls, then God gives the Spirit, and only then does the man set his hand to the work. No one can appoint himself God's craftsman, and no one can manufacture gifts in the flesh. But conversely, the called person cannot use "waiting for inspiration" as an excuse to delay beginning — the Spirit is given so that God's work may be done. Many of us hold an ordinary-looking job in our hands: writing, accounting, repairing cars, caring for children, programming, cleaning rooms. Bezalel's story gives us at least one solid reminder: ordinary does not mean unspiritual, and manual work does not mean second-class. When God calls a person by name and equips him by His Spirit, the work in his hands can become part of worship.

### III. Not Working Alone — Oholiab and the Company of Craftsmen (24:00-42:00)

Verse 6 begins with God saying: "And I, behold, I have given with him Aholiab, the son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan" (31:6, KJV). God called Bezalel, but He did not make him carry the whole tabernacle alone. Oholiab's name is also recorded; his tribe and his father's name are also written into Scripture — Dan was not a prominent tribe among the twelve, which shows all the more that God does not assign fellow workers by human rank. The word "given / appointed" shows that fellow workers are not a team Bezalel recruited for his own convenience; they are God's own arrangement. This order is deeply instructive for service today: we often assemble our own preferred people first and then ask God to bless them; in the text, God appoints the people first, and then the people work together.

Oholiab appears again later: chapter 38 says he was "an engraver, and a cunning workman, and an embroiderer in blue, and in purple, and in scarlet, and fine linen" (38:23, KJV). That is, he was a master of weaving and embroidery, exactly what the tabernacle's extensive textile work required. The tabernacle needed metalwork and woodwork, but also weaving and embroidery; it needed a man to design the whole, and men and women to weave every thread into place. God's project is never accomplished by one gift doing everything; it is accomplished by many gifts each in its place. Bezalel is the chief craftsman, but he is not the only craftsman. Oholiab willingly takes the second place, but he is by no means dispensable. Scripture remembers both their names, and it remembers them fairly.

The second half of verse 6 widens the view one step further: "and in the hearts of all that are wise hearted I have put wisdom, that they may make all that I have commanded thee" (31:6, KJV). Here is a fact often overlooked: besides the two leading craftsmen, there is a company of "wise hearted" people, and God puts wisdom in them all. Here the gift shows its corporate character — it is not that one man is filled with the Spirit while everyone else watches; wisdom is distributed to a community, so that the whole workforce is equipped. Chapter 28, on Aaron's garments, likewise says that Moses shall speak to "all that are wise hearted, whom I have filled with the spirit of wisdom," that they may make Aaron's garments (28:3). Chapter 35 records that craftsmen and craftswomen alike were moved to work, and that every wise-hearted woman spun with her hands (35:25-26). Read together, these passages show the tabernacle as a project of the whole people before God: some offered materials, some offered skills, some offered time, and God wove all these scattered offerings into His dwelling place.

This picture corrects how we view church life today. We habitually divide service into front-stage and back-stage, important and unimportant, as if only what happens on the platform counts as ministry, while preparing the room, arranging chairs, repairing equipment, and keeping accounts do not. The corporate picture of chapter 31 does not accept that division. In God's eyes, setting a stone straight, weaving the curtain tightly, and casting the bronze base firmly are all work He Himself gave wisdom for men and women to do. Of course, corporateness does not mean the abolition of leadership: Bezalel remains the one specially named, and the project still needs someone to coordinate and take responsibility. The biblical vision of community never cancels order; it makes order serve under God's arrangement — the leader does not exalt himself, the helper does not belittle himself, and each acknowledges that his portion was received from God.

Verses 7-11 contain a list of objects: "The tabernacle of the congregation, and the ark of the testimony, and the mercy seat that is thereupon, and all the furniture of the tabernacle, And the table and his furniture, and the pure candlestick with all his furniture, and the altar of incense, And the altar of burnt offering with all his furniture, and the laver and his foot," together with the finely wrought garments and the holy garments for the priests, "all that I have commanded"; in short, they shall do according to all that God has commanded (31:7-11, KJV, summarized). Readers familiar with Exodus will immediately hear the echo: this list is almost a table of contents for chapters 25-30 — the ark in chapter 25, the table and lampstand following close behind, the altar of incense, the altar of burnt offering, the laver, the anointing oil, and the holy garments distributed through the later chapters. This is an observation at the factual and structural level: chapter 31 gathers the commands of the preceding five or six chapters into the single sentence, "they shall do accordingly," and builds a bridge to the actual construction from chapter 35 onward. The function of the list is to fasten God's word and human hands together again: nothing the craftsmen make is of their own invention, and nothing God has commanded may be discounted.

One detail in the list deserves a further word: the garments are called finely wrought garments of ministry, made for Aaron and his sons to minister in the priest's office. The craftsmen's needlework will finally be worn by priests as they serve God; and however honourable the priestly office is, it needs craftsmen behind the scenes preparing it stitch by stitch. The chain of service is linked ring to ring in just this way: no link can exist independently, and no link may despise another. In the church today, the preacher needs those who prepare the room, the worship leader needs those who manage sound and song sheets, and those who visit need others praying and handling administration behind them. Chapter 31 writes every link of this chain into the word of God, which is to say: God remembers the people at every link.

This section ends without any celebration of the craftsmen's enthusiasm and without recording any grand vows from them — only a calm summary: all that God has commanded, they shall do. Sometimes the deepest obedience has no grand words at all. God's word has been spoken clearly; human hands do it accordingly; and the project rises, piece by piece, in the middle of the camp. This is also the duty of all our service: not to replace command with creativity, nor to replace obedience with busyness, but honestly to do our own portion well before the word God has already spoken.

### IV. Rest in the Middle of the Project — The Sign of the Sabbath (42:00-56:00)

Just when the reader expects the text to move straight into construction, verse 12 inserts what looks like a digression: the LORD speaks to Moses again, about the Sabbath. The position of this passage is itself part of the message — the reaffirmation of the Sabbath is placed in the very middle of the tabernacle project, with the calling of the craftsmen on one side and the delivery of the tablets on the other. It is as if a sign were erected at the busiest construction site: even sacred work must not swallow up rest; even urgent schedules must not cross the boundary God has drawn for His people. This is a structural observation: the Sabbath is not an incidental reminder attached to the project; it is the theological brake on the project.

Verse 13 says: "Speak thou also unto the children of Israel, saying, Verily my sabbaths ye shall keep: for it is a sign between me and you throughout your generations; that ye may know that I am the LORD that doth sanctify you" (31:13, KJV). The key word here is "sign." In the covenant context, a sign is the visible mark of a covenant relationship: the rainbow is the sign of God's covenant with Noah (Gen. 9:12-17), circumcision is the sign of God's covenant with Abraham (Gen. 17:11), and now the Sabbath is named as the sign between God and Israel throughout their generations. Covenants in the ancient Near East likewise valued marks and memorials, by which both parties declared their belonging and obligations; the biblical use has its own character: the sign of the Sabbath is not an object worn on the body, but a rhythm of time — on the seventh of every seven days, the people stop, and by resting they declare that this people belongs to the God who created heaven and earth and who led them out of Egypt. To keep the Sabbath is to bear witness with the calendar.

Verses 14 and 15 are very severe, and we shall read them honestly, neither softening nor exaggerating them: "Ye shall keep the sabbath therefore; for it is holy unto you: every one that defileth it shall surely be put to death: for whosoever doeth any work therein, that soul shall be cut off from among his people. Six days may work be done; but in the seventh is the sabbath of rest, holy to the LORD: whosoever doeth any work in the sabbath day, he shall surely be put to death" (31:14-15, KJV). Death and "being cut off" stand side by side here. "Being cut off" is a grave expression in the Law, meaning that a person is removed from the covenant community; scholars have long discussed its precise mode of execution. Standing alongside the death penalty, it shows that in the judicial order of the Old Covenant, violating the Sabbath was no small matter, because what was violated was not merely a rule about hours, but the public tearing of the sign of the covenant. Chapter 35 repeats the Sabbath command when work resumes, forbidding even the kindling of fire (35:2-3), and Numbers later records the outcome for a man who gathered sticks on the Sabbath (Num. 15:32-36). It is right that we read these texts with heavy hearts. But honesty in the pulpit also requires us to state the difference of setting: these penalties belong to the judicial order of the covenant God made with Israel at Sinai, and they cannot be transferred directly as a basis for punishment in the church or society today. How the New Testament church receives the meaning of the Sabbath must be handled according to the New Testament's own teaching, without confusion and without skipping steps. To speak lightly of the Old Testament's severity as "merely symbolic," or to apply Old Testament penalties directly to people today, are both dishonest to the text.

What, then, is the ground of the Sabbath? Verse 17 gives the answer: "for in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, and on the seventh day he rested, and was refreshed" — and therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day and hallowed it (cf. 31:17; 20:11, KJV). This corresponds exactly to the reason given for the Sabbath commandment in the Ten Commandments (20:11). Rest is not a benefit invented because human beings got tired; it is a rhythm inscribed in the order of creation: God Himself rested on the seventh day, and when human beings keep the Sabbath, they imitate their Creator and confess that the world is not kept turning by their own ceaseless labour. Verse 17 contains a moving expression: on the seventh day God "rested, and was refreshed" — the original depicts God as, so to speak, catching His breath and being refreshed. When the craftsmen heard such words, they should have understood: the God who commands them to work six days also commands them to lay down their tools on the seventh. Laying them down is not laziness; it is worship. Stopping work is not delaying the project; it is precisely the deepest meaning of the project — for if the tabernacle were built by builders crushed into slaves who know no rest, it would be no different from the brick kilns of Egypt.

This pierces us today. We live in an age that glorifies busyness, and even service to the Lord easily becomes an endless rush: activity upon activity, messages in the group day and night, exhaustion worn like a medal of faithfulness. Chapter 31 stops us in the tightest part of the project: God does not want His people to prove their zeal by burning themselves out. The Sabbath is a confession of God's sovereignty — on the seventh day I stop, and the world is still His world, and the project is finally His project. At the level of homiletical use, we may say plainly that the principle of the Sabbath still speaks to the church today: set aside whole blocks of time to stop work, to worship, to be with family, and to let body and mind be restored. As for the concrete form of observance for New Testament believers, churches through the ages have arranged it according to their own understanding of Scripture, and the pulpit should respect those differences and not preach disputes about form as disputes about faith. The principle is firm; the application is living; both require proportion.

Note also the wording of verse 16: "Wherefore the children of Israel shall keep the sabbath, to observe the sabbath throughout their generations, for a perpetual covenant" (31:16, KJV). Here the Sabbath is itself called a "covenant," which shows its weight within the Sinai covenant. Week after week it reminded Israel of two things: they are creatures, who must live by their Creator's rhythm; and they are redeemed, for when the Sabbath command is restated in Deuteronomy 5 from the angle of the exodus, the memory of redemption is placed within the reason for rest. Creation and redemption are the twin foundations of the Sabbath. A person who truly understands that he is both created and redeemed will have hands that can both work diligently and let go in peace.

### V. The Stone Tablets Written by the Finger of God — Conclusion (58:00-60:00)

Verse 18 has only one sentence, yet it carries the weight of the whole chapter: "And he gave unto Moses, when he had made an end of communing with him upon mount Sinai, two tables of testimony, tables of stone, written with the finger of God" (31:18, KJV). God's speaking is finished, and what is delivered is not a sheet of paper, but two tablets engraved in stone — chapter 24 already mentioned that God would give the tablets (24:12); here they are formally placed in Moses' hand. Stone signifies the solemnity and permanence of the Law; God's own writing signifies that its source is not Moses' compilation, but God's own testimony. As for the anthropomorphic expression "written with the finger of God," it will echo again after the incident of the golden calf in chapter 32 and at the remaking of the tablets in chapter 34 (32:15-16, 19; 34:1, 28): the tablets in Moses' hands as he comes down the mountain will stand in glaring contrast to the shouting of the people below — but that is the story of the next chapter. Here we offer only a structural pointer and do not steal the content of what follows. The congregation need only remember the picture at this moment: on the mountain, God solemnly hands over His word; below the mountain, the craftsmen have been called and the work is about to begin. Word and work, Moses and craftsmen, tablets and tabernacle meet in this one verse.

Looking back over the whole chapter, the craftsman whom God called by name leaves us three sentences to take home. First, God knows the worker by name — your name, your skill, and that portion of work of yours that seems unseen by anyone are not anonymous before God. Second, God equips people for work by His Spirit — talent is to be offered in the light of the Holy Spirit and used under the norm of His word; gift and obedience never part company. Third, God establishes rest in the middle of work — the most spiritual worker is also the one who best knows how to stop, because he knows the Master of the project is not himself.

At the level of homiletical use and New Testament reading — and this must be stated clearly: this is a New Testament reading, not the direct meaning of chapter 31 itself — the apostle Paul teaches that the Spirit distributes gifts to each member of the church as He wills (1 Cor. 12:4-11), and Peter exhorts everyone to minister to one another according to the gift each has received (1 Pet. 4:10). At Pentecost the Holy Spirit descended, so that this equipment is no longer limited to individual craftsmen but comes upon all God's children. When we read these New Testament texts and look back at Bezalel, gratitude rises: in the Old Testament, for one craftsman to be filled with the Spirit was already a rare grace; in the New Testament, through Christ, this grace comes richly upon the church. But gratitude does not change one fact: the purpose of the gifts has never changed. It is still building — the building of the tabernacle and the building of the church are both for the presence and glory of God. May God also call us by name, equip our hands, and teach us to work diligently for six days and rest on the seventh, until His dwelling is lifted high among us. Amen.

[8] 'ot אוֹת (H226): sign, mark, token, denoting the visible mark of a covenant (Gen. 9:12-17, the rainbow; Gen. 17:11, circumcision; Exod. 31:13, 17, the Sabbath). See BDB, s.v. אוֹת; HALOT, s.v. אות.

[9] shabbat שַׁבָּת (H7676): Sabbath, from the verb shabat (שָׁבַת, H7673, to cease, to rest). In Exod. 31:13-17 the Sabbath is called a "sabbath of solemn rest" and a "perpetual covenant." See BDB, s.v. שַׁבָּת; HALOT, s.v. שבת.

[10] "Cut off": the karet expression in Exod. 31:14 denotes being cut off from among the people, a grave covenant penalty in the Mosaic Law, whose precise historical mode of execution scholars discuss differently; this sermon simply places death and cutting off side by side as the text does, without excessive elaboration. See Durham, *Exodus*, WBC, on 31:14-15.

[11] The creation ground of the Sabbath: Exod. 31:17, "in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, and on the seventh day he rested," echoes Exod. 20:11, with its source in Gen. 2:2-3. The closing phrase of 31:17, "rested, and was refreshed," depicts God's refreshment and breathing freely, in anthropomorphic language.

[12] 'etzba' Elohim אֶצְבַּע אֱלֹהִים (H676): the finger of God. 'etzba' (אֶצְבַּע) denotes a finger; "tables of stone, written with the finger of God" occurs in Exod. 31:18, with similar expressions in Exod. 8:19; Deut. 9:10; Ps. 8:3; Luke 11:20. See BDB, s.v. אֶצְבַּע; HALOT, s.v. אצבע.

[13] The two tablets: the "tables of testimony" (luchot ha'edut) of Exod. 31:18 form a narrative line with the promise in Exod. 24:12, the breaking in chapter 32, and the remaking in chapter 34; this sermon offers only a structural pointer, to be treated in detail when the golden calf incident is preached.

[14] Note on the level of the New Testament comparison: the comparison between the Spirit upon the craftsmen and Pentecost (Acts 2) and the gifts of the body (1 Cor. 12; 1 Pet. 4:10) belongs to the level of homiletical use / New Testament reading, not to the direct meaning of Exodus 31, and must be labelled as such when preached, not read back into the original sense.

## Devotional Excerpt

Before God named the materials for the tabernacle, He named a person. Bezalel was called by name and filled with the Spirit of God, so that his work in gold, wood, and stone became holy work. We often assume that only pulpits, prayers, and missions are spiritual, and forget that God also remembers the hands that make things. The words you write at work, the things you repair, the patience you show in caring for your family — when they are done for God, they are not small things. God also placed Oholiab beside Bezalel and gave wisdom to many others, reminding us that service is never a solo performance. Most precious of all, the Sabbath stands in the middle of the project: God does not want us so busy that we forget Him. Work with all your heart for six days; on the seventh, rest with peace, confessing that God is the Master of the work. When you feel that your post is ordinary and unseen, remember: Bezalel began as only a craftsman in the camp, yet God saw him first. Bring your difficulties, your weariness, and your unfinished work into prayer, ask God for wisdom and strength sufficient for today, and by His help finish faithfully this one small thing at hand — this itself is worship. Today, hand your work back to the Lord who called you by name. Ask His Spirit to fill your mind and your hands, and to give you faith to lay down your tools and rest in Him.

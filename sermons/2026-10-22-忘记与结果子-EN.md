## Summary (about 100 words)

The cupbearer forgot Joseph for two full years. When the two years were full, Pharaoh dreamed, no one could interpret, and only then was Joseph remembered. He shaved, changed his clothes, and stood before Pharaoh: first he gave the glory to God, then he interpreted the dream and offered a plan, and he was made ruler over all the land and fathered Manasseh and Ephraim. The two names are his theology: God made him forget his sorrow, and made him fruitful in the land of his suffering.

---

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The end of the previous chapter is a sentence that chills the heart: the cupbearer returned to the palace, but forgot Joseph in prison. Joseph had interpreted his dream and asked only one thing: when it goes well with you, remember me, speak of me, and bring me out of this prison. The cupbearer promised gladly, then turned around and forgot. Today's chapter opens by carefully marking the scale of time: "at the end of two full years, that Pharaoh dreamed." Two years, more than seven hundred days. Joseph counted them one by one in prison; the cupbearer lived them one by one in the palace. No news, no reply, no one mentioned his name, as if such a person had never existed in this world.

Brothers and sisters, being forgotten is one of the hardest tastes in life to bear. It is not being scolded, not being beaten; it is being treated as if you do not exist. The person you helped prospers and does not remember you; the letter you wait for never comes; the door you pray for does not open even a crack in two years. Joseph lived inside exactly such two years. Today we will see how those two years ended and how God turned them into the highest point of his life; even more importantly, we will see the names Joseph gave his two children after the turning — those two names tell us how he looked back on his own suffering.

Let me first state clearly how we will read today: on the surface this chapter tells the story of a prisoner whose fortune turned; underneath, it tells how God treats time. People see two years as a blank; God sees two years as preparation. People see prison as an end; God sees prison as a way station. Today we will not turn Joseph into a lucky man in an inspirational story, nor Pharaoh's dreams into a puzzle game. We will walk honestly with the text and watch how God's hand worked in the place where people forgot, and how, in one man's household, through the names of two children, He left the deepest comfort for everyone in later generations who would wait.

Let us first read several key passages:

"And it came to pass at the end of two full years, that Pharaoh dreamed: and, behold, he stood by the river. And, behold, there came up out of the river seven well favoured kine and fatfleshed; and they fed in a meadow. And, behold, seven other kine came up after them out of the river, ill favoured and leanfleshed; and stood by the other kine upon the brink of the river. And the ill favoured and leanfleshed kine did eat up the seven well favoured and fat kine." (Genesis 41:1-4, excerpt, KJV)

"Thou shalt be over my house, and according unto thy word shall all my people be ruled: only in the throne will I be greater than thou." (Genesis 41:40, excerpt, KJV)

"And Joseph called the name of the firstborn Manasseh: For God, said he, hath made me forget all my toil, and all my father's house. And the name of the second called he Ephraim: For God hath caused me to be fruitful in the land of my affliction." (Genesis 41:51-52, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, many among us are living through those two years right now — waiting so long that our hearts grow anxious, feeling forgotten by the world and almost forgotten by You. Through Joseph's story today, tell us: people forget, but You do not forget; two late years in Your hand are not wasted. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Two Full Years — Pharaoh's Dreams and Human Forgetting (41:1-13)

First, look at the pictures in these two dreams. Pharaoh stood by the river. Egypt's river is the Nile, the lifeline of Egypt; all the nation's grain, water, and wealth came from this river. Pharaoh dreamed that seven cows came up out of the river, fine-looking and fat, feeding among the reeds. At first the dream showed a picture of abundance and peace. Then seven more came up, ugly and gaunt, and the Scripture adds a note: as Pharaoh later retold it to Joseph, their ugliness was such as he had never seen in all the land of Egypt. These seven lean cows did something contrary to nature: they ate up the seven fat cows. Cows eat grass, not cows; the abnormality in the dream was meant to tighten the heart. Stranger still, after eating, the lean cows were still lean — when Pharaoh awoke, the text says that after eating, the lean cows looked "as at the beginning" (Genesis 41:21, KJV): the fat went in, and the lean gained nothing from it.

Pharaoh could not sleep, and dreamed a second dream: seven ears[3] of grain came up on one stalk, full and good; then seven more ears sprang up, thin and withered, blasted by the east wind. These thin ears swallowed the seven full ears. Two dreams — one of livestock, one of crops; one by the river, one in the field — yet they spoke of two sides of the same matter: first abundance, then a devouring power arrives; the abundance is swallowed whole and leaves no trace.

At daybreak Pharaoh was troubled in spirit and sent for all the magicians and wise men of Egypt. These were professionals kept by the Egyptian court — books, dream manuals, spells, all complete. But this time, "there was none that could interpret them unto Pharaoh." Note the weight of this sentence: it was not that they interpreted poorly; they could not open their mouths at all. God made all the wisdom of Egypt fall silent that night, so that Pharaoh's ears would be emptied to hear a man from prison.

At exactly that moment, the cupbearer spoke. He said a sentence two years late: "I do remember my faults this day." This sentence is very interesting. What was the cupbearer's fault? On the surface, the old case in which he had offended Pharaoh and been imprisoned. But what he remembered today was probably not only that old case. He remembered the young man in prison, the benefit he had received, the promise he had made, and the fact that he had forgotten the man for two full years. People often remember the kindnesses they owe only when they need someone. The cupbearer told Joseph's story from the beginning: Pharaoh had once been angry and put him and the chief baker in prison; on the same night each dreamed a dream; there was a young Hebrew man in the prison, a servant of the captain of the guard; they told him, and he interpreted for them, and it turned out exactly as he had interpreted: the cupbearer was restored to his office, and the baker was hanged.

Brothers and sisters, do not rush to scold the cupbearer. The cupbearer is a portrait of most of us: overflowing with gratitude when we receive kindness, then, once our days run smoothly, putting the benefactor at the back of the mind — not deliberate evil, but too busy, too comfortable, too centered on ourselves. But the Bible records these two years plainly to let us see two things. First, human memory is unreliable and human promises expire; stake your whole hope in life on a cupbearer, and sooner or later you will be let down. If Joseph had stood at the prison door every day for those two years staring toward the palace, he would have gone mad. Second, God's timing does not follow the cupbearer's memory. The cupbearer forgot for two years; God did not forget for a single day. Those two years were not God napping; they were God waiting for a more precise moment: waiting for Pharaoh to dream, for the magicians to fall silent, for all Egypt to have no answer, and only then sending Joseph onto the stage. At that time Joseph would appear not as a prisoner promoted by the cupbearer, but as the answer God Himself had prepared. Late is not never; waiting is not wasted. In the two forgotten years God never stopped working; He was only building the stage where people could not see.

One more detail deserves a pause: before Joseph came out of prison, "they brought him hastily out of the dungeon: and he shaved himself, and changed his raiment, and came in unto Pharaoh." Shaving and changing clothes was Egyptian court etiquette, and also the outward mark of Joseph's reversal: half an hour earlier he was still a prisoner; in a moment he would stand before the most powerful man in all the land. But notice: Joseph changed his clothes; he did not change the person inside. Thirteen years of slavery and prison had not turned him into a man who only complained against heaven and people, nor into a man who grabs at opportunity to climb. As soon as he opened his mouth, he first drew a line. That line is the first key of the whole chapter, and we will take it up slowly in Part Two.

Notice also the other side of the sentence with which Pharaoh summoned Joseph. When the cupbearer recommended him to Pharaoh, he spoke fully of the young man's ability to interpret dreams in prison — but he did not mention that he had forgotten the man for two years, nor the promise he had once made. When people recommend people, they often tell only the half that benefits themselves. On the surface Joseph came out of prison because the cupbearer finally remembered; looking deeper, God brought together three things on the same morning — Pharaoh's anxiety, the magicians' silence, and the cupbearer's late-awakened conscience — and pushed him out. Missing any one of them, he would still have been waiting in prison. So those who trust God need not calculate every day which person will remember them or which door will open for them. You only need to prepare yourself in the low place: in his two years in prison Joseph did not abandon himself; he managed affairs, he interpreted dreams, he feared God as before. On the day opportunity arrived, the man standing before Pharaoh was a man already prepared, not a man cramming at the last moment. Waiting is not lying down and waiting; waiting is crouching in the low place, practicing the skills that must be practiced and guarding the heart that must be guarded, so that when God calls your name, you can rise and be used.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: It Is Not in Me — Interpretation Belongs to God, the Plan Belongs to Man (41:14-36)

Pharaoh first made the matter plain: "I have heard say of thee, that thou canst understand a dream to interpret it." This was a test, and also a trap. If Joseph had climbed the pole and said, "That is exactly my skill," the glory would have landed on himself; if he had shown fear and said, "Your servant dares not," the opportunity would have slipped away. Joseph's answer became one of the finest opening lines in the whole Bible: "It is not in me: God shall give Pharaoh an answer of peace."

Carve this sentence on your heart. "It is not in me" — this was not polite modesty; it was Joseph's lesson of thirteen years. He knew exactly how much he weighed: at seventeen he dreamed two dreams and drew his brothers' hatred; in prison he interpreted dreams and drew two years of being forgotten. If interpreting dreams were his own ability, that ability could not save himself. What could bring a man from prison to palace was not his ability, but the God who gave him dreams and also interpreted dreams through him. So standing before Pharaoh, the first thing he did was draw the line clearly: the answer is not with me; the answer is with God; I am only the messenger.

Brothers and sisters, this is the first lesson every person used by God must learn: before being lifted up, learn not to steal God's glory. Many people are defeated not in the valley but at the moment they are first raised up — people praise them: the ability is yours, the opportunity is yours, the platform is yours — and they truly believe it. Joseph did the opposite: the higher he stood, the sooner he opened his mouth to say, it is not in me. This sentence also gave Pharaoh a settled heart: what you are about to hear is not a prisoner's flattery, but God's answer of peace. The word "peace" matters greatly. Pharaoh's spirit had been troubled all night; Joseph first told him that what God would speak was a word of peace — not to frighten him, but to prepare a way out for him and his people.

Then Joseph interpreted the dream. First he pointed out the unity of the two dreams: "The dream of Pharaoh is one: God hath shewed Pharaoh what he is about to do." The two dreams were one matter told twice, and precisely because it was told twice, the matter was established by God and would surely come to pass quickly. Then he matched the pictures one by one: the seven good cows were seven years, and the seven good ears were also seven years; there would be seven years of great plenty throughout the land of Egypt. After them, the seven ugly, gaunt cows and the seven withered ears were also seven years — the seven years of famine[4] that followed immediately. The famine would be very severe; all the land would be consumed by it, and the abundance of the former plenty would not be remembered. The grain of the fat years would be eaten clean by the lean years, just as the lean cows swallowed the fat cows and the thin ears swallowed the good ears, without even changing their appearance.

Interpreted to this point, the work was already complete. But Joseph did not stop. He took one step further, and this step is where the chapter's wisdom shows most: he offered a plan. He said Pharaoh should select a discerning and wise man and set him over the land of Egypt, and appoint overseers to take a fifth of the produce in the years of plenty, gather food, and store it under Pharaoh's hand as reserve for the years of famine, so that the land would not perish through the famine.

Here we must keep the theology clear, lest we preach it crooked: interpreting the dream was revelation — God showing future things to man — and Joseph merely conveyed it; he himself said repeatedly, this is God's matter, it is not in me. Offering the plan was not revelation; offering the plan was the application of wisdom — God had not written in the dream that a fifth must be collected, nor that overseers must be appointed. This was Joseph turning the truth he had received into executable governance: how much to collect, who collects it, where to store it, why to store it, and for which year to store it. Brothers and sisters, faith is never receiving a prophecy and then sitting there waiting for fulfillment. God tells you about the famine years in advance not so that you marvel that God is accurate, but so that you get to work in the years of plenty. You know hardship is coming — save now. You know the seasons will change — arrange now. Joseph's piety was not a piety that only interprets dreams; it was a piety that interprets dreams and also does accounts, that prays and also builds storehouses. Parents among us, managers among us, those who run household finances among us — learn this lesson: God's moving you is one thing; turning that moving into a budget, a list, and a schedule is another. Only when both are done are you a faithful steward.

One more thing must be seen: the plan Joseph offered was offered for Egypt. What kind of place was Egypt? The place that had bought him as a slave, the place that had falsely accused him and put him in prison, the place that had forgotten him for two years. By ordinary human feeling, Joseph could perfectly well have interpreted the dream and offered no plan, and watched this nation eat the fruit of its own deeds in the famine. He did not. He offered the nation that had oppressed him a life-saving plan. From Abraham onward Genesis has one line running through it: God chose this family so that all the families of the earth would be blessed through them. The first thing Joseph did outside prison lived out this line: the blessed person must become a blessing to others, and even those who wronged him were within the range of his blessing. Brothers and sisters, on the day God lifts you up after you have been wronged, what tests you is not whether you can finally hold your head high, but whether you can share life-saving grain with the very people who once made you suffer. Cross this step, and you have truly come out of prison; fail to cross it, and though the person has left prison, the heart is still locked inside.

Set Joseph beside Daniel, and this line becomes even clearer. Centuries later, Daniel stood before Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, to interpret a dream, and his opening meant the same thing: this secret was revealed not because his wisdom exceeded that of any living man, but because God would make known to the king the thoughts of his heart. Joseph before, Daniel after — two young men living under another's roof, standing before two imperial kings, both first pushed the glory away and gave it to God. This is no coincidence; this is the common mark of God's servants: the more real ability a person has, the less he boasts by his ability; the more surely the answer is in his grasp, the sooner he says the answer is not in me. Fellow workers in the church today and brothers and sisters in the workplace should learn this sentence too: when a thing is accomplished, say first that it was God's grace; do not write your own name first in the book of merit. Give the merit to God, and the human heart will not rot inside success. This one sentence of Joseph's protected his road for the fourteen years that followed; how many people fall precisely on the "It is not in me" that they never said aloud in their moment of triumph.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: A New Name and Two Sons — Manasseh and Ephraim (41:37-57)

Pharaoh and his servants heard all this and said it was good. Pharaoh's words fixed Joseph's origin with one stroke: "Forasmuch as God hath shewed thee all this, there is none so discreet and wise as thou art." Note what Pharaoh acknowledged: not that Joseph the man was clever — he acknowledged that God had shown these things to Joseph. Joseph first gave the glory to God, and God, through the mouth of a pagan king, returned the honor to him doubled. Then Pharaoh announced in court: you shall be over all the land of Egypt; only in the throne will I be greater than you; all my people shall be ruled according to your word. He took the ring from his own hand — the signet ring, the seal, the credential of royal authority — and put it on Joseph's hand; he arrayed him in fine linen, put a gold chain around his neck, made him ride in the second chariot, and had men cry before him: Bow the knee. The man who had come out of prison that year at thirty years of age became, within a single day, the ruler of Egypt.

Pharaoh also gave him a new name: Zaphenath-paneah[5]. This Egyptian name has through the ages been explained along the lines of "revealer of secrets," which is a traditional explanation; scholarship has reached no settled conclusion to this day, so we can only cite it with reserve and must not preach it as settled. But one layer of meaning needs no dispute: a new name marks a new identity — in human eyes, Joseph from then on was an Egyptian, Pharaoh's man. Pharaoh also gave him Asenath, daughter of Potipherah priest of On, as wife, receiving him into Egypt's highest priestly family. Joseph was thirty years old. The Scripture deliberately records his age: "And Joseph was thirty years old when he stood before Pharaoh king of Egypt." Sold at seventeen, ruler at thirty — the thirteen years in between were not one of them written on the stage; all were written underground.

Brothers and sisters, at this point, first three words of application for those who are still waiting. First, the two years of waiting are not wasted, and God's timing is not delay. If Joseph had come out one year earlier, Pharaoh would not yet have dreamed, and at most he would have been a free man appreciated by the cupbearer; if he had been remembered only after the dreams were finished, it would also have been too late. The day God brought him out was exact to the minute: the very day Egypt most needed an answer. The matter you today complain is late may not be late at all; it may simply not yet have reached the moment when its effect must be shown. Second, in the hour of exaltation still give the glory to God. The greater the honor Joseph received, the more weight his earlier "It is not in me" carried. Acknowledging God in prosperity is far harder than acknowledging Him in adversity — in adversity a person has no one to seek but God; in prosperity everyone comes to lift you up. The person who can still hand the merit over amid applause is the person truly refined by God. Third, prepare for the famine years while it is still the years of plenty. As soon as Joseph took office he went out through all the land, and in the seven years of plenty, following the very plan he had offered, he gathered grain city by city. The text says the grain he stored was very much, like the sand of the sea, beyond counting, until he stopped counting it. The man who stores grain in the years of plenty does so because he first believed that dream. We too, while we are strong and our circumstances are steady, must store spiritual grain, relational grain, and financial grain. The famine years never send notice in advance, but the famine years never treat unkindly those who prepared.

Then the Bible turns the camera from the palace into Joseph's home. Before the famine came, Joseph first had two sons. In Genesis, naming a child is always the parents' confession of theology. Joseph named his firstborn Manasseh, saying: "God hath made me forget all my toil, and all my father's house." The name Manasseh shares its root with the word "to make forget" [1]. Read this sentence accurately: what Joseph wanted to forget was not his father, not his brothers, not his own roots — later he recognized his brothers, wept on their necks, and personally brought his father down to provide for him, proving he had truly forgotten none of that. What he wanted to forget was "all my toil": the scene of his brothers throwing him into the pit, the price at the slave market, the garment torn off him in Potiphar's house, the stone walls of two years in prison. Forgetting here is not amnesia; it is healing: those events are still there, but they no longer bite him every day; the wounds are still there, but they no longer rule how he treats people today. Brothers and sisters, one of the greatest releases God gives is making a person able to forget — not pretending it never happened, but that, having happened, it need not define the rest of one's life. Some brothers and sisters cannot walk out of the past, not because their memory is too good, but because that sorrow has not yet been handed into God's hand. What you cannot forget by yourself, God can make you forget: hand the sorrow to Him, and He turns "memory that bites" into "a quiet past."

The second son was named Ephraim, saying: "God hath caused me to be fruitful in the land of my affliction." Ephraim shares its sound and source with the word "to be fruitful, to increase" [2]. This sentence goes one step further than the one before. Manasseh speaks looking backward: the sorrow — forgotten. Ephraim speaks standing right here: in the land of affliction itself, in Egypt itself, on the very ground that sold me, falsely accused me, and forgot me, God made me bear fruit. Joseph did not wait until he returned to Canaan to bear fruit; he did not wait until the environment improved, the offenders apologized, and the injustice was reversed to bear fruit. The place where he bore fruit was precisely the place where he suffered. Brothers and sisters, this is the one sentence today's whole sermon most wants to give you: do not always think you must change places to start over. When God makes a person fruitful, He often does not move the place; it happens right where you are — in the post that made you weep, in the relationship that wronged you, in the city you thought was a prison. The land is still the same land and the person still the same person, yet God makes it turn from a land of affliction into a land of fruitfulness. Manasseh first, Ephraim after — the order cannot be reversed: first learn to forget the bitterness, and only then are your hands free to bear fruit. A person who turns over old accounts in his heart every day cannot bear new fruit in his hands.

The seven years of plenty passed, and the seven years of famine arrived as appointed. Famine covered all the earth, but Egypt had grain. The people cried to Pharaoh for bread, and Pharaoh said the sentence that most plainly showed Joseph's standing: go to Joseph; whatever he says to you, do it. Joseph opened the storehouses and sold grain to the Egyptians; the heavier the famine grew, the busier he became. The text closes by saying: "And all countries came into Egypt to Joseph for to buy corn; because that the famine was so sore in all lands." The boy once sold by his brothers had now become the doorway at which all the world bought grain. God did not let Joseph bypass Egypt to receive glory; God let Joseph become a blessing to all nations in Egypt — just as God had said to his great-grandfather Abraham, in your seed shall all the nations of the earth be blessed. The land of affliction had become the land where all nations received grain.

We must also add honestly: Joseph's fruitfulness was not the work of a single night. First he spent the seven years of plenty doing the least glamorous work — traveling, collecting, building storehouses, keeping accounts, city by city, storehouse by storehouse. The ruler's splendor was on the day the ring was given; the seven years after were all ledgers and granaries. Fruitfulness is never standing on a stage to receive a prize; it is gathering in what must be gathered and storing what must be stored, year after year. When the famine arrived, the difference appeared: households without preparation could not buy grain even with money in hand; the one who had prepared opened the storehouses, and what was saved was all the land — even the children and grandchildren of enemies were among the saved. So do not envy the composure of others in the famine years; that composure was saved up day by day in the years of plenty. Whether you are willing to do the plain, unglamorous work in the years of plenty decides whether you can stand in the years of famine. The dream God gave Joseph was grace; Joseph's seven years of storing grain were faithfulness. Grace plus faithfulness produced the later buying of grain by all nations. A person with grace but no faithfulness may dream fine dreams, but his storehouse is empty.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked the steepest stretch of Joseph's road: when the two years were full, people had forgotten him, but God remembered him; standing before Pharaoh, he first said, it is not in me; after being made ruler, he named his two sons — one Forgetfulness, the other Fruitfulness. Brothers and sisters, Joseph's theology is half written in the palace and half written in the nursery. The half in the palace is: glory to God, wisdom at work, prepare in plenty for famine. The half in the nursery is: God made me forget all my toil, and God made me fruitful in the land of my affliction. If you are still in those two years, ask today first for the grace of Manasseh: ask God to make you able to forget, no longer letting bitterness rule the house. If you have already come out, ask again for the grace of Ephraim: do not change places; bear fruit in the land of affliction itself, so that the grain you receive can be shared with more people.

Let us pray together: Lord, Your timing never delays and Your memory never decays. When people forgot us, You did not forget; when we waited two years, five years, ten years, You did not stop working for a single day. Make us forget all the toil we should no longer carry, and heal the memories that bite; and make us fruitful in the land of our affliction, not fleeing the place You have measured out for us. Let us give glory to You in exaltation, prepare for famine in the years of plenty, and share grain with others when we receive grain. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And Joseph called the name of the firstborn Manasseh: For God, said he, hath made me forget all my toil, and all my father's house. And the name of the second called he Ephraim: For God hath caused me to be fruitful in the land of my affliction." (Genesis 41:51-52, KJV)

Joseph was forgotten by the cupbearer for two full years. If a person lives only by human memory, two years are enough to drain hope dry. But God's timing is not delay: when the two years were full, Pharaoh dreamed, Joseph was remembered, and in a single day he went from prison to palace. Even more precious are the two names he gave his children after his rise. Manasseh is forgetting — not amnesia, but sorrow that no longer bites; Ephraim is fruitfulness — not in a different place, but fruitful in the very land of his affliction. Brothers and sisters, the past you cannot forget, hand it first into God's hand; He can make you forget. The land you despise, do not rush to flee it either; God can make you bear fruit right where you are. Manasseh first, then Ephraim: lay down the bitterness first, and only then will fruit be borne. Ask for both of these graces today, in the place where you now stand.

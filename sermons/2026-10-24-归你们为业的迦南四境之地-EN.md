## Summary

The record of the journey is finished (chapter 33, preceding-text layer), Moses is still standing east of Jordan, and the word of the LORD has already crossed the river ahead of him: first the four borders of the land of Canaan are drawn for the people, side by side. The frame of the chapter's head: "When ye come into the land of Canaan; (this is the land that shall fall unto you for an inheritance, even the land of Canaan with the coasts thereof:)" (34:2) The south border starts from the outmost coast of the salt sea eastward, turns by the ascent of Akrabbim, Zin, and Kadesh-barnea, goes on to Hazar-addar and Azmon, fetches a compass to the river of Egypt, and its goings out are at the sea (34:3-5); the west border is the briefest, one sentence — the great sea for a border (34:6); the north border is pointed out from the great sea to mount Hor, through the entrance of Hamath and Zedad and Ziphron, to Hazar-enan (34:7-9); the east border runs from Hazar-enan to Shepham, down to Riblah and the side of the sea of Chinnereth eastward, and down to Jordan, its goings out at the salt sea (34:10-12) — the four borders close, and the closing sentence says: "this shall be your land with the coasts thereof round about." (34:12) The borders drawn, Moses at once states the scope of the receiving: this land is inherited by lot by the nine tribes and the half tribe (34:13), because Reuben, Gad, and half the tribe of Manasseh have already received their inheritance east of Jordan (34:14-15). Last, the LORD Himself names the men who divide the land: Eleazar the priest and Joshua the son of Nun have the charge of it (34:17), and one prince from each tribe is taken to help them (34:18); the names of the ten princes, with their fathers' names, are set down one by one (34:19-28), and the closing sentence says: "These are they whom the LORD commanded to divide the inheritance unto the children of Israel in the land of Canaan." (34:29, quoted in full as it stands) There is not one battle in this chapter, and not one marvel — only a map of borders and a list of names: the land has not yet been entered, and its borders are already drawn clear; the inheritance is not yet in hand, and the men who will divide it are already named.

## Main Text

### Part One: First the Frame — The Land Falls to You for an Inheritance (Numbers 34:1-2) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) Verse 1, the opening of the oracle, registered in its substance: And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying — the words of this chapter are the LORD's words to Moses, and Moses in turn commands the people; the "Command the children of Israel, and say unto them" of verse 2 is exactly the switch of that transmission, recorded as it stands. Verse 2, the frame, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "Command the children of Israel, and say unto them, When ye come into the land of Canaan; (this is the land that shall fall unto you for an inheritance, even the land of Canaan with the coasts thereof:)" (34:2) The items registered one by one: first, the occasion begins with "When ye come into the land of Canaan" — the words are spoken before the arrival; the people are at this point still east of Jordan (preceding-text layer of this book), the land not yet entered and the borders already drawn. Second, the nature of the land is stated as "that shall fall unto you for an inheritance" — in this chapter Canaan is first of all not an object to be conquered but an inheritance to be received. Third, the object described is "the land of Canaan with the coasts thereof" — what the next ten verses draw is not scattered plots but one whole land closed on its four sides; the phrase "with the coasts thereof" stands before any single border is named, the frame before the details, recorded as it stands.

(Argument) Notice the order of this command: God does not speak first of how to fight, but of how large the land is. The people have not yet crossed the river; they have not yet even caught sight of Jericho's walls, and God first puts into their hands a map with its four borders distinct. The use of this map is not to direct a campaign but to settle hearts — the place you are going is not an ownerless tract to be ringed off at sword-point as far as the sword can reach; its four coasts have already been measured, bounded, and recorded in the command. Faith often begins not from seeing but from hearing a boundary: to know that the promise has edges, borders, and an extent settles a man instead. A promise without borders would become an excuse for greed — everywhere is mine, as far as I can seize is mine; once the promise has its four coasts, what the people receive is an inheritance they can dwell in, not a battlefield of endless expansion. So it is when we receive God's leading today: first ask where the extent He gives lies, and then be faithful within that extent; expansion across the boundary line, even under the banner of the promise, is no longer receiving an inheritance — it is encroachment.

(Argument) Look again at the weight of the words "for an inheritance" in this chapter. An inheritance is not spoil: spoil seized must still be guarded against being seized back; an inheritance is written under a name and handed down to children. A piece of land is first called "your inheritance," and only then is there talk of the lot and of dividing — the order cannot be reversed. The people do not first take the land and then ask whose it is; they first know whose it is, and then cross the river to receive it. This gives us a reminder for how we look at our own work and dwelling today: what is in our hands must first be recognized in our hearts as a trust and an inheritance, not as plunder; and the act of recognizing takes place before the crossing, before the hand is put to anything. So with the land of Canaan, so with our days — the man who first hears the frame clearly walks without panic.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Inheritance" is naḥălâ (H5159), the portion of land received as an inheritance; the word-family runs through this chapter (34:2 "for an inheritance," 13 "inherit by lot," 14 and 15 "received their inheritance," 17 in the Chinese base text's wording "divide the land for an inheritance," 29 "divide the inheritance"); by this sermon's own count the chapter builds its argument on this word-family at no fewer than six places, the details standing in Endnote 4 — with one difference between the base texts registered there: at verse 17 the KJV carries no inheritance-word, and at verse 18 it carries "by inheritance" where the Chinese reckoning does not list the verse. "Coast / border" is gĕḇûl (H1366), the boundary and the territory within the boundary; at verse 2 it stands in "with the coasts thereof," and from verse 3 the four borders use it side by side. "Canaan" is kĕnaʿan (H3667), standing in this chapter as the general name of the land, twice in verse 2 (the land of Canaan, and the land of Canaan with the coasts thereof, used in succession), recorded as it stands.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The correspondence between this chapter's borders and the promised extent in Genesis is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one reads the two as borders at two levels — the promise of Genesis 15:18 stands as "from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates" (preceding-text layer), an extent far larger than this chapter's four borders, while this chapter draws the four coasts of the land the new generation actually receives as its inheritance, the promise as realized in that one generation; the two are promise and receiving, and need not be made the same. Another reads this chapter's four borders as the statement of an ideal extent — its north border is pushed to the neighbourhood of the entrance of Hamath, beyond what the division record of Joshua actually reached (following-text layer), and the reading attends to the text's setting the border at its largest extent, later actual dwelling having its own contraction and expansion. A third looks to literary form — the writing that strings place-names side by side through the four directions resembles the border documents of the ancient Near East in genre, its function lying in the document of the covenant rather than in surveying. This sermon registers only the points of view of the three readings and the textual facts (this chapter's four borders state themselves complete; the extent of Genesis 15 states itself otherwise), and does not adjudicate the relation of the two extents at the fact layer; the main text builds only on this chapter's own statement of its four borders and its definition of the land as an inheritance.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of Part One: one verse of oracle-opening and one verse of frame; three definitions in the frame (when ye come into the land of Canaan; that shall fall unto you for an inheritance; the land of Canaan with the coasts thereof); and one preview of the structure below — ten verses of four borders (34:3-12), three verses on the scope of the receiving (34:13-15), and fourteen verses on the men chosen to divide (34:16-29), the threefold division of the chapter's 29 verses, the details standing in Endnote 4.

### Part Two: The South Border and the West Border — A South Drawn by Winding, a West in One Sentence (Numbers 34:3-6) (10:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 3, the starting point of the south border, quoted verbatim in full: "Then your south quarter shall be from the wilderness of Zin along by the coast of Edom, and your south border shall be the outmost coast of the salt sea eastward:" (34:3) The items registered one by one: the southern tract is called the "south quarter," its territory running from the wilderness of Zin along by the coast of Edom; the starting point of the south border line is "the outmost coast of the salt sea eastward" — the line takes up its pen at the eastern end and unfolds westward, recorded as it stands. Verse 4, the course of the south border, registered in paraphrase: the border turns from the south to the ascent of Akrabbim, passes on to Zin, goes forth from the south to Kadesh-barnea, goes on to Hazar-addar, and passes on to Azmon — five turning place-names within one verse (the ascent of Akrabbim, Zin, Kadesh-barnea, Hazar-addar, Azmon), the verbs running in succession "shall turn," "pass on," "shall be from the south to," "shall go on" — the line is a broken line, not a straight one, recorded as it stands. Verse 5, the end of the south border, registered in its substance: from Azmon the border fetches a compass unto the river of Egypt, and the goings out of it are at the sea — the south border's western end stops at the sea, its end joining the west border, recorded as it stands. Verse 6, the west border, quoted verbatim in full: "And as for the western border, ye shall even have the great sea for a border: this shall be your west border." (34:6) Registered: the west border uses one verse, one sentence, and one border-mark (the great sea); it is the briefest of the four borders. "The great sea" occurs twice in this chapter (34:6, 7; the count stands in Endnote 4); that it denotes the Mediterranean belongs to the register of common geographical knowledge — this chapter calls it only "the great sea" and gives it no proper name. Recounted by this sermon's own hand: the south border has three verses (34:3-5), and its course names eight places counting the starting point (the outmost coast of the salt sea eastward, the ascent of Akrabbim, Zin, Kadesh-barnea, Hazar-addar, Azmon, the river of Egypt, the sea), the details standing in Endnote 4.

(Argument) The south border is the most winding of the four: turn, fetch a compass, pass on, go on — verb upon verb, each more laborious than the last. That is no surprise: the south is the direction of the people's road in, the wilderness side it took them forty years to finish walking. When God drew this border He did not pull it into a convenient straight line; He let it run along by the coast of Edom, round the ascent, and through the belt where they had camped, station by station. Kadesh-barnea stands inside this border line (34:4) — the place from which the spies set out and to which they brought back the evil report (preceding-text layer of this book), the doorway at which the whole camp was sentenced back to the wilderness. Now this place is not bypassed as a place of sorrow; it is written straight into the border of the inheritance: your south border passes right beside the place where you fell. God's border lines for a man do not avoid his road in; He gathers the places where failure camped into the map of the inheritance, so that every time the people look south they remember where they were brought out from. Our own border lines should be drawn the same way: do not whitewash the road in, do not delete the stations of defeat — let the south border wind honestly.

(Argument) The west border, by contrast, uses a single sentence: "ye shall even have the great sea for a border." (34:6) Not because the west is unimportant, but because the sea is itself a border and needs no further tracing. Between this brevity and that elaboration there is a piece of spiritual common sense: some border lines need to be drawn carefully and charged again and again, because on that side the temptations are many, the disputes are many, and crossing is easy — the south runs along by Edom and joins the wilderness, the side on which the people most easily turn back and most easily become entangled with their neighbours, and so it is drawn in the finest detail; other border lines are distinct by nature — a man standing at the seashore knows there is no road further on, and little needs to be said. So it is with discipline and pastoral care: what is drawn in fine detail is not necessarily the more important; what is carried in one sentence is not necessarily held lightly. Moses does not spend his strength evenly in transmitting the command, and we should not spend our attention evenly in hearing it — we should be most attentive exactly where God has drawn most finely, for that is often the side on which we are most likely to come to grief.

(Argument) There is one further layer, the south border's relation to the people's memory. Several of the places this border line passes are wilderness edges the people themselves walked, camped at, and even buried companions beside. God did not draw the inheritance's border in a far country they had never reached; He let it unfold along the outer edge of their road in — stand on the south border and look back, and it is forty years of wilderness; turn and look forward, and it is the inheritance to be received. The border is therefore also a line of memory: it tells the new generation where your land begins and where your story comes from. A people that forgets what lies beyond the south border will not keep a grateful heart even after entering Canaan; the man who remembers the road in knows that every inch of ground under his feet is received, not owed. 

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "The salt sea" is yam-hammelaḥ (the compound of H3220 and H4417), occurring twice in this chapter (34:3 "the outmost coast of the salt sea eastward," 34:12 "shall be at the salt sea"), bearing the same name as the register in Genesis, "the vale of Siddim, which is the salt sea" (preceding-text layer); only the base text's rendering is recorded. "The river of Egypt" is naḥal miṣrayim (H5158 and H4714), the name of the watercourse at the south border's western end in verse 5; the KJV renders it "river," where the Chinese base text's wording is "small river," and each manuscript records its own text. "Ascent" is maʿălê (H4608), the terrain word inside "the ascent of Akrabbim" at verse 4; only the word-form is recorded. Most of this section's place-names are transliterated proper names; where the examination of a word-form or meaning is disputed (as whether "Akrabbim" contains the sense "scorpions"), this sermon marks it uniformly pending verification and records only the base text's rendering, as in Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The identification of the south border's points with modern sites is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one holds that the south border can be broadly reconstructed — the salt sea, Kadesh-barnea, and the river of Egypt have other scriptures and geographical material to refer to, and though the points between are disputed, the line's general course westward along the southern edge of the Negev holds together. Another holds that the middle points (Hazar-addar, Azmon) have widely divergent proposed sites with no other corroboration, and that to join the line by force would draw conjecture as fact. A third looks to the reference of "the river of Egypt" — one reading takes it to be the Wadi el-Arish, another takes it to be the eastern branch of the Nile, the two still standing side by side and bearing on where the south border's western end falls. This sermon registers only the points of view of the three readings and the textual facts (of the south border's eight place-names, three have corroboration elsewhere and the rest are mostly solitary names), and does not settle the modern site of any point at the fact layer; the main text builds only on the order of the course as this chapter itself states it.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of Part Two: the south border, three verses, eight course place-names, four kinds of turning verbs (turn, pass on / go on, go forth, fetch a compass); the west border, one verse, one border-mark (the great sea); two of the four borders now stand, the north and east borders to be unfolded in the next part. And it is registered: the salt sea, the south border's starting point, is also the east border's end (34:12); the four borders join head to tail at the salt sea — this structural fact is registered here only, and its argument is reserved for Part Five, where the borders are read together.

### Part Three: The North Border and the East Border — The Coasts Closed Round About (Numbers 34:7-12) (24:00-36:00)

(Fact) Verse 7, the opening stretch of the north border, quoted verbatim in full: "And this shall be your north border: from the great sea ye shall point out for you mount Hor:" (34:7) Registered: the north border takes up its pen at the great sea and is pointed out eastward to mount Hor, standing opposite the south border's taking up its pen at the salt sea, recorded as it stands; whether this verse's "mount Hor" is the same mountain as the mount Hor of chapter 33 this chapter does not note — only the sameness of name is registered, and the identification stands at the discussion layer. Verse 8, the middle stretch of the north border, registered in paraphrase: from mount Hor the border is pointed out unto the entrance of Hamath, and its goings forth are to Zedad — "the entrance of Hamath" is registered as an entry-point place-name, the border line by now deep into the northern frontier. Verse 9, the close of the north border, quoted verbatim in full: "And the border shall go on to Ziphron, and the goings out of it shall be at Hazar-enan: this shall be your north border." (34:9) Registered: the north border ends at Hazar-enan as its eastern terminus; "this shall be your north border" is the only border-name closing formula among the four borders — the south and west borders each have their own closing phrases in differing wording, and each is recorded separately, not merged. Verse 10, the opening stretch of the east border, quoted verbatim in full: "And ye shall point out your east border from Hazar-enan to Shepham:" (34:10) Registered: the east border takes up its pen at Hazar-enan, the north border's terminus — the two borders join at a single point, recorded as it stands. Verse 11, the middle stretch of the east border, registered in paraphrase: this coast goes down from Shepham to Riblah, on the east side of Ain, and descends and reaches unto the side of the sea of Chinnereth eastward — the verb turns to "go down," the border line running along the mountain's eastern foot and the east of the sea, recorded as it stands. Verse 12, the end of the east border and the close of all the borders, quoted verbatim in full: "And the border shall go down to Jordan, and the goings out of it shall be at the salt sea: this shall be your land with the coasts thereof round about." (34:12) The items registered one by one: first, the east border's descent stops at the salt sea, joining the south border's starting point — the four borders are closed at this point. Second, the closing sentence reads "this shall be your land with the coasts thereof round about" — its subject is the whole land within the borders, not plot by plot, recorded as it stands. Recounted by this sermon's own hand: the north border has three verses (34:7-9) and five course place-names (the great sea, mount Hor, the entrance of Hamath, Zedad, Ziphron) besides its terminus, Hazar-enan; the east border has three verses (34:10-12) and six course place-names (Hazar-enan, Shepham, Riblah, the sea of Chinnereth, Jordan, the salt sea); the four borders total ten verses (34:3-12), the details standing in Endnote 4.

(Argument) As the four borders are drawn through to the east border, a meaningful direction appears: the east border goes "down." Down from Shepham to Riblah, and down again to Jordan — the people have not yet crossed the river, and the inheritance's east border has already been traced down the river's eastern bank, traced to the very side where they now stand. In other words, the plains of Moab under their feet are not inside the four borders; they are standing outside the border of their own inheritance, listening while the land inside the border is read out to them stretch by stretch. That is exactly the shape of faith: outside the door, listening to the map of what is inside the door. How many want to wait until they are standing inside before they will believe; the people, east of the river, had to believe the west bank's borders first — when they heard, the ground under their feet was still another's land, and in their ears it was already their own inheritance. We often read the promises in just this position: the man is still east of the river, the matter not yet accomplished, and no part of the environment bears his own name; but the command arrives first, and the borders are clear first. Those who first hear the borders clear on the east bank will not walk at random after crossing.

(Argument) Look then at the closing itself. The most moving thing in the four borders is that they join head to tail: the south border begins at the salt sea, the east border ends at the salt sea; the north border ends at Hazar-enan, the east border begins at Hazar-enan; the south border ends at the sea, and the west border is the great sea. There is no gap anywhere, and no stretch that says "the rest is for you to manage as you see fit." What God gives the people is not an open-ended licence to expand but an inheritance closed on its four sides. Closure means two things: everything inside the border is given to you — there is no need to seize more outside; and everything outside the border is not given to you — there is no need to covet more outside. Half the settled heart comes from the security of "all inside the border is mine," and the other half from the contentment of "what is outside has nothing to do with me." People today live tired lives often not because the land is too small but because the border is not closed — they keep feeling the outside ought to have a share for them too. When Moses finished reading the four borders on the riverbank, what the people received was not a larger appetite but coasts closed round about: within this, it shall be your land; beyond this, let go in peace.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The verb rendered "point out" rests on the causative form of tāʾâ (its Strong's number is marked pending verification in this sermon, see Endnote 5); verses 7 and 10 use this verb for the border-drawing — the KJV renders it "point out" at verses 7, 8, and 10 alike — the Chinese base text reading at verse 7 in its own wording "draw to" and at verse 10 "draw ... to be the east border," and this correspondence only is registered. "Go down" is yāraḏ (H3381), occurring twice in the east border's descent at verses 11 and 12, recorded separately from the north border's "point out" and "go on." "Chinnereth" is kinnereṯ (H3672), standing at verse 11 as the name of the sea — the KJV reads "the sea of Chinnereth," where the Chinese base text reads, in its own wording, "the lake of Chinnereth," and neither is conformed to the other. "Jordan" is hayyardēn (H3383), occurring twice in this chapter, at the borders and at the register of the receiving (34:12, 15), as in Endnote 4.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The likeness and difference between this chapter's north border and the four borders of Ezekiel 47 are set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one holds that the two broadly agree — the north border in both begins at the great sea, passes the neighbourhood of the entrance of Hamath, and reaches Hazar-enan or a near name; the east border in both ends at Jordan and the salt sea; they are two statements of one border tradition. Another holds that the details differ between them — individual place-names are present or absent and differently ordered (Ezekiel, for instance, carries Hethlon and Hazar-hatticon, names not seen in this chapter), showing that each passage has its own setting and genre and should not be forced point by point into alignment. A third looks to the same-name question of mount Hor — one reading takes this chapter's north-border mount Hor to be another mountain on the northern frontier (or takes "Hor" as a class name), not the mount Hor of chapter 33 where Aaron died, the two accounts standing side by side to this day. This sermon registers only the points of view of the three readings and the textual facts (the two passages' four borders are near in their structure of beginning and end and not identical in detail; mount Hor bears the same name and this chapter gives no note), and does not adjudicate the correspondence or the mountain's site at the fact layer; the main text builds only on the course as this chapter itself states it.

(New Testament Reading) The apostle Paul said to the crowd at Athens that God "hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation" (Acts 17:26, KJV phrase quoted at that place; reading layer). One comparison is registered at the reading layer: this chapter is one concrete showing of "the bounds of their habitation" determined beforehand — the borders were not ratified only after the people fought their way in; they were drawn, declared, and recorded in the command before the people dwelt there; and the New Testament words set this one drawing inside a wider picture: the times and the bounds of all nations are in the determination of the God who made all nations. The reading layer registers the comparison of the two texts only, and does not read the apostle's address back into the facts of this chapter; the origin of this chapter's four borders still rests on its own statement at verses 1-2 (the LORD spake unto Moses, who commands the people).

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of Part Three: the north border, three verses and six place-names including its terminus; the east border, three verses and six place-names; the four borders in all, ten verses, with three closings (34:6, the west border; 34:9, the north border; 34:12, all the borders); three joining points (the sea, Hazar-enan, the salt sea) — the textual grounds of the four borders' closure are complete item by item, and the question of dividing and receiving passes to the next part.

### Part Four: Nine Tribes and a Half — First Settle Clearly What Has Already Been Received (Numbers 34:13-15) (36:00-46:00)

(Fact) Verse 13, the scope of the receiving, quoted verbatim in full: "And Moses commanded the children of Israel, saying, This is the land which ye shall inherit by lot, which the LORD commanded to give unto the nine tribes, and to the half tribe:" (34:13) The items registered one by one: first, the one transmitting is Moses; the discourse turns from the divine oracle to Moses' command, continuing the chain of transmission of verses 1-2. Second, the method of division is "by lot" — the same method a third time, with the division principle of chapter 26 (preceding-text layer) and the command at the end of chapter 33 (preceding-text layer), recorded as it stands. Third, the recipients are "the nine tribes, and to the half tribe" — not the twelve tribes; the part that is missing is accounted for in the next two verses, recorded as it stands. Verse 14, those who have already received (first), registered in its substance: for the tribe of the children of Reuben and the tribe of the children of Gad, according to the house of their fathers, have received their inheritance, and half the tribe of Manasseh has received its inheritance — those who have already received are two and a half tribes, named one by one: Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh, with "according to the house of their fathers" as the measure of their receiving, recorded as it stands. Verse 15, those who have already received (second), registered in its substance: the two tribes and the half tribe have received their inheritance on this side Jordan near Jericho eastward, toward the sunrising — three qualifications of place (near Jericho; on this side Jordan, eastward; toward the sunrising, in the KJV's own wording) agreeing with the account of the division east of Jordan in chapter 32 (preceding-text layer); only its wording is registered here, and the details of execution rest on that account itself. Recounted by this sermon's own hand: the nine tribes and a half and the two tribes and a half together make the number of the twelve tribes — the nine and a half west of Jordan (Judah, Simeon, Benjamin, Dan, half of Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Issachar, Asher, Naphtali, the list standing in the next part) and the two and a half east of Jordan (Reuben, Gad, half of Manasseh) total twelve; the tribe of Levi is not in the list of those who divide land, and this chapter does not state the reason, which rests on this book's preceding text (chapter 18); only this blank is registered, the details standing in Endnote 4.

(Argument) The borders drawn, the first thing Moses does is not to urge the people across the river but to settle the account clear: who has already received, and who has not yet received — stated plainly first. The inheritance of the two and a half tribes is east of Jordan; they have already received, and received is received — this chapter spends two full verses testifying to the fact, so that those west of Jordan will not envy it later and those east of Jordan will not reach a hand westward again. Before an inheritance is divided, the account is settled first: that is the rule this chapter sets for every act of distribution — what is already settled is not reopened for debate; what is not yet divided is divided by law. Where we handle distribution — in church, family, or company — the disorder most likely to arise is precisely that dividing begins before the account is settled clear: old accounts unsettled, a new division must be disputed. The reason Moses' method of division can persuade is not first the lot but the settling: twelve tribes — two and a half east of Jordan, nine and a half west — account by account, stated clear before all. The man who states "already received" clearly has the standing to preside over "about to be divided."

(Argument) Notice also what the two and a half tribes are called here: this chapter does not call them "pioneers," nor does it call them an "exception"; it calls them only those who "have received their inheritance." The inheritance east of Jordan is an inheritance, and the inheritance west of Jordan is an inheritance too; Scripture does not discount the eastern portion, nor does it let the western portion exalt itself. It is those five words, "toward the sunrising," that repay a second look: the land they received lies on the side where the sun rises — every day, the sun shines first on the inheritance east of Jordan, and only then on the west. That is not rank but order; not grade but direction. The portions we each receive differ in direction and in order; receiving first does not mean receiving more, and receiving later does not mean receiving less. The man who enters another's "already received" frankly and largely into the ledger can receive his own portion, when it comes, with a settled heart.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Tribe" is maṭṭeh (H4294), occurring many times in this chapter, used with "half," ḥēṣî (H2677), in "the nine tribes, and to the half tribe" and "the two tribes and the half tribe," the numbers recorded as they stand. "According to the house of their fathers" rests on bêṯ-ʾāḇ (the compound of H1004 and H1); at verse 14 it denotes receiving according to the fathers' houses. "Lot" is gôrāl (H1486); the method of division at verse 13 is the same word as at chapters 26 and 33 (cross-reference layer within this book), the three places recorded separately and not merged. "Toward the sunrising" renders the sense of qēḏem and mizrāḥ (their numbers are marked pending verification, see Endnote 5); the KJV reads "toward the sunrising," and only its rendering is recorded.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The relation between the inheritance of the two and a half tribes east of Jordan and this chapter's four borders is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one holds that the four borders are confined to the west of Jordan — this chapter's four borders take Jordan as the east border, the land east of Jordan is not inside them, and the eastern inheritance is another arrangement outside the four borders: two portions of inheritance within one covenant, under two sets of borders. Another holds that the retrospective register of verses 14-15 in this chapter itself shows the unity of the whole people's inheritance — the twelve tribes come out of one covenant, and the eastern portion must be formally registered in this chapter if the western division is not to misread it as a breaking away. A third looks to the place-qualification "near Jericho" — this phrase looks back eastward from the west of Jordan, exactly opposite the direction of chapter 32's own self-statement from east of Jordan, showing that this chapter's narrative standpoint already stands with the generation about to enter the land. This sermon registers only the points of view of the three readings and the textual facts (the four borders stop at Jordan; the eastern portion has its formal retrospective register in this chapter), and does not adjudicate the covenant-law relation of the two portions at the fact layer; the main text builds only on the order of settling as this chapter itself states it.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of Part Four: one verse on the scope of the receiving (the nine tribes and the half tribe; by lot); two verses registering those who have already received (Reuben, Gad, half of Manasseh; near Jericho, on this side Jordan eastward, toward the sunrising); one item of tribal account (the nine and a half plus the two and a half make twelve; Levi is not in the list of those who divide land, and this chapter does not state the reason). The borders are drawn clear and the account settled clear; one question alone remains: by whom shall the land be divided — the next part names them.

### Part Five: The Men Who Divide the Land — First Two Named, Then Ten (Numbers 34:16-29) (46:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 16, the opening of the second oracle, registered in its substance: And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying — this chapter's two oracles (34:1, 16) govern its two halves: the first half draws the borders, the second half names the men, the structure recorded as it stands. Verse 17, those who have the charge of it, quoted verbatim in full: "These are the names of the men which shall divide the land unto you: Eleazar the priest, and Joshua the son of Nun." (34:17) The items registered one by one: first, the list heads with "Eleazar the priest," named by his office — the division is headed first by the priest, recorded as it stands. Second, "Joshua the son of Nun" follows, named by his father — Joshua's identity in this chapter is a divider of land, not a commander; this chapter does not mention his office of leading the army, recorded as it stands. Third, besides the two there are helpers, unfolded in the next verse. Verse 18, the rule for the helpers, registered in its substance: one prince is to be taken of every tribe to divide the land by inheritance — "every tribe" denotes the tribes receiving west of Jordan; since Manasseh and Ephraim each send one man (see below), the list comes to ten men, more than the number of nine and a half, this measure standing in Endnote 4, recorded as it stands. Verses 19-28, the ten princes, registered in paraphrase, their names and fathers' names recorded verse by verse as they stand in the base text: of the tribe of Judah, Caleb the son of Jephunneh; of the tribe of the children of Simeon, Shemuel the son of Ammihud; of the tribe of Benjamin, Elidad the son of Chislon; the prince of the tribe of the children of Dan, Bukki the son of Jogli; the prince of the children of Joseph, for the tribe of the children of Manasseh, Hanniel the son of Ephod; the prince of the tribe of the children of Ephraim, Kemuel the son of Shiphtan; the prince of the tribe of the children of Zebulun, Elizaphan the son of Parnach; the prince of the tribe of the children of Issachar, Paltiel the son of Azzan; the prince of the tribe of the children of Asher, Ahihud the son of Shelomi; the prince of the tribe of the children of Naphtali, Pedahel the son of Ammihud. Verse 29, the close of the list, registered in its substance: These are they whom the LORD commanded to divide the inheritance unto the children of Israel in the land of Canaan — the list closes with "whom the LORD commanded," from the same source as the naming of verse 17, recorded as it stands; the chapter closes at this point and does not record the execution of the division, which stands in the book of Joshua (following-text layer). Recounted by this sermon's own hand: ten princes and ten fathers' names; the order of the tribes is Judah, Simeon, Benjamin, Dan, Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Issachar, Asher, Naphtali — not wholly the same as the order of the numbering in chapter 26 (preceding-text layer); only its order is registered, and no reason is extrapolated from it. In the list, Caleb alone, with Joshua, is one of the spies of that year who remained (preceding-text layer of this book); this chapter does not mention that background and registers only his name, the details standing in Endnote 4.

(Argument) Inside the list of dividers lies this chapter's quietest act of thoughtfulness: Caleb stands at the head of the list. The prince of the tribe of Judah is Caleb the son of Jephunneh — forty years before, it was he and Joshua, the two of them, who stood out on the night the whole camp wailed and said, let us go up at once (preceding-text layer of this book). Now Joshua is the divider for all Israel, and Caleb is the prince for the tribe of Judah: the two men who were rejected by the whole camp for their faith forty years ago stand together, forty years on, on the list of those who divide the land — one dividing for all Israel, one dividing for his own tribe. God did not let their faithfulness rot in the wilderness — that generation fell, but these two men were preserved to the day of dividing the inheritance, and not commemorated under the title of "meritorious servants" but used in the office of "princes." If in our own service there have been years of being left in the cold for holding fast, this chapter's list is a comfort: those whose names God has recorded will not be used up in the wilderness; He keeps them until the day of dividing.

(Argument) Look then at the list's composition: every prince is recorded with his father's name attached — the son of Jephunneh, the son of Ammihud, the son of Chislon. The dividing of an inheritance is the matter most likely to stir dispute in a whole people, and for the men who preside over it Scripture gives no title as their warrant but their parentage as their surety: whose sons they are, the people know, and their fathers the people also know. In a new society just out of the wilderness, with no king yet and no court, the public credit of the division is built on exactly this transparency of "name attached to father's name." When we handle the goods of many and the affairs of the public today, we should learn the same transparency: those who handle the matter must be checkable, answerable, and traceable, and their names must bear being read aloud before all. A list that dares to read out the fathers' names one by one is what makes a division that dares to cast the lot before all — public credit is not won in the disputing after the division is done; it is established at the moment of the naming.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Prince" is nāśîʾ (H5387), the same word at verse 18, "take one prince of every tribe," and for the princes of verses 19-28, denoting the leader of a tribe; in the KJV letter the title is named at verse 18 and at verses 22-28 ("the prince of the tribe of ..."), verses 19-21 naming the men by tribe without repeating the title, and each verse is registered by its own wording. "Take" rests on the imperative statement of lāqaḥ (H3947), registered by the standard entries; at verse 18 the KJV reads "ye shall take," recorded as it stands. The personal names are all transliterated proper names; where the examination of a name's meaning is disputed (as the old account connecting "Caleb" with "dog"), it has no bearing on this chapter's argument and is uniformly recorded by the base text's rendering only, marked pending verification, as in Endnote 5. "Eleazar" is ʾelʿāzār (H499); at verse 17 he is headed by his office, "the priest," continuing the succession of the priestly office after Aaron's death in chapter 33 (preceding-text layer); only this wording is registered.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The correspondence between the list of dividers and the execution of the division in the book of Joshua (Josh 14:1; 19:51, following-text layer) is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one holds that the two agree in full — the book of Joshua states expressly that the dividers were Eleazar the priest, Joshua the son of Nun, and the heads of the fathers of the tribes, and says it was done "as the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses"; this chapter's list is the very team of that division. Another holds that the two differ in fullness — in Joshua's account of the division the ten princes mostly do not reappear name by name, the narrative taking Eleazar and Joshua as its frame, and the execution details of the list are passed over in the following text. A third looks to the list's order — this chapter places Judah first and the two tribes of Joseph in the middle, the orders of the lists in the several books differing among themselves, which one reading takes to reflect differing traditions of arrangement and another takes to be merely the order of registration. This sermon registers only the points of view of the three readings and the textual facts (the two presiding men agree in full between the two passages; the princes differ in fullness; the orders differ in part), and does not adjudicate the individual whereabouts of the list in the execution at the fact layer; the main text builds only on the naming and the closing as this chapter itself states them.

(Fact) The chapter's data, closed, each item counted (the details standing in Endnote 4): 29 verses in all; the five parts have, in order, 2 verses (34:1-2), 4 (34:3-6), 6 (34:7-12), 3 (34:13-15), and 14 (34:16-29) — 29 in all. Two oracle-openings (34:1, 16); ten verses of four borders — south three, west one, north three, east three; course place-names: south eight, north six, east six, and the west counted separately by its one mark, the great sea; nine tribes and a half receiving, two tribes and a half already received; two men presiding over the division, ten princes, and twenty-two personal names registered with the fathers' names (the two presiding men, the ten princes and their ten fathers; the tribal names of verse 14 not counted). Nine verses quoted verbatim in full (34:2, 3, 6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 17; the Chinese manuscript's closing register numbers the full quotations as "seven" while listing these same nine verses — the count here follows the list itself, and the difference is registered in the Translation Record below). This chapter's joins with the chapters before and after: it follows chapter 33's general record of the journey and its command to cross over (preceding-text layer) — the journey recorded, the borders are drawn next; and it leads into chapter 35's cities of the Levites and cities of refuge (following-text layer) — whose existence only is registered here: the list of division within the four borders does not list the tribe of Levi, whose arrangement of inheritance stands in the next chapter and is not read back into this one.

## Devotional Excerpt

"And the border shall go down to Jordan, and the goings out of it shall be at the salt sea: this shall be your land with the coasts thereof round about." (34:12) Israel was still standing east of Jordan, with not one inch of land under their feet their own, and God first read out to them, sentence by sentence, the four borders west of the river: where the south winds to, where the west stops at the great sea, to which mountain the north is pointed out, and down which river the east descends. Not yet inside, they first knew the extent; not yet in possession, they first heard the borders clear. We often ask God to give us more, and seldom ask Him to draw us the border line of "enough." A border line is not a hindrance but a protection: inside the border, you may cultivate in peace and dwell at rest, without daily comparing yourself with others; outside the border, you may let go in peace, without reaching out for it night by night. Ask God today to do for you what Moses did for the people: draw clear the four borders of this season of yours — which things are your inheritance, and tend them with your heart; which are not your portion, and release them with joy. Only inside coasts closed round about can a man sleep. Tonight, why not draw your own four borders on paper as well: which road in is your south, which great sea is your west, at which mountain your north stops, and down which river your east descends — the places you cannot write clear are exactly the places prayer must be brought to. To draw clear is not to set limits for God; it is to let yourself, within the portion He has given, dwell settled, sleep at rest, and no longer torment the days inside the border with a life outside it.

English (KJV-based):

"And the border shall go down to Jordan, and the goings out of it shall be at the salt sea: this shall be your land with the coasts thereof round about." (Num 34:12, KJV) Israel was still standing east of the Jordan when God read out the borders of a land they had not yet entered. The boundary came before the possession. Ask God to draw your borders clearly this season: tend what is inside with a quiet heart, and release what is outside without envy. A land with its coasts round about is a gift, not a limit.

The general register of the borders — south: the outmost coast of the salt sea eastward → the ascent of Akrabbim → Zin → Kadesh-barnea → Hazar-addar → Azmon → the river of Egypt → the sea; west: the great sea; north: the great sea → mount Hor → the entrance of Hamath → Zedad → Ziphron → Hazar-enan; east: Hazar-enan → Shepham → Riblah → the east side of the sea of Chinnereth → Jordan → the salt sea. The four borders join at three points (the sea, Hazar-enan, the salt sea) and close with no gap — this is a register of the text's structure, and involves no surveying of modern sites.
8. Blanks of this chapter, registered as blanks: Whether the north border's mount Hor is the same as chapter 33's mount Hor, this chapter does not note; the relation between Kadesh-barnea and Kadesh, this chapter does not note; the division of the individual tribes' plots within the four borders, this chapter does not unfold (the book of Joshua governs, following-text layer); the reason the tribe of Levi is not in the list, this chapter does not state (chapter 18 governs, preceding-text layer); the manner of the ten princes' being taken (the procedure of "take"), this chapter does not unfold; the response of the people and the princes after verse 29, this chapter does not record. The above are registered as blanks only, and are not filled by extrapolation.

# Show Me Thy Glory

Preacher: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries

- Title: Show Me Thy Glory
- Subtitle: Is the Promised Land Still the Promised Land Without His Presence?
- Scripture: Exodus 33:1-23 (King James Version)
- Preacher: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Length: About 60 minutes
- Occasion: Sunday sermon / Exodus preaching series
- Language: English (translated from the Chinese original; first translation, pending review)
- Connection to the previous chapter: In the previous chapter, Exodus 32, the people at the foot of Mount Sinai made a golden calf and sinned by worshipping an idol; Moses broke the tablets, interceded for the people, the Levites executed judgment, and a plague came — the covenant relationship had only just been established and was already broken. Exodus 33 asks, in the wake of that breach, the most fundamental question of all: will God still go with this people or not?

## Abstract

After the golden calf incident, God tells the people to set out for the promised land, yet says that He Himself will not go up among them and will send only an angel. The people mourn and strip off their ornaments. Moses pitches the tent of meeting outside the camp and speaks with God face to face, as a man speaks to his friend, then wrestles in intercession inside the tent: if Thy presence does not go with me, do not lead us up from here. The intercession is granted, yet Moses asks for more: show me Thy glory. God answers by letting His goodness pass by, covering him with His hand, proclaiming His name, and showing His sovereign grace.

## Sermon Text

### (00:00-08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all.

Today we come to Exodus 33. This chapter cannot be read by skipping the chapter before it. In the previous chapter, the people grew impatient at the foot of the mountain, pressed Aaron, and made a golden calf out of gold, saying of the idol, these are the gods that brought you out of Egypt. The marriage covenant had not yet lasted a month, and the bride had already worshipped another god. Moses came down, broke the two tablets written by God's own hand, burned the calf to ashes, ground it to powder, and made the people drink it. Judgment came, and a plague came as well. When Exodus 32 ends, the story has no resolution and the relationship has not been restored; it hangs like a breath held in midair.

Exodus 33 speaks into that held breath. It does not rush forward, does not move straight on to new tablets or to building the tabernacle. It first deals with a deeper question: will God still go or not? Is the journey still to be made, the land still to be entered, and are the people still to be God's people? If God does not go with them, then even if the road to Canaan is completed, it remains only a geographical road; even if the promised land is obtained, it remains only a piece of land. From beginning to end, this chapter is occupied with one thing only: presence. Can God's presence be lost, can what is lost be recovered, and if it is recovered, to what extent — and can a person then ask to go deeper still and see God's glory? These four questions form the skeleton of the whole chapter.

Let us first see the structure clearly. The chapter has twenty-three verses and falls into four parts. First, verses 1-6: God commands the departure, yet says that He Himself will not go up among them and will send only an angel ahead; hearing this evil tidings, the people mourn, and no one puts on his ornaments. Second, verses 7-11: the scene shifts outside the camp. Moses pitches the tent outside the camp and calls it the tent of meeting; there the LORD speaks with Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend, and the young man Joshua does not depart from the tent. Third, verses 12-17: Moses' intercession, back and forth like a wrestling match — if Thy presence does not go with me, do not lead us up from here; and God answers, My presence shall go with thee, and I will give thee rest. Fourth, verses 18-23: Moses asks to see God's glory. God lets His goodness pass before him and proclaims His name, yet also draws a boundary: man shall not see My face and live. He puts Moses in a cleft of the rock, covers him with His hand, and lets him see only His back.

Before preaching this chapter, let me state the boundaries clearly. First, there is an obvious tension in the text, and this sermon presents it as it stands: verse 11 says the LORD spoke with Moses face to face, while verse 20 says man shall not see My face and live; and Exodus 24 says the elders saw God. Scholarship generally distinguishes these passages by the degree and manner of what "face" refers to. This sermon sets the views side by side and draws no final conclusion. Second, the relationship between the tent of meeting outside the camp in verses 7-11 and the tabernacle commanded in chapters 25-31 — which came first — is a matter of scholarly discussion about dating and source layering; this sermon marks it only as the academic discussion layer. Third, theological exposition and New Testament readings are labelled as such throughout: Paul's quotation of "I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy" in Romans 9 is a New Testament reading; seeing God's glory in the incarnation is preaching appropriation, not to be read back into this chapter's original plain sense. Marking the layers does not weaken the text; it lets the text speak first.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And the LORD said unto Moses, Depart, and go up hence, thou and the people which thou hast brought up out of the land of Egypt, unto the land which I sware unto Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, saying, Unto thy seed will I give it: And I will send an angel before thee... For I will not go up in the midst of thee; for thou art a stiffnecked people: lest I consume thee in the way." (Exodus 33:1-3, KJV, excerpted)

"And Moses said unto the LORD, See, thou sayest unto me, Bring up this people: and thou hast not let me know whom thou wilt send with me. Yet thou hast said, I know thee by name, and thou hast also found grace in my sight. Now therefore, I pray thee, if I have found grace in thy sight, shew me now thy way, that I may know thee, that I may find grace in thy sight: and consider that this nation is thy people. And he said, My presence shall go with thee, and I will give thee rest. And he said unto him, If thy presence go not with me, carry us not up hence. For wherein shall it be known here that I and thy people have found grace in thy sight? is it not in that thou goest with us? so shall we be separated, I and thy people, from all the people that are upon the face of the earth." (Exodus 33:12-16, KJV)

"And he said, I beseech thee, shew me thy glory. And he said, I will make all my goodness pass before thee, and I will proclaim the name of the LORD before thee; and will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and will shew mercy on whom I will shew mercy. And he said, Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live. And the LORD said, Behold, there is a place by me, and thou shalt stand upon a rock: And it shall come to pass, while my glory passeth by, that I will put thee in a clift of the rock, and will cover thee with my hand while I pass by: And I will take away mine hand, and thou shalt see my back parts: but my face shall not be seen." (Exodus 33:18-23, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, after the golden calf, we stand where Moses stood, knowing that we are stiff-necked and yet unable to imagine a road without Thy going with us. Through this chapter today, teach us not to be content with a faith that has gifts but no presence, with a road but not Thy own company; teach us to ask honestly as Moses did, and to receive in peace, within the boundary Thou hast set, what Thou art willing to show. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

### (08:00-24:00) Part One: The Command to Depart and the Withdrawn Presence — I Will Not Go Up in the Midst of Thee (33:1-6)

The tone of the first section is the coldest in the chapter. Read it slowly.

God speaks, and His words hold first a promise, then a distance. He reaffirms His oath to the fathers: the land of Canaan is to be given to the descendants of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob — that has not been withdrawn. He also reaffirms the destination of the exodus: lead this people to the place of which I have told you. The land is still there, the road is still there, the framework of the promise still stands. But then He says two things that sink the heart. First, He no longer calls Israel "My people," but "the people which thou hast brought up out of the land of Egypt," calling the nation Moses' people, as if pushing the relationship a step away. In Exodus 32, Moses in his intercession had done exactly the opposite, reminding God that these were Thy people, whom Thou broughtest out of Egypt with great power. As chapter 33 opens, God still speaks with the distant form of address, which shows that the breach has not yet been repaired and the intercession has not yet been fully answered. The second statement is heavier still: I will send an angel before thee — I myself will not go up in the midst of thee.

Note the contrast. It is not that there is no angel: the angel goes before them, driving out the peoples of Canaan, named one by one in the text, and nothing is lacking in the functions of leading and warfare. Functionally speaking, with an angel leading, the company can still travel, the enemies can still be driven out, and the land flowing with milk and honey can still be entered. If faith counted only function and only results, the crisis of this chapter would not exist at all. But God makes it plain: the angel will go; I will not. That forces the question to the root: what do the people actually want — the land, or God? The blessings, or the One who blesses? The benefits after leaving Egypt, or the covenant relationship itself? The sin of the golden calf was precisely to degrade God into a tool for obtaining benefits, to cast a visible god out of gold to replace the invisible yet truly present God. Now God's word is like a mirror: if what you want is only the land, I can give you only the land; if what you want is only guidance, I can send only an angel to guide you. He hands the choice back to the people as it is.

God does not hide His reason for not going: for thou art a stiffnecked people, lest I consume thee in the way. "Stiffnecked" paints a literal picture: livestock that will not take the yoke, neck rigid, refusing to be led. The reason sounds severe, yet on closer reading it has two sides. One side is the reality of judgment: a holy God travelling on the same road with a rebellious people — His holiness will not overlook sin, and the nearer the company, the truer the danger. "Lest I consume thee in the way" is not threatening rhetoric; it is the fact that holiness and sin cannot coexist. The other side is a kind of restraint hidden in the words: God's not going in person for the moment is itself so that the people will not be consumed on the spot; He temporarily withdraws His presence, as one moves a flame away from what would catch fire. In his commentary on Exodus, Childs draws attention to the narrative function of this passage: it is not a logistical arrangement for the journey, but a theological treatment of the broken covenant, in which land and presence are deliberately separated so that the reader sees that a promise apart from the Promiser Himself is no longer the whole promise.[1] This sermon adopts that structural observation, while its exposition returns to the text itself: the text places "I will not go up in the midst of thee" at the centre of the command, and it wants us to feel first not an itinerary but a loss.

The people's response is where this section most deserves our lingering. Verse 4: when the people heard these evil tidings, they mourned, and no man did put on him his ornaments. The narrator himself calls God's words evil tidings; it is not the people's misunderstanding. In the narrator's eyes, news of God's not going with them is evil tidings. The people's mourning is also real: they did not celebrate that an angel's guidance was enough, did not say that having the land was enough; they mourned. That mourning matters, for it shows that deep down the people also knew what was being lost. The action that follows is more concrete still: no one put on his ornaments. In verses 5-6, the LORD tells Moses to say to the people, take off your ornaments, that I may know what to do with you; and from Mount Horeb onward the Israelites stripped themselves of their ornaments entirely. What are ornaments? They are festive dress, marks of celebration, part of the gold and silver the people had received from the Egyptians at the exodus — and the same kind of object as the earrings that were taken off and cast into the calf in chapter 32. Now they take the ornaments off with their own hands and wear them no more. This sermon marks here a structural observation: removing ornaments forms a narrative contrast. External adornment can be removed, and once removed, a person is still a person and the company still a company; but if God's presence is removed, what remains is only a company with a destination and no companion. By stripping off their ornaments the people acknowledge with a bodily action that without presence there is no fitness for celebration and there ought to be no celebration. Mourning here is not mere emotion; it is the outward form of a theological judgment.

Note also that God's words in verse 5 do not close the door: Ye are a stiffnecked people; now take off thy ornaments, that I may know what to do unto thee. The tone is still severe, but it is no longer a closing word. "That I may know what to do" means the sentence has not been finally fixed and the relationship still has room to turn. The narrative plants a seed here: God's anger is real, and His relenting is not false either, and the space between them will be filled by the tent in the next section and the intercession in the section after that. And after the people strip off their ornaments, the text does not immediately record God's answer; it cuts instead to a tent outside the camp. That cut is itself an interpretation: what answers the question of presence is not a new command, but a place where one man goes out of the camp day after day and speaks with God. Let us follow the camera out of the camp.
### (24:00-42:00) Part Two: The Tent Outside the Camp and the Wrestling Intercession — If Thy Presence Go Not With Me, Carry Us Not Up Hence (33:7-17)

Verse 7 opens with a customary tone: Moses used to take the tent and pitch it outside the camp. This was not a one-off emergency measure but the pattern of a period of time. Before the tabernacle had been built according to the pattern shown on the mountain, and before the covenant relationship had been restored, Moses first pitched a tent outside the camp and called it the tent of meeting. "Tent of meeting" — in Hebrew, the tent of appointed meeting — its point lies not in the scale of the building but in the meeting: this is the place where God and man have appointed to meet. Read its location carefully: outside the camp, yet not far from the camp. Those words are the geographical theology of the whole section. Pitched outside the camp, because the camp had just worshipped an idol; the camp was the place where the sin happened and where the plague fell, and God did not set His meeting place in the very centre of the sin, as if nothing had happened. Yet not far from the camp, because God had not gone so far away that the people could not reach Him; whoever was willing could still go out and seek Him. Separation and nearness stand together in that distance: far enough to declare holiness, near enough to declare mercy.

Everyone who sought the LORD went out to the tent of meeting outside the camp. Note that seeking was no longer waiting in one's own tent; it meant rising, leaving the camp, and walking a stretch of road. Whenever Moses went into the tent, the pillar of cloud descended and stood at the door of the tent, and the LORD talked with Moses; when the people saw the pillar of cloud standing at the door, they each worshipped at the door of their own tent. There are three distances in this picture: Moses inside the tent, talking with God; the people at their own tent doors, worshipping from afar; and the pillar of cloud at the door, the visible sign of God's presence. Nearness still has its order — not everyone could walk straight into that tent — but the order is no longer a hopeless order, because one man enters on their behalf, and a sign tells them that God is still willing to meet people. The intercessor's office is already plain in this picture: he is not a man standing between God and the people collecting a toll; he is the one who first leaves the camp, first pays the cost, and places himself before God.

Verse 11 is the warmest sentence in the chapter, and also the one with the greatest tension: "And the LORD spake unto Moses face to face, as a man speaketh unto his friend." (KJV) "Face to face" — the Hebrew panim appears here in a doubled form, face toward face.[2] The phrase is first of all an idiom of intimacy: as with a friend, without concealment, without a go-between, speaking directly and listening directly. It explains why Moses' intercession can be so bold: he is not submitting a memorial to a distant monarch, but speaking plainly — giving reasons, asking questions, even bargaining — before a God who is willing to speak with him face to face. Yet set this sentence beside verse 20, "there shall no man see me, and live," and the tension appears at once. This sermon sets the scholarly treatments side by side without drawing a conclusion: some scholars hold that "face to face" here is an idiom for the directness and intimacy of the conversation, not for seeing the essence of God's glory with the physical eye; some hold that what is distinguished is the degree and manner of the theophany — the presence in conversation and the glory whose passing Moses later requests differ in degree; others, looking at the text's layers, hold that different passages preserve different traditions of divine manifestation, set side by side without harmonisation, faithfully preserving Israel's paradox that God is both near and not to be looked upon directly. Whichever view is taken, preaching must not preach the tension away: God's nearness is real, and God's not being directly visible is also real, and both realities are to be believed within the same chapter.

At the end of this section there is a quiet detail: Moses returned to the camp, but his young assistant Joshua, the son of Nun, did not depart from the tent. Joshua as yet had no reputation as a commander and no title as successor; he was simply a young man who stayed in the tent. This sermon marks it as a narrative observation: the preparation of the next generation's leader happens first not on the battlefield but in the tent; not departing is sometimes the deepest discipline of all. This stroke also answers the stripping off of ornaments: the people remove their ornaments inside the camp, Joshua keeps his place at the tent outside the camp — mourning and keeping watch, two postures pointing to the same thing: in days when presence is in doubt, what a person can do is mourn his own sin and keep the place of meeting.

The camera returns at verse 12, and the intercession formally begins. Its manner differs from chapter 32: the intercession there was an urgent, single cry for God to relent and not destroy the people; the intercession of verses 12-17 is a wrestling one, advancing sentence by sentence — God answers one sentence, and Moses takes that very sentence and presses one step further — like Jacob's wrestling, and like Abraham's bargaining over Sodom. Follow Moses' advance sentence by sentence. First, he seizes on God's own words: Thou commandest me to bring up this people, yet Thou hast not let me know whom Thou wilt send with me; Thou hast only said, I know thee by name, and thou hast found grace in My sight. Moses' meaning is: since Thou knowest me by name, Thou shouldest not give me a command only and no companion; if the knowing is real, the going together should be real as well. To be known by name is one of Scripture's most intimate expressions: not merely knowing that a person exists, but calling his name and knowing who he is. Moses places God's grace toward him personally at the head of his intercession for the whole people, not out of selfishness, but because an intercessor can only stand within his own relationship with God when he opens his mouth for the people; if he himself had no assurance of grace, he would have no standing ground from which to seek grace for them.

Second, Moses asks for the way: If I have found grace in Thy sight, show me now Thy way, that I may know Thee; and consider that this nation is Thy people. Note the order: he first asks to know God's way, and then asks that the people's identity be considered. "Way" here is not a route map for the journey, but God's manner of acting and God's character. The intercessor asks not first for circumstances to change, but first to know the God who governs the circumstances. The word for grace, chen in Hebrew, recurs throughout this section and is the pivot of the whole intercession.[3] It denotes the favour and acceptance found in the eyes of a superior — not wages earned by man, but kindness freely bestowed. When Moses says again and again, "if I have found grace in Thy sight," it is no courtesy: he stakes everything on this one fact — if the relationship still stands, the road still stands.

God's answer in verse 14 is short, yet it is the hinge of the whole chapter: "My presence shall go with thee, and I will give thee rest." (KJV) "My presence" is, in the Hebrew, literally "my face shall go" — panai, a use of panim denoting God's face, God's personal presence.[2] Commentators generally note that "face" is used here rather than "I" precisely to meet the wrestling that went before: Moses had asked whom Thou wilt send with me; God answers, it is not a matter of sending someone — My very face will go. The distinction between angel and face is closed by God's own hand in this verse: He no longer sends only a representative; He goes in person. "Rest" here is more than a pause on the journey; it carries the echo of entering the promise and ending the wandering: rest without God's going is merely stopping, while rest with God's going is coming home. In his commentary on Exodus, Durham draws particular attention to the turning function of verse 14 in the paragraph's structure: it is the direct answer to the request for the way in verse 12, and the reversal of the withdrawal of presence announced in verses 1-3; by this one sentence the chapter turns from loss to recovery.[4]

Yet Moses does not stop here, and that is the most arresting thing about this wrestling intercession. God has already said, My presence shall go, and yet in verse 15 Moses pushes the words to the utmost: "If thy presence go not with me, carry us not up hence." (KJV) Taken alone, the sentence is close to an ultimatum; read within the intercession, it is faith at its fullest stretch. Moses is in effect saying: I would rather stay in the wilderness, in this very place that has just sinned and just been judged, than lead the people on a road to the promised land without Thee. Wilderness plus presence is better than Canaan minus presence. This is not Moses belittling the promised land — he had walked a lifetime toward that land. It is the order he reaches after weighing: the land is a gift, God is the Giver; the gift can wait, the Giver cannot be absent. In verse 16 he states the reason plainly, and this reason is the argument of the text itself at the factual level, stated by the passage itself with no invention needed from the preacher: "For wherein shall it be known here that I and thy people have found grace in thy sight? is it not in that thou goest with us? so shall we be separated, I and thy people, from all the people that are upon the face of the earth." (KJV) What has ever separated Israel from the nations is not their higher morals, not their more distinctive culture, not their longer history, but God's going with them. Take the presence away, and Israel is no different from the nations; even entered into Canaan, it would be merely one more nation that had taken a piece of land. What Moses requests is not a private experience of intimacy with God; he requests the people's very identity: without presence there is no separation, and without separation the covenant falls empty.

In verse 17 God answers a second time, and more fully than in verse 14: "I will do this thing also that thou hast spoken: for thou hast found grace in my sight, and I know thee by name." (KJV) The words Moses quoted in verse 12, God here returns to him exactly: known by name, grace found in His sight. At this point the intercession is fully granted: God will go in person, the people will not be abandoned, and all that Moses asked, God will do. Here the story could have closed: the tablets could be remade, the tabernacle begun, the company set out. Most of our prayers, answered like this, would end in thanksgiving and Amen. But Moses does not stop. Having obtained the promise that God will go with them, he opens his mouth and asks for something greater still, and this request carries the whole chapter to its peak: show me Thy glory. Note the order of the asking — presence first, glory afterwards; first asking God to go with the people, then asking to see God Himself. Reverse the order, and asking for glory becomes private spiritual ambition; keep the order, and asking for glory is the longing of an intercessor who, having already received grace, steps one step closer to the source of grace.

### (42:00-56:00) Part Three: Asking to See the Glory — Goodness Passing By, the Covering Hand, and the Back (33:18-23)

Verse 18 has only one sentence: Moses says, "I beseech thee, shew me thy glory." (KJV) Glory — kavod in Hebrew — is the last great word of the chapter.[5] Its root carries the sense of weight: glory is not a weightless halo, but presence itself, with weight and majesty. Moses had seen God's holiness at the burning bush, God's power at the Red Sea, and God's majesty in the cloud on Sinai; what he asks for is plainly not merely one more vision. He asks, at this moment when the relationship has just been restored, to know this God more directly than before: what kind of God art Thou, after all? There is an intercessor's logic in the request: he has just obtained God's going with the people, and he needs to know whether the nature of the One who goes with them is more judgment or more mercy. To ask to see the glory is, at bottom, to ask to know who God is.

God's answer falls into two halves, promise and boundary, and neither half is missing. The half of promise is verse 19: "I will make all my goodness pass before thee, and I will proclaim the name of the LORD before thee; and will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and will shew mercy on whom I will shew mercy." (KJV) Goodness — tov in Hebrew — means good, goodly, beautiful.[6] Note how God answers: Moses asks to see, and God answers with passing by and proclaiming; Moses asks for the form of glory, and God gives him the disclosure of His character. He does not say, I will let you see what I look like; He says, I will let you see My goodness, and I will proclaim My name before you. In biblical usage a name is not a label; a name is the sum of who a person is. To proclaim His name is to declare who He is. Exodus 34:6-7, immediately following, gives that proclamation in full: the LORD, merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abundant in goodness and truth. Verse 19 of this chapter is the preface to that proclamation. This sermon marks the point plainly: to understand glory as the disclosure of God's character is a direct statement drawn from this verse's own manner of answering, not an inference by the preacher: God Himself chose to answer the request to see glory with goodness and proclamation; His glory is first of all His goodness.

"I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and will shew mercy on whom I will shew mercy" — this sentence deserves its own pause. It speaks of the sovereignty of grace: grace is grace precisely because it is not a right that man can demand, but God's free gift; on whom He is gracious does not depend on who files the application first, nor on whose record of merit is thicker, but on His own good pleasure. To a people who have just worshipped the calf, this word is grace of unusual weight: their obtaining the promise that God will go with them rests not on the thoroughness of their repentance, but on God's free decision to be gracious to them again. The apostle Paul quotes this very sentence in Romans 9:15 to argue for God's sovereignty in election and mercy. This sermon marks that as the New Testament reading layer: Paul's quotation has its own argumentative context, and preaching that cites it should return to the context of Romans rather than reading all of Paul's argument back into this verse; but the quotation itself proves that, in the eyes of the New Testament author, this sentence is a key text for explaining the nature of God's grace.

The half of boundary is verse 20, and its tone is as firm as the promise: "Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live." (KJV) God does not cancel this boundary because Moses is His friend; on the contrary, precisely because Moses is His friend, God tells him the truth. The tension between this verse and verse 11's "face to face" has already been set out with the scholarly views above; here only one further cut is added: the existence of the tension is itself information. It tells us that when Scripture speaks of God's nearness, it never does so at the cost of cancelling God's transcendence. God can speak with a man as a friend, and man still cannot look directly upon the essence of His glory and live. Nearness and boundary are not two statements undermining each other; they are two sides of the same God: His nearness is grace, and His boundary is also grace, for without the boundary, finite and sinful man could not survive at all before His glory. God's refusing to let Moses see His face and God's permitting Moses to see His back are two movements of the same mercy.

In verses 21-23 God Himself arranges the manner of the seeing, with details as concrete as stage directions: "Behold, there is a place by me, and thou shalt stand upon a rock: And it shall come to pass, while my glory passeth by, that I will put thee in a clift of the rock, and will cover thee with my hand while I pass by: And I will take away mine hand, and thou shalt see my back parts: but my face shall not be seen." (KJV) Rock — tzur in Hebrew — is a common word for solid rock and a place of refuge.[7] The cleft is the crack in the rock, a hollow in which a person can be hidden. The covering hand is the most anthropomorphic stroke in the passage: God covers Moses' eyes with His hand, as an adult shields a child's eyes from a glare too strong to bear. Back — achor in Hebrew — denotes what is behind, the rear, the side seen after passing by.[8] What Moses is given to see is not God's face, but God's back as He looks back after God has passed. To use a daily analogy, and to mark it as an analogy: when a great person walks toward us, we see his face; standing by the roadside and turning after he has passed, we see his back and the traces of his passing. What Moses receives is knowledge of God recognised after His presence has passed: looking back, one understands who it was that just passed; looking directly in the moment, man cannot bear it.

Scholarly treatments of this anthropomorphic description are set side by side here without a conclusion. First, the ancient Near Eastern royal-procession analogy: when a king went out, commoners were not permitted to look directly on the king's face and could only watch his back after he had passed; this passage's passing by, covering hand, and seeing of the back are comparable to such court ceremonial imagery. Second, the degrees-of-theophany view: face and back distinguish degrees of manifestation — the face representing the direct manifestation of God's essence, the back a mediated manifestation that man can bear; what Moses receives is a real manifestation, but a covered one. Third, a narrative-theological reading: the author deliberately uses vivid anthropomorphic language to express an abstract truth, namely that man's knowledge of God is always knowledge after the passing and in retrospect — real, yet not possessive; man cannot grasp God's face in his hand, but can only know Him, after He has passed, by the traces He leaves and the proclamation He gives. The three approaches differ, but they agree in not reading this text as a manual of God's bodily construction. This sermon's preaching position keeps to one line only: anthropomorphic description is Scripture's customary manner of speaking, and the fact it denotes is serious — God truly let Moses know Him more than before, and truly did not let Moses see His face; as for how face and back are to be subdivided in theological ontology, this sermon does not draw a conclusion beyond its warrant.

More importantly, this passage leaves a lasting pattern for everyone who asks to see God: God's answers are often partial — not because He is stingy, but because man's capacity is limited; God's covering is often protection, not refusal. Moses was not disappointed at receiving only the back; he received what was given, and in the next chapter he went up the mountain again with that receiving. By way of labelled appropriation: the New Testament declares that the Word became flesh, and in the Son, Jesus Christ, men beheld His glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father (John 1:14 — a New Testament reading); Hebrews likewise says that He is the brightness of God's glory (Hebrews 1:3 — a New Testament reading). How these New Testament texts develop, upon this chapter's boundary, the seeing that is in Christ is a question for New Testament theology; this sermon only points out the contrast without reading it back into the chapter: Moses in Exodus 33 stands in the cleft of the rock and sees the back, while the disciples of the New Testament see God's glory in the face of Jesus Christ. Read side by side, boundary and fulfilment each stand, and the hearer should distinguish which belongs to Moses' receiving on that day and which belongs to the later sight in Christ.
### (58:00-60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus 33. Take three sentences with you. The first: land without presence is still evil tidings. An angel can lead the way and enemies can be driven out, but if God does not go in person, the promised land is only land; the mark by which we know we have found grace is not the gifts received, but the Giver's going with us. The second: the road of intercession is leaving the camp, wrestling, and refusing to go up alone rather than go without Him. Moses pitched the tent outside the camp, spoke with God as a friend, and argued for the people sentence by sentence, until God said, My presence shall go with thee. His boldness was not rudeness; it stood on the fact of being known by name and having found grace in His sight. The third: whoever asks to see the glory must learn to receive a manifestation given under covering. God let His goodness pass by and proclaimed His name, yet covered Moses with His hand and let him see only His back. Our knowledge of God today is likewise a looking back at the traces of His passing, faithful in the portion He gives, not demanding the whole that He has not given.

Let us pray together: Lord, forgive us for so often wanting only Thy gifts and not Thyself, only a smooth road and not Thy going with us in person. Make us honest like Moses, willing rather to remain in the wilderness than to move into a Canaan without Thee; make us willing to leave the camp, to keep the tent of meeting, and to wrestle in intercession for those around us. Pass before us in Thy goodness, proclaim Thy name to us, let us rest secure within the boundary Thou coverest, and find rest in the goodness Thou revealest. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

On the ancient Near Eastern royal-procession analogy for the anthropomorphic description and on the degrees-of-theophany view, see the discussions in the relevant sections of Childs and Durham; the sermon text relays them only at the academic level.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "If thy presence go not with me, carry us not up hence." (Exodus 33:15, KJV) "I beseech thee, shew me thy glory." (Exodus 33:18, KJV)

In this chapter Moses teaches us two kinds of prayer. The first is a prayer for presence: the land can wait, the road can wait, but God Himself must not be absent. Often we pray for tasks to be finished and troubles to be removed; Moses prayed for God Himself. He would rather remain in the wilderness than settle in a Canaan without God. Ask yourself honestly today: if the blessings remained but the nearness were gone, would you still be satisfied? The second is a prayer for glory: already graced, already promised that God would go with him, Moses dared to ask for more — to know who God is. God did not grant the request in full. He let His goodness pass by, proclaimed His name, covered Moses with His hand, and let him see His back. That was not refusal; it was protection, and grace fitted to our measure. Our deepest knowledge of God often comes not in a moment of direct sight, but in looking back at the traces of His passing. Pray today for both: that you would not exchange the Giver for His gifts, that you would rest content in the goodness He shows, submit in the limits He sets, and follow His presence step by step.

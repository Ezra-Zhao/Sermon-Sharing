# The Head and Not the Tail — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 28

> Status: First translation · Initial translation pending proofreading (首译·初译待校). This is the first English translation of the finalized Chinese sermon on Deuteronomy 28, "The Head and Not the Tail," which passed the Chinese strong re-review (ZH 强复核) on 2026-10-25. All Scripture quotations in this English text are taken verbatim, including punctuation, from the King James Version (KJV), which serves as the base text for quotations in this translation; quotations are never re-translated from the Chinese. Hebrew transliterations and Strong's numbers are carried over from the Chinese source as registered there (with the corrections of the 2026-10-25 strong re-review, as the source now stands). Counts are re-counted from the KJV text itself and registered on the KJV's own account; where the English form differs from the Chinese fixed text's form, both are stated side by side and neither is smoothed over (see notes 2–3 and the production record at the end).

> Verification of the title: Three candidates were considered. (A) "The Head and Not the Tail," from the closing sentence of the blessings in verse 13 — in the Chinese fixed text, "the LORD shall make thee the head and not the tail, but above only and not beneath" in substance; it is the best-known single sentence of the blessing section in the fixed text's own wording, and verse 44 carries its mirror image, "he shall be the head, and thou shalt be the tail" — the same pair of words, occurring once on the side of blessing and once on the side of cursing, so that the title carries both faces in one phrase. (B) "These Blessings Shall Come on Thee and Overtake Thee," from the governing sentence of verse 2: blessing is not waited for, it overtakes; the sentence is the skeleton of the blessing section. (C) "This Glorious and Fearful Name," from the theological high point of verse 58. Candidate (A) is adopted: verse 13 is the gathering-up of the fourteen verses of blessing, and verse 44 is one of the mirror images within the fifty-four verses of cursing — this sermon reads the whole chapter along the mirror line of "head and tail": the same people may be the head or may be the tail, and the difference lies only in hearkening or not hearkening. The English title is "The Head and Not the Tail," following KJV 28:13: "And the LORD shall make thee the head, and not the tail."

> Note on the base text and collation: In the Chinese source, the fixed text is the Chinese Union Version with New Punctuation (CUNPSS), the English base text is the KJV, and the original is collated against the BHS. In this English translation, quotations follow the KJV as the fixed text (chapter anchor: 申28-EN-KJV.txt, the KJV text of Deuteronomy 28 as stored for the 2026-10-25 review batch; see the production record). The numbering of this chapter was verified first-hand: CUNPSS Deuteronomy 28 has 68 verses, aligned verse by verse (n to n) with the Masoretic text and the KJV except at two fusion points, 13–14 and 58–59; the verification was made verse by verse over the whole chapter on the wd.bible verse pages (CUNPSS simplified line, the 2010 edition, KJV, and BHS in parallel). Verses 58–59 are of the same form: on the verse page for deu.28.58 the CUNPSS line contains, within one verse, the conditional sentence and the beginning of the consequence sentence (ending at a comma, the sentence unfinished), its content covering English verses 58–59 together; the verse page for deu.28.59 carries no CUNPSS line, its first line being the 2010 edition — so in the Chinese source the quotation for verse 58 follows the whole CUNPSS line, and verse 59 is additionally registered by collation against the 2010 line, with its source expressly noted, and is not passed off as a CUNPSS verse-59 line. In this English translation those two registrations are translated as registrations of the Chinese side's form; the English quotations themselves follow the KJV's own 68 verses, quoted verse by verse (verses 13 and 14 separately, verses 58 and 59 separately). The chapters of this book each take their own numbering form (chapter 12's final verse displaced, chapter 13 with two empty numbers and two fusions, chapter 15 with one empty number and one fusion, chapter 23 displaced as a whole by one, chapters 24–27 back to same-number alignment), and this chapter, like chapters 24–27, is of the same-number alignment type in its main body, with fusions only at 13/14 and 58/59 — verified chapter by chapter first-hand, with no inference from another chapter; this is the general account of the numbering (note 1). The range over which this chapter's paragraph markers were checked is registered as it stands: a samekh was seen at the end of the BHS lines for verses 14 and 68 and confirmed by direct checking; the remaining verses were not registered verse by verse, and no assertion is made for the whole chapter (note 1). The account of quotation marks in this chapter was counted verse by verse against the fixed text: the discourse quotation opens at verse 1 and re-opens in sections at several points (verses 7, 15, 25, 36, 47, 49, 58); a closing mark occurs once, at the end of the combined 13–14 line (CUNPSS has no independent verse-14 line; registered according to the facts), and the chapter's quotation finally closes at the end of verse 68 (note 1). The KJV text of this chapter carries no quotation marks; direct discourse is carried by the narrative itself, and quotations in this translation reproduce the KJV verbatim, including its punctuation. The comparison of the blessings and curses of Deuteronomy 28 with Leviticus 26, the reading of "this glorious and fearful name" in 28:58, and the readings of Habakkuk 2:4 and Galatians 3 are stated side by side and registered only, not adjudicated (note 7).

> Note on the format: This sermon consists of an introduction, four sections of body text, and a conclusion; the body covers 28:1–68, and the time codes run continuously over sixty minutes (Introduction 00:00–06:00; Section One 06:00–17:00, the blessing section, verses 1–14; Section Two 17:00–29:00, the general statement of the curses and the bodily calamities, verses 15–34; Section Three 29:00–41:00, fruitlessness, reversal, and siege, verses 35–52; Section Four 41:00–53:00, the extremity of the siege and scattering and destruction, verses 53–68; Conclusion 53:00–60:00). Within each section the writing is marked in five layers: factual layer, theological layer, summary layer, New Testament reading layer, and scholarly and historical discussion layer. Four adjudication disciplines are declared in advance: First, the agreements and differences between Deuteronomy 28 and Leviticus 26 are stated side by side and registered only; the relation between the two chapters is not adjudicated. Second, the readings of "this glorious and fearful name" in 28:58, the two readings of "faith / faithfulness" in Habakkuk 2:4, and the reading in Galatians 3 are registered only, not adjudicated. Third, the ethics and present-day application of the curse section are stated side by side in the discussion layer only, and the prohibitions on present-day transplantation are registered on the record (note 9): no curse in this chapter may be transplanted into an assertion about the sickness, calamity, poverty, or wealth of any present-day individual or group. Fourth, the names of diseases register only the fixed text's renderings and the sense of the original words; no correspondence to modern medical diagnoses is made. ## Introduction (00:00–06:00)

[Summary layer] In chapter 27 the people stand between the two mountains and answer "Amen" item by item — but that chapter carries only the text of the curses; the blessings have a position to stand in and no words. Chapter 28 supplies the other half: the blessings are written out sentence by sentence, fourteen verses; the curses are also written out sentence by sentence, fifty-four verses. Only when both sides are written in full is the double face of the covenant complete. This chapter is the gathering peak of Moses' Moab discourse: before it stand the detailed ordinances (chapters 12–26); here stands the whole map of reward and punishment behind the details — if you hearken, thus; if you do not hearken, thus. Both roads are drawn to their end, so that the generation standing at the Jordan may see clearly before they cross over. [Factual layer] Three structural facts are registered first. First, the disparity of length: blessings 14 verses, curses 54 verses, a ratio of verse counts of nearly one to four (counted by hand, note 2); the several readings of this are stated side by side in the discussion layer of Section Four. Second, the two-track form: each of the two sections opens with a governing sentence (v. 2, v. 15), and the sentence patterns are symmetrical — blessings and curses alike "come upon" and "overtake" a man. Third, this chapter is a discourse, not a rite: chapter 27 has stones, an altar, and men standing in position; chapter 28 has words only. [Theological layer] The course of this sermon's four sections: Section One reads the blessings (vv. 1–14); Section Two reads the opening of the curses and the calamities of the body (vv. 15–34), watching how the calamities mirror the blessings item by item and fall first on the body; Section Three reads labour without fruit and the reversal of society (vv. 35–52); Section Four reads the extremity of the siege and the close in scattering and destruction (vv. 53–68), and pauses at the deepest point: "this glorious and fearful name" in verse 58, and "no man shall buy you" in verse 68. This sermon's argument rests only on the text itself — mirror structure, the same words reversed, and the distribution of the counts; wherever a question concerns the relation between the two chapters or a dispute of readings, everything is stated side by side and not adjudicated (disciplines one and two).

## Section One: These Blessings Shall Come on Thee and Overtake Thee (Deut 28:1–14; 06:00–17:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 1 is the conditional governing sentence of the blessing section; in the Chinese fixed text the discourse quotation opens at this verse. The KJV text in full: "And it shall come to pass, if thou shalt hearken diligently unto the voice of the LORD thy God, to observe and to do all his commandments which I command thee this day, that the LORD thy God will set thee on high above all nations of the earth:" Registered item by item: (i) The condition carries two verbs — "hearken diligently" (šāmaʿ, H8085) and "observe and to do" (šāmar, H8104): hearkening and keeping are the master switch of every blessing and calamity in this chapter, and the governing sentence of the curses in verse 15 opens with the negative form of the same pair of verbs (note 4). (ii) "Which I command thee this day" — the point of promulgation of the commandments is fixed at this day. (iii) The first sentence of the result gives not a thing but a position: "will set thee on high above all nations of the earth" — the blessing section begins with position and also ends with position (v. 13, "the head, and not the tail"); head and tail registered. [Factual layer] Verse 2 is the governing sentence of the blessings' arrival, in full: "And all these blessings shall come on thee, and overtake thee, if thou shalt hearken unto the voice of the LORD thy God." Registered item by item: (i) "Blessings" is bərākâ (H1293), for which BDB expressly cites this verse and verse 8 — blessing here is not abstract luck; it is the itemised list that follows (vv. 3–14). (ii) The verbs are "shall come on thee" and "overtake thee": man keeps the covenant, and blessing comes pursuing man; this verb group recurs verbatim in verses 15 and 45 with the curses as its subject (note 4). (iii) In the Chinese fixed text this verse ends with a colon, after which the items are unfolded one by one — the opening form of list style; the KJV ends the verse with a full stop, and the list follows in verses 3–14. [Factual layer] Verses 3–6 are the fourfold chain of blessing, in full: "Blessed shalt thou be in the city, and blessed shalt thou be in the field. Blessed shall be the fruit of thy body, and the fruit of thy ground, and the fruit of thy cattle, the increase of thy kine, and the flocks of thy sheep. Blessed shall be thy basket and thy store. Blessed shalt thou be when thou comest in, and blessed shalt thou be when thou goest out." Registered item by item: (i) Verse 3 covers the whole territory of space — in the city and in the field, dwelling and working, blessed at both ends. (ii) Verse 4 lists five products, counted by hand: the fruit of thy body, the fruit of thy ground, the fruit of thy cattle, the increase of thy kine, and the flocks of thy sheep — man, field, and livestock, all three realms listed (note 2); "the fruit of thy body" occurs four times in the whole chapter (vv. 4, 11, 18, 53, note 4), its first occurrence here, as the first item of the blessing list. (iii) Verse 5 names two implements: the basket (for storing) and the store — in the Chinese fixed text the second is the kneading trough (for processing), and the 2010 edition likewise renders it as a kneading trough (note 3); the KJV anchor's marginal gloss at this verse ("dough, or, kneadingtroughs") preserves the same sense — blessing comes upon the everyday utensils. (iv) Verse 6 covers the whole of time in movement and rest: coming in and going out — the Chinese fixed text puts going out first and coming in after, while the KJV puts coming in first and going out after (note 3). (v) The blessed-form runs in a chain through the four verses: on the KJV's own account, "Blessed / blessed" occurs twice in verse 3, once in verse 4, once in verse 5, and twice in verse 6, six occurrences in all; on the Chinese fixed text's account, verses 3, 4, and 5 read "shall surely be blessed" (with the word for "surely") and verse 6 twice reads "also be blessed" (without that word) — the two accounts are stated side by side in note 2. This is the densest drumbeat of the blessing section. [Factual layer] Verses 7–8 treat war and the storehouse, in full: "The LORD shall cause thine enemies that rise up against thee to be smitten before thy face: they shall come out against thee one way, and flee before thee seven ways. The LORD shall command the blessing upon thee in thy storehouses, and in all that thou settest thine hand unto; and he shall bless thee in the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee." Registered item by item: (i) In the Chinese fixed text the quotation re-opens in a new section at verse 7 (note 1). "They shall come out against thee one way, and flee before thee seven ways" — a numerical contrast of one and seven, whose mirror image is in verse 25 (note 4). (ii) In verse 8 blessing comes to three places: the storehouses (what is laid up), all that thou settest thine hand unto (what increases), and the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee (the inheritance). (iii) "Shall command the blessing" — blessing is dispatched by command; it is not accidental weather. [Factual layer] Verses 9–10 treat identity and witness, in full: "The LORD shall establish thee an holy people unto himself, as he hath sworn unto thee, if thou shalt keep the commandments of the LORD thy God, and walk in his ways. And all people of the earth shall see that thou art called by the name of the LORD; and they shall be afraid of thee." Registered item by item: (i) In verse 9 the condition is restated, and the result is "shall establish thee an holy people unto himself" — identity rests on an oath ("as he hath sworn unto thee"); it is not self-conferred. (ii) In verse 10 the witness overflows outward: the peoples "shall see" and "shall be afraid" — the yārēʾ (H3372) of this verse is the peoples' fear of Israel; a niphal participle of the same root in verse 58 is Israel's fear of the name of God (note 4): the directions of fear in the two verses are exactly opposite. [Factual layer] Verses 11–12 treat plenteousness and the heavenly treasure, in full: "And the LORD shall make thee plenteous in goods, in the fruit of thy body, and in the fruit of thy cattle, and in the fruit of thy ground, in the land which the LORD sware unto thy fathers to give thee. The LORD shall open unto thee his good treasure, the heaven to give the rain unto thy land in his season, and to bless all the work of thine hand: and thou shalt lend unto many nations, and thou shalt not borrow." Registered item by item: (i) Verse 11 lists the three products again, and the measure is "plenteous in goods" — the measure of blessing: not barely enough, but more than enough. (ii) "His good treasure, the heaven" in verse 12 is the first of the chapter's four heaven images (vv. 12, 23, 24, 62, note 2): heaven is a treasure-house, and rain in its season is the opening of the store and the issuing of grain. (iii) The direction of lending is fixed here: lending out only, never borrowing in — its mirror image is in verse 44 (note 4). [Factual layer] Verses 13–14 are the close of the blessing section, in full in the KJV's own two verses: "And the LORD shall make thee the head, and not the tail; and thou shalt be above only, and thou shalt not be beneath; if that thou hearken unto the commandments of the LORD thy God, which I command thee this day, to observe and to do them: And thou shalt not go aside from any of the words which I command thee this day, to the right hand, or to the left, to go after other gods to serve them." The Chinese-side form is registered as it stands, with its sources expressly noted: in the Chinese fixed text these two verses are printed as one combined 13–14 line, within which stands the half-sentence corresponding to "not go aside ... to go after other gods to serve them," and the combined line closes with a single closing quotation mark; the 2010 edition divides them into two verses — its verse 13 does not carry that half-sentence, and its content stands in its verse 14 in a separately opening form (notes 1, 3) — the Chinese quotation's body follows the CUNPSS combined line, and verse 14 is separately registered by collation against the 2010 line. Registered item by item: (i) "The head, and not the tail" — rōʾš (H7218) and zānāb (H2180); the BDB entries for both words expressly cite this verse and verse 44. (ii) The CUNPSS combined-line form and the 2010 two-verse form are stated side by side (note 3). (iii) A closing quotation mark occurs only once, at the end of the CUNPSS combined line; the closing mark at the end of verse 14 in the 2010 line belongs to the 2010 edition's divided form, and the two forms are stated side by side (note 1). (iv) At the end of verse 14 in the BHS a samekh is seen (confirmed by direct checking, note 1) — in the Masoretic paragraph markers the blessing section closes as a small unit of its own. [Theological layer] In the blessing section, attend to the order and the coverage of the blessings: first position ("on high above all nations"), then the whole territory (city, field, body, cattle, implements, coming in and going out), then security (the enemies routed), then identity (established as a holy people), then surplus (plenteous in goods, the heavenly treasure opened, lending without borrowing), and at last back to position ("the head, and not the tail"). Blessing is not good luck at a single point; it is the whole of a people's life covered by the covenant — and the condition in every verse hangs on the same one thing: hearkening and keeping. Fourteen verses of blessing ask only one thing. [Summary layer] This section may be taken in four points: (i) blessings shall overtake thee (vv. 1–2); (ii) blessed in the whole territory (vv. 3–6); (iii) a holy people and surplus (vv. 7–12: enemies routed, the covenant established, the treasure opened); (iv) the head and not the tail (vv. 13–14, the close, and the planted point of the mirror image). [New Testament reading layer] This section registers one reading only, with no reading back: the list form of "all these blessings shall come on thee, and overtake thee" in the blessing section and the form of the New Testament's discourse on blessing (the itemised declarations of "Blessed are..." in the Sermon on the Mount) belong alike to list-style proclamation — the formal echo is registered thus; the blessings of this chapter rest on the collective situation of covenant-keeping, and the blessings of the New Testament rest on the identity of the people of the kingdom; the difference of foundation is stated side by side, and the two are not read together as one. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] This section registers one matter: the proportion of length between the blessing section and the curse section (14 to 54) — in the parallel material of ancient Near Eastern covenant documents, a curse section longer than the blessing section is a common form (registered generally at the discussion level, with no unverified text cited) — this formal background explains only the ancestry of the length; it does not answer in the chapter's own place: the text does not state its reason expressly, and this sermon does not answer in its place (note 8).

## Section Two: All These Curses Shall Come upon Thee and Overtake Thee (Deut 28:15–34; 17:00–29:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 15 is the governing sentence of the curses; in the Chinese fixed text the quotation re-opens in a new section here. In full: "But it shall come to pass, if thou wilt not hearken unto the voice of the LORD thy God, to observe to do all his commandments and his statutes which I command thee this day; that all these curses shall come upon thee, and overtake thee:" Registered item by item: (i) The condition mirrors verse 1 word by word — verse 1 has "hearken diligently / observe and to do," verse 15 has "wilt not hearken / to observe to do" in the negative: the same pair of verbs, once positive and once negative (note 4). (ii) "Curses" is qəlālâ (H7045), for which BDB expressly cites this verse and verse 45 — verse 45 is the mid-course restatement of this governing sentence. (iii) Written form: throughout the chapter CUNPSS writes the word in one character order ("curse" in its fixed order), while the 2010 edition writes it in the reverse order (note 3). (iv) In the Chinese fixed text this verse ends with a colon, in the same form as verse 2; the KJV ends it with a colon as well. [Factual layer] Verses 16–19 are the fourfold chain of cursing, answering verses 3–6 item by item, in full: "Cursed shalt thou be in the city, and cursed shalt thou be in the field. Cursed shall be thy basket and thy store. Cursed shall be the fruit of thy body, and the fruit of thy land, the increase of thy kine, and the flocks of thy sheep. Cursed shalt thou be when thou comest in, and cursed shalt thou be when thou goest out." Registered item by item: (i) Verse 16, city and field; verse 17, basket and store; verse 19, coming in and going out — in the same order and with the same objects as verses 3, 5, and 6, with only the verdict changed. (ii) Verse 18 lists only four items, one fewer than the five items of verse 4 — "the fruit of thy cattle" is missing (counted by hand, note 2) — the mirror image is not a word-for-word copy; this discrepancy is registered only and not explained away. (iii) As blessing came item by item, so cursing comes item by item: both roads cover the whole territory; there is no grey ground. On the KJV's own account the "Cursed / cursed" forms of verses 16–19 likewise number six (twice in v. 16, once in v. 17, once in v. 18, twice in v. 19), answering the six blessed-forms of verses 3–6 position by position (note 2). [Factual layer] Verse 20 is the first gathering-up of the curses, in full: "The LORD shall send upon thee cursing, vexation, and rebuke, in all that thou settest thine hand unto for to do, until thou be destroyed, and until thou perish quickly; because of the wickedness of thy doings, whereby thou hast forsaken me." Registered item by item: (i) A triple list of names: cursing, vexation, and rebuke. (ii) "In all that thou settest thine hand unto" reverses the use of the same phrase in verse 8, where blessing came (note 4). (iii) "Destroyed" in the original is šāmaḏ (H8045), for which BDB expressly cites this verse; in the whole chapter it occurs seven times, recurring at verses 24, 45, 48, 51, 61, and 63 (note 2) — it is the closing word of the curse section. On the KJV's own surface account, the destroy-family forms are registered separately in note 2 and stated side by side with the Hebrew account, not harmonised. [Factual layer] Verses 21–22 open the list of bodily calamities, in full: "The LORD shall make the pestilence cleave unto thee, until he have consumed thee from off the land, whither thou goest to possess it. The LORD shall smite thee with a consumption, and with a fever, and with an inflammation, and with an extreme burning, and with the sword, and with blasting, and with mildew; and they shall pursue thee until thou perish." Registered item by item: (i) In verse 21 "pestilence" is deber (H1698), for which BDB expressly cites this verse; the verb "cleave" is the causative form of dābaq (H1692), for which BDB expressly cites this verse: the positive face of this word is Deuteronomy 10:20, cleaving unto God (verified word by word first-hand in the Chinese source, note 6) — man is to cleave to God; for the man who does not cleave to God, the pestilence comes and cleaves to him (note 4). (ii) Verse 22 lists seven items, counted by hand: consumption (šāḥepet, H7829), fever (qaddaḥat, H6920), inflammation (dalleqet, word-form unverified as to its number), extreme burning (ḥarḥur, H2746; the original sense is violent heat, and the gap between that sense and the Chinese fixed text's rendering is registered in note 5), the sword, blasting (šiddāpôn, word-form unverified; the Chinese fixed text's interlinear note is reproduced as it stands), and mildew (yērāqôn, word-form unverified) — four names of bodily sickness and three of calamity (note 2). In the KJV the seven stand as: a consumption, a fever, an inflammation, an extreme burning, the sword, blasting, and mildew. (iii) šāḥepet and qaddaḥat each occur only once more in the whole Scripture, both in Leviticus 26:16 (verified on the lexicon pages, note 6). (iv) "They shall pursue thee" — in verse 2 blessing overtakes man; in verse 22 calamity pursues man; the two pursuits are stated side by side. [Factual layer] Verses 23–24 are the reversal of heaven and earth, in full: "And thy heaven that is over thy head shall be brass, and the earth that is under thee shall be iron. The LORD shall make the rain of thy land powder and dust: from heaven shall it come down upon thee, until thou be destroyed." Registered item by item: (i) In verse 23 heaven is brass and earth is iron — in verse 12 heaven was a treasure-house giving rain in its season; here heaven is brass: the same heaven over the head, in one place a storehouse, in the other a lid. (ii) In verse 24 the rain becomes powder and dust, "from heaven shall it come down upon thee" — three of the four heaven images have now appeared (vv. 12, 23, 24, note 2). (iii) Brass and iron are alike impenetrable and unproductive things: heaven lets no rain through, earth brings forth no grain; the calamity falls on the root of the ecology. [Factual layer] Verses 25–26 treat defeat in battle and the corpse, in full: "The LORD shall cause thee to be smitten before thine enemies: thou shalt go out one way against them, and flee seven ways before them: and shalt be removed into all the kingdoms of the earth. And thy carcase shall be meat unto all fowls of the air, and unto the beasts of the earth, and no man shall fray them away." Registered item by item: (i) In the Chinese fixed text the quotation re-opens in a new section at verse 25. "Thou shalt go out one way ... and flee seven ways" mirrors verse 7 number for number — in verse 7 it is the enemies who flee; in verse 25 it is oneself: one and seven are the same, and the subject of the fleeing has changed (note 4). (ii) The gaze of verse 10, in which the peoples were afraid of thee, here becomes a gaze at a man removed into all the kingdoms. (iii) In verse 26 the carcase has no man to fray the birds and beasts away — exposure of the corpse was among the deepest shames of the ancient world: the writing moves from the situation of the living to the treatment of the dead, and does not draw back. [Factual layer] Verses 27–28 name sicknesses of the skin and of the mind, in full: "The LORD will smite thee with the botch of Egypt, and with the emerods , and with the scab, and with the itch, whereof thou canst not be healed. The LORD shall smite thee with madness, and blindness, and astonishment of heart:" Registered item by item: (i) Verse 27 lists four items: the botch of Egypt (šəḥîn, H7822; BDB expressly cites the construct form of this verse, and the same word occurs in verse 35), the emerods, the scab, and the itch (for the last three, only the fixed texts' renderings are registered). (ii) "The botch of Egypt" and "all the diseases of Egypt" in verse 60 answer one another within the text (note 4). (iii) Verse 28 lists three items: madness, blindness, and astonishment of heart — the names of sickness turn from the skin inward to the mind and the senses. [Factual layer] Verses 29–34 are the daily life without rescue, in full: "And thou shalt grope at noonday, as the blind gropeth in darkness, and thou shalt not prosper in thy ways: and thou shalt be only oppressed and spoiled evermore, and no man shall save thee. Thou shalt betroth a wife, and another man shall lie with her: thou shalt build an house, and thou shalt not dwell therein: thou shalt plant a vineyard, and shalt not gather the grapes thereof. Thine ox shall be slain before thine eyes, and thou shalt not eat thereof: thine ass shall be violently taken away from before thy face, and shall not be restored to thee: thy sheep shall be given unto thine enemies, and thou shalt have none to rescue them. Thy sons and thy daughters shall be given unto another people, and thine eyes shall look, and fail with longing for them all the day long: and there shall be no might in thine hand. The fruit of thy land, and all thy labours, shall a nation which thou knowest not eat up; and thou shalt be only oppressed and crushed alway: So that thou shalt be mad for the sight of thine eyes which thou shalt see." Registered item by item: (i) "Grope at noonday" in verse 29 is the blindness of verse 28 turned into a picture; "no man shall save thee" occurs here for the first time and recurs in verse 31 (note 2); in the KJV the two rescuer-positions read "no man shall save thee" (v. 29) and "none to rescue them" (v. 31) — the forms differ, the positions agree. (ii) Verse 30 is a triple fruitlessness: a wife betrothed, and another man lies with her; a house built, and thou shalt not dwell therein; a vineyard planted, and thou shalt not gather its grapes — forming a positive-and-negative pair with the three exemptions of Deuteronomy 20:5–7 (treated in the sermon on chapter 20; the section index is registered only): there the law protects three great matters of life, here the three matters fall empty one by one. (iii) Verse 31 is a triple list: ox, ass, and sheep, before thine eyes slain, taken away, and given to enemies. (iv) In verse 32 sons and daughters are given to another people; the parents' "eyes shall look, and fail with longing" — the blindness of verse 28 here passes from a name of sickness into an actual scene. (v) In the Chinese fixed text verse 33 ends with a comma and the sentence crosses into verse 34 — the trajectory of the eyes within this group of verses: blindness (v. 28), groping (v. 29), before thine eyes (v. 31), failing with longing (v. 32), the sight of thine eyes (v. 34), growing heavier verse by verse and closing in madness, joined head-to-tail with the madness of verse 28. [Theological layer] In Section Two, grasp the one word "mirror image": the curses are not another composition written separately; they are the blessings turned over item by item — the same city, the same field, the same hands, the same road, the same heaven. The covenant's reward and punishment are not two worlds; they are the same world changed in colour throughout, according to hearkening or its opposite. There is a further layer within the names of sickness: the calamities fall first on the body (vv. 21–22, 27–28), then on the eyes and the mind (vv. 29–34) — the advance has a direction: from outside inward, from the skin to the heart and spirit. [Summary layer] This section may be taken in four points: (i) the governing sentence mirrored (v. 15 against v. 1); (ii) the fourfold chain turned over (vv. 16–19 against vv. 3–6); (iii) the names of sickness opened out (vv. 21–28: pestilence, the seven items, the four items, the three items); (iv) eyes and the absence of rescue (vv. 29–34: the triple fruitlessness, no man to save, the trajectory of the eyes). [New Testament reading layer] This section registers one reading only, with no reading back: the answering of "the botch of Egypt" in verse 27 and "the diseases of Egypt" in verse 60, and the formal echo of Egyptian-style plagues coming again in the New Testament book of Revelation (the relevant passages are registered by index only; this sermon did not verify them word by word first-hand and does not quote them) — registered thus, with the relation not adjudicated. For the boundary of present-day use of this section's names of sickness, see note 9. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] This section states two items side by side. First, the correspondence of the sickness lists of verses 22, 27, and 28 to ancient and modern medicine as proposed by commentators differs from one to another — under discipline four this sermon registers only the fixed texts' renderings and the sense of the original words, and makes no diagnostic correspondence (note 8). Second, the formula of verse 26, the carcase given to the fowls and the beasts as meat, belongs among the common motifs of the curse sections of ancient Near Eastern covenant documents (registered generally at the discussion level, with no unverified text cited) — this background explains only the formal ancestry of the formula; it does not lessen the weight of this verse for the men of the covenant.

## Section Three: Labour without Fruit and the Reversal of Place (Deut 28:35–52; 29:00–41:00)

[Factual layer] In verse 35 the sore botch recurs, in full: "The LORD shall smite thee in the knees, and in the legs, with a sore botch that cannot be healed, from the sole of thy foot unto the top of thy head." Registered item by item: (i) "Botch" is still šəḥîn (H7822), for which BDB expressly cites this verse in the form šəḥîn rāʿ ("a sore botch") — in verse 27 it was the botch of Egypt; in verse 35 it is a botch from the sole of the foot to the top of the head: the same word moves from a part to the whole man. (ii) "That cannot be healed" is in the same tone as "whereof thou canst not be healed" in verse 27; it is the ground note of the verdicts in this group of verses (answering the sickness-and-calamity section of vv. 58–61). (iii) The order of the parts: knees and legs (the instruments of movement) first, the sole of the foot to the top of the head (the whole body's length) after. [Factual layer] Verses 36–37 treat captivity and becoming a byword, in full: "The LORD shall bring thee, and thy king which thou shalt set over thee, unto a nation which neither thou nor thy fathers have known; and there shalt thou serve other gods, wood and stone. And thou shalt become an astonishment, a proverb, and a byword, among all nations whither the LORD shall lead thee." Registered item by item: (i) In the Chinese fixed text the quotation re-opens in a new section at verse 36. "Thy king which thou shalt set over thee" — this book's chapter 17 has the case of setting up a king (the section index is registered only): king and people are carried away together; the monarch cannot be exempted from the covenant's consequences. (ii) "Serve other gods, wood and stone" — ʿābaḏ (H5647) in its sense of serving other gods, for which BDB expressly cites this verse and verse 64: the object of service is here wholly reversed (note 4). (iii) Verse 37 is a triple list: an astonishment, a proverb, and a byword — the gaze of verse 10, in which the peoples were afraid of thee, here becomes a gaze of derision: the witness is reversed into a laughingstock, and it is still the peoples who are watching. [Factual layer] Verses 38–42 are the chain of fruitlessness in the fields, in full: "Thou shalt carry much seed out into the field, and shalt gather but little in; for the locust shall consume it. Thou shalt plant vineyards, and dress them, but shalt neither drink of the wine, nor gather the grapes; for the worms shall eat them. Thou shalt have olive trees throughout all thy coasts, but thou shalt not anoint thyself with the oil; for thine olive shall cast his fruit. Thou shalt beget sons and daughters, but thou shalt not enjoy them; for they shall go into captivity. All thy trees and fruit of thy land shall the locust consume." Registered item by item: (i) The "locust" of verse 38 is ʾarbeh (H697), for which BDB expressly cites this verse; the insect of verse 42 is ṣəlāṣal (H6767), for which BDB expressly cites this verse and notes that the species is not determined — the two verses alike concern locust calamity and use different words (note 8); the KJV renders both places with "locust." (ii) "Dress" (the vineyards) in verse 39 is still ʿābaḏ in its tillage sense (the BDB sense expressly cites this verse) — tillage is ʿābaḏ, serving God is ʿābaḏ, and serving other gods and serving enemies are also ʿābaḏ (note 4): labour and worship share one word in the original. (iii) The forms of fruitlessness change verse by verse: insects eating (vv. 38, 39, 42), fruit casting itself (v. 40), men carried away (the sons and daughters of v. 41) — field, vineyard, olive trees, sons and daughters, trees and produce: five verses in a chain, all falling empty. (iv) Verse 41 and verse 32 answer one another within the text: the list does not avoid repetition, and the repetition itself is an intensification. [Factual layer] Verses 43–44 are the hinge of the reversal of place, in full: "The stranger that is within thee shall get up above thee very high; and thou shalt come down very low. He shall lend to thee, and thou shalt not lend to him: he shall be the head, and thou shalt be the tail." Registered item by item: (i) "The stranger" is gēr (H1616), for which BDB expressly cites this verse — the ordinances of this book repeatedly command kindness to the stranger (the section index is registered only), and the reversal lies precisely here: the man commanded to be cared for rises to the top. (ii) "Very high / very low" (in the Chinese fixed text, "higher and higher / lower and lower") is a stepwise progression: the reversal does not arrive in one step; it is gradual. Verse 44 is the settlement of the reversal: verse 12, "thou shalt lend unto many nations, and thou shalt not borrow," is turned over word by word, and verse 13, "the head, and not the tail," is turned over word by word (note 4) — the sentence from which the title comes has its mirror image in this very verse. (iii) For the Chinese-side written forms at this verse, see note 3. [Factual layer] Verses 45–46 are the mid-course gathering-up of the curse section, in full: "Moreover all these curses shall come upon thee, and shall pursue thee, and overtake thee, till thou be destroyed; because thou hearkenedst not unto the voice of the LORD thy God, to keep his commandments and his statutes which he commanded thee: And they shall be upon thee for a sign and for a wonder, and upon thy seed for ever." Registered item by item: (i) Verse 45 restates the governing sentence of verse 15 in its original form and adds "overtake" — beyond coming upon and pursuing, they shall surely overtake; the third occurrence of šāmaḏ likewise stands in this verse (note 2). (ii) Only one ground is given: hearkening not, and keeping not; no second ground is given. (iii) In verse 46, "a sign and for a wonder" — ʾôt (H226) and môpêt (H4159); the BDB entries for both words expressly cite this verse, the latter noting that the word is used here of the effect of a curse: the curses are characterised as a "sign" written for the seed to read, and in the Chinese fixed text the sentence closes with an exclamation mark (present in CUNPSS, absent in the modern-punctuation edition, note 3); the KJV closes verse 46 with a full stop. [Factual layer] Verses 47–48 are the turning hinge of the whole section, in full: "Because thou servedst not the LORD thy God with joyfulness, and with gladness of heart, for the abundance of all things; Therefore shalt thou serve thine enemies which the LORD shall send against thee, in hunger, and in thirst, and in nakedness, and in want of all things: and he shall put a yoke of iron upon thy neck, until he have destroyed thee." Registered item by item: (i) In the Chinese fixed text the quotation re-opens in a new section at verse 47 and ends with a comma, the sentence crossing into verse 48; the charge is unexpected: it is not the great offence of idolatry in statutory form, but "thou servedst not ... with joyfulness, and with gladness of heart, for the abundance of all things" — śimḥâ (H8057), verified on the lexicon page, which expressly cites this verse: the general ascription of the curses lands here upon an attitude. (ii) Verse 48 lists four wants: hunger, thirst, nakedness, and want of all things — answering "the abundance of all things" in verse 47 item by item: the reverse side is not one item but all four wanting. (iii) The sharpest reversal of the whole chapter stands here: the object of "serve" in verse 47 is the LORD thy God; its object in verse 48 is thine enemies — the same word ʿābaḏ (note 4): a man who does not serve God does not thereby serve no one; the only question is whom he serves. (iv) The "yoke of iron" makes the iron earth of verse 23 concrete as a yoke upon the neck. (v) For the Chinese-side written forms at verse 47, see note 3. [Factual layer] Verses 49–52 are the enemy from far away and the opening of the siege, in full: "The LORD shall bring a nation against thee from far, from the end of the earth, as swift as the eagle flieth; a nation whose tongue thou shalt not understand; A nation of fierce countenance, which shall not regard the person of the old, nor shew favour to the young: And he shall eat the fruit of thy cattle, and the fruit of thy land, until thou be destroyed: which also shall not leave thee either corn, wine, or oil, or the increase of thy kine, or flocks of thy sheep, until he have destroyed thee. And he shall besiege thee in all thy gates, until thy high and fenced walls come down, wherein thou trustedst, throughout all thy land: and he shall besiege thee in all thy gates throughout all thy land, which the LORD thy God hath given thee." Registered item by item: (i) In the Chinese fixed text the quotation re-opens in a new section at verse 49. The eagle of "as swift as the eagle flieth" is nešer (H5404), for which BDB expressly cites this verse and notes that the word's range of sense includes the vulture class (note 5). (ii) "Shall not regard the person of the old" in verse 50 stands in contrast to Leviticus 19:32's command to honour the old (verified word by word first-hand in the Chinese source, note 6): the law commands Israel to honour the old; the coming enemy does not regard the old. (iii) Verse 51 leaves five items not remaining: corn, wine, oil, the increase of thy kine, and flocks of thy sheep — nearly the same list as the five blessed items of verse 4 (note 2); the triplet "corn, wine, and oil" already occurs in a blessing context at Deuteronomy 7:13 in this book (verified word by word first-hand in the Chinese source, note 6). (iv) In verse 52 the besieging occurs twice, and the walls pass from "wherein thou trustedst" to "come down"; for the punctuation form of the first sentence in the Chinese fixed text, see note 3. [Theological layer] Section Three grasps two hinges. The first is verse 44: the exchange of head and tail. What this chapter fears most is not poverty but reversal — the stranger risen to the top, lending reversed, head and tail exchanged: the people's dignity and order are settled item by item in this verse. The second is verses 47–48: the change of service's master. The deep ascription lies not in a list of acts but in the attitude and object of service: in the days of abundance, not serving God with joyfulness and gladness of heart; in the days of want, serving enemies of necessity — the iron yoke and gladness of heart are the two textures of two services. The calamities of this section complete a three-stage advance: body (Section Two) → livelihood (vv. 38–42) → place and service (vv. 43–48) → siege (vv. 49–52). [Summary layer] This section may be taken in four points: (i) the sore botch recurring, and captivity and becoming a byword (vv. 35–37); (ii) the chain of fruitlessness in the fields (vv. 38–42, five verses, five emptinesses); (iii) the hinge of reversal (vv. 43–48: head and tail exchanged, lending reversed, service changing masters, the four wants against abundance); (iv) the distant enemy and the siege (vv. 49–52: swift as the eagle, the five items not left, the walls come down). [New Testament reading layer] This section registers one item only, with no reading back: in verse 46 the curses become "a sign and for a wonder ... for ever" — the text itself ascribes a sign-function to the curses: this is the boundary between the factual layer and the reading layer — the fact of the sign is the text's own statement; how later ages read and use it belongs to the reading layer (the general reading of Galatians 3 is registered in Section Four) — stated side by side only. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] This section states two items side by side. First, the historical identification of the enemy from far away in verse 49 is read differently by commentators (some take it of Assyria, some of Babylon, some as a general form for a foreign enemy) — registered only, with no identification made (note 8). Second, the correspondence of the siege descriptions of verses 51–52 to the actual conduct of ancient siege warfare is registered generally at the discussion level; the passage on the extremity of the siege stands in the next section, and its principle of treatment is declared in advance: textual fact and formal registration only, with no heightening and no transplantation (note 9).

## Section Four: Eating the Fruit of Thine Own Body and Returning to Egypt by Ship (Deut 28:53–68; 41:00–53:00)

[Factual layer] Verses 53–57 are the extremity of the siege, in full: "And thou shalt eat the fruit of thine own body, the flesh of thy sons and of thy daughters, which the LORD thy God hath given thee, in the siege, and in the straitness, wherewith thine enemies shall distress thee: So that the man that is tender among you, and very delicate, his eye shall be evil toward his brother, and toward the wife of his bosom, and toward the remnant of his children which he shall leave: So that he will not give to any of them of the flesh of his children whom he shall eat: because he hath nothing left him in the siege, and in the straitness, wherewith thine enemies shall distress thee in all thy gates. The tender and delicate woman among you, which would not adventure to set the sole of her foot upon the ground for delicateness and tenderness, her eye shall be evil toward the husband of her bosom, and toward her son, and toward her daughter, And toward her young one that cometh out from between her feet, and toward her children which she shall bear: for she shall eat them for want of all things secretly in the siege and straitness, wherewith thine enemy shall distress thee in thy gates." Registered item by item: (i) "The fruit of thine own body" in verse 53 is the fourth occurrence of pərî beṭen (vv. 4, 11, 18, 53, note 4) — the vocabulary of the first item of the blessing list is used here of eating the flesh of sons and daughters; this rhetorical fact is registered only, with no heightening. (ii) The "evil eye" of verses 54 and 56 occurs twice — this book's 15:9 has a precedent (verified word by word first-hand in the Chinese source, note 6): the evil eye of chapter 15 is toward a poor brother; here it is toward a brother, a wife, and children: the object contracts from the neighbour to the nearest kin. (iii) In the Chinese fixed text verse 54 ends with a semicolon and the sentence crosses into verse 55; for the fixed text's wording at verse 56, see note 3. In the KJV verse 56 reads "which would not adventure to set the sole of her foot upon the ground." (iv) A difference of reading at verse 57 must be registered: the Chinese fixed text renders the object as the infant coming out from between her feet, while the original šilyāh (H7988), verified on the lexicon page, means the afterbirth, and the 2010 edition and modern versions mostly read it as the afterbirth — the word is not silently altered (note 5). The KJV reads "her young one that cometh out from between her feet," and its anchor marginal gloss gives "afterbirth" for "young one." (v) This group of verses is registered as fact only, with no heightening and no present-day application (note 9); Leviticus 26:29 has a single-sentence general statement of the same motif (verified word by word first-hand in the Chinese source, notes 6–7): Leviticus has one sentence, Deuteronomy an unfolding of five verses — the forms are stated side by side. For the record, the KJV wording of Leviticus 26:29, fetched first-hand in this translation round from BibleGateway, is: "And ye shall eat the flesh of your sons, and the flesh of your daughters shall ye eat." It is registered here at the comparison level of the source, exactly as the source registers the verse: as the one-sentence counterpart, not as a second text expounded. [Factual layer] Verses 58–59 are the theological hinge of the whole chapter, in full in the KJV's own two verses: "If thou wilt not observe to do all the words of this law that are written in this book, that thou mayest fear this glorious and fearful name, THE LORD THY GOD; Then the LORD will make thy plagues wonderful, and the plagues of thy seed, even great plagues, and of long continuance, and sore sicknesses, and of long continuance." The Chinese-side form is registered as it stands, with its sources expressly noted: the CUNPSS line for verse 58 contains within one verse the conditional sentence and the beginning of the consequence sentence, its content covering English verses 58–59 together, and verse 59 is separately registered by collation against the 2010 line (notes 1, 5). Registered item by item: (i) In the Chinese fixed text the quotation re-opens in a new section at verse 58. "All the words of this law that are written in this book" is the chapter's sentence of self-positioning: the purpose of the law is to fear that name. (ii) "This glorious and fearful name" is the theological high point of the whole chapter: hannikbāḏ (the niphal participle of kāḇaḏ, H3513) and hannôrāʾ (the niphal participle of yārēʾ, H3372); the BDB entries for both words expressly cite this verse — both "glorious" and "fearful" are passive participles: gloriousness and fearfulness are attributes of the name itself, not men's evaluations of it. For the Chinese-side renderings at this verse, see note 3. (iii) The "of long continuance" of verse 59 — in the original, neʾĕmānôt is a niphal participle of the ʾāman (H539) family — stands in same-root contrast with man's "none assurance" (not believing) in verse 66: the sicknesses' being settled and long-lasting, and man's not believing his own life (note 4). [Factual layer] Verses 60–62 are the calamities outside the book and the reversal of the number of the stars, in full: "Moreover he will bring upon thee all the diseases of Egypt, which thou wast afraid of; and they shall cleave unto thee. Also every sickness, and every plague, which is not written in the book of this law, them will the LORD bring upon thee, until thou be destroyed. And ye shall be left few in number, whereas ye were as the stars of heaven for multitude; because thou wouldest not obey the voice of the LORD thy God." Registered item by item: (i) "They shall cleave unto thee" in verse 60 is dābaq in its qal form, for which BDB expressly cites this verse — read against the causative form of verse 21: in verse 21 God makes the pestilence cleave; in verse 60 the diseases cleave of themselves (note 4). In the Chinese fixed text this verse ends with a comma and the sentence crosses into verse 61. (ii) "Which is not written in the book of this law" in verse 61 answers face to face "all the words of this law that are written in this book" in verse 58 — inside the writing and outside the writing are the organising axis of verses 58–61. (iii) "As the stars of heaven for multitude" in verse 62 is the reversal of promise vocabulary: the covenant of counting the stars in Genesis 15:5 and the looking back in Deuteronomy 1:10 (both verified word by word first-hand in the Chinese source, note 6) are here turned into "ye shall be left few in number" — the last of the four heaven images lands upon a number of men. [Factual layer] Verses 63–65 are the reversal of rejoicing and the opening of the scattering, in full: "And it shall come to pass, that as the LORD rejoiced over you to do you good, and to multiply you; so the LORD will rejoice over you to destroy you, and to bring you to nought; and ye shall be plucked from off the land whither thou goest to possess it. And the LORD shall scatter thee among all people, from the one end of the earth even unto the other; and there thou shalt serve other gods, which neither thou nor thy fathers have known, even wood and stone. And among these nations shalt thou find no ease, neither shall the sole of thy foot have rest: but the LORD shall give thee there a trembling heart, and failing of eyes, and sorrow of mind:" Registered item by item: (i) "Rejoiced / will rejoice" in verse 63 occurs twice, the same word śûś (H7797), for which BDB expressly cites both occurrences in this verse — the same verb, the same subject, its object turned from doing good to destroying; registered only. "Plucked" is the niphal of nāsaḥ (H5255), for which BDB expressly cites this verse. (ii) "Scatter" in verse 64 is the hiphil of pûṣ (H6327), for which BDB expressly cites this verse; "other gods, wood and stone" answers verse 36 within the text. (iii) Verse 65 lists three items: a trembling heart, failing of eyes, and sorrow of mind. "Rest" (in "neither shall the sole of thy foot have rest") is mānôaḥ (H4494), for which BDB expressly cites Genesis 8:9 and this verse in collation — the same phrase at the two ends of the beginning and the law (note 6); "sorrow of mind" is close in vocabulary to Leviticus 26:16 (note 7). [Factual layer] Verses 66–68 are the close of the whole chapter, in full: "And thy life shall hang in doubt before thee; and thou shalt fear day and night, and shalt have none assurance of thy life: In the morning thou shalt say, Would God it were even! and at even thou shalt say, Would God it were morning! for the fear of thine heart wherewith thou shalt fear, and for the sight of thine eyes which thou shalt see. And the LORD shall bring thee into Egypt again with ships, by the way whereof I spake unto thee, Thou shalt see it no more again: and there ye shall be sold unto your enemies for bondmen and bondwomen, and no man shall buy you." Registered item by item: (i) "Hang" in verse 66 is the passive participle of tālâ (H8518), of the same root as Deuteronomy 21:23 (treated in the sermon on chapter 21; registered only). The BHS of this verse, lôʾ taʾămîn bəḥayyeykā, translated directly, is "thou shalt not believe thy life" — taʾămîn is the hiphil of ʾāman (H539), for which BDB expressly cites this verse: two same-root occurrences with verse 59's "of long continuance," one positive and one negative (note 4). The KJV renders it "shalt have none assurance of thy life." (ii) The morning-and-evening words of verse 67: for the Chinese-side punctuation forms, see note 3; the quotation follows the fixed text's line as it stands. "Would God it were" in the original, mî yittēn, translated directly, is "who will give." The KJV carries no quotation marks here; the words stand within the sentence. (iii) Verse 68: brought into Egypt again with ships — the exodus went out on dry ground through the sea (registered only); the return to Egypt is by ship: direction and manner are both reversed. "Thou shalt see it no more again" is a pointing back to Deuteronomy 17:16 (verified word by word first-hand in the Chinese source, note 6). (iv) "Ye shall be sold ... and no man shall buy you" — the last cell of the curses is not being sold as slaves; it is having not even the qualification to be bought. The chapter's quotation in the Chinese fixed text closes here, and at the end of verse 68 a samekh is seen (note 1). [Theological layer] In Section Four, pause here: the end point of the whole chapter is not death but "no man shall buy you" — outside the covenant, worth falls until no one asks after it. The end point of blessing is "the head"; the end point of calamity is "no man shall buy you." On the road of blessing, blessing overtakes man; on the road of calamity, calamity overtakes man (v. 45). And before the end point, at the deepest place, the text sets up a signpost: "this glorious and fearful name" in verse 58 — the chain of ascription for the fifty-four verses of cursing (vv. 15, 45) has only one link: hearkening not, and keeping not; verse 58 speaks it through: all the words of the law have as their purpose that men should fear that name. Both roads have been written in full; which road to walk is left to the hearer. [Summary layer] This section may be taken in four points: (i) the extremity of the siege (vv. 53–57); (ii) inside the book and outside the book (vv. 58–61); (iii) many as the stars and few in number (vv. 62–65); (iv) a life hanging in doubt and no man to buy (vv. 66–68). [New Testament reading layer] This section registers two texts verified word by word first-hand (in the Chinese source against its fixed text; in this translation their KJV wording was fetched first-hand in this round — see the production record) and one statement of a word-family side by side. First, Galatians 3:10, verified word by word first-hand, in full in the KJV: "For as many as are of the works of the law are under the curse: for it is written, Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law to do them." — what Paul quotes is Deuteronomy 27:26 (registered in the preceding sermon), and his reading reads the curses within the covenant as the situation of all who are of the works of the law. Second, Galatians 3:13, verified word by word first-hand, in full in the KJV: "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree:" — Paul's general reading of the law's curses (of which this chapter is the largest list) stands here: the way out is that one was made a curse and redeemed us out; this is the New Testament reading layer, and it is not read back as the original meaning of chapter 28. (In the Chinese fixed text, Galatians 3:13 carries an interlinear note on the word rendered "being made"; the KJV text itself reads "being made a curse for us," and no corresponding note stands within the English quotation — the form is registered here.) Third, the word-family of Habakkuk 2:4 stated side by side: Habakkuk 2:4, verified word by word first-hand, in full in the KJV: "Behold, his soul which is lifted up is not upright in him: but the just shall live by his faith." Its original ʾĕmûnâ (H530) was verified against BDB, whose entry notes the divergence of rendering between "faith" and "faithfulness" — the two readings stated side by side; that word-family shares one root with the two occurrences in this chapter at verses 59 and 66 (note 4): the just lives by ʾĕmûnâ, and the cursed man does not taʾămîn his own life — the two poles, positive and negative, are stated side by side only, and their relation is not adjudicated. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] This section states three items side by side. First, the form of eating one's children in a siege in verses 53–57 has a tradition of corresponding records in later siege narratives (2 Kings 6, Lamentations 4, and Josephus's account of the siege of AD 70 — all registered only, not verified first-hand, not quoted) — the relation between the proclamation and the later records is a standing topic of discussion; stated side by side only, not adjudicated. Second, the relation of "bring thee into Egypt again with ships" in verse 68 to the ancient slave-trade routes and to the direction of the exodus narrative is read differently by different readers; registered only. Third, the explanations of the ratio of length (14 to 54) are stated side by side: the formal-convention account, the pastoral-intention account, and the redactional-strata account — no thesis is erected (note 8).

## Conclusion (53:00–60:00)

[Theological layer] With the whole chapter read, we return to the title to close. "The head, and not the tail" is the blessing of verse 13; "he shall be the head, and thou shalt be the tail" is the calamity of verse 44 — thirty-one verses apart, the same pair of words. Deuteronomy 28 gives us no third road: the people who hearken to the covenant and the people who forsake the covenant live the same kind of life — the same city, the same field, the same hands, the same heaven — and the difference lies in one thing only: hearkening and keeping, or not hearkening and not keeping. This sermon's measure closes here: every cell of the curse section is only proclaimed and registered as text — these words are Moses' solemn warning to the people within the covenant, written in the book, to make men fear "this glorious and fearful name"; they are not a warrant for identifying any man's sickness or experience today (note 9). The right posture for reading this chapter is to read verse 15 together with verse 1: first hear the condition clearly, then see the end of both roads clearly — blessings shall overtake, calamities shall overtake, and standing between them is the Lord who opens his good treasure, the heaven, and who also wrote his words in the book. In the next sermon, on Deuteronomy 29, Moses will renew the Moab covenant with the people in the face of these two roads — the men who have heard the warning are about to enter the covenant with their own mouths.

## Devotional Extract

Deuteronomy 28 sets two ways of life before us. In one, blessing pursues the man: "Blessed shalt thou be in the city, and blessed shalt thou be in the field"; blessed when thou goest out and blessed when thou comest in (in the KJV's order, "when thou comest in" and "when thou goest out"); even the basket and the store have their share. Heaven opens for thee like a treasure-house — "his good treasure, the heaven to give the rain unto thy land in his season" — the rain falls in its season, "thou shalt lend unto many nations, and thou shalt not borrow," and thou art made "the head, and not the tail." In the other, calamity overtakes the man: the same city, the same field, the same hands, turned over item by item, until he sits in the place of scattering and finds no rest for the sole of his foot. The difference between the two roads is not written in any complicated way in Scripture; it is written in one pair of verbs only: hearken or hearken not, keep or keep not. When Moses has finished speaking the fifty-four verses of cursing, suddenly, at the deepest place, he lifts up a name — "this glorious and fearful name, THE LORD THY GOD." This whole chapter, it turns out, is not written to frighten anyone; it is written to teach us fear: because we fear, we take every word of his seriously; because we fear, we serve him with joyfulness and gladness of heart in the days of our abundance, rather than waiting until the days of want to be taught by an iron yoke. And today the gospel tells us further that the curse was once borne by one in our place, that we might be redeemed from the curse of the law and enter the list of blessing. So hearkening is no longer a burden; it is an answer: he first loved us, and so we hearken diligently unto his voice and keep and do his ways. May we stand today on the side of blessing, and let his blessings come on us, and overtake us.

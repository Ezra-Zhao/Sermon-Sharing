## Executive Summary (approx. 100 words)

Genesis 10 is a roster: the descendants of Shem, Ham, and Japheth, spreading out according to their families, languages, lands, and nations. This roster is not mere filler; it demonstrates three things: God remembers all nations, not just one; the scattering of humanity over all the earth follows the blessing to "fill the earth," not a failure; and early on among the nations, Nimrodian tyrannical power emerged—man's problems followed man all over the earth. This message looks at God's heart through this roster: the earth He desires is an earth filled with inhabitants; the worship He ultimately desires is the collective worship of every nation, tribe, people, and tongue.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Introduction · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

In our last message, we bid farewell to Noah. The final verse of chapter 9 reads: "So all the days of Noah were nine hundred and fifty years; and he died." The era of the ark had closed.

Today we turn to chapter 10. To be candid: this chapter is one of the most easily skipped chapters in the entire Bible. The whole chapter contains no miracles, no dialogues, no prayers—just name after name: who begot whom, and who begot whom again, reading like a telephone directory. When many people reach this point in their Bible reading, their eyes skim past it and turn the page—after all, it seems to have "nothing to do with me."

Yet, brothers and sisters, there are no wasted pages in Scripture. That God solemnly placed this entire roster here, situated between the Flood and Babel, must carry His intent. Today, let us slow down and read this roster as Scripture, rather than skipping it as an appendix.

Let us first read the key passages (scripture quotations follow the NKJV):

"Now this is the genealogy of the sons of Noah: Shem, Ham, and Japheth. And sons were born to them after the flood." (Gen. 10:1)

"From these the coastland peoples of the Gentiles were separated into their lands, everyone according to his language, according to their families, into their nations." (Gen. 10:5)

"Cush begot Nimrod; he began to be a mighty one on the earth. He was a mighty hunter before the Lord; therefore it is said, 'Like Nimrod the mighty hunter before the Lord.' And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar." (Gen. 10:8-10)

"These were the families of the sons of Noah, according to their generations, in their nations; and from these the nations were divided on the earth after the flood." (Gen. 10:32)

Today we will divide our study into three parts:

First, 10:1-5 and the structure of the entire chapter: the roster is not an appendix—God records names one by one, and all nations have been in His heart from the beginning;
Second, 10:6-20: Nimrod arises—wherever man goes, tyrannical power and "the beginning of his kingdom" follow along; the seed of Babel first germinates here;
Third, 10:21-32: scattering is the pathway of blessing—establishing nations according to language and land is precisely the inch-by-inch fulfillment of "fill the earth," whose culmination is the worship of every tribe and tongue in Revelation.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. You did not let the Flood be the conclusion of the human story; You caused one family to become all nations, and made the empty earth once again filled with inhabitants. We pray that You open our eyes today to see Your heart within a chapter of names: You remember not merely one race or one person; You desire that all peoples come to know You. Remind us also that wherever people go, they must carry Your blessing with them, and not fill the whole earth with tyrannical power and pride. In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: The Roster Is Not an Appendix; God Records Names (The Structure of 10:1-32)

### 1.1 "This is the genealogy": Genealogies in the Bible Are the Main Text, Not Filler

The opening word of verse 1, "genealogy" (or "generations"), is the Hebrew *toledot* (H8435, descendants, generations, genealogy) [1]. This word forms the skeletal framework of Genesis: chapter 2's "the history of the heavens and the earth," chapter 5's genealogy of Adam, chapter 6's genealogy of Noah—all use this term. Each time it appears, it serves as the door handle to a new era of history. God uses genealogies to link history piece by piece, signifying: He does His work with orderly progression; He is never careless in remembering people.

Chinese people are no strangers to genealogies. In the ancestral temples back home, clan genealogies contain page after page filled with names; outsiders see a roster, but family members see their roots. Biblical genealogies are the same: behind the names are real individuals who truly lived—bearing children, tilling the ground, migrating, growing old, and dying. That God preserved their names in Scripture is tantamount to saying: these individuals are counted and accounted for with Me.

Warren W. Wiersbe, in his commentary on Genesis discussing the Table of Nations, reminds readers to note that chapter 10 reveals God sovereignly overseeing the rise of the nations; history is not the exclusive domain of world powers, for God remembers the origins of every people group.

Let us insert a word of application here: Do you feel that no one remembers your name? At work, you are just a minor clerk; at home, you are the one constantly overlooked; at church, you sit in the back row and no one greets you. Chapter 10 first tells you that God is a God who remembers names. If He preserved in Scripture even the name of a clan that appears only once thousands of years ago, how much more does He know you by name (cf. Isa. 43:1)?

### 1.2 Three Sons, Three Lines: Not a Single Line Is Redundant

The structure of the whole chapter is very neat: the descendants of Japheth (10:2-5), the descendants of Ham (10:6-20), and the descendants of Shem (10:21-31), concluding with verse 32 as a summary. The sequence is intriguing: it is arranged not by birth order, but from far to near—first recounting those scattered to coastlands and distant places, then those nearby, and finally resting upon the line of Shem, because the line of promise must proceed through Shem; the second half of chapter 11 picks up Shem's genealogy, running all the way to Abram.

"Families," Hebrew *mishpachah* (H4940, family, clan) [2]; "languages," *lashon* (H3956, tongue, language) [3]; "nations," *goy* (H1471, people, nation) [4]. These three words recur throughout the chapter, driving home one truth like nails: God views the nations not as an undifferentiated mass of humanity, but clan by clan, language by language. Diversity is not confusion; diversity is the texture of His creation.

Psalm 87 contains a very extraordinary statement, where God personally registers the peoples: "I will make mention of Rahab and Babylon to those who know Me; behold, O Philistia and Tyre, with Ethiopia: 'This one was born there.' ... The Lord will record, when He registers the peoples: 'This one was born there'" (Ps. 87:4, 6, excerpted). God has a book in heaven, recording not only Israel, but the native places of all peoples. Chapter 10 is the earthly shadow of this heavenly register.

### 1.3 Names You Recognize in the Roster: Later Biblical Stories Are All Planted Here as Seeds

Do not read this chapter too quickly; buried within it is the map for the next several thousand years: among the descendants of Ham are Egypt ("Mizraim" is Egypt, cf. 10:6) and the various peoples of the land of Canaan (10:15–19), with the border of Canaan drawn all the way to Sodom and Gomorrah; among the descendants of Shem is Eber (10:24–25), which is the very root of the designation "Hebrew," and Abram would later be called a Hebrew (cf. Gen. 14:13).

There is also a weighty word hidden within Shem's lineage: verse 25, "To Eber were born two sons: the name of one was Peleg, for in his days the earth was divided." Peleg, *peleg* (H6389), means "division." In his days the earth was divided—within a person's name, the Bible quietly recorded this monumental event of humanity's great dispersal, which is precisely what the next sermon on Babel will address.

From Genesis onward, God's plan of salvation was never prepared for just one nation; the church preaching the gospel to the ends of the earth is a response to the desire God had from the very beginning.

To sum up Part One in a single sentence: **Chapter 10 is not a telephone directory; it is a roster of all nations registered by God's own hand—He records names, He distinguishes families, He watches languages, and from the very first page after the flood, not a single one among all nations was overlooked under His watchful eye.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Nimrod Emerges, and the Seeds of Babel Sprout First (10:6–20)

### 2.1 "He Began to Be a Mighty One on the Earth": The Flood Washed the Earth, but Did Not Wash Away Man's Ambition

Reading through the list to the descendants of Ham, the rhythm suddenly changes. Verse 8 pauses to introduce a specific man:

"Cush begot Nimrod; he began to be a mighty one on the earth."

"Mighty one," in Hebrew *gibbor* (H1368, powerful, warrior, champion, mighty man)[5]. Please note that this word appeared before the flood: chapter 6 says that in those days there were "mighty men who were of old, men of renown" (Gen. 6:4, excerpt), precisely a generation when tyrants roamed the earth and violence filled it. After the flood, this word returns, resting upon Nimrod. The earth was washed once by water, but man's heart was not automatically washed clean. Those who emerged from the ark brought the problems of the old world into the new world—just as with the drunkenness in the vineyard in the previous sermon, so it is today with the despotic power in this roster.

"Nimrod," *Nimrod* (H5248)[6], is a name whose linguistic feel in Hebrew is close to the word for "rebellion," as commentators throughout history have often noted; this manuscript records it merely as a common view without making a definitive claim. He was "a mighty hunter"; a hunter is not merely one who hunts animals, as the second half of the sentence immediately makes clear: he began building a kingdom—"And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel." What was he hunting, and what was he building? The Bible places the two things together, and the reader must be alert.

### 2.2 "The Beginning of His Kingdom Was Babel": The Protagonist of the Next Sermon Appears Here First

Verse 10: "And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar." Verses 11 and 12 further state that from that land he went to Assyria and built Nineveh and other great cities.

Two place names must be remembered: Babel, and Shinar. The setting of the story of the Tower of Babel in chapter 11 is precisely the plain of Shinar. In other words, Babel does not emerge out of thin air in chapter 11; in chapter 10, it is already the beginning of a tyrant's empire. The Bible gives the map first, and then the event: it first shows you how despotic power spreads out, and then shows you how that tower was built.

This is not to say that building cities or establishing kingdoms is itself a sin. A city is a container for human life, and the final picture in the Bible is also a city—the New Jerusalem. The issue is never the city itself; it is who sits at the center of the city: is it God, or a man who considers himself a god? Nimrod's problem, as commentators have long pointed out, lies in the contrast behind the expression "before the Lord"—in the original text, this phrase carries a double entendre: all his deeds are before the eyes of the Lord, with nowhere to hide; yet some interpretations also read into it an overtone of defiance. This manuscript adopts a measured reading: whichever way it is read, the fact that the Bible reckons his expansion under God's watchful gaze is in itself a warning.

The words of Proverbs fit perfectly here: "Pride goes before destruction, And a haughty spirit before a fall" (Prov. 16:18). When Deuteronomy 32:8 looks back to summarize the boundaries of the nations, it says that the Most High separated the sons of man and set the boundaries of the peoples—boundaries are set by God, not seized by tyrants. A tyrant may draw maps temporarily, but the final ledger is in God's hands (cf. Deut. 32:8, general sense excerpted).

### 2.3 Ham's List Is the Longest: Do Not Use the Roster to Rank Peoples

The section on Ham's descendants is the longest, stretching from Egypt and Cush all the way to the various Canaanite peoples. When reading this, we must adhere to the principle established in the previous sermon: the target of the curse in chapter 9 was Canaan, not all of Ham's descendants; nor is the roster in chapter 10 a license for later generations to rank peoples hierarchically by ethnic group. Quite the contrary, in Acts 17:26 Paul says in Athens that God "has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings"—all nations share a single origin, standing at the very same starting line before God. What this roster reveals is common origin and diversity, not hierarchy.

Let us ignite this truth here.

Brothers and sisters, Nimrod is not far from us. In every era, in every industry, and even in every workplace, there is the temptation of being a "mighty one": gathering people by ability, making a name by the crowd, and ultimately suppressing people by that name. If you possess a measure of influence today—a team, a group, a company, a ministry—first ask yourself this question: In what I am building, who sits at the center?

Today, not tomorrow, examine this question when you go home: What do I desire most to hold on to—is it truly God's calling, or my own name? Whether Nimrod's name remains in Scripture as a monument or as a warning sign has already been answered by the way the Bible is written. Do not wait until the tower is half built to realize that what you have been building is Babel. While it is still today, yield the center—let God sit back in the middle, so that your influence will not become a burden to others, nor a snare to yourself.

To sum up Part Two in a single sentence: **Wherever man goes, problems follow; the first to emerge in the roster is not a saint, but a tyrant—the history of all nations reminds us from its very first page: expansion is not what is terrifying; what is terrifying is having the wrong person seated at the center.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: Dispersal Was the Path of Blessing All Along (10:21–32)

### 3.1 "Divided into Their Nations on the Earth": The Blessing of Chapter 1 Fulfilled in Chapter 10

The final verse of the entire chapter provides the summary: "from these the nations were divided on the earth after the flood" (Gen. 10:32).

Please read this alongside Genesis 1:28 and chapter 9, verse 1: "Be fruitful and multiply; fill the earth." How does man fill the earth? Not by building a great city higher and higher, but by family after family moving outward, settling afar, being fruitful, establishing nations, and speaking their respective languages. The entirety of chapter 10 is the execution record of these words: "fill the earth." Dispersal here is not a punishment; it is what blessing looks like when it touches the ground.

This point must be made clear, because in the next sermon, what the people of Babel feared most was dispersal; they wanted to build a tower "lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth" (Gen. 11:4). Regarding the exact same thing, God sees it as a blessing, while man sees it as a threat. Where does the difference lie? What God desires is filling the earth; what man desires is clustering together to preserve his own name. In reading Scripture up to this point, first correct your mindset: the judgment in chapter 11 is not "dispersal," for dispersal was the path God ordained from the beginning; the problem in chapter 11 is that man resisted this path, building a tower in defiance of the blessing.

### 3.2 Looking Back from Acts: Boundaries Are Set by God So That Man Might Seek Him

Paul's sermon in Athens explains the theology of chapter 10 thoroughly: "He has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings, so that they should seek the Lord, in the hope that they might grope for Him and find Him" (Acts 17:26–27, excerpt).

Where the nations live, where their boundaries lie, and where their appointed times are set are not matters of chance; they were pre-determined by God with a single purpose: so that people should seek Him. What city you live in today, what language you speak, and what ethnic group you were born into are not accidental misdraws in God's eyes. He has buried the clues to seeking Him in the very map where you were born.

Psalm 86:9 sings of the ultimate destination: "All nations whom You have made shall come and worship before You, O Lord, and shall glorify Your name." From the roster of chapter 10 to this psalm, the line runs straight: God made all nations not so that they would despise one another, but so that one day all nations would come together to worship.

### 3.3 The Roster in Revelation: Every Nation, Tribe, People, and Tongue

The final book of the Bible connects the roster of chapter 10 to heaven:

"After these things I looked, and behold, a great multitude which no one could number, of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, clothed with white robes, with palm branches in their hands," (Rev. 7:9)

Revelation 5:9 sings the song of the Lamb even more specifically: "For You were slain, and have redeemed us to God by Your blood out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation."

Brothers and sisters, notice this detail: in heaven, every tribe is still a tribe, and every tongue is still a tongue. God did not melt all nations down into a single color, a single language, and a single flavor before bringing them into the new heaven and new earth. What He redeems is the very diversity of all nations—different faces, different tongues, different songs, surrounding the same throne. Those names in chapter 10 that are so difficult to pronounce will one day stand before the throne clothed in white robes. This is the ultimate end of this roster.

If a church’s worship is left with only one culture and one flavor, we must look back and examine ourselves: are we moving closer to, or further away from, that picture in Revelation of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues?

To sum up the third part in one sentence: **Scattering is the pathway of blessing, not a mark of defeat; the destination of the roster is not on an earthly map, but before the throne—when every tribe and tongue stands there, every single name in chapter 10 finds its echo.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

Today's chapter speaks to six categories of people:

**To seekers:** You may feel that the Christian faith is a foreign religion that has nothing to do with your roots. Chapter 10 first corrects this misconception: all nations come from one family; your people, your language, and your birthplace have been on God’s roster from the very beginning. He does not demand that you first become someone else before He accepts you; in the worship He prepared in Revelation, there was always a place for your people.

**To new believers:** When you come across genealogies while reading the Bible, do not skip the page. Try reading through chapter 10 slowly, praying as you read: pray for the people groups on this roster, and for your neighbors and coworkers of different ethnicities, blessing them by name one by one. The breadth of your prayer map reflects the scope of your faith.

**To seasoned believers:** Having believed for a long time, it is easy to care only about your own people group, your own congregation, and your own preferred style of worship. This chapter broadens our horizons: take the initiative to get to know a brother or sister from a different ethnic background or language, and listen attentively to their testimony. Once this roster enters your heart, the church is no longer merely our own little circle of "us."

**To parents:** When your children ask, "Why do other people look different from us and speak differently from us?" do not give a perfunctory answer. Chapter 10 is the best answer: God caused all nations to dwell according to their tongues and lands; diversity is His creation, not a standard for superiority or inferiority. Read this roster to your children from an early age, and when they grow up, they will not easily look down on others based on ethnicity or nationality.

**To those striving in the workplace:** Your coworkers, clients, and partners come from every nation and tribe. The workplace ethic of chapter 10 is: boundaries and appointed times are ordained by God; whom you meet is no accident. Do not follow Nimrod by forming factions or using titles and reputation to dominate others as a "chief among heroes"; learn to be someone who knows people by name—remember their names, respect their origins. This is the simplest and most genuine witness.

**To those currently suffering:** You may find yourself in days of being "scattered": moving homes, relocating to another city, leaving familiar people behind, or even drifting in a foreign land. Listen to the message of chapter 10: in God's hands, scattering can be a pathway of blessing, not a sign of abandonment. When Noah’s descendants journeyed outward, the land was empty and there were no roads, yet God still caused people to be fruitful and establish households in every new territory. This unfamiliar ground beneath your feet—He can make it a place to build a home as well.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Summary · Responsive Prayer

Let us review the three key takeaways from today:

First, the roster is not an appendix—God records names according to families, tongues, and nations; all peoples have been in His heart from the very beginning;
Second, Nimrod rises, and the seeds of Babel sprout first—wherever humanity goes, problems follow; who sits at the center of expansion is a matter of life and death;
Third, scattering is the pathway of blessing—from filling the whole earth to every tribe and tongue standing before the throne, the final destination of the roster is worship.

Now let us bow our heads and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. After the flood, You caused one family to become all nations, repopulated the empty earth, and recorded every family and every tongue in Your book. Please forgive our tendency to skip pages when reading genealogies, and forgive the sin in our hearts of ranking people by ethnicity or background. Remind us to stay far from the way of Nimrod—not seeking to make a name for ourselves through power, nor using others as stepping stones. May You use our generation to seek You and bear witness to You within the boundaries of our dwelling places, until that day when we stand together with every nation, tribe, people, and tongue to worship before Your throne. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

[1] *toledot*: Hebrew תּוֹלְדוֹת (H8435), generations, descendants, genealogy; see Gen. 2:4; 5:1; 6:9; 10:1; serves as the structural framing word for the divisions of Genesis; definitions according to BDB and HALOT.

[2] *mishpachah*: Hebrew מִשְׁפָּחָה (H4940), clan, family, tribe; see Gen. 10:5, 20, 31–32; definitions according to BDB and HALOT.

[3] *lashon*: Hebrew לָשׁוֹן (H3956), tongue, language, dialect; see Gen. 10:5, 20, 31; definitions according to BDB and HALOT.

[4] *goy*: Hebrew גּוֹי (H1471), nation, people, gentiles; see Gen. 10:5, 32, and "great nation" in Gen. 12:2; definitions according to BDB and HALOT.

[5] *gibbor*: Hebrew גִּבּוֹר (H1368), mighty, warrior, hero; see Gen. 6:4; 10:8–9; definitions according to BDB and HALOT.

[6] Nimrod: Hebrew נִמְרֹד (H5248), Nimrod, personal name; the association of his name with "rebellion" (*marad*, H4775) is a common interpretive view throughout church history, though not directly stated in the biblical text.

[7] Concerning "Peleg" in Gen. 10:25: *peleg*, Hebrew פֶּלֶג (H6389), meaning division, to divide; Chinese Union Version note: "for in his days the earth was divided"; English comparison: translations in KJV, NIV, and NRSV convey the same meaning as this verse and are not directly quoted in this manuscript.

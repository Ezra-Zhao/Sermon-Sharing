# Not for Thy Righteousness — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 9

> Status: First translation, pending proofreading (初译待校). English version translated in full from the finalized Chinese manuscript "不是因你的义" (Deuteronomy 9; Chinese text reviewed and finalized, ZH strong re-check, third Deuteronomy batch, 2026-10-25). Scripture quotations in this English version are taken verbatim from the King James Version (KJV) anchor text of the whole chapter, not translated from the Chinese. See the Production Record at the end of this manuscript.
>
> Title verification: Five candidates were considered — (1) "Not for Thy Righteousness," from the negative sentence that occurs twice in vv. 5–6, "Not for thy righteousness"; (2) "A Stiffnecked People," from the diagnostic phrase of vv. 6 and 13, which covers only the indictment sections; (3) "Let Me Alone," from God's words on the mountain in v. 14, which covers only the Horeb section; (4) "They Are Thy Inheritance," from the opening and closing lines of the prayer in vv. 26 and 29, which covers only the intercession section; and (5) "Remember, and Forget Not," from the command of v. 7, which covers only the retrospect sections. Candidate (1) is adopted: the argument of the whole chapter of twenty-nine verses has only one axis — the reason Israel obtains the land does not lie on Israel's side (not for its righteousness, not for the uprightness of its heart), but on God's side (the wickedness of the nations, and the covenant sworn to the fathers); even the chapter's longest retrospect (the golden calf) and its longest prayer (the forty days and nights of falling down) are evidence marshalled to dismantle this one boast. Chapter 8 dismantles the boast of acquiring wealth (8:17); chapter 9 dismantles the boast of acquiring land; the two form a pair. English title as settled: Not for Thy Righteousness (from KJV 9:5, "Not for thy righteousness").

## Main Body

Introduction (00:00–05:00). [Fact layer] The place of this chapter in the whole book is registered first: it belongs to the opening group (chs. 6–11) of Moses' second great discourse (chs. 5–26), immediately following chapter 8. What chapter 8 guards against is the boast that comes after fullness — "My power and the might of mine hand hath gotten me this wealth" (8:17, KJV); what chapter 9 guards against is the boast that comes after obtaining the land — "For my righteousness the Lord hath brought me in to possess this land" (v. 4). The sickness of the two chapters is the same: crediting God's gift to one's own account; the medicine of the two chapters is also the same: remembering. Only, what chapter 8 remembers is God's leading and giving of strength, while what chapter 9 remembers is the people's own history of rebellion. The whole chapter has twenty-nine verses; its structure is registered: the crossing and the fire that goes before (vv. 1–3, 3 verses), not for thy righteousness (vv. 4–6, 3 verses), the first half of the Horeb retrospect (vv. 7–14, 8 verses), the breaking of the tables and the intercession (vv. 15–21, 7 verses), the catalogue of rebellion and the full text of the prayer (vv. 22–29, 8 verses) — twenty-nine verses in all (Endnote 1). The words quoted in the whole chapter come from three parties: one sentence from the people's heart (v. 4), two stretches of God's words on the mountain (v. 12; vv. 13–14), and one stretch of Moses' prayer (vv. 26–29) — Moses lays the words of all three parties on the table and lets the hearers hear for themselves: whose words can stand. [Theological-inference layer] The manner of this chapter is almost merciless: on the eve of a nation's entry into the promised land, the leader does not speak of triumph but of a shameful history; he does not say you deserve it, but you do not deserve it. Moses knows full well: what will one day kill this nation is not the Anakims' high cities, but that sentence in their hearts, "for my righteousness." So east of the Jordan he sets to work dismantling that sentence first — dismantling it with forty years of old accounts, with God's own words, and at last with his own prayer. [Summary layer] The governing sentence of this chapter can be put like this: the land is not a prize awarded to good people: the nations are driven out for their wickedness; Israel is brought in because of the oath God swore to the fathers. And Israel itself, in this chapter's file, has only four words to its name: a stiffnecked people. That they can still stand by the river today and listen to a sermon is not because their file is clean, but because forty years ago a man fell down for forty days and forty nights.

### I. The Fire That Goes Before (9:1–3; 05:00–14:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 1, quoted in full: "Hear, O Israel: Thou art to pass over Jordan this day, to go in to possess nations greater and mightier than thyself, cities great and fenced up to heaven," Register the items one by one: first, the opening is "Hear, O Israel" — of the same form as chapter 6's "Hear, O Israel" (registered in that sermon); the hearer of the discourse is still Israel in the singular. Second, the enemy situation is set out in three items in a row: nations greater and mightier than thyself, cities great, and fenced up to heaven — Moses does not shrink the enemy; he states the difficulty in full first. Third, "this day" here refers to the day of crossing: the whole chapter's sense of time stands at a threshold, and the words must be finished before the crossing. [Fact layer] Verse 2, quoted in full: "A people great and tall, the children of the Anakims, whom thou knowest, and of whom thou hast heard say, Who can stand before the children of Anak!" Register the items one by one: first, that the Anakims are "great and tall" is something the people "knowest" — not a rumour, but a consensus. Second, Moses cites a proverb in evidence: "Who can stand before the children of Anak!" — this rhetorical question has circulated among the people for a long time (the spies' report and the people's fear in Deuteronomy 1 are in the same key; registered in that sermon). Third, this verse registers only the fear and gives no answer — the answer comes in the next verse, and it does not lie with the people. (In the KJV the people are named "the children of the Anakims" in the verse's own statement and "the children of Anak" inside the proverb — registered as the KJV's own wording.) [Fact layer] Verse 3, quoted in full: "Understand therefore this day, that the Lord thy God is he which goeth over before thee; as a consuming fire he shall destroy them, and he shall bring them down before thy face: so shalt thou drive them out, and destroy them quickly, as the Lord hath said unto thee." Register the items one by one: first, the original's "as a fire" phrase is ʾēš ʾōkəlâ, literally "a devouring fire," the same word and the same rendering in the base text, "consuming fire," as at 4:24 (registered in that sermon). Second, the account of verbal subjects, recounted by hand: the verbs in this verse with God as subject are three — goeth over (before thee), destroy, and bring down; the verbs with the people as subject are two — drive out, and destroy — and both hang under "as the Lord hath said unto thee": the one who goes before is God, and the people's action is of the following kind. (On the KJV's own reckoning the word "destroy" occurs twice in this verse, once for God's act and once for the people's; for the Hebrew lemmas behind them see Endnote 3.) Third, "Understand therefore this day" forms a chain with v. 1's "Hear" and v. 6's "Understand therefore": most of this chapter's commands are cognitive — first recognize the facts clearly, then cross the river. [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: first, Moses' pre-battle mobilization runs opposite to the world's mobilization: he does not boast of his own troops, but of the God who goes before; he does not belittle the enemy, but sets the enemy before God's fire to be measured. However tall the Anakims stand in v. 2, v. 3 does not take up height; it takes up fire — the cure for fear is not to deny the giants, but to change the object of one's gaze. Second, the sentence "as the Lord hath said unto thee" fixes the nature of the people's participation: the driving out is real, but the initiative is not theirs; if victory comes, the account must be entered under the name of the one who went before — this is exactly the preventive inoculation against that sentence in the heart which the next section will dismantle. [New Testament reading layer] This section's echo in the New Testament is registered only at the reading layer: Hebrews 12:29, "our God is a consuming fire" (registered paraphrase), connects with this verse's "consuming fire" motif; and Stephen's discourse in Acts 7 closes its whole history with the other line of this chapter (stiffnecked; see Section V) — registered only. Neither is read back as this chapter's original meaning. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters set side by side. First, the readings of "fenced up to heaven" set side by side: (a) the documentary reading: the scale of Canaanite city defences was indeed considerable at the time, and the sentence is a factual exclamation; (b) the rhetorical reading: "up to heaven" is a hyperbole in common ancient Near Eastern use, of the same form as Genesis 11:4's "a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven" (registered paraphrase), meant to depict what was insurmountable in the people's eyes. The two readings stand side by side. Second, the reference of the Anakims is registered: scholarship has sought to infer their origin from Egyptian and Canaanite documents (registered as a proposal); the text itself gives only three pieces of information: great and tall, dwelling in Canaan, and known to all — this sermon registers only the text's facts and settles no origin. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: before the crossing, Moses first measures two things for you: how tall the enemy is, and how devouring God's fire is. The battle is not yet fought, and the account is already entered: the one who goes before is He; the destroying and the bringing down are His; you only follow in, as He has said.


### II. Not for Thy Righteousness (9:4–6; 14:00–24:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 4, quoted in full: "Speak not thou in thine heart, after that the Lord thy God hath cast them out from before thee, saying, For my righteousness the Lord hath brought me in to possess this land: but for the wickedness of these nations the Lord doth drive them out from before thee." Register the items one by one: first, Moses once again rehearses his hearers' inner monologue in advance (7:17 registers words of fear, 8:17 words boasting of acquiring wealth; registered in those sermons) — in this chapter the monologue changes its object: not wealth, but land; not ability, but righteousness. Second, the occasion of the "speak not" is specified: it is "after that … hath cast them out" — words that would be spoken after the victory, which Moses blocks in advance. Third, the corrective sentence comes first: the reason they are driven out lies not in Israel's righteousness, but in "the wickedness of these nations" — the ground of judgement is set out before the ground of election. [Fact layer] Verse 5, quoted in full: "Not for thy righteousness, or for the uprightness of thine heart, dost thou go to possess their land: but for the wickedness of these nations the Lord thy God doth drive them out from before thee, and that he may perform the word which the Lord sware unto thy fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob." Register the items one by one: first, the negation is doubled here: not for thy righteousness, and not for the uprightness of thine heart (in the original, yōšer ləḇāḇ, uprightness of heart) — both items are struck out together, blocking every re-dressed form of self-righteousness. Second, the positive reasons are two in a row: the wickedness of these nations (repeating v. 4), and that he may perform the word which he sware unto the fathers — one is judgement, the other is covenant. Third, the trio of fathers (Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob) occurs twice in this chapter (vv. 5, 27): once in God's promise, once in Moses' prayer, answering each other across the chapter. [Fact layer] Verse 6, quoted in full: "Understand therefore, that the Lord thy God giveth thee not this good land to possess it for thy righteousness; for thou art a stiffnecked people." Register the items one by one: first, the negation occurs for the third time here — vv. 4, 5, and 6 form a chain of negations (Endnote 4), the most densely argued stretch of the whole chapter. Second, the word "good land" picks up chapter 8 (8:7, 10; registered in that sermon): chapter 8's good land is joined to praise and warning, chapter 9's good land is joined to "not for thy righteousness" — the two chapters meet over the same piece of land. Third, the diagnostic sentence lands here: "for thou art a stiffnecked people" — in the original, ʿam-qəšēh-ʿōrep (people, stiff, neck); this phrase occurs twice in this chapter (vv. 6, 13), and at v. 13 it is spoken by God himself: Moses' diagnosis and God's diagnosis are in the same words — registered on the record. [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: first, within three verses Moses tears up Israel's merit book for obtaining the land three times: righteousness will not do, uprightness will not do, and no change of wording will do. Every reason he gives lies on God's side: the nations' wickedness is an account of God's justice; the fathers' oath is an account of God's faithfulness — in both books Israel is only a beneficiary, not a creditor. Second, this section must be read together with chapter 7: chapter 7 says the election was not because you were many, nor because you were strong, but because of God's love and oath (7:7–8; registered in that sermon); chapter 9 says obtaining the land is not for your righteousness, nor for your uprightness, but for their wickedness and His oath. Election and obtaining the land run on the same logic: grace at the source, warning downstream. Third, "a stiffnecked people" is placed at the end of this section by design, in this order: first grace is spoken (giveth thee … to possess it), then the diagnosis (for thou art) — the diagnosis is not there to convict, but to remove the last brick of self-righteousness: a stiffnecked people that has obtained the good land admits of no explanation but grace. [New Testament reading layer] This section's echoes in the New Testament are registered only at the reading layer: Romans 3:27, "Where is boasting then? It is excluded" (registered paraphrase), is in the same key as this section's structure excluding self-righteousness; Ephesians 2:8–9, "not of yourselves … not of works, lest any man should boast" (registered paraphrase), is a later echo of the same motif as "Not for thy righteousness." Both are registered only, not read back. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters set side by side. First, the readings of "for the wickedness of these nations" set side by side (connecting with the discussion layer on the extermination command in Deuteronomy 7, where the several readings are registered in that sermon; here only this chapter's structure of statement is registered): (a) the judgement reading: Canaan's wickedness has its historical content (cf. Genesis 15:16, "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full," registered paraphrase), and the driving out is the execution of judgement; (b) the contrast reading: this chapter's point is not to explain Canaan, but to warn Israel — v. 20's own wording (that Israel too would perish the same way; see the conclusion's register) shows the same standard applied in return; (c) the discourse reading: "the wickedness of these nations" is one link in this chapter's argument, whose function is to strike the credit off Israel's account, and it does not develop the Canaanite ethical question on its own. The three readings stand side by side, without verdict. Second, the semantics of the word "righteousness" in this chapter set side by side: (a) the character reading: it refers to the people's actual moral standard, which Moses, on forty years of history, judges to be no righteousness at all; (b) the status reading: it refers to qualifying standing within the covenant, which Moses judges Israel never to have used to obtain the land. The two readings apply in the same direction in this chapter (both exclude self-righteousness); their difference lies only at the level of the word's meaning — registered side by side. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: in three verses Moses says "not" three times: not for thy righteousness, not for the uprightness of thine heart, and still not for thy righteousness. The account of obtaining the land must be entered like this to be correct: their wickedness, His oath; and on your side, only one diagnosis — a stiffnecked people.

### III. Remember, and Forget Not: On Mount Horeb (9:7–14; 24:00–36:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 7, quoted in full: "Remember, and forget not, how thou provokedst the Lord thy God to wrath in the wilderness: from the day that thou didst depart out of the land of Egypt, until ye came unto this place, ye have been rebellious against the Lord." Register the items one by one: first, the command is double: remember, and forget not — the remembering of this chapter has the opposite object from the remembering of chapter 8: chapter 8 remembers God's leading (8:2), chapter 9 remembers one's own rebellion; both must be remembered, and if either is missing, the account is entered wrongly. Second, the account of time: from the day of departing out of Egypt, until ye came unto this place — the whole forty years are summed up in one sentence, "ye have been rebellious" (in the original, māmərîm, a "rebels" form, the same word as in v. 24). Third, this verse is the general heading of the retrospect that follows: v. 7 pronounces the verdict, vv. 8–24 present the evidence. [Fact layer] Verse 8, quoted in full: "Also in Horeb ye provoked the Lord to wrath, so that the Lord was angry with you to have destroyed you." Register the items one by one: first, Horeb is Deuteronomy's usual name for the mountain of the covenant (chs. 4 and 5 use the same name; registered in those sermons); the Exodus narrative calls it Sinai (registered). Second, this verse is the title sentence of the golden calf event: place (Horeb), reaction (was angry), and intent (to have destroyed you) are all present; the details unfold below. Third, "destroy" (šāmaḏ) occurs six times in this chapter (vv. 3, 8, 14, 19, 20, 25; Endnote 3) — God's anger in this chapter has one fixed verb, and Moses does not change the word; the hearers cannot evade it. (On the KJV's own count of the English word "destroy," see Endnote 3.) [Fact layer] Verse 9, quoted in full: "When I was gone up into the mount to receive the tables of stone, even the tables of the covenant which the Lord made with you, then I abode in the mount forty days and forty nights, I neither did eat bread nor drink water:" Register the items one by one: first, the narrative here turns to first-person eyewitness: the purpose of going up is written only as receiving the tables of the covenant — when Moses went up, nothing had yet happened below the mountain. Second, "forty days and forty nights" occurs four times in this chapter (vv. 9, 11, 18, 25; Endnote 2): the first occurrence is here, referring to the stay on the first ascent, and it states expressly, "I neither did eat bread nor drink water." Third, the account of the tables' names in this chapter: this verse calls them "the tables of stone … the tables of the covenant," v. 10 "two tables of stone," v. 11 "the two tables of stone, even the tables of the covenant," and v. 15 "the two tables of the covenant" — the name "tables of the covenant" runs from v. 11 onward; registered on the record. [Fact layer] Verse 10, quoted in full: "And the Lord delivered unto me two tables of stone written with the finger of God; and on them was written according to all the words, which the Lord spake with you in the mount out of the midst of the fire in the day of the assembly." Register the items one by one: first, the tables' author is named: "written with the finger of God" (ʾeṣbaʿ) — the tables are not Moses' work, and the gravity of breaking them is planted here in advance. Second, the tables' content is named: "according to all the words, which the Lord spake with you in the mount out of the midst of the fire in the day of the assembly" (yôm haqqāhāl) — that is, the ten words (already rehearsed verse by verse in chapter 5; registered in that sermon). Third, this verse closes with "all the words": what Moses received was the whole covenant, not an abridgement. [Fact layer] Verse 11, quoted in full: "And it came to pass at the end of forty days and forty nights, that the Lord gave me the two tables of stone, even the tables of the covenant." Register the items one by one: first, this verse is read against v. 9: v. 9 records the ascent and the stay, v. 11 records the stay's completion and the handing over of the tables — v. 10 in between is an inserted explanation (the tables' author and content); the narrative order is registered. Second, this verse has no "I neither did eat bread nor drink water" clause (v. 9 has it, v. 18 has it, this verse does not): the accompanying clauses of the three "forty days and forty nights" are not uniform — registered only, not harmonized. [Fact layer] Verse 12, quoted in full — God's first stretch of words: "And the Lord said unto me, Arise, get thee down quickly from hence; for thy people which thou hast brought forth out of Egypt have corrupted themselves; they are quickly turned aside out of the way which I commanded them; they have made them a molten image." Register the items one by one: first, God here calls the people "thy people which thou hast brought forth out of Egypt" — the credit for the bringing out is temporarily entered under Moses' name, while the people's sin is heard by Moses (of the same form as Exodus 32:7, "thy people, which thou broughtest out of the land of Egypt, have corrupted themselves:" — verified directly in the cross-reference column of this verse's verse-by-verse page; the KJV wording here follows the cross-reference anchor). Second, the charges are three: they have corrupted themselves; they are quickly turned aside out of the way which I commanded them; they have made them a molten image — "quickly" in the sense of "quickly turned aside" occurs twice in this chapter on the Chinese base's reckoning (vv. 12, 16): Moses was on the mountain forty days, and the people below the mountain "quickly" completed the betrayal. (On the KJV's own reckoning "quickly" occurs four times: v. 3, "destroy them quickly"; v. 12, "get thee down quickly" and "quickly turned aside"; v. 16, "turned aside quickly" — registered separately in Endnote 3's companion register.) Third, the "molten image" in the original is massēkâ (a molten image); in v. 16 it is specified as a "calf" (ʿēgel massēkâ, a molten calf): first the name is heard, then the form is seen — registered on the record. ### IV. The Broken Tables of the Covenant and the Man Who Fell Down (9:15–21; 36:00–47:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 15, quoted in full: "So I turned and came down from the mount, and the mount burned with fire: and the two tables of the covenant were in my two hands." Second, "the mount burned with fire" is the same scene as the burning-mountain records of chapters 4 and 5 (registered): after forty days the mountain's fire had not yet gone out, and the people's sin was already accomplished. [Fact layer] Verses 16–17, quoted combined (one sentence across the verse boundary in the base text): "And I looked, and, behold, ye had sinned against the Lord your God, and had made you a molten calf: ye had turned aside quickly out of the way which the Lord had commanded you. / And I took the two tables, and cast them out of my two hands, and brake them before your eyes." Second, the nature of the breaking of the tables set side by side: (a) the covenant-ceremony reading: breaking the covenant tables is a symbolic act annulling a treaty in the ancient world, and Moses by action declares the covenant already broken by the people's side; (b) the anger reading: read against Exodus 32:19's "Moses' anger waxed hot," the breaking is anger's natural outburst. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: on the day of the descent, Moses did three things: he broke the tables God had written with his own finger, he fell down for forty days and forty nights, and he prayed for Aaron, the hardest man to defend. Broken tables can be engraved again (next chapter); people destroyed would leave nothing at all — Moses knew which of the two was more urgent.

### V. The Catalogue of Rebellion and the Full Text of the Intercession (9:22–29; 47:00–57:00)

[Fact layer] Verse 22, quoted in full: "And at Taberah, and at Massah, and at Kibrothhattaavah, ye provoked the Lord to wrath." [Fact layer] Verse 24, quoted in full: "Ye have been rebellious against the Lord from the day that I knew you." Second, "from the day that I knew you" — Moses testifies from forty years of firsthand experience: this is not an accusation, but testimony. Third, after this verse the pen turns: from v. 25 on, Moses turns the camera from the people's rebellion to his own falling down — as long as the catalogue of sin is, so long is the full text of the prayer. [Fact layer] Verse 25, quoted in full: "Thus I fell down before the Lord forty days and forty nights, as I fell down at the first; because the Lord had said he would destroy you." Register the items one by one: first, the words "as I fell down at the first" expressly set this verse's falling down against v. 18's — the readings of one and the same falling down, twice mentioned, and of two separately mentioned fallings down, are set side by side in the discussion layer. Second, this verse is the introduction to the longest quotation of the whole chapter: in vv. 26–29 Moses recites, sentence by sentence for the people, the prayer of that year in its original words — a prayer of forty years before has, forty years later, become a sermon. [Fact layer] Verses 26–29, quoted combined — the full text of Moses' prayer: "I prayed therefore unto the Lord, and said, O Lord God, destroy not thy people and thine inheritance, which thou hast redeemed through thy greatness, which thou hast brought forth out of Egypt with a mighty hand. / Remember thy servants, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; look not unto the stubbornness of this people, nor to their wickedness, nor to their sin: / Lest the land whence thou broughtest us out say, Because the Lord was not able to bring them into the land which he promised them, and because he hated them, he hath brought them out to slay them in the wilderness. / Yet they are thy people and thine inheritance, which thou broughtest out by thy mighty power and by thy stretched out arm." Register the items one by one: first, the prayer's address is "O Lord God" (ʾădōnāy YHWH) — the chapter's only "Lord" title as an address; the register of the prayer's diction is noted here. Second, the prayer's three petitions, recounted by hand: the first, God's inheritance and God's acts (v. 26) — "thy people and thine inheritance" (naḥălâ), "which thou hast redeemed" (pādâ) "through thy greatness," "which thou hast brought forth out of Egypt with a mighty hand": Moses' first sentence says nothing of the people's present state, and speaks of God's acts. The second, God's remembering (v. 27) — "Remember thy servants, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; look not unto the stubbornness of this people, nor to their wickedness, nor to their sin:": remembering and not looking stand exactly opposed — he asks God to remember the fathers and not to look upon the people's three evils: stubbornness (qəšî, of the same root as the stiff neck), wickedness, and sin. The third, God's name (v. 28) — "Lest the land whence thou broughtest us out say": the Egyptians would discuss God's being "not able" to bring this people into the land he promised them, and his "hating" them and so bringing them out to slay them in the wilderness — of the same form as Exodus 32:12 ("Turn from thy fierce wrath, and repent of this evil against thy people," verified directly in the cross-reference column of the 9:28 verse-by-verse page; KJV wording from the cross-reference anchor) and Numbers 14:16 (verified directly in the same column): intercession for God's name is the shared link in Moses' prayers of the two crises. Fourth, the register of the counterpart with Exodus 34:9: later, Moses places the same diagnosis inside a confession — "for it is a stiffnecked people; and pardon our iniquity and our sin, and take us for thine inheritance" (verified directly in the cross-reference column of the 9:26 verse-by-verse page; KJV wording from the cross-reference anchor): the prayer of chapter 9 asks God not to look upon the people's sin; the prayer of Exodus 34 confesses the people's sin and asks pardon — the two prayers stand side by side, and both close with "thine inheritance." [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: first, Moses' prayer is the landing place of the whole chapter's theology: pleading for the people's life, he brings out three things — God's acts, God's covenant, God's name — and not one of them lies with the people. This is precisely the prayer-version of the argument of vv. 4–6: obtaining the land is not for thy righteousness, and surviving is not for thy righteousness either. Second, v. 27's "look not unto" deserves to be recorded on its own: Moses does not deny the three evils (stubbornness, wickedness, sin — he lists them, not one missing); what he asks for is the order of God's remembering: remember the fathers first, look at the people afterwards — grace in this chapter takes the covenant-before-sin order even in the syntax of prayer. Third, the juxtaposition of the catalogue (vv. 22–24) and the prayer (vv. 26–29) is Moses' manner of preaching: he first has the people read their own file through, and then has them hear how, forty years before, someone fell down for that very file — shame and grace, read out in the same sitting. This is an inference, marked as such here. (verified directly in the cross-reference column of the 9:26 verse-by-verse page) — "redeem" and "a peculiar people" (a people for his own possession) connect with the "redeemed — inheritance" motif of vv. 26 and 29 in this chapter — registered only. (the verse's opening likewise registers "thy rebellion, and thy stiff neck") — an inner-Old Testament echo, registered here. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters set side by side. First, the relation between the falling down of v. 25 and the falling down of v. 18 set side by side: (a) the same-event reading: v. 25 is a backward reference to v. 18 ("as I fell down at the first"), and vv. 26–29 are the prayer content of that falling down; the whole chapter records only one forty-days-and-nights falling down in intercession; (b) the separate-reference reading: vv. 18 and 25 each refer to one falling down, and the prayer is attached to the latter. The two readings stand side by side; the only text facts that can be settled are these: two mentions, both of forty days and forty nights, and the full text of the prayer recorded only once. Second, the readings of the name-argument of v. 28 set side by side: (a) the rhetorical reading: Moses uses the nations' talk as a lever, which is the standing pattern of intercessory speech (Exodus 32:12; Numbers 14:13–16, the same form); (b) the theological reading: the witness of God's name among the nations is itself one of the covenant's purposes (cf. Deuteronomy 4:6–8; registered in that sermon), and what Moses raises is not a lever, but the purpose itself. The two readings stand side by side, without verdict. [Summary layer] The section's governing sentence: Moses' way of reading a file falls in two halves: the first half is the people's catalogue — Horeb, Taberah, Massah, the graves of lust, Kadesh, station after station all rebellion; the second half is his prayer — thy people, thine inheritance, thy servants, thy name. As long as the catalogue is, so long is the prayer; that the people are still alive today — the whole answer lies in the second half.

Conclusion (57:00–60:00). [Fact layer] The whole chapter rehearsed, heading by heading: the one who goes before is the consuming fire (vv. 1–3); the account of obtaining the land is entered under God's covenant and judgement (vv. 4–6); the sin at Mount Horeb is characterized by God's own mouth (vv. 7–14); the tables are broken before the people's eyes, and Moses falls down forty days and forty nights (vv. 15–21); and after the catalogue of rebellion comes the full text of the prayer (vv. 22–29). [Theological-inference layer] If this chapter were allowed to leave only one sentence to the next generation entering the land, it would be v. 6 read whole: the Lord thy God giveth thee this good land to possess it, not for thy righteousness; for thou art a stiffnecked people. Grace and diagnosis, spoken in one sentence — remember only the first half, and you will grow proud; remember only the second half, and you will despair; remember both halves, and you have the remembering Moses wants. And the chapter's last layer of meaning hides in its order: the whole chapter begins with the people's sin (corrupted, rebellious, stiffnecked) and ends with Moses' prayer (thy people, thine inheritance). The sin has not been abridged, and the prayer has not been absent. The people standing before God today have a file scarcely different from Israel's; and their ground is likewise only one: not for our righteousness, but for His covenant, His name, and His stretched out arm. [Summary layer] Remember, and forget not — remember your own stiff neck, and remember still more His falling down and being hearkened to: the whole weight of Deuteronomy 9 is pressed between these two rememberings.


## Devotional Extract

Scripture (base text) — Deuteronomy 9:26, 29: "O Lord God, destroy not thy people and thine inheritance, which thou hast redeemed through thy greatness, which thou hast brought forth out of Egypt with a mighty hand." … "Yet they are thy people and thine inheritance, which thou broughtest out by thy mighty power and by thy stretched out arm." (Here quoted in the KJV, the English version's text; the Chinese devotional's base-text verses are the same two verses.)

Reflection: Moses fell down for forty days and forty nights for this people. What did he take to speak with before God? Not the people's righteousness — this chapter has just finished the reckoning: stiffnecked, rebellious throughout. He held only three things: they are Thy people, Thine inheritance; remember Thy servants Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; and let Thy name not be talked against among the nations. The ground of intercession has never been that those prayed for deserve it; it is God's own covenant, God's own name, God's own acts. We come before God today likewise not for our righteousness: the ground we stand on is the fact that He brought us out by His mighty power and His stretched out arm. If you are falling down for your family, for your church, lay down first the illusion "we are good enough," and lay down also the despair "there is no hope" — pray as Moses prayed: hand God's inheritance back to God, and remind God of God's promise. Scripture records the ending of that occasion: the Lord hearkened unto me at that time also.

Prayer: O Lord God, we confess: all that we have obtained is not for our righteousness. We are a stiffnecked people, yet we are Thy people and Thine inheritance. Remember the oath Thou swarest unto the fathers; look not unto our stubbornness, our wickedness, our sin; and for Thy name's sake, bring us through to the end. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

Prayer: O Lord God, we confess that all we have is not for our righteousness. We are a stiffnecked people, yet we are Thy people and Thine inheritance.

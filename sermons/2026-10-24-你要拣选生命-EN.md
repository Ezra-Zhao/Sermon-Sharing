# Choose Life — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 30

> Status: First translation · Initial translation pending proofreading (首译·初译待校). This is the first English translation of the finalized Chinese sermon on Deuteronomy 30, "Choose Life" (你要拣选生命), which passed the Chinese strong re-review (ZH 强复核) on 2026-10-25. All Scripture quotations in this English text are taken verbatim, including punctuation, from the King James Version (KJV), which serves as the base text for quotations in this translation; quotations are never re-translated from the Chinese. Cross-references quoted in full were fetched first-hand in this round from BibleGateway / Bolls KJV (see the production record at the end); any passage that could not be fetched is kept at the registration level and is not quoted. Hebrew transliterations and Strong's numbers are carried over from the Chinese source as registered there (61 items listed at the head). Counts are re-counted from the KJV text itself and registered on the KJV's own account; where the English form differs from the Chinese fixed text's form, both accounts are stated side by side and neither is smoothed over.

> Verification of the title: Three candidates were considered. (A) "Choose Life," from the command of verse 19, "therefore choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live" — the volitional peak of the whole chapter: return, the circumcision of the heart, and the nearness of the word all gather at last into this one verb. (B) "The Word Is Very Nigh unto Thee," from verse 14, the chapter's best-known sentence and the source of the quotation in Romans 10:8. (C) "He Is Thy Life," from the closing sentence of verse 20. Candidate (A) is adopted: verse 19 is the closing act of Moses' whole discourse — not one more piece of information, but a summons to the hearer to make a choice on the spot; and the word "choose" (bāḥar, H977) occurs only this once in the chapter, standing at a distance opposite God's choosing of Israel in 7:6–7 (registered only, not made a thesis). The English title is "Choose Life."

## Introduction (00:00–06:00)

[Summary layer] At the end of chapter 29 Moses leaves the word standing on a boundary line: "The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law." (Deut 29:29, KJV, verbatim as fetched) Chapter 30 begins on the "revealed" side of that line: if the worst has all happened — the curses of chapter 28 have come one by one, and the people have been driven out among all the nations — what then? This chapter is the gathering-up of the whole discourse of Deuteronomy: first it writes the return after the scattering (vv. 1–10), then it removes the last excuse — this word is not too hard, and it is not far away (vv. 11–14); at last it sets life and death before the hearer and leaves him one act only: to choose (vv. 15–20). [Factual layer] Four structural facts are registered first. First, the chapter has twenty verses, the first ten and the last ten exactly half and half: the first half writes God's turning and man's return, the second half the nearness of the word and man's choosing. Second, the chapter's pivotal verb is šûb (H7725, "to turn back, to return") — seven occurrences in six verses (note 2): man's returning to God three times, God's turning three times (including the "again" of His rejoicing once), and the word's being brought back to the heart once; one and the same root moves back and forth between man and God. Third, this chapter is a rare "future-tense" passage within Moses' discourse: verse 1 opens with "when all these things are come upon thee," presupposing that the curses of chapter 28 have already been fulfilled — chapter 28 has no repentance passage (registered in note 7 of the Deuteronomy 28 sermon), and that blank is supplied by this chapter. Fourth, "this day" (hayyôm) occurs seven times in the chapter (note 2): the return speaks of generations to come, yet the choosing uses the tone of "this day" — the chapter's temporal tension lies exactly here: the farthest thing (gathering from the uttermost parts of heaven) and the nearest thing (choosing this day) are placed by one speaker within a single breath. [Theological layer] The course of this sermon's four sections: Section One reads verses 1–5, the order of return — first remembering in the heart in the land of scattering, then returning with all the heart and all the soul, then God's compassion, gathering, and bringing back; Section Two reads verses 6–10, the foundation of return — God Himself handles that heart; Section Three reads verses 11–14, the nearness of the word — not in heaven, not beyond the sea, but in the mouth and in the heart; Section Four reads verses 15–20, the immediacy of choosing — heaven and earth are called to record, and the choice is to be made today. This sermon's argument rests only on the text itself: its verb chains, counts, sentence forms, and the form of its fixed text; everything touching relations between texts and disputes of reading is stated side by side and not adjudicated (disciplines one to three).

## Section One (06:00–18:00) Driven Out unto the Outmost Parts of Heaven (30:1–5)

[Factual layer] Verse 1 is the gate of the whole chapter; the discourse quotation opens here. The KJV text is given in full: "And it shall come to pass, when all these things are come upon thee, the blessing and the curse, which I have set before thee, and thou shalt call them to mind among all the nations, whither the LORD thy God hath driven thee," Registered item by item: First, the opening presupposes that the whole breadth of chapter 28's curses has come — this chapter does not begin from prosperity, it begins from scattering; "all these things" joins "those things which are revealed" of 29:29, the part written in the book, readable by all. Second, "driven" is the causative stem of nādaḥ (H5080): the text says expressly that it is the LORD who has driven the people out among the nations — the scattering is not an accident outside the covenant, it is an execution within it (note 4). Third, the first step of return is not in the feet, it is in the heart: the original reads "and thou shalt bring (these things) back to thy heart" (wəhăšēbōtā ʾel-ləbābeḵā, the causative of šûb); CUNPSS renders it freely, "thou shalt call the words of blessing to mind in thy heart," and the KJV "thou shalt call them to mind" (note 3) — among the nations a man first remembers the words in the book, and only then does return begin. [Factual layer] Verse 2 writes man's half. The KJV text in full: "And shalt return unto the LORD thy God, and shalt obey his voice according to all that I command thee this day, thou and thy children, with all thine heart, and with all thy soul;" Registered item by item: First, the subject carries "thy children" with it — return is a cross-generational matter; the scattering spans generations, and the promise spans generations too. Second, "with all thine heart, and with all thy soul" (bəkol-ləbābəkā ûbəkol-napšəkā) occurs three times in the chapter (vv. 2, 6, 10; note 2), its first occurrence here: the measure of returning is not behaviour first, it is the whole turning of heart and soul. Third, returning and obeying stand side by side in one sentence: šûb and šāmaʿ (H8085) — the direction of the turning is at once realised as the content of obeying, "according to all that I command thee this day," with nothing picked out. [Factual layer] Verse 3 writes God's half. The KJV text in full: "That then the LORD thy God will turn thy captivity, and have compassion upon thee, and will return and gather thee from all the nations, whither the LORD thy God hath scattered thee." Registered item by item: First, šûb occurs twice in this verse, and the subject has changed to God — "will turn thy captivity" and "will return and gather thee": to man's one step of returning to God, God's turning answers with compassion (rāḥam, H7355) and gathering (qābaṣ, H6908, piel stem). Second, "will turn thy captivity" renders šəbût (H7622): the original is the abstract noun "thy captive condition"; CUNPSS renders it concretely, "thy captive people" (note 3); the KJV reads "turn thy captivity." Third, "scattered" is the causative of pûṣ (H6327), forming an exact antonym pair with "gather": the One who scattered is the One who gathers back (note 4). [Factual layer] Verse 4 pushes the radius of gathering to its end. The KJV text in full: "If any of thine be driven out unto the outmost parts of heaven, from thence will the LORD thy God gather thee, and from thence will he fetch thee:" Registered item by item: First, "driven out" is the passive participle of nādaḥ — the same root as God's causative "hath driven" in verse 1 (note 4): where a man has been driven to does not matter; what matters is that the Driver Himself comes to gather. Second, "the outmost parts of heaven" in the original is "the end of the heavens" (biqṣê haššāmayim) — geographical distance at its utmost. Third, the "fetch" of "from thence will he fetch thee" is lāqaḥ (H3947): beyond gathering there is also a taking-out and bringing-back, stamped over the gathering of verse 3 at the end of verse 4. [Factual layer] Verse 5 lands on the land. The KJV text in full: "And the LORD thy God will bring thee into the land which thy fathers possessed, and thou shalt possess it; and he will do thee good, and multiply thee above thy fathers." Registered item by item: First, the verb of possessing the land, yāraš (H3423), occurs twice — the fathers "possessed" and thou "shalt possess": the same land, two generations, a scattering between them, and the land covenant unbroken. Second, "will do thee good" (yāṭab, H3190, causative) and "multiply" (rābâ, H7235, causative) both have God as subject: return is not a return to the starting point, it is more abundant than the fathers — the measure of the promise exceeds the Exodus generation. [Factual layer] One further syntactic registration: in the original, verses 1–3 are one long sentence — verse 1 opens with "when all these things are come upon thee…," verse 2 continues with the condition of returning, and only the "then" of verse 3 lets down the main clause: God will have compassion, God will gather. CUNPSS prints the three verses as separate sentences, reproduced as they stand; the grammar of return is itself an "if… then…": man's remembering and returning are written in the premise, God's compassion and gathering in the main clause; however long the premise, the main clause will arrive. [Theological layer] The order of this section deserves a pause: remembering (v. 1) → returning (v. 2) → compassion (v. 3) → gathering (vv. 3–4) → bringing back to possess the land (v. 5). Man's acts are only the first two, and the first is only "calling to mind in the heart" — before the return has even set out, God's half of the verbs is already full: have compassion, turn the captivity, return, gather, bring in, do good, multiply. The text does not write that man first earns merit and God then approves; it writes that when a man among the nations remembers the words in the book, God is already on the road of turning. [Summary layer] This chapter is to Deuteronomy what a door is to the dead end of chapter 28: chapter 28 ends with "none shall buy you" (28:68), chapter 30 opens with "thou shalt call them to mind" — in one and the same book, the preceding chapter blocks the road dead, and this chapter points out, on the blocked wall, the road back. [New Testament reading layer] No New Testament verbatim quotation of this section was confirmed by first-hand verification in this round; the "scattering—gathering" theme's echoes in the New Testament (such as the gathering of the elect in Matt 24:31) were not verified word by word in this round and are registered only, not quoted (note 6). [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The return passage is stated side by side with Deuteronomy's other return text: Deuteronomy 4:29–31 likewise presupposes scattering — "But if from thence thou shalt seek the LORD thy God, thou shalt find him, if thou seek him with all thy heart and with all thy soul." "When thou art in tribulation, and all these things are come upon thee, even in the latter days, if thou turn to the LORD thy God, and shalt be obedient unto his voice;" "(For the LORD thy God is a merciful God;) he will not forsake thee, neither destroy thee, nor forget the covenant of thy fathers which he sware unto them." (Deut 4:29, 30, 31, KJV, verbatim as fetched) The three arrangements are stated side by side: chapter 4 is "seek—find," chapter 30 is "remember—return—be gathered"; the verb chains differ in length, while mercy and remembering the covenant are present in both — the relation is stated side by side only, not adjudicated. Nehemiah's prayer is one actual use of this section in the age of return from captivity: the wording of Nehemiah 1:9 itself rehearses Moses' words in quotation form — "But if ye turn unto me, and keep my commandments, and do them; though there were of you cast out unto the uttermost part of the heaven, yet will I gather them from thence, and will bring them unto the place that I have chosen to set my name there." (Neh 1:9, KJV, verbatim as fetched) "Though… unto the uttermost part of the heaven… yet will I gather them from thence" corresponds plainly to the wording of 30:4; the relation between use and source text is registered only, not adjudicated.

## Section Two (18:00–29:00) He Will Circumcise Thine Heart (30:6–10)

[Factual layer] Verse 6 is the theologically deepest sentence in the chapter. The KJV text in full: "And the LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, that thou mayest live." Registered item by item: First, the original verb is mûl (H4135, "to circumcise") — "the LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart and the heart of thy seed"; CUNPSS renders it freely, "will take away the defilement of thy heart and of thy seed's heart," the same rendering as at 10:16 (note 3); the 2010 edition renders it in the literal circumcision form, and the two forms are stated side by side (note 5). Second, the agent is God: 10:16 is a command — "Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart, and be no more stiffnecked." (Deut 10:16, KJV, verbatim as fetched) — man is told to do it himself; 30:6 is a promise form — God says this knife He will wield. One and the same phrase, two voices, stated side by side in the discussion layer. Third, the purpose clause has two layers: "to love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul" — the second of the three occurrences of "with all thine heart, and with all thy soul" (note 2), here bound to love (ʾāhab, H157); "that thou mayest live" (ləmaʿan ḥayyeḵā) — living is the purpose of the circumcision, and the chapter's "life" theme appears here for the first time, laying the line for the choosing of verses 15–20. [Factual layer] Verse 7 is the transfer of the curses. The KJV text in full: "And the LORD thy God will put all these curses upon thine enemies, and on them that hate thee, which persecuted thee." Registered item by item: First, "all these curses" (hāʾālôt, ʾālâ, H423) answers "when all these things are come upon thee, the blessing and the curse" of verse 1, head to tail — the same body of curses fell first on the covenant-breaker, and after the return is transferred to the persecutor. Second, the recipients are threefold: enemies (ʾōyēb, H341), them that hate thee (śānēʾ, H8130), and them which persecuted thee (rādap, H7291) — not outsiders in general, but the company that persecuted this people on the road of return. [Factual layer] Verse 8 is what return looks like when it lands. The KJV text in full: "And thou shalt return and obey the voice of the LORD, and do all his commandments which I command thee this day." Registered item by item: First, the fifth occurrence of šûb (note 2), with the subject back to man: the heart God has handled in verse 6 grows obedience in verse 8 — "return, obey, do," three verbs in one breath, of the same shape as verse 2's "return—obey," and more concrete. Second, "all his commandments" answers verse 2's "according to all that I command thee": in both verses the scope of the commandments is "all"; return is not partial obedience. [Factual layer] Verses 9–10 are the abundance after return and the restatement of its condition. In CUNPSS they are printed merged (see the head note); in this translation they are quoted in the KJV's divided form, verse by verse. Verse 9: "And the LORD thy God will make thee plenteous in every work of thine hand, in the fruit of thy body, and in the fruit of thy cattle, and in the fruit of thy land, for good: for the LORD will again rejoice over thee for good, as he rejoiced over thy fathers:" Verse 10: "If thou shalt hearken unto the voice of the LORD thy God, to keep his commandments and his statutes which are written in this book of the law, and if thou turn unto the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul." Registered item by item: First, CUNPSS merges the English division's verse 9 (abundance) and verse 10 (condition) into one entry, folding the conditional clause "If thou shalt hearken… and turn with all thine heart and with all thy soul" into the head of the entry, while the KJV divides them into verses 9 and 10, effect first and cause after — the two arrangements stated side by side (notes 1, 5); this registration of the Chinese merged form is carried over as a registration of the Chinese side's form, and the English quotations follow the KJV's twenty verses. Second, "will make thee plenteous" renders the causative of yātar (H3498) — the same word in the same stem as the "plenteous" of 28:11: the abundance promised in the curse section returns unchanged in the return section. Third, the three items of produce, "the fruit of thy body, and the fruit of thy cattle, and the fruit of thy land," are of the same list as the blessing list of 28:4 (registered in note 2 of the Deuteronomy 28 sermon). Fourth, "will again rejoice" — the sixth occurrence of šûb, functioning as "again," its verb subject being God's rejoicing (śûś, H7797): in 28:63 God once "rejoiced" to destroy the covenant-breakers (two occurrences in that verse, registered in note 4 of the Deuteronomy 28 sermon); in 30:9 God "will again rejoice… for good," and the rejoicing's reference point is "as he rejoiced over thy fathers" — the rejoicing of destruction is not the last word; the rejoicing of the fathers' days is. Fifth, the Masoretic paragraph marker petuhah at the end of verse 10 closes this section (note 1): verses 1–10 form one large unit in the original's layout. [Theological layer] This section's skeleton is "God acts first": after man's return (v. 2), the text does not stop at man's resolve, but presses on — that heart which once "hath not… an heart to perceive" (Deut 29:4, in the Chinese wording, "the LORD hath not given you an heart to perceive") — by what can it love, by what can it keep? Verse 6's answer is that God circumcises the heart with His own hand; the order is therefore: return (man) → circumcision (God) → obedience (man, v. 8) → abundance (God, v. 9). When man's obedience appears in verse 8, it is no longer the heart of 29:4, never given, straining on its own; it is a heart that has been operated on, answering. Note also this section's sandwich structure: God's two outward acts — the transfer of the curses (v. 7) and the abundance of produce (v. 9) — enclose the heart (v. 6) and the obedience (v. 8) between them: return does not change the inner man only and leave the circumstance, nor change the circumstance only and leave the heart; in circumstance and in heart both, it is God's hand, and man's share is only in the middle: return, obey, do. [Summary layer] If Section One writes the road of return, Section Two writes the man who returns: however far the road (the outmost parts of heaven), he can be gathered; however hard the heart, it can be circumcised — the two despairs of scattering (distance of place, hardness of heart) are dismantled one by one in the chapter's first ten verses. [New Testament reading layer] The echoes of the heart's circumcision in the New Testament (Rom 2:28–29, "circumcision is that of the heart," and Col 2:11) were not verified word by word in this round and are registered only, not quoted (note 6); this sermon's verbatim New Testament quotation registrations are concentrated in Section Three. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Four "heart" texts, stated side by side: First, the command form of Deuteronomy 10:16 — man is to circumcise the foreskin of his own heart. Second, the promise form of Deuteronomy 30:6 — God will circumcise man's heart. Third, Ezekiel's prophetic text — "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh." "And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them." (Ezek 36:26, 27, KJV, verbatim as fetched) — a new heart and the gift of the Spirit together, obedience as their fruit. Fourth, Jeremiah's new-covenant text — "But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the Lord, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people." (Jer 31:33, KJV, verbatim as fetched; the divine name is flattened to "the Lord" in the BibleGateway fetch, registered as fetched) — the law moved from tablets of stone into the heart. The four texts differ in their arrangement of agents and in wording: command and promise, circumcision and a new heart, writing on the heart and giving the Spirit — which is earlier, which later, which quotes which and which answers which, is stated side by side only, not adjudicated (note 7). The diagnosis of 29:4 is also stated here side by side: that generation's heart "to perceive" had not yet been given, so the surgery of 30:6 is not consolation only; it is a prescription written against the diagnosis — the relation of diagnosis and prescription is the text's own order, and this sermon registers it only thus far.

## Section Three (29:00–41:00) The Word Is Very Nigh unto Thee (30:11–14)

[Factual layer] Verse 11 removes the first excuse; the discourse quotation reopens here. The KJV text in full: "For this commandment which I command thee this day, it is not hidden from thee, neither is it far off." Registered item by item: First, "commandment" is singular (hammiṣwâ, H4687) — it does not mean rules counted out one by one, but this whole body of command given today; its range of reference (this chapter's command, or the law as a whole) is stated side by side with verse 10's "his commandments and his statutes which are written in this book of the law" in the discussion layer. Second, "it is not hidden" renders niplēʾt (pālāʾ, H6381, niphal participle): the word's own sense is "too wonderful, beyond reach"; CUNPSS renders it "not too hard to do," the KJV "hidden," and modern versions mostly of the "too hard" family — the range of renderings registered (note 5). Third, "neither is it far off" (rāḥôq, H7350) stands exactly opposite verse 14's "very nigh" (qārôb, H7138): far and near are this section's spatial axis. [Factual layer] Verse 12 pushes "far" up to heaven. The KJV text in full: "It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it?" Registered item by item: First, the inner quotation is imagined speech — words the people might say, spoken for them first, then refuted. Second, the verb chain is "go up (ʿālâ, H5927) — bring it — that we may hear it (šāmaʿ, causative) — and do it (ʿāśâ, H6213)": the imagined mediator is to complete the two steps "bring—hear" for the crowd, leaving the crowd only "do." Third, the "us" in "Who shall go up for us" — the excuse's form is collective: not "I cannot," but "no one will go and fetch it for us." [Factual layer] Verse 13 pushes "far" beyond the sea. The KJV text in full: "Neither is it beyond the sea, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go over the sea for us, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it?" Registered item by item: First, "beyond the sea" in the original is "on the other side of the sea" (mêʿēber layyām, ʿēber, H5676; yām, H3220) — the Mediterranean's far shore as the utmost distance, forming with verse 12's heaven the two poles, vertical and horizontal: cannot go up, cannot cross over. Second, the two verses' imagined speeches are word-for-word of the same shape, only the place-word changed: a rhetorical couplet that exhausts every shape of "unreachable" at once. [Factual layer] Verse 14 is the section's landing point. The KJV text in full: "But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it." Registered item by item: First, "the word" (haddābār, dābār, H1697) — verse 11's "commandment" is here renamed "the word": the command is not a decree in a distant place, it is a word already spoken, already heard. Second, the near place has two locations: the mouth (peh, H6310) and the heart (ləbāb) — the mouth is the place of recitation and confession, the heart the place of thought and decision; the word's being in both places at once means it can both be spoken and be thought. Third, "that thou mayest do it" is a purpose clause: near, not in order to know, but in order to do — this section's final verb is word-for-word the same as the "and do it" at the end of the imagined speeches of verses 12 and 13: what the imagination said required ascending heaven and crossing the sea, verse 14 says is attainable with the word near in mouth and heart. Fourth, the Masoretic paragraph marker setumah at the end of verse 14 (note 1): the four verses form a small unit of their own, separately framed in the layout. [Theological layer] This section's argumentative form is "set up, then break down": it first fills out the two most extreme ways of fetching — heaven and beyond the sea were the frontiers of that age's imagination — and then takes them all back in one sentence, "But the word is very nigh unto thee." What it dismantles is in fact not distance but the fantasy of a mediator: no one needs to ascend heaven and fetch the law for you, because the word is already in your mouth and in your heart; the question that remains was never "can it be got," it is "will it be done." One correspondence is also registered: the Shema teaching of Deuteronomy 6 likewise places the word in the two locations of heart and mouth — upon the heart, and spoken of in teaching the next generation (that passage is registered in this sermon only, not quoted, note 6); the "in thy mouth, and in thy heart" of 30:14 is therefore not a new phrase, but the two organs of the people's daily recitation: the word's "nearness" is as near as the distance of daily recitation. [Summary layer] The position of verses 11–14 in their context is also worth registering: before them, the circumcision of the heart (v. 6); after them, the choosing of life and death (v. 19) — "nearness" is sandwiched between "God has already acted" and "man must choose": the word's nearness is neither man's achievement (the heart is circumcised) nor a substitute for choosing (v. 19 still requires a choice); it is the precondition that makes choosing possible. [New Testament reading layer] This section is the most famous New Testament quotation of Deuteronomy; under the discipline it is registered in this layer only: Paul in Romans 10:6–8 takes up this section sentence by sentence — "But the righteousness which is of faith speaketh on this wise, Say not in thine heart, Who shall ascend into heaven? (that is, to bring Christ down from above:)" "Or, Who shall descend into the deep? (that is, to bring up Christ again from the dead.)" "But what saith it? The word is nigh thee, even in thy mouth, and in thy heart: that is, the word of faith, which we preach;" (Rom 10:6, 7, 8, KJV, verbatim as fetched) Four observable facts of rewriting are registered: First, before the quotation Paul adds the frame "Say not in thine heart" (the original imagined speech of Deut 30:12–13 reads "that thou shouldest say," the opposite tone). Second, Deuteronomy 30:13's "beyond the sea" becomes in Romans 10:7 "into the deep" (the abyss). Third, the object of "bring down / bring up" is expressly fixed as Christ — ascending heaven to fetch the law becomes bringing Christ down, descending into the deep becomes bringing Christ up from the dead (the parentheses are Paul's own). Fourth, 30:14's "the word" is in Romans 10:8 recognised as "the word of faith, which we preach," and the order "in thy mouth, and in thy heart" is the same as in 30:14. The KJV collation (Rom 10:8): "The word is nigh thee, even in thy mouth, and in thy heart: that is, the word of faith, which we preach;" — the English likewise keeps both places, mouth and heart. The above registers Paul's form of use only; it does not adjudicate the relation between this reading and chapter 30's original sense (note 7), and it is not read back into the chapter's original sense. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two items stated side by side: First, the two readings of the reference of the singular "commandment" in verse 11 — read as the command of this chapter (or of Moses' discourse that day), and read as the law as a whole, stated side by side; the text's own neighbouring evidence is verse 10's "his commandments and his statutes which are written in this book of the law" and verse 16's three names in a row, "his commandments and his statutes and his judgments"; the dispute over range is not adjudicated. Second, the double pole of heaven and sea is in ancient Near Eastern rhetoric the customary extreme for "unreachable" (this genre observation registered only); this section's use is a reversal: the extremes are denied one by one, and what remains is "very nigh" — the rhetoric serves not information but responsibility: the word being already near, the choosing of verse 19 admits no excuse. This observation is registered as genre only, and no theological conclusion is built on it.

## Section Four (41:00–53:00) Life and Death Set before Thee (30:15–20)

[Factual layer] Verse 15 is the outline of the whole section; the discourse quotation reopens here for the third time. The KJV text in full: "See, I have set before thee this day life and good, and death and evil;" Registered item by item: First, "See" (rəʾēh, rāʾâ, H7200, singular imperative) — not a statement, but the hearer's gaze pressed onto the arrangement; "I have set before thee" (nātan ləpāneḵā) occurs three times in the chapter (vv. 1, 15, 19; note 2): in verse 1 what is set is the curse, in verse 15 the two groups of life and death, in verse 19 set once more as a parting restatement. Second, the two pairings are "life and good / death and evil": life (ḥayyîm, H2416) paired with good (ṭôb, H2896; CUNPSS renders it "blessing," the original word is "good"), death (māwet, H4194) paired with evil (raʿ, H7451) — CUNPSS punctuates with a comma, "life and blessing, death and cursing," reproduced and registered (note 3). Third, the object is the singular "thee" — the two ways are set before one person, not within the statistics of a nation. [Factual layer] Verse 16 unfolds the content of the way of life; CUNPSS follows verse 15 directly, with no independent subject opening (note 3). The KJV text in full: "In that I command thee this day to love the LORD thy God, to walk in his ways, and to keep his commandments and his statutes and his judgments, that thou mayest live and multiply: and the LORD thy God shall bless thee in the land whither thou goest to possess it." Registered item by item: First, the way of life's acts are a triad — love (ʾāhab, the second of its three occurrences in the chapter, note 4), walk in his ways (hālak bədərākāw, hālak, H1980; derek, H1870), and keep (šāmar) his commandments, statutes, and judgments (miṣwâ / ḥuqqâ / mišpāṭ, H4941, the three names in a row answering verse 10). Second, the results are three: live (ḥāyâ, H2421), multiply, and blessing in the land (bārak, H1288) — of the same list as the return section's "do thee good, and multiply" in verse 5: the shape of the way of life has already been rehearsed in the return section. Third, "the land whither thou goest to possess it" — the hearers still stand on the east bank of Jordan, the land is across the river: choosing comes before crossing, blessing after crossing; the order may not be inverted. [Factual layer] Verses 17–18 unfold the way of death. The KJV text in full: "But if thine heart turn away, so that thou wilt not hear, but shalt be drawn away, and worship other gods, and serve them;" "I denounce unto you this day, that ye shall surely perish, and that ye shall not prolong your days upon the land, whither thou passest over Jordan to go to possess it." Registered item by item: First, the way of death starts in the heart: "thine heart turn away" (pānâ, H6437, the heart turning) — exactly opposite the heart's circumcision of verse 6: a heart circumcised by God can love; a heart that turns away of itself walks toward perishing; this chapter's heart is, from beginning to end, the battleground. Second, "be drawn away" is the passive of nādaḥ (wəniddaḥtā) — the same root as God's driving of the people in verse 1 and the people's being driven out in verse 4 (note 4): in the land of scattering God uses this word to gather men back; in the promised land, if a man lets this word change its subject — drawn away by other gods — the same road walks back to scattering. Third, "worship… and serve" (šāḥâ, H7812, hitpael stem, to bow down; ʿābad, H5647) other gods: the way of death's end is not godlessness, it is a change of god; the reading of 30:17 in the Chinese text was settled as "serve" from the traditional-character line and the whole-chapter page (note 5; in the KJV the word is "serve" throughout). Fourth, in verse 18 the person turns plural: "I denounce unto you this day" (nāgad, H5046, causative, to declare plainly) — the warning's object widens from the singular "thou" to "you": choosing is personal, its consequences are communal. Fifth, "ye shall surely perish" is the doubled form of ʾābad (H6) (infinitive absolute plus imperfect, as if "perishing ye shall perish"); "ye shall not prolong your days" stands exactly opposite verse 20's "the length of thy days" (note 4). [Factual layer] Verse 19 is the chapter's volitional peak. The KJV text in full: "I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live:" Registered item by item: First, "I call heaven and earth to record" (ʿûd, H5749, causative) — the witnesses are heaven and earth: longer-lived than the congregation, longer-lived than one generation; Deuteronomy 4:26 and 31:28 likewise have the calling of heaven and earth, registered in this sermon only, not quoted (note 6). Second, the order of what is set has changed: verse 15 reads "life and good, and death and evil," verse 19 reads "life and death, blessing and cursing" — life and death are first made a pair, blessing and cursing follow; at the moment the choosing arrives, the text asks first about life and death, then about blessing and cursing. Third, "therefore choose life" (ûbāḥartā baḥayyîm, bāḥar, H977) — the chapter's only command to choose, its verb opening with the conjunction, as if "therefore, choose": all the setting-forth of the preceding eighteen verses is a road laid for this one sentence. Fourth, the choosing's beneficiaries carry "thy seed" with them — answering verse 2's "thou and thy children," head to tail: an individual's choice, a generation's consequences. [Factual layer] Verse 20 is the closing sentence; the chapter's discourse quotation closes here in full. The KJV text in full: "That thou mayest love the LORD thy God, and that thou mayest obey his voice, and that thou mayest cleave unto him: for he is thy life, and the length of thy days: that thou mayest dwell in the land which the LORD sware unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give them." Registered item by item: First, the way of life's triad reappears unchanged in the closing sentence — love, obey, cleave (dābaq, H1692): "cleave" is the same word as 10:20's "serve him, and to him shalt thou cleave" (the Deuteronomy 28 sermon, note 6, verified that verse word by word); the same root in chapter 28 is sickness "cleaving" to a man (registered in note 4 of the Deuteronomy 28 sermon), and in this chapter man cleaving fast to God — the object of the cleaving decides the cleaving's nature (note 4). Second, the reason clause is the chapter's theological summit: "for he is thy life" (ḥayyeḵā), "and the length of thy days" (wəʾōrek yāmeḵā, ʾōrek, H753) — life is not the prize of choosing; life is the very One who is chosen: to choose life is to choose Him. Third, the land covenant's close names the three generations of fathers — Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (šābaʿ, H7650, to swear): from the scattering among the nations of verse 1 to the fathers' covenant of verse 20, the chapter walks a full circle: the covenant began in the oath to the fathers, passed through curse, scattering, and return, and at last comes back to dwelling (yāšab, H3427) in the land that oath gave. Fourth, the Masoretic paragraph marker petuhah at the end of verse 20; the chapter closes, in the original's layout, with a large paragraph unit (note 1). [Theological layer] This section's argument is the whole discourse in miniature: setting forth (v. 15) — unfolding (vv. 16–18) — witness (v. 19a) — choosing (v. 19b) — reason (v. 20). Moses does not teach the two ways as an information lesson; he stages them as an oath-taking: an arrangement, witnesses, and a verb on the spot. Note especially the position of verse 20's reason clause — "for he is thy life" is placed after the choosing: it is not that a man first understands and then chooses; it is that, having chosen, he knows that life was never a thing, it is He. Note further the track of the three occurrences of "set before" (note 2): verse 1 first sets the curse forth once, verse 15 sets it forth again as the two groups of life and death, verse 19 sets it forth a third time under the witness of heaven and earth — the same contents laid out three times: the first time so that men know, the second so that men see clearly, the third so that men bear responsibility; the number of settings-forth is the measure of Moses' patience. [Summary layer] The four sections together: the chapter's movement draws inward — from all the nations (v. 1) to one land (v. 5), from one land to one heart (v. 6), from one heart to one mouth (v. 14), from one mouth to one choosing (v. 19). Geography grows smaller and smaller, responsibility greater and greater; by verse 19, heaven and earth have withdrawn to be witnesses, and only "thou" remains standing before life and death. [New Testament reading layer] This section's echoes in the New Testament (such as the two ways of Matt 7:13–14) were not verified word by word in this round and are registered only, not quoted (note 6); this sermon's verbatim New Testament quotation registrations are concentrated in Section Three (Rom 10:6–8), and under the discipline are not read back into this section. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] The two-ways motif in three presentations, stated side by side (discipline two): First, the presentation of Deuteronomy 11:26–28 — "Behold, I set before you this day a blessing and a curse;" "A blessing, if ye obey the commandments of the Lord your God, which I command you this day:" "And a curse, if ye will not obey the commandments of the Lord your God, but turn aside out of the way which I command you this day, to go after other gods, which ye have not known." (Deut 11:26, 27, 28, KJV, verbatim as fetched; the BibleGateway fetch flattens the divine name to "the Lord," registered as fetched) — the object is the plural "you," the wording is blessing and curse, and the landing point is bound to Mount Gerizim and Mount Ebal (in that chapter's sequel; registered in this sermon only). Second, the presentation of Deuteronomy 28 — the two ways unfolded into the full text of fourteen verses of blessing and fifty-four verses of cursing, with no command to choose and no calling of witnesses, closing with "none shall buy you." Third, the presentation of this chapter, 15–20 — the two ways each compressed into one group of words, the object drawn back to the singular "thou," furnished with the witness of heaven and earth and the command "choose life," closing with "he is thy life." The three presentations differ in length, object, and close: whether they are three advances of one discourse or parallel proclamations on different occasions is stated side by side only, not adjudicated (note 7). One further registration: 11:28 and 30:17 both have the wording of serving other gods, and the divergence of readings between the simplified and traditional lines on wd.bible takes the same form at both places (note 5) — the two chapters' kinship of wording is registered only thus far.

## Conclusion (53:00–60:00)

[Theological layer] The whole chapter read, we return to the title to close. "Choose life" is not a slogan; it is the last lesson of Moses' discourse after thirty chapters. This chapter first thinks the worst through for man: the curses come, he is driven to the outmost parts of heaven — and then it says, even at that point the road is not cut off: calling to mind in the heart is the first step, God Himself gathers, God Himself circumcises the heart, the word is near in the mouth and in the heart, and at last only one act remains: choosing. The chapter's measure gathers here: the promise of return may not be taken to identify the political situation of any present-day group, and the warning of perishing may not be used as an instrument of intimidation (note 9); this chapter permits one use only — that the hearer himself stand under the witness of verse 19 and ask himself which side he is choosing today. And remember verse 20's reason: what we choose is not a set of rules, not one way of living; we choose Him — "for he is thy life, and the length of thy days." Remember also the chapter's account counted twice onto the next generation — verse 2's "thou and thy children," verse 19's "thou and thy seed": when Moses spoke this chapter, standing before him was precisely the generation about to cross the river; the words were spoken to them, and through them to their children. Choosing was never one man's matter only: one man's today is a family's future. In the next chapter, Deuteronomy 31, Moses is old, and can no longer bear the weight of the discourse; Joshua is to take up the mission of leading the people across the river — the word is already laid here, and the next generation is to carry this chapter's choosing into that land.

## Devotional Extract

Deuteronomy 30 first thinks through the worst case for us: the curses have come, we have been driven out among all the nations, and even the road home is out of sight. Yet Moses says the first step of return is unexpectedly small — it is not walked back, it is remembered back: "thou shalt call them to mind." In the farthest place, if a man only remembers God's word, remembers the God who once made a covenant, the road has begun. Then God does more than we do: He has compassion, He gathers, He brings us back; even our hardened heart He circumcises with His own hand, so that we can love Him with all our heart and all our soul. At the end He gives us no new problem, only sets one thing before us — life — and says: choose life. For this word was never far: not in heaven, not beyond the sea, but in our mouth and in our heart. Sometimes we think obedience is hard because we think we must first run to a far place and fetch the strength back; in fact the word is already here, the heart has already been touched by His hand, and what is lacking is only today's one choosing. To choose life is not to choose a set of rules; it is to choose Him — for "he is thy life, and the length of thy days." May we choose today, and not wait until we are scattered to the outmost parts of heaven to remember; may what our mouth speaks and our heart believes both return to Him.

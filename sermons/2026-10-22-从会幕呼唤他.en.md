# From the Tabernacle He Called Him

Preacher: Zhao Guangyi, composed from Scripture and the commentaries

Status: Reviewed · Published. Passed cross-review on 2026-10-23 (independent Gemini review + two rounds of self-review); published in Sermon-Sharing.

Review: Passed cross-review on 2026-10-23 (independent Gemini review + two rounds of self-review; passed after parent-level minor fixes).

Scripture: Leviticus 1:1-17 (KJV, aligned verse by verse with the Chinese original)

Translator's note: Translated from the Chinese original manuscript of the same date. This is a first translation awaiting proofreading.

Continuity note: This sermon follows directly on Exodus chapter 40 — the glory fills the tabernacle and Moses cannot enter; Leviticus then begins with God actively calling Moses from within the tabernacle.

Summary: Exodus ends with the glory filling the tabernacle, yet man cannot enter; in the first sentence of Leviticus, God calls Moses from the tabernacle and, through the burnt offerings of the bullock, the sheep, and the bird in three grades, teaches lay worshippers how to bring an offering and draw near to God, learning in the order of sacrifice a life wholly given to the Lord.

## I. After the Glory Filled It, God Spoke First 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, when we open Leviticus we easily feel that this is a book far removed from us. It is full of blood and altars, of skinning and cutting into pieces, of inwards and legs washed with water, of a bird's crop thrown beside the ash heap. A modern reader turns two pages and wants to close it, asking in his heart: what has this to do with me? Yet if we skip Leviticus we shall miss a decisive turn in Scripture. Exodus tells how God brought Israel out of Egypt; Leviticus tells how a people brought out may draw near to God and dwell with Him. The former is redemption, the latter is nearness; the former is the exodus, the latter is entering the tabernacle. Without Leviticus, the Exodus story stops at the foot of the mountain, outside the tent: the people have gained freedom, but they do not know how to live before God.

Notice how the two books join. This is a fact at the level of structural observation. The final passage of Exodus is written with great tension: the cloud covered the tent of the congregation, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle; and Moses was not able to enter into the tent of the congregation, because the cloud abode thereon, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle (Exod 40:34-35, KJV). Who is Moses? The man who led the people out of Egypt, who spoke with God face to face on the mountain, who oversaw the whole building of the tabernacle. Yet even Moses cannot enter before the glory. The tabernacle is finished — the candlestick, the table of shewbread, the altar, the laver all set in place according to the pattern — and the glory has descended, and now the problem appears: the holy God dwells in the midst; how shall flesh-and-blood man enter? The glory is both invitation and barrier. It declares that God has truly come, and it declares that man cannot force his way in by himself.

The first sentence of Leviticus answers this problem. The text says: "And the LORD called unto Moses, and spake unto him out of the tabernacle of the congregation, saying" (Lev 1:1, KJV). Note: not from the mountaintop, not from high in the pillar of cloud, but out of the tabernacle — from within that very space which the glory had just filled and which Moses could not enter — God speaks. God is the one who takes the initiative. It is not Moses devising a way to squeeze in, nor the people inventing a rite to please God; God Himself calls from within, gives the word, and shows the way of nearness. This is the narrative hinge between the two books: Exodus stops at "was not able to enter"; Leviticus begins with God calling from within. On one side is man's inability, on the other God's initiative. Without this call, the glory would only make man afraid; with this call, there is a voice within the glory, teaching, and a way that can be followed.

The Hebrew Bible takes the name of this book from the first word of this first sentence: Vayyiqra, "and He called." The name is not a title casually added by later hands, but the result of the Hebrew custom of naming a book by its opening word. It reminds every reader: do not first read Leviticus as a cold manual of regulations; first hear a voice. God calls man; God wills to speak with the people He has redeemed. Most of the book is the content of that call. God calls Moses, and commands Moses to pass the word to Israel. From the very start, then, the tone of Leviticus is not a record of man searching for God, but the revelation of God teaching man how to draw near to Him.

This matters greatly for us today. Many imagine worship as something we do for God, and nearness to God as our offering of enthusiasm, emotion, and performance. Leviticus sets the order the other way round: first God calls, first God appoints the manner, and man responds accordingly. Drawing near to God has never been a road man designs by imagination. That sinners can come before the holy God is not because man found a clever device, but because God in His mercy has Himself spoken, Himself prepared, Himself accepted. As we read this chapter today, let us not stand as spectators watching the ancients sacrifice; let us stand as those who are called, and listen: God called Moses from the tabernacle, and through this word He calls us too, asking whether we are willing, in the manner He has appointed, honestly, concretely, and wholly, to come before Him.

We must, of course, distinguish levels honestly. That the first word of Leviticus is the book's Hebrew name, and that 1:1 forms a narrative joint with Exodus 40, are textual facts and structural observations. That God's active call shows His willingness for man to draw near is a homiletical exposition drawn from this structure and from the ordinances that follow, not a doctrinal proposition already stated in so many words in the original text. Distinguishing levels does not weaken preaching; it makes preaching stand firm: facts as facts, exposition as exposition. We do not pass off our own words as the words of Scripture, nor do we shrink the richness of Scripture into dry textual study. God called him from the tabernacle — and that call has a voice, an addressee, and content. Next, the content of the call falls upon the general rule of sacrifice.

## II. The Layman Brings an Offering and Comes Before God 08:00-24:00

God says to Moses: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them" (Lev 1:2, KJV). This is the addressee of the chapter — a matter of the factual level, not to be blurred. The word of Leviticus 1 is first of all spoken not only to the priests, still less is it a professional code for clergy alone. God tells Moses to speak to the children of Israel, to the whole people. In the sacrificial system the priests have their appointed office — sprinkling the blood, burning the offering, handling the bird offering — but the one who initiates the sacrifice, who brings the animal, who lays his hand on it, who slaughters the beast, is the lay offerer himself. In other words, drawing near to God is not a performance completed by a few specialists on behalf of everyone else; it is something in which every Israelite must personally take part. The priest assists and handles the offering in the order God appointed, but he cannot replace the offerer's own heart and action in coming before God.

The text says: "If any man of you bring an offering unto the LORD, ye shall bring your offering of the cattle, even of the herd, and of the flock" (Lev 1:2, KJV). The word here for offering is qorban. In its lexical sense it is connected with the root meaning to bring near, to present; it denotes what is brought before God and offered to Him. The notes will give its original form, transliteration, and dictionary definitions; here we grasp its use in this chapter: an offering is not something discarded and unwanted, not man's disposal of his surplus, but a man's taking livestock that belongs to him, bringing it to the door of the tabernacle, bringing it before the LORD. The first step in sacrifice is bringing near. While the bullock is still in the stall it is the farmer's property; brought to the door of the tabernacle, its head laid on by hand, slaughtered and offered, it becomes an offering. Nearness to God is not an abstract wish; it has a concrete bringing, a concrete presenting, a concrete cost.

"If any man of you" also deserves attention. It shows that in the context of this chapter the burnt offering carries the colour of a voluntary offering. This does not mean that Israel's sacrificial system as a whole was optional, nor that man may decide by his own preference whether to worship; but apart from compulsory rites such as those of the Day of Atonement, the ordinary burnt offering was often the people's active coming before God, expressing belonging and obedience. If a man is willing to come, God instructs him how to come. Coming actively, and coming in God's manner — neither is dispensable. Activeness without God's manner easily becomes fleshly zeal; rules without an active heart easily become empty form. Leviticus 1 sets the two together: man offers actively, God appoints the ordinance.

The offering is to be brought "of the cattle, even of the herd, and of the flock," and verse 14 adds the bird. Why these? Because these are the animals Israel kept in daily life, possessed, and could actually bring. God did not require a man to offer what he did not have, did not require the poor man to sell his house to buy a bullock, and did not turn nearness to God into something only the rich could join. The three grades of offering that follow unfold from here. First the general rule, then the particulars: how the bullock is offered, how the sheep is offered, how the bird is offered. God's teaching is very concrete — concrete as to the sex and wholeness of the animal, concrete as to the place of slaughter, concrete as to where the blood is sprinkled, how the inwards are washed, how the wood is laid. This concreteness is not fussiness; it is preciousness. Coming before God is not a vague matter. God values how man offers, and man must not substitute carelessness for reverence.

We should also see that this chapter does not yet speak directly of the sin offering and trespass offering, which deal with particular sins. The burnt offering stands first among the five offerings, and its most prominent feature is that it is wholly burned: the entire animal is burned on the altar, no portion is left as food for the priest, and no portion is returned to the offerer to eat. This feature will be clearer when we follow the procedure for the bullock in the next section. Here we set down a general recognition: what the burnt offering expresses first is the giving of the whole offerer, the whole animal, to God. The offerer does not fob God off with scraps at the edge; he brings a whole animal before God, acknowledging that God is worthy of everything. When man comes before God, the first thing to learn is not how to get benefits from God, but how to present honestly before God all that he has, to let Him accept it and to let Him be Lord.

There is another easily overlooked detail: the offering is to be brought before the LORD, and the phrase about being accepted before the LORD, which appears in the next section, is already prepared for here. Sacrifice is not a rite performed for men to see. People at the door of the tabernacle will see it, the priest will take it in hand, the congregation will know who offered a bullock and who a sheep — but the true object of sacrifice is the LORD, and the true judgment is whether God accepts it. A dignified offering is not the same as an accepted offering; outward grandeur is not the same as God's pleasure. God's appointed order, the wholeness of the animal, and the offerer's own participation together make up what this chapter calls offering. Today we have no bullocks or sheep to bring, and we no longer perform this rite literally, but the principle still reminds us: nearness to God must be concrete, must cost something, must follow God's word rather than man's concern for appearances. It is easy to say with empty hands that we love God; bringing what we truly have before God shows the reality of the heart.

At this point, let us not hurry to jump to the New Testament. Reading Leviticus as Leviticus — seeing clearly its addressees, its general rule, and its order — is itself reverence. God calls Moses; Moses speaks to Israel; if any man of Israel brings an offering, he brings it of the herd or of the flock. The layman is the active party; God is the Lord who appoints the rule and who accepts the offering. Once this picture is clear, we shall look step by step at how the burnt offering of the bullock is made — at the hand laid on the animal's head, at the blood sprinkled round about the altar, at the sweet savour wholly burned.

## III. The Bullock Without Blemish, the Burnt Offering Wholly Burned 24:00-42:00

From verse 3 the text enters the first grade of offering: the bullock. "If his offering be a burnt sacrifice of the herd, let him offer a male without blemish" (Lev 1:3, KJV). Without blemish — Hebrew tradition stresses the wholeness and soundness of the offering; and a male is among the most valuable and strongest of the herd. For a household living by farming and herding, a bullock is not small change, not a thing lightly thrown away; it bears on ploughing, breeding, and the livelihood of the whole family. What God requires to be offered is the good, the whole, the valuable. This does not mean that God covets man's property, nor that the higher the price the more spiritual the offering; it means that the language of sacrifice itself declares: God is worthy of man's best, not of the leftovers, the damaged, the things he himself does not want. If a man brings the blemished animal he was culling and offers it to God, the act itself lies — his mouth says God is supreme, his hands give the worst.

The animal is to be brought to the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, before the LORD. A man may not simply kill a bullock at some place of his own choosing at home and call it a burnt offering. The place is the place God appointed; the presence is the presence of the LORD. Sacrifice has a public character, and it has a sacred boundary. The door of the tabernacle is the nearest point to the sanctuary that the people may reach, and the altar stands there. To bring the bullock there is to bring one's offering into the place of meeting God appointed, acknowledging that nearness to God follows the boundary God set, and that man does not set up altars wherever he pleases. This boundary is not God making things difficult for man; it is what holiness itself requires: God wills man to come near, but does not allow man to intrude carelessly.

The text then says that so it shall be accepted for him before the LORD. The word for acceptance belongs to the family of favour, delight, and goodwill; the notes will list its original form and number. In this chapter it is a key theological word: the purpose of sacrifice is not merely to burn the flesh of an animal, but that this offering be accepted before God. Man offers, God accepts; man presents, God receives with favour. This acceptance is not wages man has earned, but the result of God's grace in the manner He Himself appointed. Man offers according to God's word, and God accepts the offering. If man invents his own manner, however zealous he may be, he stands outside this promise.

Then comes the act the offerer himself must do: "And he shall put his hand upon the head of the burnt offering" (Lev 1:4, KJV). The Hebrew verb for this act is samakh, meaning to lean on, to press, to lay the hand heavily upon — not a light touch. The offerer lays his hand on the head of the animal about to be slaughtered, and at that moment, in the rite, man and animal are joined together in a conspicuous way. Scholarship holds three views of the meaning of the laying on of the hand, set out side by side; we draw no final conclusion. The first is identification: the laying on of the hand shows that the animal represents the offerer — the offerer identifies himself with the offering, and the offering of the animal counts as his own offering. The second is transfer of ownership: the laying on of the hand shows that the animal from now on belongs to God; ownership passes from the offerer, and the beast is no longer his property but is assigned to God's name. The third is transfer of sin: the laying on of the hand transfers the offerer's sin onto the animal, and the animal dies in his place. Each view has textual and ritual support, and each has its limits. On the Day of Atonement the high priest lays his hands on the goat's head and confesses all the iniquities of Israel over it, and there the sense of transfer is comparatively clear; but whether the laying on of the hand in the ordinary burnt offering carries the same sin-transfer function on every occasion is a matter on which scholars are not agreed. The sound way to preach it is this: the laying on of the hand at least establishes representation and connection — what is offered is not a bullock unrelated to me, but an offering that represents me, is joined to me, and is given to God. Preaching may apply this connection, but it must not present any one of the three views as an undisputed conclusion.

After the laying on of the hand, the offerer himself slaughters the bullock before the LORD. The slaughter is not done by the priest in his place; the offerer performs it with his own hand. Modern readers often slide past this point, yet it carries great weight. Sacrifice is not handing a living animal to professionals and standing by to watch until it is over. The man who brought it must himself end the animal's life. He will hear the sound, see the blood flow, and truly feel that sacrifice is not a clean and tidy religious decoration, but the cost of a life. The death of the animal cannot be separated from the offerer. Afterward the priests, Aaron's sons, receive the blood and sprinkle it round about upon the altar that is by the door of the tabernacle of the congregation. The handling of the blood is the priest's office. Sprinkling is not random splashing; the blood is sprinkled round about the altar, so that the relation between altar, offering, and offerer is established in the manner God appointed. In the theology of Leviticus blood represents life, and the handling of life must be solemn, never at man's arbitrary disposal.

After the slaughter comes skinning and cutting the offering into its pieces. Skinning and cutting open the offering completely, with nothing hidden. Then the priest puts fire upon the altar and lays the wood in order on the fire. Every part of the offering — head, fat, and pieces — is laid in order. The text mentions in particular that the inwards and the legs shall be washed in water. The inwards are the innermost part, the place where filth is gathered; the legs are the part that walks and touches the dust; even these must be washed before they are burned. This detail is not merely a requirement of hygiene; in the language of the rite it declares that what is offered to God — inside and outside, hidden and visible — must all be clean. Man offers the whole animal; God inspects the whole. No part may be held back from offering, and no corner may say: this part is not under God's rule.

Finally the priest burns all of it on the altar. The text sums it up: "to be a burnt sacrifice, an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD" (Lev 1:9, KJV). The Hebrew name of the burnt offering is olah, connected in its basic sense with going up — the offering that wholly goes up, turned to smoke ascending. The notes will give the lexical details. Its defining feature is that it is wholly burned: the hide has its own rule assigning it to the priest later in the book, but as to the flesh of the animal, no portion is left for the offerer, and no table of a meal shared between man and God is set. The contrast with the peace offering's shared eating is only a structural pointer — the peace offering, in chapter 3, has a portion shared by the offerer and the company; the burnt offering goes wholly to the fire of the altar. Set side by side, the language of the burnt offering is clear: this is a giving that is complete, an offering without reserve. The offerer comes leading a bullock and goes back empty-handed. His gain is not in taking anything back, but in this offering's being accepted by God.

The phrase "sweet savour" needs careful handling. The text uses the phrase reach nichoach, literally a scent of rest, a soothing fragrance — the Bible's anthropomorphic expression for God's acceptance of an offering. To say God smells the sweet savour is not to bring God down to the level of an idol needing the scent of food, but to express in human language God's acceptance and the settling of rest. Milgrom discusses this phrase at length, both preserving the traditional line of reading it as that which gives God rest and acceptance, and examining the various explanations of the anthropomorphism, reminding the reader that this language concerns the restoration of the relation between God and man, not a physical need of God. We set this discussion side by side: we do not preach the anthropomorphic expression as a physical fact about God, nor do we lightly reduce it to a mere metaphor and lose its weight. What the text truly says is this: such an offering God delights in, God accepts, and the tension between man and God comes to rest.

So this passage on the burnt offering of the bullock is, from beginning to end, one complete chain of actions: choose a male without blemish, bring it to the door of the tabernacle, lay the hand on it, slaughter it, sprinkle the blood, skin it and cut it into pieces, wash the inwards and legs, lay the wood and put fire, and burn all of it as a sweet savour. Every step has its appointed actor; no step may be omitted. The offerer is not a spectator, the priest is not the protagonist, God is the Lord who accepts. Reading this passage slowly, we understand that offering wholly is not a slogan, but a series of concrete acts of obedience: bringing, laying on the hand, slaughtering, washing, burning all. Men today no longer perform this rite, but its questions still fall upon us: is what we offer God our best, or our leftovers? Is it everything, or have we kept back a hidden part like the inwards? Is it according to God's word, or according to our own convenience? These are applications at the level of homiletical exposition, drawn from the ritual structure; they do not pretend to be direct commands of the original text, yet they are questions we cannot evade when the original text is set before us.

## IV. Sheep and Bird: The Same Road of Nearness, Different Measures 42:00-56:00

The second grade of offering is the sheep. The text says: "And if his offering be of the flocks, namely, of the sheep, or of the goats, for a burnt sacrifice; he shall bring it a male without blemish" (Lev 1:10, KJV). The procedure runs parallel to that of the bullock: the offerer takes part, the priest sprinkles the blood, the offering is cut into pieces, the inwards and legs are washed in water, and all is burned on the altar, summed up in the same words — an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD. Since it is parallel, preaching need not repeat every step, but grasp the differences. One conspicuous detail the sheep offering adds is the place of slaughter: it is to be killed "on the side of the altar northward before the LORD" (Lev 1:11, KJV). The north side of the altar is the appointed place for slaughtering offerings, used also for other offerings later in the book. An appointed place again shows that the order of sacrifice is not decided by man on the spot. In God's appointed place and God's appointed manner, and in this ordered regularity itself, the people learn reverence.

The difference between sheep and bullock is, still more, one of measure. A sheep is smaller than a bullock and of lower value. For a man of middling means, who has no spare bullock to offer, the sheep is what he can offer. The system does not refuse him because what he offers is smaller than his neighbour's, nor does it write the sheep offering as a second-class worship. The text writes the sheep offering in nearly parallel language, and sums it up with the same "sweet savour." This itself declares: acceptance is not ranked by the market price of the offering. Whether a man offers a bullock or a sheep, what matters is that it be without blemish, that it follow God's order, that it be wholly burned and given to God. Size is an economic fact; acceptance is God's grace; the two must not be confused. To read sacrifice as a competition of the rich is to read Leviticus exactly backwards.

The third grade of offering is the bird. "And if the burnt sacrifice for his offering to the LORD be of fowls, then he shall bring his offering of turtledoves, or of young pigeons" (Lev 1:14, KJV). The procedure for the bird differs markedly, and so does the actor: the offerer brings the bird to the priest, and the handling that follows is mainly performed by the priest. The priest wrings off its head — the head is wrung off in the manner the text describes and burned on the altar — and wrings out its blood at the side of the altar. Then he takes away its crop with its filth and casts it beside the altar on the east part, by the place of the ashes. The ash heap is where the ashes of the altar are poured; what is unclean is cast there and not left upon the altar. Then the priest cleaves it with its wings — cleaving it open, but not dividing it asunder — and burns it on the altar, on the wood that is upon the fire. The text sums it up in the same words: "it is a burnt sacrifice, an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD" (Lev 1:17, KJV).

Notice that the summing-up sentence of the three passages is almost identical. The bullock is a sweet savour, the sheep is a sweet savour, and the bird is a sweet savour too. The text deliberately grants the smallest offering and the greatest offering the same sentence of verdict. This is the most moving structural fact of the chapter. A turtledove is not worth much money, and a young pigeon is the poorest possession in a poor man's hand. Yet God does not say the bird offering is only a makeshift; He does not say the poor man's offering counts only as half. Offered in the manner God appointed, the bird offering too is wholly burned, ascends in the same way, and is accepted with the same verdict of sweet savour. Man looks at the measure; God looks at the offering that comes according to His word. Man grades by price; God's verdict draws the three grades into the one sentence of sweet savour.

That the bird offering is the lowest grade, appointed for those who cannot afford livestock, is not the preacher's kind invention; it is supported by intertextual facts at the factual level beyond this chapter. Leviticus 5:7 says that if a man's hand cannot reach to a lamb, he may bring two turtledoves or two young pigeons for his sin; 12:8 says that if a woman after childbirth is not able to bring a lamb, she may bring birds — and this is exactly the grade Mary performed after the birth of Jesus; 14:22 and other places make the same graded provision for the poor in the rite of cleansing. The sacrificial system, we see, builds economic grading in: he who has a bullock offers a bullock, he who has a sheep offers a sheep, he who has only a bird offers a bird. God lowers the threshold so that even the poorest can cross it, yet He does not lower the standard into "anything will do." The bird too must be handled according to the rule: its blood wrung out, its crop removed, its wings cleaved open, and all of it burned. Compassion is not a lowering of holiness; it is the opening of holiness's road to the poor as well. Grading is not indulgence of carelessness; it acknowledges that men's strength differs, and grace therefore arranges the road of nearness according to each man's strength.

Present this honestly — neither exalting nor belittling. Not exalting means not beautifying an ancient system into a modern welfare scheme; it remains a sacrificial system in an ancient Near Eastern setting, with blood, fire, and a priesthood. Not belittling means not mocking its detail as fussiness, and not overlooking its real compassion for the poor. The three grades of offering point to one and the same thing: everyone may draw near. This is homiletical exposition, and its level must be marked — the textual fact is that the three grades stand side by side and are all called a sweet savour; "everyone may draw near" is the summary and application of that fact. The rich man leading a bullock must not boast; the middling man leading a sheep need not feel inferior; the poor man cupping a young pigeon in his hands need not hide behind the crowd. The road to the door of the tabernacle is the same road for all three. The fire on the altar burns the bullock and the sheep — and it burns the bird as well. The call of God comes out of the tabernacle not only to the capable, but to the man with empty hands and only a dove.

This section also corrects our misunderstanding of worship. We often think we must wait until we have enough, and good enough, before we may decently come before God. The order of Leviticus is: come with what you have. If you have the heart to offer a bullock, good; if you have no bullock, a sheep will do; if you have no sheep, a dove offered according to God's word is also a sweet savour. God does not ask a man to offer what he does not have; God asks a man to bring what he truly has, wholly, according to His word, before Him. Delay often borrows the excuse of humility when it is in fact reserve. Leviticus leaves reserve no excuse: whatever is in your hand today, begin there to draw near to God.

## V. From the Altar of Whole Burning to Bodies Offered 58:00-60:00

Brothers and sisters, Leviticus chapter 1 begins with a call and ends with a sweet savour. God calls man from the tabernacle; man brings bullock, sheep, and bird to the altar; the smoke of the whole burning rises, and God accepts it. What follows belongs to the level of New Testament reading and must not be read back into Leviticus as its plain original meaning: chapter 10 of Hebrews looks back on these burnt offerings and points out that the blood of bulls and of goats cannot take away sins; offered year by year, the sacrifices instead bring sins to remembrance, and it directs the reader's gaze to the One who offered Himself once and accomplished eternal redemption. The altar of Leviticus is shadow and preparation; the New Testament's interpretation shows the substance to which it pointed. Ephesians 5:2 says Christ loved us and gave Himself for us, "an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweetsmelling savour" (KJV) — the word for sweet savour in its New Testament use echoes Leviticus, but it lands upon Christ's giving of Himself. The homiletical use of Romans 12:1 belongs to the same level: Paul beseeches the brethren to present their bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God. A living sacrifice is no longer a beast dying on an altar, but the believer's body and life in daily obedience. The use is legitimate, but it must be marked as New Testament and homiletical application; it must not be said that Leviticus 1 in its original text was already speaking of what Romans means.

Let us close with this chapter itself. God still speaks first today, and still calls man to draw near according to the word He has given. May we not fob God off with leftovers, not hide the part within, and not draw back because we hold only a dove. Whatever we have, let us offer it; however much we have, let us offer it wholly according to His word. He who leads a bullock, he who leads a sheep, and he who cups a dove are all alike accepted, all alike called a sweet savour. This is the call from the tabernacle: may we hear it, and answer.

## Notes

1. The joint between Leviticus 1:1 and Exodus 40:34-35: the cloud covering the tabernacle, the glory filling it, and Moses unable to enter, read against Leviticus 1:1, where the LORD calls Moses out of the tabernacle, form a narrative hinge. This is structural observation; see Wenham's discussion in his introduction and on 1:1, and Hartley on the theology of the tabernacle and the call motif at the opening of the book.

2. vayyiqra: Hebrew וַיִּקְרָא, transliteration vayyiqra, verb, to call, call out, proclaim, read aloud, name, Strong number H7121. Meaning according to BDB: to call, call out, recite, read, cry out, proclaim; HALOT likewise lists calling, inviting, and reading aloud among its senses. The first word of the chapter is both its narrative verb and the Hebrew name of the book. God is the subject of the calling and Moses the object; this grammatical fact is the textual basis of the exposition in this sermon.

3. qorban: Hebrew קָרְבָּן, transliteration qorban, noun, offering, oblation, Strong number H7133. Meaning according to BDB: offering, oblation; HALOT: offering, gift brought near. It is connected with the root meaning to bring near and to present, and stresses what is brought before God, rather than a gift in the general sense. In this chapter it denotes the animal or bird offered, at 1:2, 1:3, and elsewhere.

4. 'olah: Hebrew עֹלָה, transliteration 'olah, noun, burnt offering, whole burnt offering, Strong number H5930. Meaning according to BDB: whole burnt-offering, related to the root of going up; traditionally explained as the offering whose smoke and substance ascend; HALOT likewise glosses it as burnt offering. Its mark in this chapter is that the animal is burned wholly on the altar, with no portion returned to the offerer for sharing.

5. samakh: Hebrew סָמַךְ, transliteration samakh, verb, to lay the hand on, lean on, support, Strong number H5564. Meaning according to BDB: to lean, lay, rest, support; in ritual use, to lay the hand heavily upon the head of an animal or person. HALOT lists ritual senses including conferring and identifying by the laying on of the hand. For the three views of the meaning of the laying on of the hand — identification, transfer of ownership, and transfer of sin — set out side by side, see Milgrom's extended discussion on 1:4 and Wenham's survey of the rite of laying on of the hand; this sermon draws no final conclusion.

6. ratzon: Hebrew רָצוֹן, transliteration ratzon, noun, acceptance, delight, favour, goodwill, Strong number H7522. Meaning according to BDB: pleasure, delight, favour, goodwill, acceptance; HALOT: favour, goodwill, pleasure. The expressions of acceptance in 1:3 and 1:4 belong to this word family and stress that the offering obtains delight and reception before God, rather than that man's act of presenting alone completes the matter.

7. reach nichoach: Hebrew רֵיחַ נִיחֹחַ, transliteration reach nichoach, a scent of rest, a soothing fragrance. Of its parts, reach is רֵיחַ, scent, odour, Strong number H7381, BDB: scent, odour; nichoach is נִיחֹחַ, rest, acceptance, Strong number H5207, BDB: soothing, quieting, tranquillising, connected with the semantic field of rest and ceasing; HALOT glosses it as soothing fragrance. The phrase is an anthropomorphic expression. Milgrom discusses the traditional reading of it as that which gives God rest and acceptance, and examines the theological limits of the anthropomorphic language; this sermon sets his discussion side by side and gives no physiological interpretation.

8. "Without blemish": the original wording relates to wholeness and freedom from defect, denoting an animal without maiming or disease. Leviticus 22 sets out the qualifications of offerings more fully, and the requirements of 1:3 and 1:10 in this chapter correspond to it. Wenham and Hartley both note that the requirement of wholeness is connected with the holiness of God and the offerer's precious regard, and is not to be interpreted as a contest of value.

9. The north side of the altar: see 1:11. The appointed place of slaughter is connected with the spatial order of the altar; Milgrom treats the north side as the place for slaughtering and handling the most holy offerings, and Hartley likewise notes that this detail displays the sacred grading of ritual space.

10. The procedure for the bird offering: see 1:14-17 — wringing off its head, wringing out its blood at the side of the altar, removing its crop with its filth, casting it by the place of the ashes east of the altar, cleaving open its wings without dividing it asunder, and burning it wholly. The crop is the bird's pouch-like organ for storing food. The bird offering is handled mainly by the priest, unlike the bullock and sheep offerings, which the offerer slaughters; Wenham explains this difference.

11. Intertexts for the grading of the bird offering: Leviticus 5:7, 5:11, 12:8, 14:22, 27:8, and other places set substitution and lowering of grade according to the offerer's means. The bird offering of 12:8 is the provision for the poor in the rite of purification after childbirth, and Luke 2:24 records that Mary offered birds accordingly — an intertext at the factual level. This sermon relies on it only to show that economic grading is built into the system.

12. Sources at the level of New Testament reading: Hebrews 10:1-14 on the inability of the sacrifices to take away sins and Christ's offering of Himself once for all; Ephesians 5:2, describing Christ's giving of Himself with the phrase "a sweetsmelling savour"; Romans 12:1, exhorting believers with the figure of a living sacrifice to present their bodies. All three are New Testament interpretations and uses of the Old Testament sacrificial language; when preaching cites them, the level must be marked, and they must not be read back as the plain original meaning of Leviticus 1.

## Devotional Excerpt

At the end of Exodus the glory fills the tabernacle, yet Moses cannot enter; in the first sentence of Leviticus, God calls Moses from within the tabernacle. The way to draw near is not for man to force his way in, but for God to speak first, and first to teach man the order of nearness. Today I need not prove myself with my best performance, nor shrink back because what I hold is small. Bring an ox if you have an ox, a lamb if you have a lamb, and if you have only a dove, offer the dove according to God's word. Ox, lamb, and bird are all called a sweet savour — God delights not in the size, but in a heart brought wholly before Him. I think too of the offerer laying his hand on the animal's head and leading it himself to the door of the tabernacle: nearness is not an empty phrase, but a concrete coming, bringing one's real life before God. Lord, teach me not to offer You leftovers, nor to hide any part within; let me bring what I truly have today — my time, strength, worries, and thanks, and even the reserves I am unwilling to surrender — before You, hear Your call, answer according to Your word, and rest in Your acceptance.

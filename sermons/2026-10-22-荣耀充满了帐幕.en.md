## Abstract

On the first day of the first month in the second year after the Exodus, Moses set up the tabernacle as the LORD had commanded him, placed the ark and all its vessels, anointed the tabernacle, and installed Aaron and his sons in office. When everything had been done accordingly, the cloud covered the tent of meeting and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle, and Moses was not able to enter. Thereafter, when the cloud was taken up the people journeyed, and when the cloud rested the people camped: Exodus closes not with the people arriving in the promised land, but with God dwelling in the midst of His people and journeying with them.

## 00:00-08:00 Introduction: Finished, but Not Yet Set Up

Brothers and sisters, peace to you.

We have read Exodus together for a long time, chapter by chapter, and today we come to the last chapter of this book. Look back over the road of this book: it begins with brick kilns and bitter labour in Egypt, with the infant Moses hidden in an ark of bulrushes, with the words "I AM THAT I AM" spoken from the burning bush; it passes through the ten plagues, through the blood of the Passover lamb struck on the doorposts, through the Red Sea divided, the people walking on dry ground, and Pharaoh's army sinking beneath the waters; through manna and quails in the wilderness, through thunder and lightning and the Ten Commandments on Mount Sinai, through the golden calf — that apostasy that nearly destroyed the nation — and through Moses interceding for the people on the mountain, and God renewing His covenant with them; through the people's willing offerings, through Bezalel and Oholiab filled with the Spirit of God and working with wisdom, through vessel after vessel made according to the pattern shown on the mountain. At the end of the previous chapter, all the work was finished. Moses looked upon all the work, and saw that they had done it as the LORD had commanded, and he blessed them. By all appearances, the story had reached a satisfying close: what God commanded, the people had made; what the people had made, Moses had inspected, and blessed.

But notice: up to that point, the tabernacle was still only a set of finished components. The curtains were folded, the boards were leaning, the ark, the table, the lampstand and the altars stood piece by piece on the ground — not set up, not assembled, not placed, not anointed, with no sacrifice offered and no priest ministering within. A finished tabernacle and an erected tabernacle are two different things. What is finished is the fruit of human obedience; what is erected is the dwelling into which God will enter. Exodus does not stop at the completion of the work. It devotes an entire chapter to the command to set up the tabernacle, the carrying out of that setting up, and the great thing that happened afterwards: the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle. This is the concluding chapter of the book, and it is also its climax. Every promise, every pattern, and every piece of workmanship in the preceding thirty-nine chapters must come to rest in this one picture: God has moved in.

First, let us see the structure of the chapter clearly. Its thirty-eight verses fall into three parts, fitting the narrative movement exactly. The first part, verses 1-16, is the command to set up the tabernacle: the LORD speaks to Moses, fixes the date, and commands item by item the order of erection, the placing of the vessels, the rite of anointing, and the installation of Aaron and his sons. The second part, verses 17-33, is the execution: on that day Moses sets up the tabernacle with his own hands, step by step, the narrative advancing through a chain of actions, and at the end of each major step it closes with the sentence, "as the LORD commanded Moses" — like a seal of inspection stamped down, one after another. The third part, verses 34-38, has two layers: first, the glory fills — the cloud covers the tent of meeting, the glory of the LORD fills the tabernacle, and Moses is not able to enter; then, the close of the whole book — when the cloud is taken up the people journey, when the cloud is not taken up the people wait, by day in the cloud and by night in the fire, in the sight of all the house of Israel. The last sentence of Exodus is not something the people said, nor something Moses said, but how God was present with His people and journeyed with them. The book ends with a picture of journeying together.

In preaching this chapter, this sermon keeps the old rule of this series and distinguishes its levels clearly, lest we mistake our own feelings for the meaning of the text. First, the factual level: dates, order, vessels, actions, and counts — the facts the text states expressly — are presented here exactly as written, and where a count is made, its result is given, so that brothers and sisters can open their Bibles and check. Second, the structural-observation level: for example, that this chapter is the closing of the book's whole narrative arc, that the ordination rite of chapter 29 is only carried into execution at the end of the book here in chapter 40, and that the promises of chapters 25 and 29 are fulfilled here — these are observations drawn from reading this chapter within the structure of the whole book, and this sermon will mark them as structural observations, not pass them off as the words of the text itself. Third, the homiletical-application level: exhortations drawn from the text for life today will be identified as application, so that hearers can distinguish which sentence the Bible speaks and which sentence the preacher has received. With these three levels kept distinct, we may preach with boldness the greatest thing in this chapter: the filling of God's glory is not an atmosphere manufactured by man; it is God Himself coming to the dwelling prepared according to His own command. What man can do is to do it accordingly; the filling is God's own act.

Let us read together several key passages of this chapter: "On the first day of the first month shalt thou set up the tabernacle" (Exod 40:2, excerpt, KJV). "Thus did Moses: according to all that the LORD commanded him, so did he" (Exod 40:16, KJV). "And it came to pass in the first month in the second year, on the first day of the month, that the tabernacle was reared up" (Exod 40:17, KJV). "Then a cloud covered the tent of the congregation, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle. And Moses was not able to enter into the tent of the congregation, because the cloud abode thereon, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exod 40:34-35, KJV). "And when the cloud was taken up from over the tabernacle, the children of Israel went onward in all their journeys: But if the cloud were not taken up, then they journeyed not till the day that it was taken up" (Exod 40:36-37, KJV).

Let us pray: Lord, this book begins with You hearing the cry of Your people, and it ends with You dwelling in the midst of Your people. Open our eyes today to see how, step by step, You made the promise of Your presence into fact; and open our hearts, so that we are not content merely to finish the work, but long for You Yourself to fill Your dwelling. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## 08:00-24:00 The Command to Set Up: On the First Day of the First Month, from the Inside Out (40:1-16)

The first sentence of this chapter is that the LORD spoke unto Moses. Throughout this book, the LORD speaks to Moses more times than can be counted, but the place of this speaking is very particular: it is not on the mountain, not after a plague, and not after the people's sin; it is after all the work has been inspected and found complete. God did not leave Moses to decide by his own mind the day and the manner of setting up the tabernacle. A matter as great as this erection still comes, from date to order, sentence by sentence, from God's command. Verses 1-16 are the full text of that command. Let us look at it item by item.

First, the date. Verse 2 specifies: "On the first day of the first month shalt thou set up the tabernacle." Verse 17 then fixes this date in the calendar: in the first month in the second year, on the first day of the month, the tabernacle was reared up. This "second year" means the second year after leaving Egypt. Set several dates side by side, and the timeline becomes clear — this is a factual-level comparison: Exodus 12 records that, in the land of Egypt, God instructed Moses and Aaron to make the first month the beginning of months; the people killed the Passover lamb at twilight on the fourteenth day of the first month and left Egypt on the fifteenth day. After the Exodus, the people left Egypt in the first month, came to Mount Sinai in the third month, and camped at the mountain for nearly a year, receiving the law and building the tabernacle. On the first day of the first month in the second year, the tabernacle was set up. Looking further to Numbers: Numbers 1:1 records that on the first day of the second month in the second year, the LORD spoke to Moses in the tabernacle of the congregation and commanded the numbering of the people. That is, one month after the tabernacle was set up, God began, in the tabernacle already filled with glory, to command the numbering and the journeys. The order cannot be reversed: first God's dwelling stands at the centre of the camp, first God's presence becomes fact, and only then come the people's organisation and itinerary. Presence first, organisation afterwards; dwelling first, marching afterwards. This sermon draws one structural observation from this timeline: Exodus gives the first day of the new year to the setting up of the tabernacle; the new year does not begin with the people's plans — it begins with God moving in.

Next, the order of erection. The order of this command runs from the inside out, and this sermon lists it item by item according to the text; notice the direction in which it advances. Verses 2-3: first the tabernacle itself is set up, then the ark is placed within and the veil hung to cover the ark. The ark is the innermost vessel of the tabernacle; within it are the tables of the testimony, the commandments written by God's own hand; above it is the mercy seat. So the first step of erection is not to arrange the outer things, but to install the innermost, most central thing. Verse 4: the table is brought in and what belongs on it is set in order; the lampstand is brought in and its lamps are lit. Table and lampstand stand in the holy place — on one side the showbread, on the other the burning lamps. Verses 5-7: the golden altar is set before the veil before the ark, and the curtain for the door of the tabernacle is hung. The golden altar is small, but its place is directly before the veil of the Holy of Holies — the vessel in the holy place nearest to the ark. Verses 9-11: the tabernacle and all that is in it are anointed with the anointing oil and sanctified; the altar of burnt offering and all its vessels are anointed; the laver and its base are anointed. Anointing is not decoration; anointing is separation: what has been anointed is set apart from ordinary objects and reserved exclusively for God. The oil anoints the innermost first and the outermost afterwards, in the same order as the placing — still from the inside out. Within verses 6-7, the altar of burnt offering is commanded to be set before the door of the tabernacle, and the laver between the tent of meeting and the altar, with water put therein. Verses 12-15: Aaron and his sons are brought to the door of the tent of meeting, washed with water, Aaron is clothed with the holy garments and anointed and sanctified to minister as priest; his sons are clothed with coats and anointed, as their father was anointed, so that they shall be priests for generations. Verse 8, too, belongs to the command: the hangings of the court are set up round about and the curtain is hung at the court gate. Read through the whole command, and one line stands out clearly: beginning from the ark in the Holy of Holies, passing through the table, the lampstand and the golden altar in the holy place, anointing the whole tabernacle, then to the altar of burnt offering and the laver in the court, then to the hangings of the court, and finally landing on the installation of the priests. From the core of God's presence at the innermost place, it moves outward circle by circle, out to the court that the people may approach, and is then embodied in the persons who minister between God and man.

Here we must pause to speak of the installation of Aaron and his sons, for this section carries great weight in the structure of the book — this is the structural-observation level. Chapter 28 records God's command to make holy garments for Aaron and his sons, for glory and for beauty; chapter 29 records the full rite of ordination: washing, clothing, anointing, and sacrifice — how priests are consecrated, God explains there item by item. But notice: chapters 28 and 29 record commands and patterns received on the mountain; the priests' garments were not made until chapter 39, and the ordination rite is taken up again here in chapter 40, within the command to set up the tabernacle, and is truly carried out in what follows. The words of verses 13-15 are most solemn: put the holy garments upon him, and anoint him and sanctify him, that he may minister unto Me as priest; and anoint his sons, as their father was anointed; they shall be priests throughout their generations. In other words, on the same day the tabernacle is set up, the priesthood is also formally established: there is a dwelling, there are vessels, and there are also persons ministering in the dwelling. The tabernacle is not an empty sanctuary; while God prepares a dwelling, He also prepares those who will serve. This line runs further forward: Leviticus 8 will record the execution of the ordination rite again in detail — that belongs to the next book, and this sermon only marks this chapter's place here: at the close of this book, the ordination rite passes from blueprint into a command under execution.

At the end of the first part, verse 16 is a set sentence which this sermon calls the general charter of inspection for the whole chapter — this is the factual level: "Thus did Moses: according to all that the LORD commanded him, so did he" (Exod 40:16, KJV). Notice the position and weight of this sentence. It is written not after the execution, but at the end of the command and before the execution — like a general seal stamped in advance at the end of a written order: every step recorded below falls under the covering of this sentence. Nor does this sentence appear in this book for the first time: from the building of the tabernacle onward, the formula "as the LORD commanded Moses" recurs again and again, threading through all the work; here in Exodus 40:16 it appears in its most complete form — according to all that He commanded, so did he; the word "all" fills out the scope, and the word "so" makes the carrying out complete. Of what God commanded, nothing was done selectively, nothing was altered, nothing was left undone. The execution record that follows is the item-by-item unfolding of this general charter. Brothers and sisters, at this point in the command to set up, there is one homiletical application, marked as such: the dwelling of God's presence is prepared beginning from the innermost place. When a person prepares to meet God, he should also begin from the innermost place: not first arranging the outer court for others to see, but first letting the ark — God's word — be placed in the deepest place; first lighting the lamp, first burning the incense, first being sanctified; only then can there be talk of outward service and journey. Reverse the order, and however lively the outside may be, the inside remains empty.

## 24:00-42:00 The Execution: Moses Sets It Up with His Own Hands, as Commanded (40:17-33)

The second part begins at verse 17, and the date lands first: "And it came to pass in the first month in the second year, on the first day of the month, that the tabernacle was reared up" (Exod 40:17, KJV). The first day of the first month of the command is, in the execution record, not off by a single day. Immediately, the pace of the narrative changes markedly: in the first part God is commanding, and the subject of the verbs is the LORD; in the second part Moses is at work, and the narrative advances through a chain of actions with Moses as subject — Moses reared up the tabernacle. This sermon lists this string of verbs according to the text; this is a factual-level textual observation: he reared up the tabernacle, fastened its sockets, set up its boards, put in its bars, and reared up its pillars; he spread the tent over the tabernacle and put the covering of the tent above upon it; he took and put the testimony into the ark, set the staves on the ark, and put the mercy seat above upon the ark; he brought the ark into the tabernacle and set up the veil of the covering to cover the ark of the testimony; he put the table in the tent of meeting and set the showbread in order upon it; he put the lampstand in the tent of meeting and lit its lamps; he put the golden altar in the tent of meeting before the veil and burned sweet incense thereon; he set up the hanging at the door of the tabernacle; he put the altar of burnt offering by the door of the tabernacle and offered upon it the burnt offering and the meal offering; he set the laver between the tent of meeting and the altar and put water there; he set up the hangings of the court and hung up the hanging at the court gate. More than a dozen actions are recorded in one breath, with not one sentence of commentary and not one sentence of lyricism between them. Scripture records the setting up not with adjectives piled up for atmosphere, but with verbs recording facts: reared up, fastened, put in, took and put, set up, set in order, lit, offered, put water. The tabernacle was set up precisely like this, board by board and step by step: every board has its socket, every bar its ring, every vessel its place. Obedience here is not a mood; it is a chain of actions done rightly.

Notice again the order of this string of actions: it fits the order of the command in the first part exactly, still from the inside out — first the frame and the tent are raised, first the ark is installed, then the table, the lampstand and the golden altar of the holy place, the door hanging is hung, then the altar of burnt offering and the laver in the court, and finally the court is set up. As the command spoke, so the execution did; even the order was not changed. This is precisely the textual fact in this section most worth counting: at the end of each major step, the narrative closes with the sentence, "as the LORD commanded Moses." This sermon counts them verse by verse: in the execution record of verses 17-33, this closing sentence appears seven times, at verses 19, 21, 23, 25, 27, 29, and 32: verse 19, after the covering of the tent was put on; verse 21, after the ark was brought into the tabernacle and the veil hung; verse 23, after the showbread was set in order; verse 25, after the lamps were lit; verse 27, after the incense was burned; verse 29, after the burnt offering and the meal offering were offered; and verse 32, after the matter of washing has been stated. Seven closings, like seven seals of inspection, one stamped at each step. Together with the general charter of verse 16 — "according to all that the LORD commanded him, so did he" — the whole execution portion of chapter 40, from general charter to individual items, has not one step falling outside the command. This sermon makes a structural observation here: Exodus writes obedience not with words of praise, but with a repeated sentence-form — by the time the reader reaches the seventh "as commanded," the weight of obedience needs no one's explanation; it presses down of itself. Moses' greatness in this chapter is written with not one adjective, but only with this repeated sentence: so did he.

In this section there are also two details that must not be passed over lightly. The first is the offering on the altar, verse 29: Moses put the altar of burnt offering by the door of the tabernacle of the tent of meeting, and offered upon it the burnt offering and the meal offering. On the very day the tabernacle was set up, there were offerings on the altar. The first use of the prepared dwelling was not a tour and not a celebration, but sacrifice: offerings laid on the altar, a meal offering presented before God. This outermost altar in the court is the first vessel a person meets in approaching the tabernacle; the order of erection day reminds us here that the way of approaching God begins at the altar. The second is the washing, verses 30-32: Moses set the laver between the tent of meeting and the altar and put water there, "to wash withal: Moses and Aaron and his sons washed their hands and their feet thereat: When they went into the tent of the congregation, and when they came near unto the altar, they washed; as the LORD commanded Moses" (Exod 40:30-32, KJV). Notice especially who washes: not only Aaron and his sons — Moses himself is among them. The one who set up the tabernacle did not stand outside the rule: he set up the tabernacle with his own hands, and he also washed his hands and feet in the laver with his own hands. The text places Moses in the same laver-water as Aaron and his sons; this sermon states the fact and draws no more from it than this one point: in the cleanness of service there are no exceptions — even the leader is included. Verse 33 closes the whole section: Moses set up the court round about the tabernacle and the altar, and set up the hanging of the court gate. "So Moses finished the work" (Exod 40:33, KJV). The words "finished the work" carry great weight: from receiving the pattern on the mountain in chapter 25, through the willing offerings, the workmanship, the inspection, and the erection, the project spanning fifteen chapters draws to its close here. In Genesis, God created heaven and earth and finished His work; in Exodus, Moses set up the tabernacle according to the pattern and finished the work. This sermon marks this as a structural observation of an intertextual echo: read side by side, the two "finished" passages let us see a correspondence — the creating God has now, in the midst of His people and according to His pattern, obtained a dwelling in which He may abide. Man's work was finished; what happened next was no longer man's work.

## 42:00-56:00 The Glory Fills: The Cloud Covers, and Moses Is Not Able to Enter (40:34-38, first part)

The third part has only a few verses, yet it is the climax of the whole book. Verse 34: "Then a cloud covered the tent of the congregation, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exod 40:34, KJV). The word "then" nails down the time: at the very moment Moses finished the work, after everything had been done accordingly — not many days later, and not waiting for the people to do anything more. The order here is of the utmost importance, and this sermon states it as fact: first man prepares everything according to the command, and afterwards God Himself fills. Man cannot manufacture the filling; man can only prepare accordingly; the verb of filling has the glory of the LORD as its subject, not Moses and not the people. The cloud covers, and the glory fills — two actions stand side by side: the cloud is the sign visible from outside; the glory is the reality filling the inside. Seen from outside, the tabernacle is covered by the cloud; within the tabernacle, it is filled with glory. Verse 35 then says something unexpected: "And Moses was not able to enter into the tent of the congregation, because the cloud abode thereon, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exod 40:35, KJV). The one who set up the tabernacle cannot enter the tabernacle he himself set up. The one who installed the ark with his own hands stands outside the veil he himself hung. The coming of the glory and the halting of man are set side by side in these two verses, and this sermon presents them at the factual level without softening the tension for the text: God's presence is real nearness, and it is also real holiness; the glory fills to such a degree that even Moses must halt. Presence does not pull God down to man's measure; presence is God, according to His own holiness, dwelling in the midst of man, so that man must learn anew before Him what reverence is, and what waiting is.

Here there are two matters of intertext and recapitulation that must be spoken clearly, and whose levels must be marked. The first is a factual-level intertext: 1 Kings 8:10-11, at the dedication of Solomon's temple — "And it came to pass, when the priests were come out of the holy place, that the cloud filled the house of the LORD, So that the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud: for the glory of the LORD had filled the house of the LORD" (1 Kgs 8:10-11, KJV). The two records are highly parallel in sentence-form and picture: in both, the dwelling is completed and offerings made; in both, cloud and glory fill; in both, those who minister cannot stand and cannot enter. Tabernacle and temple — one in the wilderness, one in Jerusalem; one set up by Moses, one built by Solomon; yet God's response is the same: He seals the dwelling by filling it, and He manifests holiness by halting man. This sermon cites this parallel as a factual-level textual comparison; it does not pour the temple record back into this chapter, but lets brothers and sisters see that Scripture, with the same picture, witnesses fore and aft to the same God. The second is a recapitulation at the structural-observation level, and must be marked as such: in Exodus 24, Moses went up the mountain, the cloud covered the mountain, and the glory of the LORD abode upon Mount Sinai, appearing in the sight of the children of Israel like devouring fire, and Moses was in the cloud forty days and forty nights; in chapter 33, after the golden calf incident, Moses in the tent asked God, "I beseech thee, shew me thy glory" (Exod 33:18, KJV), and God promised to let His goodness pass before him, but covered his face, saying that man shall not see His face and live. At that time, the glory was on the mountain, in the cloud, and Moses could only, from the cleft of the rock and under the covering of God's hand, see the back of the glory as it passed by. In chapter 40, the glory no longer merely passes by on the mountain; the glory fills the tabernacle at the centre of the people's camp. The glory Moses asked to see in chapter 33 now fills the midst of the people — this is a structural observation this sermon draws from the narrative arc of the whole book: from the glory passing by on the mountain to the glory filling the camp, what lies between is precisely this whole stretch of obedience — the people working according to the pattern and Moses setting up according to the command. God did not lower His holiness to accommodate the people; God prepared a dwelling, sanctified vessels and persons, and then personally moved in.

Go one layer deeper, and here the book's earliest promise is fulfilled — this, too, is a structural observation, and hearers are asked to keep the distinction. Exodus 25:8: before giving the pattern, God said, "And let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them" (Exod 25:8, KJV). Exodus 29:45-46 says it still more completely: "And I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will be their God. And they shall know that I am the LORD their God, that brought them forth out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them" (Exod 29:45-46, KJV). The purpose of the Exodus is disclosed already in chapter 29: He brought them out not only to deliver them from Egypt, but to dwell among them; the end of the Exodus is not the wilderness — it is presence. In Exodus 40:34, the glory filled the tabernacle, and the words of chapters 25 and 29 landed on the ground, word for word: He really has moved in. This sermon calls this line the fulfilment of the promise; it is a structural observation, not the wording of this chapter itself; but this line has verses that can be checked, and brothers and sisters may read Exodus 25:8, 29:45-46, and 40:34 side by side for themselves — set the three side by side, and the skeleton of the whole book stands up. What Exodus speaks of has never been only a deliverance; it is also a welcoming: God brought the people out of Egypt in order to bring Himself into the midst of the people.

Therefore, the homiletical application of this section — marked as application — is only one sentence: do not treat the filling as a programme man can schedule. Man's side of the work has been written out in full by Scripture: prepare according to the command, place in order, be sanctified, wash hands and feet, offer sacrifice on the altar, and do accordingly all that can be done accordingly. Then man must learn to stand still outside the veil, to watch the cloud cover and the glory fill, and to confess that the filling is God's sovereignty and grace. In church life, the mistake man most easily makes is to substitute busyness for presence and projects for filling. Chapter 40 sets the order straight: the work must be done, and done accordingly to its finish; but when the work is finished, man must step back one step and let God be God. That Moses was not able to enter the tent of meeting was not Moses' failure; it was precisely the success of the erection: if the dwelling had no filling of glory, Moses might come and go as conveniently as he pleased, yet it would be no more than an exquisitely made tent; once the glory fills, Moses halts, and the tabernacle truly becomes the tent of meeting — the place where God and man meet.

## 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: When the Cloud Is Taken Up They Journey; Cloud by Day, Fire by Night (40:36-38, second part) and Closing

The last three verses pull the lens of the camera back at once — from the tabernacle to the whole camp, from one day to all the years of the wilderness. Verses 36-37 say: "And when the cloud was taken up from over the tabernacle, the children of Israel went onward in all their journeys: But if the cloud were not taken up, then they journeyed not till the day that it was taken up" (Exod 40:36-37, KJV). The people's timetable is henceforth no longer in their own hands: when the cloud rises, they break camp; when the cloud rests, they encamp; if the cloud is not taken up for some days, they wait some days — without urging, without detouring. Verse 38 is the last sentence of the whole book: "For the cloud of the LORD was upon the tabernacle by day, and fire was on it by night, in the sight of all the house of Israel, throughout all their journeys" (Exod 40:38, KJV). "In the sight of all the house of Israel" — the watchers of the book's last sentence are the whole house, every one of the people: the filling of the glory is not only a matter of the Holy of Holies; it is a presence the whole house can look up and see — cloud by day and fire by night, guiding when they journey and watching when they rest. Exodus closes with God journeying with His people. This sermon offers here only one sentence of structural hint: from Joseph's dying words at the end of Genesis, "God will surely visit you" (cf. Gen 50:24-25), to the cloud and the fire before the eyes of the whole house at the end of Exodus, the arc of fulfilment closes across the two books — the God who promised to visit now stands at the centre of the camp, journeying with the people and encamping with them. One question remains open: Moses was not able to enter the tent of meeting; how, then, can man draw near? Leviticus 1:1: the LORD called unto Moses out of the tabernacle — He Himself speaks first and carries the way forward; this is one stroke of structural hint, to be preached in the next book. Today we remember only the end of this book: projects will be finished, and Moses will halt, but God's presence will not disperse. He dwells in the midst of His people, and He journeys with His people, until He brings them into the place He has promised. May we also live our whole lives beneath the cloud and the fire: when He journeys, we follow; when He rests, we rest. Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] mishkan: Hebrew מִשְׁכָּן, transliteration mishkan, Strong's number H4908. Meaning according to BDB: dwelling-place, tabernacle; according to HALOT: dwelling, tabernacle. The word shares a root with the verb shakan (to settle down, to dwell) and is the core term for God's dwelling in the second half of Exodus (Exod 25:9; 26:1; 40:2, 9, 17, etc.). In this chapter, Exod 40:2 and 9 command mishkan to be set up and anointed; 40:17 and 34-35 record its erection and its being filled with the glory.

[2] shakan: Hebrew שָׁכַן, transliteration shakan, Strong's number H7931. Meaning according to BDB: settle down, abide, dwell; according to HALOT: dwell, settle. In Exod 25:8, God's "I may dwell among them" uses the verbal form of this word; 29:45, "I will dwell among the children of Israel," uses it as well. The filling with glory in this chapter is the narrative realisation of the shakan promise.

[3] 'edut: Hebrew עֵדֻת, transliteration 'edut, Strong's number H5715. Meaning according to BDB: testimony — the testimony of the covenant borne by the tables; according to HALOT: testimony, evidence. The tabernacle is closely bound to the ark and the tables of the testimony: the tables are called the testimony (Exod 25:16, 21; 40:3, 20, where the tables are put into the ark). In this chapter, 40:3, 20, and 21 repeatedly mention the placing of the ark and the tables, as the starting point of the inside-out order of erection.

[4] Timeline comparison (factual level): Exodus 12 makes the first month the beginning of months, and the people left Egypt on the fifteenth day of the first month (cf. Num 33:3); Exod 40:2 and 17 fix the erection on the first day of the first month in the second year; Num 1:1 records the numbering of the people in the tabernacle on the first day of the second month in the second year. Read together, the three dates show the tabernacle was erected about one month before the numbering and the organisation of the journeys. See the discussions of the chronological frame of Exodus 40 in Childs and Durham.

[5] "As the LORD commanded Moses": the count made in this sermon for Exod 40:17-33 is seven occurrences, at 40:19, 21, 23, 25, 27, 29, and 32; 40:16 is the general-charter sentence, "according to all that the LORD commanded him, so did he." This sentence-form recurs throughout the building and erection narratives of Exodus 35-40 and constitutes the inspection framework of that narrative. The count follows the corresponding sentence-form in the Chinese Union Version and the Hebrew text; versification and wording differ slightly among translations, and the count rests on the verses listed, which may be checked one by one.

[6] kavod: Hebrew כָּבוֹד, transliteration kavod, Strong's number H3519. Meaning according to BDB: glory, honour — in its basic sense related to weight and worth; according to HALOT: glory, honour, wealth. Exod 40:34-35 twice says the glory of the LORD (kevod YHWH) filled the tabernacle; the glory Moses asked to see in 33:18 and the glory on the mountain in 24:16-17 both use this word; the three passages constitute the glory-line of the whole book.

[7] 'anan: Hebrew עָנָן, transliteration 'anan, Strong's number H6051. Meaning according to BDB: cloud; according to HALOT: cloud, cloud-mass. In Exodus the cloud is the visible sign of God's presence and guidance (Exod 13:21-22, the pillar of cloud and pillar of fire; 14:19-20; 24:15-18; 33:9-10; 40:34-38). In this chapter, in 40:34 the cloud covers the tent of meeting, and in 40:36-38 whether the cloud is taken up or not decides whether the people journey or not.

[8] male': Hebrew מָלֵא, transliteration male', Strong's number H4390. Meaning according to BDB: be full, fill; according to HALOT: be full, fill up. Exod 40:34-35 uses the predicative form of this word to record that the glory filled the tabernacle; 1 Kings 8:10-11, where the glory fills the temple at its dedication, likewise uses the same root, constituting a factual-level intertextual parallel.

[9] The 1 Kings 8:10-11 parallel (factual-level intertext): when the priests came out of the holy place, the cloud filled the house of the LORD, and the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud, for the glory of the LORD had filled the house of the LORD. Its three elements — cloud, glory filling, and ministers unable to enter — correspond one by one with Exod 40:34-35. This sermon uses it only as a textual comparison and does not claim that either record rewrites the meaning of the other.

[10] The execution of the ordination rite (structural observation): Exodus 28 gives the command for the holy garments, chapter 29 the command for the ordination rite, chapter 39 records the garments made, and 40:12-15 takes up again, within the command to set up, the washing, clothing, and anointing, commanding their execution, with the washing carried out at 40:31-32; Leviticus 8 separately records the full execution of the ordination rite in detail. In the structure of the book, this chapter is the gateway where blueprint passes into execution.

[11] Anointing and sanctification: Exod 40:9-11 commands the anointing of the tabernacle, its vessels, the altar of burnt offering, and the laver with the anointing oil, so that they become holy; verses 13-15, the anointing of Aaron and his sons. In this book anointing marks separation unto God (cf. Exod 30:22-33, the recipe for the holy anointing oil and its exclusive use). The sentence in the sermon text, "anointing is separation," is a summary drawn from this usage.

[12] The connection with Leviticus 1:1 (structural hint): Leviticus opens with the LORD calling unto Moses out of the tabernacle of the congregation. The question left by Exod 40:35 — that Moses was not able to enter the tent of meeting, how then can man draw near — is taken up by the next book through God's own initiative in calling and through the sacrificial ordinances. This sermon offers only a structural hint and does not develop the content of Leviticus.

## Devotional Excerpt

On the day the tabernacle was set up, Moses put every vessel in its right place and did all that he could do exactly as commanded — and then he stood outside the tent of meeting, unable to enter. Not because he had done something wrong, but precisely because everything was right: the glory had filled it, the cloud rested upon it, and he could only stand still. Reading this chapter today, I am reminded that the end of service is not more busyness, but learning to stand still. So often, once the work is done, we hurry to prove we are still useful, to press into every scene, to make God's presence make way for our plans. Moses teaches another lesson: to prepare as commanded is our duty; to fill is God's sovereignty. Set the ark in the innermost place, light the lamp, burn the incense, wash your hands — then step back and let God be God. When the cloud rises, follow; when it rests, rest; when the way is hidden, wait beneath the cloud and the fire. That resting cloud is still the sign today: He dwells among us, and He has not departed. Today, may I first prepare the inner place, do with my hands all that He has spoken, rest my feet before Him, then stand quietly before Him and watch Him fill His own dwelling — and fill even a lowly person like me.

## Summary (approx. 100 words)

After Terah died in Haran, God spoke to seventy-five-year-old Abram: Get out of your country, from your family and from your father's house, to a land that I will show you. God repeatedly declares "I will": He will make him a great nation, He will bless him, He will make his name great, He will make him a blessing, and in him all the families of the earth shall be blessed. Abram went. Arriving at Shechem and Bethel, he built altars along the way and called on the name of the Lord. This sermon looks at three things: the call begins with a command to leave before a series of promises unfolds; a name need not be snatched, for God Himself makes great the name of the obedient; and the blessed are not a terminus but a pipeline—wherever the altar-builder walks, the blessing flows.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Introduction · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace be with you, brothers and sisters!

The conclusion of our last message was somewhat heavy: Terah died in Haran. A family headed in the right direction, made a good start, walked halfway, settled down, and remained there until the leader passed away—and their luggage was never reopened.

Today, as we turn to chapter 12, the atmosphere completely changes. Chapter 11 is filled with man: man building a tower, man halting his steps, man growing old, man dying. In the very first verse of chapter 12, the subject changes—"Now the Lord had said to Abram." Where man stopped, God spoke; where the genealogy ran out, the promise began. One of the most monumental turning points in the entire Bible is found in the first three verses of this chapter: through one man, God was going to pour out His blessing upon all the people of the earth once again.

Furthermore, the very first words from God's mouth are not pleasant to the natural ear; they constitute a verb, an imperative: "Get out." Leave your country, leave your family, leave your father's house. The promise is magnificent, but the ticket of admission to the promise is departure. Today, in this message, let us follow Abram and walk through this doorway: what he left behind, what God promised, and what he did after he arrived.

Let us first read the key passages together:

"Now the Lord had said to Abram:
'Get out of your country,
From your family
And from your father’s house,
To a land that I will show you.
I will make you a great nation;
I will bless you
And make your name great;
And you shall be a blessing.
I will bless those who bless you,
And I will curse him who curses you;
And in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.'" (Gen. 12:1-3)

"So Abram departed as the Lord had spoken to him, and Lot went with him. And Abram was seventy-five years old when he departed from Haran." (Gen. 12:4)

"Then the Lord appeared to Abram and said, 'To your descendants I will give this land.' And there he built an altar to the Lord, who had appeared to him." (Gen. 12:7)

"...there he built an altar to the Lord and called on the name of the Lord." (Gen. 12:8b)

Today, we will examine this in three parts:

First, 12:1-3, "You must leave, and I will bless"—the order of the call is departure before sight; the heart of the promise is that one might become a blessing.
Second, 12:4-6, "So Abram departed"—setting out again at seventy-five years old, taking his entire household and his nephew Lot, entering the land where the Canaanites still lived.
Third, 12:7-9, The Altar-Builder—the Babelites built a tower for their own name; Abram built altars to call on the name of the Lord. Wherever he went, an altar was raised.

Let us first bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. Where Terah stopped, You did not stop. You spoke to Abram: Your first word was a call to leave, and everything that followed was promise. We pray that You would speak to us in the same way today: show us the old way of living in our country, our family, and our father's house that we must leave behind, and speak Your 'I will' into our hearts, phrase by phrase. Teach us not to snatch at names, not to cling to towers, but to be altar-builders and conduits of blessing. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: You Must Leave, and I Will Bless (12:1-3)

### 1.1 "Get Out": Three Concentric Circles, Each Closer to the Heart

God's command tightens layer by layer: "your country, from your family and from your father’s house."

Your country is where you grew up—your environment, customs, and sense of security; your family (kindred) is your clan, your social circle, your connections, and your face; your father's house is the most intimate—your paternal home, your origin, your reliance, and that entire way of life you took for granted. From the outside in, each circle is harder to sever than the last. God did not ask Abram to test the waters first; He called out all three circles at once: this was not merely moving houses; it meant uprooting his entire being from the old order.

"Get out" is *lekh-lekha* in the original Hebrew (go, walk; an intensified imperative that can be understood as "go for yourself, go on your own account") [1], with the root verb *halakh* (H1980, to walk, to go) [2]. Commentators have historically noted this emphatic tone: while this command to leave appears on the surface to be an order, beneath it lies Abram's own good—leaving was precisely his pathway to blessing. This manuscript merely records the standard exposition. There is another detail: "To a land that I will show you"—at that time, no address was given as to where he was going. God gave a direction, not a map; a promise, not an itinerary. The first lesson in obedience is taking the very first step before seeing the full journey.

The words of Proverbs 3:5-6 serve as a footnote to this lesson: "Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding; in all your ways acknowledge Him, and He shall direct your paths." The path is directed as it is walked, not after browsing through the GPS in Haran's living room before making up one's mind. Brothers and sisters, how many people's callings have died because they said, "Let me figure it all out before I go"? God's order has always been: you leave first, and I will show you.

### 1.2 A Succession of "I Will": The Subject of the Promise Is Entirely God

Following the command is a succession of promises; please notice the subject of every verb: "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great."

A great nation, divine blessing, a great name—not one of these was something Abram had to strive for on his own; all of them are "I will." When this verse is set alongside Babel, the biblical contrast is so sharp it feels deliberate: in chapter 11, the Babelites said, "let us make a name for ourselves," where the subject is "us," resulting in confusion and scattering; in chapter 12, God says, "I will make your name great," where the subject is "I," resulting in blessing for all nations. For those who snatched at a name, their name was shattered; for the one who waited for God to give a name, his name endures to this day—and the way his name was established was not by building a tower, but by becoming a blessing.

In his commentary on Genesis, Warren W. Wiersbe, explaining Abram's call, reminds readers to observe that the promises in chapter 12, verses 1 to 3, flow entirely from God's initiative and grace; man's duty is simply to go forth in faith and obedience, while the fulfillment rests with God. (See Warren W. Wiersbe, *The Bible Exposition Commentary: Genesis*).

This order is a bitter pill for those who are in a rush to "make a name" today: personal branding, reputation, and influence have a completely different algorithm in the kingdom of God—it is not that they cannot be great, but that they cannot be seized; it is not that they cannot be seen, but that the order cannot be reversed. First become a blessing, and the name is the fruit; if you seize the name as prey first, before the fruit is ripe, the tree will wither first.

### 1.3 "And You Shall Be a Blessing": The Blessed Person Is a Conduit, Not a Reservoir

The climax of the promise is in verse 3: "And in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed."

Please look closely at the scale: God said to a seventy-five-year-old man whose wife was barren that all the families of the earth—every single clan and every single language on the list in chapter 10—would be blessed through him. "Blessing," in Hebrew *berakhah* (H1293, blessing) [3]; "to bless," *barakh* (H1288, to bless, praise) [4]; in contrast to blessing, in "I will curse him who curses you," the lighter word carries the meaning of "to treat with contempt, to disdain" (*qalal*, H7043) [5], while the heavier word is *arar* (H779, to curse) [6]. The covenant God established here is equivalent to declaring to the whole world: one's attitude toward Abram and his offspring is one's attitude toward blessing.

Galatians 3:8 connects this promise directly to the gospel: "And the Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the gospel to Abraham beforehand, saying, 'In you all the nations shall be blessed.'" It turns out that these three verses in chapter 12 are the very first preview of the gospel. Abram's calling was not the domestic affair of a single ethnic group; it was a turning point for all humanity—from the offspring coming out of his household would come that One, the Christ who causes all families to be truly blessed.

Let us conclude Part One with an application: examine the blessings in your hands—time, income, skills, opportunities—have they entered a reservoir, or are they flowing through a conduit? In God's promise to Abram, nowhere does it say, "Just enjoy the blessing." "You shall be a blessing" is an integral part of the promise, not an optional clause. The water in a reservoir turns stagnant, while the water in a conduit is ever fresh; spiritually, this law never fails.

To sum up Part One in a single sentence: **The order of calling is to depart first, then to see: God repeatedly says "I will," taking the great nation, the great name, and the great blessing entirely upon Himself, while man has only one duty—to go forth, and to be a blessing.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: So Abram Departed (12:4–6)

### 2.1 "Departed as the Lord Had Spoken to Him": Obedience Takes Only a Few Words in Writing

Verse 4 is the shortest yet weightiest sentence in the entire chapter: "So Abram departed as the Lord had spoken to him."

There is no record of bargaining, no checklist of conditions, no "wait until I settle my father's funeral and estate first"—as we learned in the previous sermon, he set out after Terah died (cf. Acts 7:4). The funeral was over, his days in Haran had been enough, and when the word of God came again, he went. Hebrews 11:8 provides an eternal footnote to this sentence: "By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called to go out to the place which he would receive as an inheritance. And he went out, not knowing where he was going."

"Not knowing where he was going"—these words are the definition of faith. Faith is not calculating the future completely before taking a step; it is hearing clearly who is speaking and daring to step into the unknown. What people today lack most is precisely this step: we insist on seeing the contract first, calculating the return first, and confirming we won't get shortchanged before we are willing to move. Yet almost every great work in the kingdom of God begins with a step of "not knowing where he was going."

### 2.2 Seventy-Five Years Old: Setting Out Again, No Age Is Too Late

The Bible deliberately records his age: "and Abram was seventy-five years old when he departed from Haran" (Gen. 12:4).

Seventy-five years old, even today, is an age many years past retirement. According to human calculation, what one should do at this age is settle down, enjoy life, and dote on grandchildren; yet his wife was barren, without even a hint of grandchildren. God chose precisely this age to entrust him with the greatest departure of his life. Brothers and sisters, age in God's hands is never a deadline for a calling. Moses was called at eighty (cf. Exod. 7:7); Caleb, at eighty-five, said "I am as strong" and sought to enter Canaan to take cities (cf. Josh. 14:10–11, paraphrased).

Today, someone sitting out there has already pronounced the verdict of "too late" upon themselves: in my forties or fifties, I can no longer learn new things; the children are grown, the marriage is just what it is; I didn't seize ministry opportunities when I was young, and now it's not my turn. I say to you in the name of chapter 12: Abram was seventy-five when he left Haran, and the true story of his life only began to be written at seventy-five! If God has not finished speaking, your story is not finished. Today, not tomorrow, take back that phrase "too late." What God uses is not your age, but your "departed." If a seventy-five-year-old could leave Haran, you too can take this step today. Do not let the comfort of Haran and the excuse of age conspire to bury the Canaan of the second half of your life.

### 2.3 Taking Lot, Entering the Land of the Canaanites (12:5–6)

Verse 5 records his entourage: Sarai, Lot, the possessions they had gathered, and the people they had acquired, all taken along. This was a relocation of his entire household and all his property, not a light-travel exploration. We should also honestly note: Lot's traveling along was out of familial affection—Abram did not abandon the son of his deceased brother; yet this companionship would later bring a series of lessons (cf. Gen. 13, 14, 19). The people and things carried along on the journey of calling are not all helps; some will become the heaviest lessons down the road. The Bible does not sugarcoat this, and as we read, we need not fret on Abram's behalf; God would teach him step-by-step along the way how to handle it.

In verse 6, he reached Shechem, arriving at the terebinth tree of Moreh. The Bible adds a calm narration: "And the Canaanites were then in the land."

The Promised Land was filled with other people at that time. God said this land would be given to his descendants, but after he arrived, what he saw were other people's cities, other people's fields, and other people living in complete stability. Brothers and sisters, this is the most realistic scene on the path of obedience: between the promise and reality, there often stands the phrase, "And the Canaanites were then in the land." The prayer is answered, but the circumstances have not yet changed; the calling is clear, but the position is still occupied. Do not doubt that you have taken the wrong road just because you see the Canaanites—the promise was spoken by God, the occupation is temporary, and the order is to enter first, and then possess.

To sum up Part Two in a single sentence: **Obedience takes only a few words in writing, but walking it out is the journey of a lifetime: seventy-five is not too late, daring to walk even without an address, seeing the Canaanites first upon entering the Promised Land—none of this alters the promise; it only reveals faith.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The Altar Builder (12:7–9)

### 3.1 "To Your Descendants I Will Give This Land": God Specifies the Promise at Shechem (12:7)

Arriving at Shechem, the Lord appeared in person and took the promise of verses 1 to 3 a step closer to reality: "To your descendants I will give this land."

Notice the location where God appeared: beside the terebinth tree of Moreh at Shechem. "Moreh," in Hebrew *Moreh* (H4176) [7], is interpreted by some commentators as a place of "teacher, instructor," mentioned here only as background. Abram did not yet have a single descendant at that time—his wife's barrenness was an obvious fact—yet God specifically said, "your descendants." With each step forward, the promise pierced more concretely into that impossibility: the land was to be given to descendants, yet you did not have a single descendant. This is how God trains human faith: not by removing the dilemma, but by standing before the dilemma and speaking the word even more clearly.

Abram's response was not shouting for joy, but building an altar: "And there he built an altar to the Lord, who had appeared to him." "Altar" in Hebrew is *mizbeach* (H4196, altar) [8]. This was the first altar Abram built in Canaan. From then on, his itinerary could almost be traced by altars: wherever he went, he built an altar. Brothers and sisters, the first action of a called person in the Promised Land is not staking a claim, not planting a flag, but building an altar—establishing worship first, declaring first that the sovereignty over this land belongs to God. If the order is wrong, a person will turn the Promised Land into a new Babel; if the order is right, before possession even begins, worship is already grounded.

### 3.2 From Shechem to Bethel: Calling on the Name of the Lord (12:8)

In verse 8, he moved south to the mountain east of Bethel, between Bethel and Ai, and there he built another altar and "called on the name of the Lord."

"Called," in Hebrew *qara* (H7121, to call, proclaim, invoke) [9]; "name," *shem* (H8034, name, renown) [10]—that word again. Place Babel and Bethel side by side, and the contrast across this whole section of Genesis becomes complete:

The people of Babel: build a tower for their own name, its top reaching to heaven; the result is that their language is confused, and the people are scattered.
Abram: builds an altar, calling on the name of the Lord; the altar is not high, yet the man is called; the result is that all the families of the earth are blessed through him.

A tower, an altar; one for one's own name, one calling on God's name. Brothers and sisters, whether what you are building throughout your life is a tower or an altar can be seen from whose name you call upon: whenever something happens, if the first thing you call upon is your own resources, connections, and reputation, that is a tower; whenever something happens, if the first thing you call upon is the name of the Lord, that is an altar. A tower must be built higher and higher before one feels secure; for an altar, as long as there is fire, it is enough.

We mentioned back in Genesis 4 that in the generation of Seth, men began to call on the name of the Lord (cf. Gen. 4:26); now Abram brings this thread of worship from Ur and Haran all the way into Canaan. The most precious luggage he brought into the Promised Land was not gold, silver, or goods, but the habit of calling on the name of the Lord.

### 3.3 "Journeying Toward the South": Having Arrived in Canaan, the Road Is Not Yet Finished (12:9)

Verse 9 concludes: "So Abram journeyed, going on still toward the South." The original phrasing carries the sense of continuous travel: moving forward, stage by stage.

In other words, entering Canaan did not mean the story was over, nor did building two altars mean he could settle down. In the Promised Land, Abram remained a sojourner, migrating, moving forward. Hebrews 11:9–10 captures his mindset completely: he dwelt in the land of promise as in a foreign country, dwelling in tents, waiting for the city which has foundations. The tent versus the altar: these were the two marks of Abram's entire life. The altar was settled for God; the tent was mobile for himself—God's matters were handled with the greatest permanence, while his own affairs could pull up stakes at any moment.

In the next sermon, we will see that this road moving forward was not smooth: following the South came famine, and following Canaan came Egypt (Gen. 12:10–20). Even a called person goes down to Egypt, even a called person is weak—that will be the lesson of our next sermon. Today, let us first stand firm on this lesson of setting out: the departure is real, the promise is real, the altar is real—and the detours ahead, God will teach through them lesson by lesson as well.

Brothers and sisters, between the tower and the altar, today you must choose one. You cannot build a tower while building an altar; you cannot snatch a name while calling on His name. Some people have built their towers very high over the years: they have the reputation, they have the position, yet inside you know that no matter how high that tower's top reaches, it cannot reach heaven, and in the middle of the night you fear its collapse more than anyone else. Today God offers you another way: come down from the tower, and come beside the altar. Hand over your name, let the blessing flow out, and once again put calling on the name of the Lord ahead of everything else. Right today, on whatever ground you stand—your home, your workstation, your city—build your first altar: a sincere prayer, an authentic offering, an action that personally passes a blessing to someone else. The person who builds altars does not fear being scattered, because wherever he goes, there is an altar; the person who builds altars does not fear being nameless, because his name is personally remembered by God.

Let us sum up the third part in one sentence: **The people of Babel built a tower for their own name, and in the end were scattered; Abram built an altar to call on the name of the Lord, and wherever he went, he brought worship with him—the tower will halt construction, but the fire of the altar will not be quenched.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Categories of People

Today's chapter speaks to six categories of people:

**To seekers:** The first sentence God spoke to Abram was not "you must mend your ways," but "Get out of your country... to a land that I will show you... and I will bless you." The starting point of faith is not that you first become good, but that you first set out, handing your direction over to Him. That line in verse 3, "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed," has already reached you today: you too are a member of all the families; this blessing originally included a share for you, and only awaits your response.

**To new believers:** Learn Abram's first lesson thoroughly: leave first, see later. Do not ask God for the entire map; first take the step you have already clearly heard—an old habit that should be broken, an old circle that should be left behind, a new lesson that should begin. Also, whenever you arrive in a new environment, build an altar first: pray first, worship first, surrender sovereignty first, before talking about anything else.

**To brothers and sisters who have been in the Lord for many years:** What needs the most self-examination after many years is whether the tower and the altar have swapped places: having served for many years, without realizing it you are building a tower for your own name—caring about titles, caring about being noticed, caring about who gets the credit. Measure yourself today by the standard of Bethel: do I have more prayers calling on the Lord's name, or more thoughts managing my own name? If it is uncovered, just return to the side of the altar and start over; there is no shame in it—Abram returned to the altar many times as well.

**To parents:** Abram took his whole family on the journey; the children and his nephew were watching. The sight of the parents' backs as they "departed" is a child's earliest textbook on faith. You do not have to explain your entire life's path to your children—Abram himself did not know where he was going—but let your children see: this family is a family that sets out upon hearing God's word; when blessing reaches this home, it flows out and is not hoarded.

**To those striving in the workplace:** Your workplace is one of your Canaan lands: at that time the Canaanites were in the land, and the rules are not entirely according to God's will. In such a land, Abram's way was to build an altar—maintaining the habit of calling on the Lord's name at your workstation, doing things in a way that blesses others, not participating in the competition of tower-building. Whether your name becomes great, leave that to God; whether blessing flows, you decide that today.

**To those currently in suffering:** You may be standing in the reality of "the Canaanites were then in the land": you have heard the promise, yet the circumstances have not yielded; or you may be in the seventy-five-year-old sense of "it's too late." Today's word is just one sentence: God appeared at Shechem, speaking the promise even more specifically to one who "had no descendants yet." He is not intimidated by your impossibility; He speaks right in the face of impossibility. Build an altar first, call on Him first, lay your tears together with your prayers upon the altar, and as for the land and the descendants, He will fulfill them one by one.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Closing Prayer

Let us review today's three sentences:

First, get out, and I will bless you—leave first, see later; a name need not be grabbed, for God will surely make the name of the obedient great;
Second, Abram departed—seventy-five years old is not too late; having no address, he still dared to go; entering the Promised Land and seeing the Canaanites first, the promise was not discounted because of it;
Third, the altar builder—not building a tower to proclaim one's own name, but only building an altar to call on the name of the Lord; wherever he goes, blessing flows there.

Let us now bow our heads and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. Every 'I will' You spoke to Abram remains effective today. We pray that You would show us the country, the family, and the father's house we must leave, and give us Abram-like faith: even not knowing where we are going, still willing to say, 'I will go.' Tear down the towers in our hands that we have built for ourselves, and teach us to build altars in our families, our workplaces, and our cities, continually calling upon Your name. May we not hoard blessings when we are blessed, but become channels of blessing, so that the people around us are blessed through us. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

[2] *halakh*: Hebrew הָלַךְ (H1980), to walk, go, depart; see Gen. 12:1, 4–5, etc.; definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

[3] *berakhah*: Hebrew בְּרָכָה (H1293), blessing; see the context of the related promise in Gen. 12:2; definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

[4] *barakh*: Hebrew בָּרַךְ (H1288), to bless, praise, kneel in praise; see Gen. 12:2–3; definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

[6] *arar*: Hebrew אָרַר (H779), to curse (heavier, formal); see Gen. 12:3, "I will curse him"; definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

[7] *Moreh*: Hebrew מוֹרֶה (H4176), Moreh, personal/place name term, Gen. 12:6 "terebinth tree of Moreh"; its connection with the root for "teacher" reflects the preacher's exposition; this manuscript provides it only as background explanation.

[8] *mizbeach*: Hebrew מִזְבֵּחַ (H4196), altar; see Gen. 12:7–8; definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

[9] *qara*: Hebrew קָרָא (H7121), to call, proclaim, invoke; see Gen. 12:8 "called on the name of the Lord," cf. Gen. 4:26.

[10] *shem*: Hebrew שֵׁם (H8034), name, renown; see the contrast between Gen. 11:4 "make a name for ourselves" and Gen. 12:2 "make your name great," and 12:8 "the name of the Lord"; definitions based on BDB, HALOT.

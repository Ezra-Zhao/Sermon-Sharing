## Summary (about 100 words)

Chapter 21 dealt with persons; chapter 22 first settles the account of property: theft is repaid in multiples, negligence is repaid at value — restitution is restoration, not revenge. Then it draws the lines of what must not be done, and suddenly turns to the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless — for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt. No interest may be taken on a loan to the poor, and a pledged garment must be returned before sunset; written into that clause is God's self-introduction: I am gracious. The chapter ends in holiness: the firstborn belong to God, and even small daily matters are set apart as holy.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter we read the opening of the Book of the Covenant. The ordinances did not begin with sacrifice or with feasts; they began with the Hebrew servant, the maidservant, the injured person, and the person gored by an ox. Righteousness falls into daily life, and its first fall is on human beings. Today we read on into chapter 22, the second half of the Book of the Covenant. If chapter 21 asked how human life and the human person are to be counted, chapter 22 asks questions that are more miscellaneous and more everyday: a neighbour's ox has been stolen — how is it repaid? Deposited goods have gone missing — who bears the loss? A borrowed animal has died — whose account does it fall on? What things must not be done at all among God's people? When the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless are wronged, who stands up for them? When a poor man borrows money, is interest to be charged? When a poor man's garment is taken in pledge, must it be returned before dark? And finally, how are the produce of the land and the firstborn of children and livestock to be given to God? Thirty-one verses, almost entirely case law and ordinances — no thunder, no thick darkness, only the small affairs of tents and fields. But do not despise these small affairs: what a community truly resembles of God shows itself precisely in them. To make a covenant on the mountain is easy; to go home and repay an ox is hard. To say with the mouth that we fear God is easy; to carry a pledged garment back before sunset is hard. Chapter 22 measures the covenant of the mountain, inch by inch, into the people's everyday life.

Let us first see the structure of the whole chapter clearly, lest we read it as a heap of scattered articles. The chapter falls roughly into four parts. The first is restitution for property, verses 1 to 15: beginning with the theft of an ox or a sheep, passing through livestock grazing in a field, fire burning a field, deposits and borrowing — all of it asks whose the loss is and at what multiple it is repaid. The second is the things that must not be done, verses 16 to 20: only five short verses, yet every one a grave offence, from seducing a virgin to sorcery, bestiality, and sacrificing to other gods. The third is the protection of the vulnerable, verses 21 to 27: first the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless — the three groups with the least backing — and then lending to the poor without interest, and returning a garment taken in pledge on the same day. The fourth is holiness toward God and toward man, verses 28 to 31: God and the ruler must not be reviled, the produce and the firstborn must not be delayed in being given to God, and the whole closes with the summary line, "ye shall be holy men unto me." The order of the four parts still carries a message: property first, then the boundary lines, then the vulnerable, and at the end, holiness. The account of property must be settled clearly and the lines of what must not be done must be drawn plainly, but the centre and the end of the chapter are not in property; they are in persons — above all in the persons most easily wronged — and in holiness. The title of this chapter, "You Must Not Oppress a Stranger," is taken from the first sentence of the third part; that is the eye of the whole chapter, and when we reach it, we shall slow our steps.

In preaching this chapter, this manuscript still keeps the three rules laid down in chapter 21, and states them honestly at the outset. First, it does not spread out article by article, but grasps the structure and the theological anchors: thirty-one cases read one by one would exhaust the time, and hearers could not retain them; we are to see what this body of ordinances says as a whole. Second, difficulties are marked honestly with their context — nothing is whitewashed and nothing is forced into a modern mould. For example, verse 18 — a sorceress shall not be suffered to live — was repeatedly invoked in later history and drawn into the witch trials of Europe and North America, in which many people lost their lives; that is fact, and when this manuscript reaches that verse it will state the history of abuse honestly, neither evading it nor using the verse to endorse the violence of history. Third, the multiples of restitution and the lines of liability are the case law by which ancient Israel judged in its own world; this manuscript teaches their principles and proportions, but does not carry ancient multiples directly over into today's statutes. Fact is fact and exposition is exposition; the two are labelled separately — that is the rule this series has kept all along.

Let us read several key passages together:

"If a man shall steal an ox, or a sheep, and kill it, or sell it; he shall restore five oxen for an ox, and four sheep for a sheep." (Exodus 22:1, KJV, excerpt)

"Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him: for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt. Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child. If thou afflict them in any wise, and they cry at all unto me, I will surely hear their cry." (Exodus 22:21-23, KJV, excerpt)

"If thou at all take thy neighbour's raiment to pledge, thou shalt deliver it unto him by that the sun goeth down: for that is his covering only, it is his raiment for his skin… and it shall come to pass, when he crieth unto me, that I will hear; for I am gracious." (Exodus 22:26-27, KJV, excerpt)

"And ye shall be holy men unto me." (Exodus 22:31, KJV, excerpt)

Let us pray: Lord, Your covenant does not stop on the mountain; You measured Your heart into our wallets, our fields, and our relations with our neighbours. Open our eyes today to see how You settle the account of restitution and how You hear the cry of the weak; and remind us that we were once strangers in Egypt — people who have received grace must never turn around and oppress the stranger. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Restitution Is Restoration, Not Revenge — The Property Ordinances and the Lines of Liability (22:1-15)

The first part of the chapter, a full fifteen verses, is all about property: theft, damage, deposit, and borrowing. Notice its opening case: the theft of an ox or a sheep. The text says: if a man steals an ox or a sheep and kills it or sells it, he shall restore five oxen for an ox and four sheep for a sheep. Why fivefold and fourfold, instead of simply repaying the original number? First distinguish one great principle, the key to the whole section: restitution in Scripture is not revenge. The mind of revenge wants the other party to hurt, and the more he hurts the more satisfying it is; the mind of restitution is entirely different, and works in two directions at once. In one direction it makes the victim whole again: the lost animal returns to his pen, and his livelihood is not broken off by another's evil. In the other direction it makes the wrongdoer feel the pain: for one stolen animal, five must be led out of his own pen, and only when his hand truly touches the loss does the cost of stealing lodge in his memory. If only the original number were repaid, stealing would become a risk-free business: succeed, and you gain for nothing; be caught, and the goods merely return to their owner. Once the multiple is set, the account is completely different. The homiletical point may stand here: the multiple of restitution is the price tag set on malice. As for the difference between fivefold and fourfold, the text itself gives no reason; the explanation commonly given by commentators is that an ox is more valuable than a sheep and is the main strength of ploughing, so that the loss of a trained ox that can work the field is heavier and is therefore repaid more heavily. This manuscript marks this for what it is: the common explanation of commentators, not the direct statement of the text — keep that in mind as you listen.

The articles that follow separate the degrees of malice layer by layer. If the thief is caught in the act and the stolen goods are still found alive in his hand — whether ox, ass, or sheep — he shall restore double. Notice the condition: slaughtered or sold, the goods already disposed of, already destroyed, already passed into another's hand — fivefold and fourfold; the goods still in hand, the original thing still existing — double. The malice is the same theft, but the way the goods were disposed of differs, and the multiple differs with it: to destroy the goods or pass them on shows the theft completed and made absolute, and the victim's hope of recovering the original is cut off, so the restitution is heavy; the original still existing, the cost of restoration is low, so the restitution is light. That the text grades even theft in this way is itself a teaching: God's righteousness is not a mass of anger, but a balance that weighs extremely fine. In the same passage there is also one boundary, mentioned in passing: if a thief breaking in by night is struck and dies, the one who struck him is not guilty of blood; but if the sun has risen when he strikes him dead, he is guilty of blood. In the ancient world, without lamplight and without police, there was no way to tell whether a night intruder was friend or foe, thief or murderer, and so the latitude of a householder's self-defence was wide; in daylight, when everything can be seen clearly, to strike a fatal blow changes its nature. The line is drawn between light and darkness, and what it draws is still the old saying: defence is permitted, revenge is not; evil may be stopped, but to injure beyond the measure of the evil is not permitted.

Next come the losses of the field, two cases side by side. The first is livestock grazing in a field: if a man turns his livestock loose and lets it feed in another man's field or vineyard, he shall make restitution from the best of his own field and the best of his own vineyard. To keep watch over livestock is the owner's duty; if he cannot control them, however much the animals' mouths have eaten, the owner's hand must repay — and repay with the best, not with inferior produce passed off as payment. In the matter of wronging a neighbour, even the quality of the restitution is watched over by the ordinances. The second case is fire burning a field: if a man kindles a fire among thorns and it spreads and consumes another's stacked grain, standing grain, or the whole field, the one who kindled the fire shall surely make restitution. Fire is not like livestock: once fire is let loose it acknowledges no master. The text's judgment is simple and direct: you kindled the fire, so the loss of its spreading is yours. Here one verse must be read alongside chapter 21, with the difference of source marked: the previous chapter dealt with restitution where a pit is dug and left uncovered and an animal falls in (Exodus 21:33-34); it belongs to the same line as grazing and burning here — loss caused by negligence, repaid at value. The difference is that negligence has no malice, so restitution stops at making the other party whole and does not multiply upward. Negligence is reckoned as negligence and malice as malice; the difference in multiples is still a difference of the heart.


What follows is liability for deposits and borrowing — the most detailed group in the first part, and the closest to daily life. If a man delivers money or goods to his neighbour for safekeeping — a deposit — and the keeper's house is broken into and the goods stolen, the judgment divides into two branches. If the thief is caught, the thief restores double: the old rule for theft. If the thief is not caught, the keeper shall come before God and swear that he did not put his hand to the deposited goods. The oath here is no formality: in an ancient society without surveillance and without forensic evidence, a man's oath before God was the last evidence on which a case could be judged; if the keeper dared to say before God that his hands were clean, the case ended there, and the depositor had to accept the result. The text adds a general rule: in any dispute between two parties — whether over an ox, an ass, a sheep, clothing, or anything lost, where one party says it is his — both parties shall bring the case before the judges, and whomever the judges condemn shall restore double to the other. Notice the use of double here: it punishes not only theft but also false accusation. Whoever charges his neighbour without grounds with swallowing his property, drags him to court, and is himself condemned, repays at the thief's price. The ordinances put a bridle on litigation as well, lest the court become a tool for taking advantage.

The deposit of livestock is more complicated than that of goods, because animals die, are injured, and are torn by beasts. The text separates the circumstances article by article. If a deposited animal dies, is injured, or is driven away, and no one saw it, the keeper shall swear before God that he did not lay his hand on it, and the owner shall accept it, with no restitution. If the animal was plainly stolen from the keeper, the keeper shall make restitution to the owner: the trust that applied to goods tightens by one notch when it comes to animals that can run and move. If the animal was torn by beasts, the keeper shall bring the torn remains as evidence; if he can produce the evidence, he need not make restitution. The word evidence shows most plainly the realism of ancient judgment: without evidence, words fly everywhere; with that torn carcass in hand, the keeper has no room either to have stolen the animal himself or to have invented a story. Oath and evidence — one governs the invisible heart, the other visible things — and the ordinances use both.

Last come the two verses on borrowing, short, yet drawing the line with complete cleanness. If a man borrows an animal from his neighbour and it is injured or dies while borrowed, the judgment turns on one thing: whether the owner was present or not. If the owner was not with it, the borrower shall surely make restitution, because the animal came to grief in your hands and at your work, and the duty of care rests wholly on you. If the owner was with it, the borrower need not make restitution, because the owner watched with his own eyes, and the risk was borne by both households together. If the animal was hired, its loss is already reckoned in the hire: the wages the hirer paid already contained this layer of risk, and no separate restitution is due. Presence or absence, borrowing or hiring — a difference of one word, and the liability is entirely different. Having read this far, we may gather the principle of the whole section into one sentence: in the account of restitution, the multiple follows the malice, and liability follows the custody. Deliberate theft with disposal of the goods: fivefold and fourfold. Goods still in hand: double. False accusation: double. Loss by negligence: repaid at value. Disaster and wild beasts, with evidence produced: no restitution. A borrowed animal lost with the owner absent: full restitution; with the owner present: shared. Not one account is muddled through; every one asks the same set of questions: where is the malice, in whose hands is the liability, and how can the loss be made good again? This body of judgments is the case law of ancient Israel; this manuscript teaches only its principles and does not carry it article by article into today's courts. But the principles stand to this day: make the victim whole, make the wrongdoer feel the cost, let unintentional loss stop at making good, and let deliberate evil pay a multiplied price — that is the clear line between restitution and revenge.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: What Must Not Be Done and Who Must Not Be Wronged — The Boundary of Grave Offences and the Eye of the Chapter on the Vulnerable (22:16-24)

Having settled the account of property, the text turns sharply and enters a group of only five verses, yet every one of them weighty: some things must not be done at all among God's people. The first is seducing a virgin who is not betrothed. The text says: if a man seduces a virgin who is not betrothed and lies with her, he shall pay the bride price and take her as his wife; if the girl's father utterly refuses to give her to him, he shall still pay money according to the bride price of virgins. Here the ancient marriage context must be stated honestly, without covering it directly with today's reading. In that society, a daughter's marriage was arranged by her father and the bridegroom's family paid a bride price; if an unbetrothed girl was seduced and lost her virginity, her prospects in marriage negotiations were greatly damaged, while the man who seduced her could most easily slip away, leaving the whole cost to fall on the girl and her father's house. The purpose of the clause is to block that way of escape: since you have touched her, you must take up the responsibility of marriage — either pay the bride price formally and marry her, or pay the fixed bride-price sum in silver — and the clause also preserves the father's right to refuse the match. This is not a modern ethic of dating; it is a protective case ruling inside an ancient patriarchal marriage system: a man may not treat a girl's reputation and future as a plaything, to be used and walked away from. This manuscript states this layer plainly as a matter of factual context; to force modern applications across the context would misread the text.

The second is the verse this chapter must face most honestly: "Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live." The woman here, in the original, is mekhashephah (מְכַשֵּׁפָה, H3784), a sorceress, a feminine participle [6], denoting one who manipulates spiritual powers by spells and sorcery. In the verse's original setting, sorcery was no folk parlour trick; it was directly bound up with the religion of Egypt and Canaan. The people had just come out of Egypt, where the magicians practised their arts against God — a thing they had seen with their own eyes. God's prohibition of sorcery is a prohibition against the people transferring the trust of their lives from the covenant God to hidden powers: it is the first commandment, "no other gods," applied to the affairs of the spiritual realm. But to preach this verse and speak only of its original setting, without speaking of later ages, would be dishonest. What follows is historical fact, and must be kept distinct from the original meaning of the text: this verse was later invoked again and again in the witch-hunting movements of late medieval and early modern Europe, and it appears also in the witch trials of colonial North America; across several centuries, a great number of the accused — most of them women, though men also — were put to death amid torture, forced confessions, and hasty trials, and many of them were innocent by any just standard of today. This manuscript writes its position plainly here: in its original setting, the verse prohibits the practice of sorcery itself; it supplies none of the methods of accusation and none of the trial procedures that later witch hunts used, and it cannot be used to endorse the violence of the witch hunts in history. To tear one statute out of the context of the covenant community and turn it into a licence for framing neighbours and doing violence to the defenceless is an abuse of the text, and the cost of that abuse in history was real human lives. Whoever reads the ancient text must remember both the original setting and the history of abuse; neither may be hidden.

The third and fourth matters have one sentence each, yet their weight is not light: whoever lies with a beast shall surely be put to death — bestiality is the trampling of the created order at its lowest level, and the ordinance leaves no room; and whoever sacrifices to any god, save unto the LORD only, shall be utterly destroyed. This sentence answers the first commandment head to tail: Israel is a people brought out of Egypt and belonging solely to the LORD, and to sacrifice to another god is to break covenant within the covenant. Read side by side, the four forbidden things share one common line: what they injure is not only some individual victim; they injure the very root by which this community subsists as a covenant people — the boundary of sex, singleness toward the spiritual realm, the order of creation, and faithfulness to God. Toward the theft of an ox the ordinances still speak of multiples and of restoration; toward these four they speak only of boundary and of removal, because some losses cannot be repaid — they can only be barred and forbidden. Here a measure of proportion must also be added: these capital clauses are the penal law of the ancient covenant community; this manuscript states their original setting and theological purpose honestly, and does not treat them as a penal list that today's church or society may carry over and execute. Fact is fact; any citation today that steps over this layer is a misuse.

With the boundary of grave offences drawn, the tone of the text suddenly softens, and suddenly grows severe, and enters the eye of the whole chapter: the three groups of the vulnerable. Verse 21: "Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him." The stranger, in the original, is ger (גֵּר, H1616), a sojourner [1] — one who has left his own people and sojourns among the Israelites: with no local fields, no clan to lean on, and no network of kin to speak for him in court — the kind of person most easily squeezed out, short-paid, and bullied in the ancient world. The ground clause that follows is the weightiest sentence in the whole chapter: "for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt." God sets out no abstract argument for this article; He only calls the people to memory: you yourselves were strangers; you know what it tastes like, in a foreign land, to be treated as outsiders, shouted at by overseers, and shorted on rations. You have tasted that taste, and you must not let another taste it again at your hands. Memory here is the root of the law: a community's morality is measured not from how strong it is, but from whether it remembers that it was once weak.

Verse 22 turns to the second and third groups: "Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child." To afflict is 'anah (עָנָה, H6031), to afflict, to oppress, to humble [2]; the widow is 'almanah (אַלְמָנָה, H490) and the fatherless is yatom (יָתוֹם, H3490), two words that often appear as a pair in Scripture [3], both denoting those whose household pillar has fallen and whose fields and livelihood are left without support. Notice what these three groups have in common: the stranger has no clan, the widow no husband, the fatherless no father — in court, none of them has a voice of their own. The arrangement of the ordinance here is deeply meaningful: if the people bully them, no earthly court will stand up for them, so God takes their case into His own hands. The words of verses 23 and 24 grow heavier sentence by sentence: if they cry at all unto God, God says, I will surely hear their cry; and He will kindle His wrath and kill the oppressors with the sword, so that their wives become widows and their children fatherless. This is a mirror warning: as you made another's wife a widow and his children fatherless, so, when judgment comes, your own wife and children will fall into the same condition; the suffering your hand manufactured will return, exactly as it was, to your own doorstep. Here the measure of the preaching must be marked: this is a warning declaration within the law, expressing the degree of God's hatred for the sin of oppressing the weak; this manuscript conveys it honestly according to the text, and does not preach it as a formula of retribution to be expected literally today. But one thing the text states directly and without ambiguity: God presents Himself as the hearer of the weak person's suit. The weak have no advocate; God is their advocate. The cry of the weak, which no one on earth hears — God says that voice reaches Him directly. The stranger, the widow, and the fatherless did not appear in chapter 21; in chapter 22 they are placed right in the middle of the chapter, like a nail: however clearly the account of property is settled, if this nail comes loose, the righteousness of the whole chapter collapses.


## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: A Self-Introduction Inside the Pledge Clause, and Holiness in Daily Life — Lending, What Is Given to God, and the Firstborn (22:25-31)

After the eye of the chapter, the text does not leave the vulnerable, but goes further into detail, into the two most everyday hardships of the weak: borrowing money, and giving a pledge. Verse 25: "If thou lend money to any of my people that is poor by thee, thou shalt not be to him as an usurer, neither shalt thou lay upon him usury." Interest is neshekh (נֶשֶׁךְ, H5392), interest, usury [4]; the word shares a root with "bite" — to take interest is like biting a mouthful of flesh off the poor man, and the word itself carries its warning. Note the object first: the text speaks of a poor fellow countryman living alongside you, who borrows from you mostly not to make money in trade, but to buy grain, to see a doctor, to survive the lean gap between seasons. To take interest on a life-saving loan is to turn a neighbour's hardship into one's own business; the line the ordinance draws here is that no profit may be made by grinding on a fellow countryman's distress. But a boundary must honestly be added: the prohibition in this verse, in the original, is fixed on relief loans to poor fellow countrymen; this manuscript states it honestly within the original's range and does not expand it into a blanket condemnation of interest on all commercial lending — that would preach the text beyond its measure.

Verses 26 and 27 are the tenderest ordinance in the chapter, and the most solemn: "If thou at all take thy neighbour's raiment to pledge, thou shalt deliver it unto him by that the sun goeth down." To take in pledge is the verb chaval (חָבַל, H2254), to take by force, to take a thing as a pledge [5]. A poor man who borrows has nothing to pledge but the garment on his back; the creditor who takes it is completely within his legal rights. Yet the ordinance inserts one article right into the middle of the lawful procedure: before the sun goes down, return it. The reason is written in verse 27, plain enough to ache: for that is his covering only, it is his raiment for his skin; wherein shall he sleep? Then comes the sentence that echoes through the whole chapter: "And it shall come to pass, when he crieth unto me, that I will hear; for I am gracious." Read this sentence slowly: inside an ordinance about a pledge, there stands, of all things, God's self-introduction. At the opening of Exodus, God introduced Himself to Moses as I AM THAT I AM; on Mount Sinai God proclaimed His name, saying that He is a God merciful and gracious; and here, in a case ruling about a garment in the Book of the Covenant, God introduces Himself once more: I am gracious. And this self-introduction is not written in a hymn; it is written inside a creditor's duty: if you keep the poor man's garment overnight, and the poor man lies cold in the night and cries out, that cry will reach Me — and I, who answer that cry, am gracious — meaning, if you refuse to learn this graciousness, you will face this gracious God taking up the matter in person. This answers, head to tail, the cry of the vulnerable in the preceding section: God hears the cry of the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless, and He hears also the cry of the poor man who has lost his garment. In God's ordinances, the cry of the weak is never noise; it is a petition that reaches the heavenly court directly.

In the last four verses the text turns from the neighbour to God, and from God falls back into daily life: holiness toward God and toward man. Verse 28: "Thou shalt not revile the gods, nor curse the ruler of thy people." (The King James rendering "the gods" here renders the Hebrew 'elohim, which the Chinese Union Version renders as God; this manuscript follows the sense of the verse as a prohibition of reviling God.) To revile God is to treat the covenant God as an object that may be cursed at will; to curse the ruler is to treat the order of rule that God permits to be established as a target that may be insulted at will. What this verse guards is not the ruler's face, but a community's basic reverence upward and toward government: a person whose mouth holds no reverence for God or for those in authority has in fact already breached the dike in his heart. In preaching this verse, keep the balance: what it commands is no reviling and no cursing; it does not say that a ruler never errs and may never be remonstrated with. Elsewhere in Scripture the prophets rebuke kings in the severest words, and that is another matter; what this verse prohibits is the replacing of reverence and order by insult and cursing. This manuscript states it honestly within this verse's range — no more, no less.

Verses 29 and 30 speak of giving: "Thou shalt not delay to offer the first of thy ripe fruits, and of thy liquors: the firstborn of thy sons shalt thou give unto me. Likewise shalt thou do with thine oxen, and with thy sheep: seven days it shall be with his dam; on the eighth day thou shalt give it me." Grain, new wine, and oil are the first ripe produce of the land; the firstborn of sons and the firstborn of oxen and sheep are the first portion of the family and of the flock. The words "thou shalt not delay" strike at man's truest instinct: what is to be given to God, a man always wants to wait until he is better off, to wait until he sees whether this year's harvest is secure. The ordinance says no: the best, the first portion, goes to God first, and delay itself is putting God behind oneself. That the firstborn belong to God carries a deeper layer of memory: on the night of the Passover, every firstborn in Egypt was struck down, while Israel's firstborn were preserved through the blood of the lamb (Exodus chapters 12-13; cited here as intertextual background). Israel's firstborn therefore belong to God not as a tax but as a vow repaid — a remembrance that their own firstborn were ransomed back. Even for the firstborn of oxen and sheep, the ordinance keeps one tender detail: seven days it shall be with its dam; on the eighth day thou shalt give it Me. The newborn animal stays at its mother's side and nurses for a full seven days before it is offered; the law counts even the first days of a beast and its mother together. Its severity is never the same as coldness.

The last sentence of the whole chapter is its summary — and, besides the title, the sentence most worth carrying away: "And ye shall be holy men unto me: neither shall ye eat any flesh that is torn of beasts in the field; ye shall cast it to the dogs." The great word holiness lands on a surprisingly small point: not in the tabernacle, not beside the altar, but on a piece of dead flesh found in the field. An animal that died of itself or was torn, of uncertain origin and with its blood undrained, was unclean within Israel's distinctions of food; and that small pang of regret in the finder's heart — a perfectly good piece of meat, wasted if thrown away — is exactly where holiness takes charge. Holiness is not a few great occasions in a year; holiness is the separation observed at three meals a day and before every small bargain: God's people are to be distinct from others even in what enters their mouths, because their whole selves belong to God. Gather the four verses together: reverence for God's name and for the order of rule; the first portion given without delay; seven days of tenderness kept even for livestock; the mouth governed against unclean flesh — in chapter 22 holiness contains not one grand pronouncement; it is all small matters. And that is precisely the end point of the second half of the Book of the Covenant: beginning from restoring five oxen, the reckoning runs all the way to whether a piece of dead flesh may be eaten, and God has measured the whole of His people's life; and having measured it, He leaves only one sentence: ye shall be holy men unto me.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 22. Remember first its way of reckoning: restitution is not revenge; it makes the victim whole and makes the wrongdoer feel the cost. The multiple follows the malice, liability follows the custody; unintentional loss stops at making good, and deliberate evil pays a multiplied price. Remember next the eye of the chapter: neither vex a stranger, for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt; afflict no widow or fatherless child, for God Himself hears their cry. Remember also that self-introduction inside the pledge clause: when he crieth unto me, I will hear, for I am gracious. And remember last the end point of the whole chapter: ye shall be holy men unto me. Holiness is not only on the mountain; it is also in the garment returned before sunset, in the first portion not delayed, and in that piece of flesh cast to the dogs. May we who were once strangers in Egypt and have received grace go home and settle our accounts clearly, keep our boundaries, guard the weak well, and live every small matter of every day in the pattern of being set apart as holy.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You for writing Your heart down in such detail: how many fold to restore, by what hour to return, and which cries You will hear — all of it is clear. Forgive us for being shrewd in reckoning our own accounts and muddled in reckoning our neighbour's; forgive us for playing deaf before the weak and blind before small bargains. Engrave Your graciousness on our hearts, so that as we have received grace, so we treat others; and keep our memory long, never forgetting where we were brought out from. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him: for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt." (Exodus 22:21, KJV) "And it shall come to pass, when he crieth unto me, that I will hear; for I am gracious." (Exodus 22:27, KJV)

Meditate today on one word only: memory. When God gave Israel the law protecting the stranger, the reason He gave was not an abstract principle but a reminder: you yourselves were strangers. The sickness of people who have received grace is forgetting quickly — scarcely out of Egypt, and already learning Egypt's ways; scarcely lifted up by another's hand, and already forgetting to lift others. But God nailed His law to memory, so that whenever we face a person with no one to lean on, we first remember the days when we had no one to lean on. Meditate also on that garment: the poor man has only one garment to cover himself, and God counted even his cold at night into the law, saying with His own mouth: when he crieth unto me, I will hear, for I am gracious. God's graciousness is not a theological term; it is as concrete as a garment returned before sunset, as concrete as a cry that no one else hears — and He says He hears it. Ask the Lord today for a long memory and a tender heart: if your hand is holding anything that ought to be returned, if your heart has heard anyone's hardship, do not wait for sunset — return it first, listen first, because our God is a gracious God. A person with a long memory will not have a hard hand; a person with a tender heart, seeing the stranger, the one who lacks clothing, the one who has no voice, will not pass by on the other side. May we be such people today: handing on, just as it was given to us, the grace we ourselves received.

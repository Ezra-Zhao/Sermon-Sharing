# Offered as a Wave Offering — A Sermon on Numbers 8

- Text: Numbers 8:1-26
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending cross-review; first translation, awaiting proofreading (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Numbers 8; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations; verses not checked word for word are given in summary only, never as quotations.
- Note on the title: The tentative topic labels for this chapter (the lampstand, the cleansing of the Levites, the age of service) were checked against the Chinese base text and set aside, because none of them is an unbroken phrase of that text. The corrected Chinese title takes the unbroken seven-character wording of Numbers 8:21 in that edition — "he offered them as a wave offering," literally "took them as a wave offering" — Aaron offering the Levites as a wave offering before the LORD; the same wording stands in the command at verse 11. The English title renders the corrected Chinese title: "Offered as a Wave Offering." One KJV fact must be registered here, and it is developed in Endnote 2: at verses 11, 13, 15, and 21 the KJV renders the Hebrew tenuphah simply as "an offering," and the word "wave" does not appear in the KJV of this chapter at all; the wave-offering wording of the title rests on the Hebrew text and on the Chinese base text, not on the KJV's rendering at those verses.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Chapter 7 records the twelve tribal chiefs bringing their gifts over twelve days, until every vessel and cart of the tabernacle has found its place. Chapter 8 turns the camera back inside the sanctuary and onto the Levites. Its twenty-six verses fall into three parts. The first four verses (vv. 1-4) treat the lighting of the golden candlestick: the LORD tells Moses to tell Aaron that when he lights the lamps, the seven lamps are to give light over against the candlestick; Aaron does so; and the text adds how the candlestick was made — of beaten gold, unto its shaft and unto its flowers beaten work, made according to the pattern the LORD had shewed Moses. The middle eighteen verses (vv. 5-22), the chapter's main body, record the cleansing and dedication of the Levites: they are taken out and cleansed — water of purifying sprinkled upon them, a razor over all their flesh, their clothes washed; two young bullocks are prepared, one with its meat offering and one for a sin offering; the whole congregation is gathered, the children of Israel lay their hands on the Levites, Aaron offers the Levites before the LORD as an offering of the children of Israel, and the Levites lay their hands on the heads of the bullocks, one for a sin offering and the other for a burnt offering, to make atonement for the Levites. God's own words then lay bare the meaning of the rite: the Levites shall be His, for they are wholly given unto Him from among the children of Israel, instead of the firstborn; and He has given them as a gift to Aaron and his sons, to do the service of the children of Israel in the tabernacle and to make atonement for them. At the end Moses, Aaron, and all the congregation do according to all that was commanded; the Levites are purified, Aaron makes atonement for them, and after that the Levites go in to do their service before Aaron and his sons. The last four verses (vv. 23-26) fix the age of service: from twenty-five years old and upward they go in to wait upon the service; from the age of fifty they cease, and serve no more, but minister with their brethren to keep the charge. This sermon reads the chapter in the text's own order: first the candlestick's light; then, item by item, the rite of cleansing and dedication; then the chapter's own figures — its verses, its four wave-offerings, its two layings on of hands, its two bullocks — counted and checked one by one; and finally the side-by-side statement, without harmonisation, of this chapter's twenty-five years against chapter 4's thirty. The thread it traces is this: a man is cleansed first, and only then offered; he belongs to God first, and only then is given out to serve — and the order of dedication cannot be reversed at a single step.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 The seven lamps give light over against the candlestick: first the light, then the men who are offered

(Fact) Chapter 8 opens with the LORD speaking to Moses, and His first word is not about the Levites but about the lamps. The command stands in the KJV: "Speak unto Aaron, and say unto him, When thou lightest the lamps, the seven lamps shall give light over against the candlestick." (Num 8:2, KJV) Mark the wording exactly: the lamps give light "over against the candlestick" — the KJV's own phrase for the Chinese base text's "toward the front of the lampstand." Seven lamps, not one more and not one fewer, and one direction only: forward, over against the candlestick itself. The candlestick stood in the holy place, outside the veil, over against the table of shewbread (Exod 26:35, Summary). The sanctuary had no window; by day and by night its light was these seven lamps. The man who lights them is Aaron — the first person seen at work in this chapter is not Moses but the high priest himself. The daily rule of the lamps' oil and hours stands elsewhere (Exod 27:20-21; Lev 24:1-4, Summary); this chapter does not repeat oil or hour, only direction: the light must shine forward.

(Fact) Verse 3 records the doing, in the KJV: "And Aaron did so; he lighted the lamps thereof over against the candlestick, as the Lord commanded Moses." (Num 8:3, KJV) Between command and performance the text inserts not one word of explanation: the LORD commands Moses, Moses tells Aaron, and Aaron "did so" — the quietest and strongest formula of obedience in Numbers, the same formula that will be used of the whole congregation and the Levites at verses 20-22 (Summary; see section IV). Verse 4 then describes the candlestick itself, in the KJV: "And this work of the candlestick was of beaten gold, unto the shaft thereof, unto the flowers thereof, was beaten work: according unto the pattern which the Lord had shewed Moses, so he made the candlestick." (Num 8:4, KJV) In one verse "beaten work" is said twice: unto the shaft and unto the flowers — from the lowest shaft to the topmost flowers — nothing cast, nothing assembled, nothing soldered; one piece of gold beaten out blow by blow. The "candlestick" is mənōrâ (Strong's H4501); the "beaten work" is miqšâ (Strong's H4749), work hammered into shape. In preparing this sermon the verse's Hebrew was checked word by word: miqšâ stands twice in verse 4, answering exactly to the doubled "beaten work" (see Endnote 8). And "according unto the pattern which the Lord had shewed Moses" names the source: the pattern was not Moses' design but the LORD's showing; its original record is Exodus 25, where the candlestick's making and its seven lamps are prescribed and the passage closes with the charge to make all things according to the pattern shewed on the mount (Exod 25:31-40, Summary). Verse 4 of this chapter is a backward reference, not a new rule; this sermon marks that level here and does not read Exodus' details back as this chapter's record.

(Application) Why does a chapter about the dedication of the Levites begin with lamps? The order itself is the message. The Levites are not yet cleansed, and the lamp is already lit; the men are not yet offered, and the light is already shining in the sanctuary. In other words, God's house has light first, and men come in afterwards to serve — men do not come in, fill the place with activity, and produce the light by their busyness. The order is the other way round, and it rebukes and comforts everyone who serves: first light the lamps. Without the lamps lit, a sanctuary full of cleansed men and dedicated bullocks is still men groping in the dark. The seven lamps give light forward, over against — their direction is outward and ahead, never back upon the candlestick itself; it is the nature of light not to illuminate itself but what lies before it. And that the whole candlestick is beaten work deserves a pause: a lump of gold that has not passed under the hammer is only gold; it cannot be a candlestick. Shaft and flowers come from the same hammering; foundation and beauty from the same work. No one whom God makes into a vessel skips the hammer — the only question is whether we will stay quietly on the anvil and let every blow fall according to the pattern shewed.

(Application) One more word, for the man who lights the lamps. Lighting them is Aaron's daily task, not a single exploit: the chapter records no vision and no voice attending his lighting, only that he "did so." The sanctuary's light does not rest on one burst of fervour; it rests on the seven lamps being lit, one by one, day after day, according to the command, and made to shine in the same direction. The most durable witness in service is very often not the great deed but the daily office: one small thing done as commanded, done for decades, without changing shape or direction. Aaron appears twice in this chapter — lighting the lamps at the opening, and offering the Levites as an offering in the middle (vv. 11, 21) — and the two acts, one small and one great, are the same obedience: as the command says, so he does. The man who has first learned to light lamps is fit to present a whole tribe before God; unsteady hands cannot offer steadily either.

### II. 08:00-24:00 Thus shalt thou do unto them, to cleanse them: sprinkling, shaving, washing — and two bullocks

(Fact) At verse 5 the LORD speaks to Moses again, and the subject turns to the Levites; verse 6 stands in the KJV: "Take the Levites from among the children of Israel, and cleanse them." (Num 8:6, KJV) Two acts, in clear sequence: first take them out, then cleanse them. The taking out singles this one tribe out from among Israel — its root reason was given in full in chapter 3, where the Levites are taken instead of all the firstborn (Numbers 3, Summary); this chapter does not repeat the reason but takes up the procedure: men who have been taken out must still be cleansed before dedication can be spoken of. "Cleanse" is the causative form of ṭāhēr (Strong's H2891), "to make clean, to pronounce clean"; its occurrences at verses 6 and 7 were checked verse by verse in preparing this sermon (a checked selection, not an exhaustive list; see Endnote 9). Verse 7 lists the cleansing item by item, in the KJV: "And thus shalt thou do unto them, to cleanse them: Sprinkle water of purifying upon them, and let them shave all their flesh, and let them wash their clothes, and so make themselves clean." (Num 8:7, KJV) Three acts, to be set out one by one. First, water of purifying sprinkled upon them — "sprinkle" is nāzâ (Strong's H5137); the verb form of this verse was checked in the Hebrew (see Endnote 9). The Chinese base text names this water "water for removing sin": in the Hebrew it is literally "water of sin" (mê ḥaṭṭāʾt), ḥaṭṭāʾt (Strong's H2403) being both "sin" and "sin offering" — water appointed for the removal of sin. Whether it is the same water as that of Numbers 19, made with the red heifer's ashes, is a scholarly question, kept at the discussion level at this section's end. Second, a razor over all their flesh — not a trimming of the edges but the whole body shaved, not one hair of the old growth left; the KJV's "shave all their flesh" and the Chinese base text's "scrape the whole body with a razor" name the same act. Third, the washing of clothes — the man washed, and his garments washed with him, cleansed inside and out together. The verse closes with "and so make themselves clean": the sprinkling is done to them by another's hand, but the shaving and the washing are their own doing — cleansing is half received and half risen up and done.

(Fact) Cleansed, they prepare the offerings; verse 8 stands in the KJV: "Then let them take a young bullock with his meat offering, even fine flour mingled with oil, and another young bullock shalt thou take for a sin offering." (Num 8:8, KJV) Count the animals plainly: one bullock, and another — two young bullocks in all. Of the first, this verse says only that it is taken with its meat offering of fine flour mingled with oil; its name as an offering is not yet given. The second is named here: a sin offering. Only at verse 12 is the assignment of the two fully disclosed — the one for a sin offering, and the other for a burnt offering, unto the LORD, to make an atonement for the Levites (v. 12, Summary). This sermon follows the text's own order of disclosure: it does not call the first bullock a burnt offering at verse 8, because the text waits until verse 12 to say so. In preparing the English manuscript this point was re-checked independently against the KJV of verse 8, and the Chinese manuscript's restraint was found correct. The weight of the offerings also deserves notice: for the dedication of one tribe the animals are not lambs or doves but two bullocks — the heaviest grade of offering in the Old Testament. The Levites are no small matter, and God does not allow them to be dispatched with small offerings.

(Fact) The offerings ready, the rite moves into the open (vv. 9-10, Summary): the Levites are brought before the tabernacle of the congregation, and the whole assembly of the children of Israel is gathered together; the Levites are brought before the LORD, and the children of Israel put their hands upon the Levites. This laying on of hands is the first of two in the chapter, and its direction must be set straight: it is Israel who lays hands on the Levites. The whole congregation lays its hands on this one tribe and owns them as standing before God in the congregation's stead: the Levites stand instead of the firstborn (vv. 16-18), and the firstborn belonged to every house; now every house lays its hands on them and acknowledges publicly — this tribe is ours; where they stand, we stand. The laying on of hands is no decorative rite; it is the public confirmation of a representative relation. Then it is Aaron's turn; verse 11 stands in the KJV: "And Aaron shall offer the Levites before the Lord for an offering of the children of Israel, that they may execute the service of the Lord." (Num 8:11, KJV) Here the English reader must be told plainly what the KJV does and does not say: the KJV renders the Hebrew tenuphah at this verse simply as "an offering." The Chinese base text reads "as a wave offering" (当作摇祭）, and the Hebrew noun is tə̂nûpâ (Strong's H8573), from the verb nûp (Strong's H5130), to wave, to lift up and move in presentation — the ordinary wave offering waved a thing, the breast of the peace offerings or the shoulder of the heave offering (Leviticus 7, Summary); here what is "waved" is not a thing but men: a whole tribe of Levites presented before the LORD as an offering. A thing, once waved, went to the priests; men, once "waved," belong to God for His service — and the verse ends by fixing the purpose: "that they may execute the service of the Lord." The "service" is ʿăḇōḏâ (Strong's H5656).

(Fact) Verse 12 is the second laying on of hands, its direction exactly opposite to the first (v. 12, Summary): the Levites lay their hands upon the heads of the bullocks. In the first, hands are laid on the Levites, and the Levites are the party borne and represented; in this one, the Levites lay hands on the bullocks' heads, and the Levites become the party offering and confessing: their sin and their uncleanness pass, by the laying on of hands, onto these two bullocks. Then the one is offered for a sin offering and the other for a burnt offering, unto the LORD, to make an atonement for the Levites. Set the two layings side by side and the chapter's chain of representation is clear: Israel lays hands on the Levites — the Levites stand before God for the people; the Levites lay hands on the bullocks — the bullocks die on the altar for the Levites. The man who stands in the middle has the congregation's hands resting on him from behind, and his own hands resting on the offering that dies in his stead. Verse 13 then fixes their standing (v. 13, Summary): the Levites are set before Aaron and before his sons, and offered for an offering unto the LORD — the cleansed man's final standing is not among the congregation but before the priests: from now on they are men who serve under Aaron and his sons, a standing that will reappear when the rite is performed at verse 22 (see section IV).

(Application) The three acts of the cleansing rite deserve to be read slowly by everyone preparing to serve. The sprinkling comes first: the water is sprinkled on you by another; you cannot sprinkle yourself — the beginning of cleansing is always reception: someone must first sprinkle the water of purifying on you before your own doing can be spoken of. The shaving comes in the middle: where the razor passes, not one hair of the old growth remains — the Levites are not tidied up and sent on duty; they are scraped clean from head to foot, to begin again like newborn children. How many of us will wash our clothes but will not shave all our flesh: willing to change the outward appearance, unwilling to let the blade travel against the skin. The washing of clothes comes last: even what we wear, the daily, close, visible layer, must be washed with the man. And the order of the three cannot be rearranged: without the received sprinkling, however clean a man shaves himself it is only the pride of self-cleansing; without the thorough shaving, the washed clothes are a performance for onlookers. This chapter sets the three side by side to tell us: the man God wants is one who has received from within, been scraped clean from without, and had even his clothes washed — the whole man cleansed, and only then the whole man offered.

(Application) The two bullocks must also be preached through. Atonement for the Levites is made with bullocks — not because the Levites' sin is greater than other men's, but because the place where they stand is nearer than other men's: the nearer a man stands to the sanctuary, the heavier the offering that covers him. The congregation could offer lambs for itself; this tribe, which is to stand in the tabernacle and serve on the people's behalf, must first offer for itself the heaviest sin offering and burnt offering before it may make atonement for others (v. 19). The lesson servants most easily forget is this: the greater the service I do for others, the more I myself must first be covered by atoning blood. The moment when the Levites lay their hands on the bullocks' heads is the negative from which their whole life's service is printed: they are not righteous men doing holy work, but atoned men doing holy work. If the pressure of that hand is forgotten, service will sooner or later turn into seniority. May we remember, every time we lay our hands on our work, that we once laid them on the offering that died in our stead — we can do the work not because we are clean, but because blood was shed for us first.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) The composition of the water of purifying is stated here as discussion only, and not decided. Verse 7's water is, in the Hebrew, literally "water of sin." Whether it is the same water as the water of separation of Numbers 19, mixed with the ashes of the red heifer, commentators have argued both ways: some hold that it is indeed the water of chapter 19, the sin-removing force of the ashes answering exactly to the name ḥaṭṭāʾt; others, that since the rule of chapter 19 is given later in the narrative than this chapter's scene, the water here is cleansing water under a general name and need not be identified with the red heifer mixture. Milgrom treats this water and the sin-offering rite at length in his commentary on Numbers; Ashley and Wenham each handle it in their notes on this chapter (see the Bibliography). This sermon states the existence and drift of the two views only: the text itself says no more than that water of purifying is sprinkled upon them, and does not give its composition in this chapter — and what the text has not said, the preacher must not say for it in full.

### III. 24:00-42:00 The Levites shall be mine: instead of the firstborn, wholly given — and then given away as a gift

(Fact) Halfway through the rite the text pauses the sacrificial action and uses six whole verses (vv. 14-19) to lay bare the theological foundation of the Levites' rite; this is the heart of the chapter. Verse 14 stands in the KJV: "Thus shalt thou separate the Levites from among the children of Israel: and the Levites shall be mine." (Num 8:14, KJV) The end of the separation is not separation itself; it is a transfer of belonging: the Levites come out from among Israel not to be idle men but to be God's. Verse 15 fixes the order (v. 15, Summary): only after that shall the Levites go in to do the service of the tabernacle — and before the "after that" stand the cleansing and the offering of them as an offering. The order is cleansing, offering, then entering to serve; no step can be skipped, and no step can be taken early.

(Fact) The root of the belonging stands at verse 16, in the KJV: "For they are wholly given unto me from among the children of Israel; instead of such as open every womb, even instead of the firstborn of all the children of Israel, have I taken them unto me." (Num 8:16, KJV) Three layers, one by one. First, "wholly given": in the Hebrew the participle of nātan (Strong's H5414, "to give, to deliver") is used twice over, nəṯunîm nəṯunîm — "given, given" — a doubled force: given wholly and utterly, not half in the camp and half in the tabernacle (the form was checked word by word for this sermon; see Endnote 10). Second, "have I taken them unto me": the initiative in the belonging is God's — the Levites did not volunteer themselves to God; God took them to Himself. Third, "instead of the firstborn": substitution is the key word this chapter shares with chapter 3 — the place the Levites stand in was, house by house, the firstborn's; God took the Levites and filled the firstborn's place. Verse 17 traces this substitution to its source (v. 17, Summary): all the firstborn of the children of Israel, both man and beast, are God's; on the day He smote every firstborn in the land of Egypt, He sanctified them for Himself. Verse 18 then says the taking again, in short (v. 18, Summary): He has taken the Levites for all the firstborn of the children of Israel. A note of the translator's independent review belongs here: the Chinese manuscript speaks of verses 16-18 as stating the same matter three times over; checked against the KJV, verse 17 strictly grounds the claim in the Exodus event — that the firstborn are God's because of the night of the smiting — rather than restating the substitution itself, which verses 16 and 18 state. The English manuscript therefore states the matter in this more exact form, and the difference is registered in Endnote 5. The repetition, thus stated, still stands: the text is in no hurry, because this is the Levites' birth certificate as a tribe — they are God's not because they are stronger than the firstborn, but because on that one night in Egypt, smiting and taking fell together: the firstborn's lives were spared, and the Levites' persons were taken up in their place.

(Fact) The belonging stated, verse 19 states the giving out, in the KJV: "And I have given the Levites as a gift to Aaron and to his sons from among the children of Israel, to do the service of the children of Israel in the tabernacle of the congregation, and to make an atonement for the children of Israel: that there be no plague among the children of Israel, when the children of Israel come nigh unto the sanctuary." (Num 8:19, KJV) Within the one passage the Levites are given twice: at verse 16 they are wholly given to God; at verse 19 God gives them as a gift to Aaron and his sons. The two givings do not conflict; they are the chapter's skeleton: only men who have first belonged to God are then given out by God — given to Aaron and his sons as helpers, to do Israel's service in the tabernacle. Their charge is stated here as two things: to do the service of the children of Israel, and to make an atonement for the children of Israel. Its effect is stated in one clause: that there be no plague among the children of Israel when they come nigh unto the sanctuary. The Levites stand between the people and the sanctuary as a God-given buffer of grace: the sanctuary the people may not approach directly, the Levites approach for them, serve for them, and make atonement for them — and the plague does not fall on the people not because the sanctuary is not holy, but because a cleansed, offered, and appointed tribe stands in between.

(Application) Two words of verse 16 — "wholly given," the Hebrew saying "given" twice — are this chapter's heaviest word to everyone who would dedicate himself. It is no carelessness of style; it is emphasis by doubling: the Levites are not given in part — weekends to God and weekdays to themselves; nor given on trial — three years, and then we shall see; they are given wholly, given until even the years of their entering and their ceasing are not theirs to choose (vv. 23-26). The reserve we most commonly keep in dedication is the half we keep back: half the time kept for our own plans, half the strength for a way out, half the talent for the market. The Levites of this chapter had nothing to keep back with: they had no land inheritance (Numbers 18, Summary), and their food and clothing rested entirely on the congregation's offerings — and the man who is given wholly is also kept wholly: when God takes a man wholly, He does not leave that man to starve in his wholeness. "Wholly" runs in both directions: the man given wholly, and God keeping wholly.

(Application) And the word "gift" at verse 19 must be preached to everyone who serves in a supporting place. The Levites belonged to God, and then God gave them as a gift to Aaron and his sons — in human eyes, to be another man's gift and another man's helper looks like a step down: the firstborn replaced, the priest assisted. But the text sets the order out in glory: they are first God's "wholly given" men, and only afterwards Aaron's gift — the identity is fixed in God's hand first, and the post assigned in men's hands afterwards, and then no post can cheapen a man. If a man hunts for his identity in men's hands first, the helper's seat will breed grievance within three days; if "mine" has been settled with God first, a man can be given into anyone's hands and still know whose man he is. The supporting office in the church is the hardest seat to keep steady, and it is hard exactly where the identity has not been settled first. The Levites show us the reckoning: belonging first, mission afterwards; wholly God's first, and then glad to be given as a gift to men.

(Fact) At this section's end, let the chapter's wave-offerings be counted in the open, in preparation for the full reckoning in section IV. The Hebrew tenuphah, the Chinese base text's "wave offering," is used of the Levites four times in this chapter: at verse 11, in the command, Aaron offers them as an offering of the children of Israel (quoted above); at verse 13, they are set before Aaron and his sons and offered for an offering unto the LORD (Summary); at verse 15, Moses is to cleanse them and offer them for an offering (Summary); and at verse 21, in the performance, Aaron offers them as an offering before the LORD (quoted in section IV). The four verses were read and counted one by one for this sermon: four occurrences, no more and no fewer. In preparing the English manuscript the count was re-derived independently from the Hebrew noun and the Chinese text, and one KJV fact was registered against the Chinese manuscript's wording: in the KJV all four places read simply "offering," and the word "wave" never appears in the chapter — the count of four is a count of the Hebrew tenuphah (and of the Chinese base text's term), not of any KJV word. The first three occurrences stand in the command (vv. 11, 13, 15), the last in the performance (v. 21): the dedication spoken three times in the command is done, not one time fewer, in the performance — a correspondence of command and performance that is Numbers' structural habit, and that verses 20-22 will display head-on in the next section.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 According to all that the LORD commanded: performed, atoned for — and after that, they went in; with the chapter's full reckoning

(Fact) The fourth passage (vv. 20-22) records the performance, in three verses that advance layer by layer (vv. 20-22, Summary and quotation together). Verse 20 states the whole: Moses, Aaron, and all the congregation of the children of Israel did to the Levites according to all that the LORD commanded Moses concerning the Levites (v. 20, Summary). The agents of the performance have now widened to the maximum: Moses, Aaron, and the whole congregation, the three named together — no party absent, and no party adding to or subtracting from the command on its own authority. Verse 21 records the Levites' own acts and Aaron's closing acts, in the KJV: "And the Levites were purified, and they washed their clothes; and Aaron offered them as an offering before the Lord; and Aaron made an atonement for them to cleanse them." (Num 8:21, KJV) Of the three acts commanded at verse 7 — sprinkling, shaving, washing of clothes — the performance at verse 21 expressly restates the purification and the washing of the clothes; the shaving is not restated item by item in the performance. In the independent review for this English manuscript that statement of the Chinese manuscript was checked against the KJV of verse 21 and found exact: "were purified" and "washed their clothes" are there; no razor is mentioned. This sermon states what the performance records and what it does not record, and does not supply the unrecorded act on the performance's behalf. Aaron's two closing acts — offering them as an offering, and making atonement for them to cleanse them — answer to the command one for one. Verse 22 records the effect (v. 22, Summary): and after that the Levites went in to do their service in the tabernacle of the congregation before Aaron and before his sons; as the LORD had commanded Moses concerning the Levites, so did they unto them. "And after that" — went in: those words are the lock on the whole chapter's order. Cleansing first, offering in the middle, atonement after, and entering to serve last of all; until the lock turns, no man may enter the tabernacle early to serve.

(Fact) Let the chapter's figures now be reckoned in the open, every item counted verse by verse and checked in preparing this sermon, with the working and the verse references set down together. First, the verses: the chapter has twenty-six verses (vv. 1-26), in three parts — the first part, verses 1-4, four verses; the middle part, verses 5-22, eighteen verses; the last part, verses 23-26, four verses; and four plus eighteen plus four makes twenty-six, in agreement with the chapter's own verse count. Second, the openings "the LORD spake unto Moses": three in the chapter — at verses 1, 5, and 23 — each part beginning with it, and no fourth anywhere; the count was made verse by verse and agrees with the threefold division. Third, the wave-offering (Hebrew tenuphah): four occurrences (vv. 11, 13, 15, 21), counted in section III. Fourth, the laying on of hands: two occurrences — at verse 10 the children of Israel lay hands on the Levites (Summary), at verse 12 the Levites lay hands on the two bullocks (Summary), in opposite directions, one occurrence each. Fifth, the bullocks: two (v. 8) — one with its meat offering and one for a sin offering, the assignment disclosed at verse 12 as one sin offering and one burnt offering. Sixth, the lamps: seven (v. 2). These six counts each carry their verse references and can be checked; this sermon makes no numerical claim it has not counted verse by verse. What the text does not give, it does not give: the number of the Levites themselves at this dedication is nowhere recorded in this chapter; chapter 8 contains no numbering of the Levites' persons — the numbering chapters are chapters 3 and 4 — and this chapter records the rite, not a figure.

(Application) "So did they unto them" — those words are the most moving thing in this passage. The command took eighteen verses; the performance takes three — not because the performance is careless, but because the command was so detailed that nothing remained to deliberate: who is gathered, whose hands are laid first and whose afterwards, which bullock is offered for what, before whom the men are to stand — all of it was already in the command, and the performance had only one thing left to do: to do according to it. We commonly make obedience complicated, as if it needed our own improvisations to prove its sincerity. This chapter's performance shows another beauty: the whole congregation, from Moses to the last Israelite, added nothing to the rite and took nothing from it. To add is to be clever at God's expense; to subtract is to take authority that was not given; to do according to the command is to lay both cleverness and authority down and keep only faithfulness. The three verses of performance run without a ripple, and set all eighteen verses of command on the ground without dropping one of them — the strongest performance reports in Scripture are often written exactly this flatly: as it was commanded, so they did.

(Application) The words "and after that went in" must be given separately to everyone in a hurry to get onto the field. Between being taken out and going in to serve, the Levites pass sprinkling, shaving, washing, the preparing of bullocks, the gathering, the laying on of hands, the offering, and the atonement — a whole rite, no step of it superfluous, and no step of it waivable on the ground that "they are Levites; serving is their business anyway." Identity is settled by the taking out; fitness is settled by the completed rite. To be taken settles only who you are; to pass through cleansing and dedication settles whether you may go in. There is an impatience common among us: once chosen, a man wants to enter at once, and counts the preparatory rite as delay. This chapter spends eighteen verses of command and three of performance to set a whole forecourt of steps before the two words "went in" — not because God is reluctant to use men, but because He values the men He uses: the man who will serve in the sanctuary must first, at the tabernacle's door and before the whole congregation, give a clean account of himself from head to foot. The man whose account is not clean, the greater his work inside, the deeper his defilement; the man who enters only after the account is clean may work more slowly, and it does not matter — every step he takes stands on the order of the command.

### V. 58:00-60:00 In at twenty-five, out at fifty: to keep the charge, and do no service — with chapter 4 set beside it

(Fact) In the last passage (vv. 23-26) the LORD speaks to Moses a third time and fixes the Levites' years of service. Verse 24 stands in the KJV: "This is it that belongeth unto the Levites: from twenty and five years old and upward they shall go in to wait upon the service of the tabernacle of the congregation:" (Num 8:24, KJV) The Chinese base text's phrase at this verse — "come forward to take up the service" — is the very phrase that titles the companion sermon on chapter 4, recurring there at Numbers 4:3, 4:23, and 4:47 (Summary, and the quotations in that sermon). The same phrase, come to this chapter, carries a different age: here the service begins at twenty-five. Verse 25 stands in the KJV: "And from the age of fifty years they shall cease waiting upon the service thereof, and shall serve no more:" (Num 8:25, KJV) Verse 26 completes the measure of retirement, in the KJV: "But shall minister with their brethren in the tabernacle of the congregation, to keep the charge, and shall do no service. Thus shalt thou do unto the Levites touching their charge." (Num 8:26, KJV) Retirement is not departure: the man of fifty does no service, yet he is still in the tabernacle — ministering with his brethren, keeping the charge. Two Hebrew words stand side by side in this verse: the ministering, the assisting service, is šārēṯ (Strong's H8334), and what is kept is mišmereṯ (Strong's H4931), the entrusted charge — the verse's Hebrew was checked for this sermon (see Endnote 11): the retired man keeps the watch, but does not do the service; his person is in the tabernacle, his hands are off the office — the text draws even the measure of retirement clearly for the man in service. The term, reckoned in the open: in at twenty-five, out at fifty — a term of twenty-five years (fifty minus twenty-five makes twenty-five; the working is exact).

(Fact) Here chapter 4's reckoning must be set out side by side exactly as it stands, neither covered over nor harmonised. Chapter 4, numbering the men in service of the three houses, sets the beginning at thirty years, in the words of the KJV already quoted in the companion sermon: "From thirty years old and upward even until fifty years old, all that enter into the host, to do the work in the tabernacle of the congregation." (Num 4:3, KJV) Set the two side by side: chapter 4 (vv. 3, 23, 30, Summary) begins at thirty and ends at fifty, a term of twenty years; this chapter (vv. 24-25) begins at twenty-five and ends at fifty, a term of twenty-five years. The upper bound is fifty in both; the lower bound differs by five years. This sermon sets out the two texts, each in its own words, each with its own figures, altering neither — it does not change twenty-five into thirty, nor thirty into twenty-five, to manufacture agreement. The companion sermon on chapter 4 established this rule, and this sermon follows it: a difference the text itself has written down must first be honestly owned before application is preached, and any application that can stand only by stepping around the difference does not stand at all.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) This difference of five years between twenty-five and thirty has long been noticed by commentators, who have offered more than one reading. First, an apprenticeship reading: entry at twenty-five was a year of preparation and assistance, learning and helping under the brethren's hands (compare this chapter's verse 26, where even those who have ceased may still minister with their brethren and keep the charge, Summary), and only at thirty did a man independently take up the heavy bearing charges assigned in chapter 4 — the two passages speaking of two stages of one line of service. Second, a difference-of-charge reading: chapter 4 treats the special charge of bearing the most holy things and the tabernacle's heavy frame on the wilderness march, the highest threshold of all; this chapter treats the Levites' tabernacle service in general, a wider scope, and so sets the entry wider. Third, a difference-of-period reading: the two rules belong to different periods, the starting age being later relaxed or tightened; later practice has its own record — Chronicles relates that David in his last days numbered the Levites from twenty years old for the service of the house of the LORD (1 Chron 23:24-27, Summary), which shows that the starting age was not immovable in Israel's history. Fourth, at the textual level there has also been discussion of variant readings in ancient copies and versions at this figure; this sermon records its existence only, and does not expand it. It states the existence and drift of these discussions, and decides for none of them; nor does its argument rest on the five years. This chapter's own rule is clear verse by verse — in at twenty-five, out at fifty — and this sermon preaches this chapter from this chapter's own words.

(Application) The retirement at fifty must be preached to everyone who cannot bear to retire. The Levite's ceasing is not an expulsion; it is the end of a term: when the twenty-five years are full, the load comes off, yet the man does not leave the tabernacle — he still stands beside his brethren, ministering with them, keeping the charge; only, he shall "do no service." There is great honour in this measure: the retired man does not snatch the serving man's work, and he is not severed from the sanctuary either; with twenty-five years of experience he stands alongside, keeping watch, assisting, reminding — with his hands withdrawn from the office. How many teams are ruined in the space between retiring and not retiring: the old clinging, the young unable to come into place, and the work pinched between the two. This chapter's order handles the matter cleanly: an age to enter, an age to cease, and a standing after ceasing — the man who retires retires with name and measure; the man who enters enters with warrant and ground. If a man truly loves this work, he must love it enough to withdraw his hands when his term is full: withdraw your hands, and the work can be handed on; stand alongside, and your brethren are not left alone. To keep the charge and do no service is not uselessness; it is another manner of presence.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First, cleansing before offering: water sprinkled, the whole body shaved, the clothes washed, not one step fewer — zeal that has never been cleansed, offered at the altar, is still defilement; only when the whole man has given a clean account can the whole man be offered. Second, God's before men's: the Levites are wholly the LORD's first, and only then given as a gift to Aaron and his sons — the man whose identity is fixed with God first stands steady in any post. Third, an age to enter and a measure to cease: in at twenty-five, out at fifty, and after ceasing, keeping the charge and doing no service — the term is measured out by God, and to be glad both in entering and in ceasing is to have walked the twenty-five years through. May what we covet be not the conspicuous place, but the completion of this chapter's order: taken out, cleansed, offered as a wave offering, appointed to serve in the tabernacle, and at the fullness of the years withdrawing quietly to keep the charge beside our brethren — a whole life in the tabernacle, with not one step snatched by ourselves and not one step walked in vain.

(New Testament reading) Two New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this chapter; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. The first is John 8: there the Lord Jesus calls Himself the light of the world, and declares that he who follows Him shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life (John 8:12, Summary) — the seven lamps giving light over against the candlestick answer, in the New Testament reading, to this One who is Himself the light: on one side, lamps shining forward as commanded; on the other, the Light Himself walking into the world — the lamps were the preparation, He is the light itself. The second is Romans 12: there believers are beseeched to present their bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is their reasonable service (Rom 12:1, Summary) — a whole tribe offered as a wave offering and then going in to serve answers, at the reading level, to that presenting of the body as a living sacrifice, and serving so: on one side, a tribe offered at the tabernacle's door; on the other, every believer exhorted to be offered in the everyday. But the layers must be kept straight: Numbers 8 is first of all itself — the direction of seven lamps, the cleansing of sprinkling and shaving, the assignment of two bullocks, the term of twenty-five to fifty years. The New Testament reading is the light in which the church, in Christ, looks back on it; it must not be used to dissolve this chapter's rules. The more solid this chapter's rules — its quotations checkable word by word, its counts verifiable item by item, its two ages set side by side unharmonised — the more weight the reading layer's correspondence carries: the tribe cleansed and offered at the tabernacle's door points forward to the One who offered Himself wholly, and who makes all who are His a living sacrifice too.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 8 first showed me only a complicated rite: water sprinkled, the whole body shaved, clothes washed, two young bullocks taken, hands laid on, a wave offering made. Only when I set the order out slowly did I see its shape: first taken out, then cleansed; first cleansed, then offered; first offered as a wave offering before the LORD and atonement made for them, and after that the Levites went in to do their service in the tabernacle of the congregation. Not one step too many, not one too few, and not one that can change places. What touches my heart most is the word, "they are wholly given unto me" — in the Hebrew the word "given" is said twice over. They did not offer their service as one part of life among others; a whole tribe, a whole life, was wholly given. And once given, the LORD gave them as a gift to Aaron and to his sons, to do the service in the tabernacle. I so often do it backwards: I want first to go in and serve, to be seen and approved, and leave the cleansing and the offering to be made up afterwards. The text tells me that when the order is wrong, everything is wrong: without the water of purifying sprinkled upon me, without the razor over all my flesh, what I carry into service is only the uncleansed old man. So I pray today: Lord, cleanse me first, and then use me. Sprinkle upon me the water of purifying; make me willing for Thy razor to pass over all my flesh, and for my clothes to be washed as well; then offer me as a wave offering — keeping nothing back for my own plans, wholly Thine. If Thou givest me as a gift to anyone, in any place, I am content, because first I am Thine. However long or short the term, let my entering and my ceasing both follow Thy years; and on the day I cease, let me still stand with my brethren to keep the charge — not snatching the work, and not leaving the place, but quiet in Thy tabernacle.

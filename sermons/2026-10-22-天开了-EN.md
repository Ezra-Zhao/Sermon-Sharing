## Summary (about 100 words)

After obtaining the blessing by deception, Jacob is forced to flee. He spends the night in the wilderness, sleeping with a stone for a pillow. In a dream he sees a ladder set on the earth reaching to heaven, with angels ascending and descending, and the LORD reaffirms the promise of land, descendants, and His presence. He wakes in fear, calls the place the house of God and the gate of heaven, sets up the stone as a pillar, pours oil on it, and vows to give a tenth. This sermon looks at how God takes the initiative to come to a person on the road of flight.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The end of the previous chapter is the sound of a family falling apart. Outside the door Esau weeps bitterly, father Isaac trembles violently, and mother Rebekah quickly sends her younger son away, saying, go to your uncle Laban and stay there a few days until your brother's anger subsides, and then I will send for you and bring you back. She did not know that this stay would last twenty years, and that she would never see this most beloved son again in her lifetime.

Today we follow in Jacob's footsteps. He left Beersheba alone, heading for Haran. No servant, no camel, no bride-price, nothing at all. He had been a quiet man, dwelling in tents, always at his mother's side. Now for the first time he travels alone, for the first time he must spend the night in the open field, and for the first time he discovers that a blessing obtained by deception cannot serve as a blanket, nor can it protect him from wild beasts.

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several of the most important passages:

"And Jacob went out from Beersheba, and went toward Haran. And he lighted upon a certain place, and tarried there all night, because the sun was set; and he took of the stones of that place, and put them for his pillows, and lay down in that place to sleep." (Genesis 28:10-11, excerpt, KJV)

"And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it. And, behold, the LORD stood above it, and said, I am the LORD God of Abraham thy father, and the God of Isaac: the land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed." (Genesis 28:12-13, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob awaked out of his sleep, and he said, Surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not. And he was afraid, and said, How dreadful is this place! this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven." (Genesis 28:16-17, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob vowed a vow, saying, If God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat, and raiment to put on, So that I come again to my father's house in peace; then shall the LORD be my God: And this stone, which I have set for a pillar, shall be God's house: and of all that thou shalt give me I will surely give the tenth unto thee." (Genesis 28:20-22, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, among us too there are those walking on a road of flight; some tonight are sleeping in the wilderness with no certainty in their hearts. Through Jacob's night, let us see that heaven is not shut, that You have not gone far away, and that You still speak in the place where we lie down. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Fugitive Sleeps in "That Place" (28:10-11)

First, consider why Jacob is on this road. He is not traveling, nor is he going out to start a business; he is fleeing for his life. In chapter 27 he put on his brother's clothes, covered his hands with goat skins, carried the savory food his mother had made, and said before his blind father, I am Esau thy firstborn. The words were a lie; the blessing was obtained by deception. Now the blessing is in his hand, and the price is also in his hand: his brother wants to kill him, his father cannot keep him, his mother sends him away. He got what he wanted, but he lost the home where he could live in peace. This is often the result sin gives a person: the thing is grasped in the hand, but underfoot there is no longer a place to stand firm.

The Bible records his journey in a single sentence: "And Jacob went out from Beersheba, and went toward Haran." Beersheba was the place where his grandfather Abraham planted a tamarisk tree and called on the name of the LORD, where his father Isaac dug a well and made a covenant — the home where he grew up. Haran was several hundred kilometers away, a place he had never been. Walking this road alone, he hurries by day, and the night is the hardest to endure.

"And he lighted upon a certain place, and tarried there all night, because the sun was set." Notice this wording: a certain place. The original word is maqom (מָקוֹם, H4725), meaning place, location [1]. The text does not first give a place name, nor does it say this is a holy place; it only says he came to a place. The sun had set, he could walk no farther, and he dared not walk farther, so he stopped there. In the wilderness night there was no tent, so he took a stone of that place and put it under his head. Brothers and sisters, what a sorrowful pillow this is. At home he slept in a bed; on the road he used a stone as a pillow. The first lesson of Jacob's life was not how to grasp a blessing, but how, after grasping and finding emptiness, he could still lie down.

I invite you to notice the narrator's restraint. He does not scold Jacob, saying he deserved it, nor does he cover up for Jacob. He only says: the sun set, the man stopped, and he took a stone for a pillow. God's grace is often hidden in such restraint. Human words might say, you deceived, you fled, you brought this on yourself. God's word, however, first walks with him until dark, and then speaks. When a person has fallen to this point, those nearby often react in two ways: one is to gloat, the other is to preach big principles. God uses neither. God waits until he falls asleep.

Here too is a detail we easily miss: Jacob fell asleep, completely defenseless, completely unable to do anything. In the whole story, the one who takes the initiative is God, not Jacob. There is no record of Jacob praying, no record of him crying out, no record of him offering a sacrifice; he was simply tired and lay down. If grace had to wait until a person was prepared before it came, nothing would have happened that night for Jacob. Some among us have not prayed for many years and feel hollow inside, thinking that in such a state God probably will not pay attention. But the story of Bethel first tells us: it was not Jacob who climbed up to find God; it was God who opened heaven and came to the place where Jacob slept. When people sleep, God does not sleep. The psalm says, He that keepeth thee shall neither slumber nor sleep. Jacob slumbered; God did not.

Moreover, the word "place" appears again and again in this chapter: he came to a place, he took a stone of that place, he lay down there. Later he woke and said, how dreadful is this place. In the end he named this place Bethel. A place that originally had no name, merely a temporary overnight stop for a traveler, became a holy place remembered by later generations because God spoke there. Brothers and sisters, the "place" where you are today may be a hospital corridor, the bedside of a rented room, a city you cannot return home from after a business failure, or a living room where a marriage has grown so cold that no one wants to speak. You think it is only a temporary, nameless place to get through one night. But what God loves most to do is to reveal Himself in nameless places. For Moses it was the burning bush; for Gideon it was the winepress; for Jacob it was a stone and a dream. It does not matter that the place is ordinary; when God comes to it, the place is no longer ordinary.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Ladder Set on Earth Reaching to Heaven — God Stands Above and Speaks (28:12-15)

Jacob fell asleep and had a dream. This dream was not the product of his daytime thoughts; this dream was God opening heaven and letting him see the real world for a moment.

"And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it." Ladder — the original word is sullam (סֻלָּם, H5551), ladder, stairway, staircase [2]. This word appears only this once in the entire Old Testament; it is a unique word. A word used only once in the whole Bible, used in a unique dream: earth is no longer only earth, heaven is no longer only unattainable heaven; between them stands a ladder, set on the ground where Jacob lay, its top reaching to heaven.

Notice the verbs: the angels of God ascending and descending. Not descending and ascending, but ascending and descending. The order matters greatly. The angels first go up from the earth, then come down from heaven. In other words, there was already a pathway between heaven and earth, and the angels were traveling on this road, not only beginning from the moment Jacob dreamed. Jacob thought he was alone sleeping in the wilderness; in fact above his head there had always been angels coming and going — he had simply never seen them. Brothers and sisters, we often pray, God, please come to me here. Bethel corrects us: God has always been here; it is our eyes that have always been shut. The dream did not bring God down; the dream opened Jacob's eyes.

Look higher still: "And, behold, the LORD stood above it," some translations render it as stood beside him. Whether above or beside, the point is one: the One speaking at the top of the ladder is the LORD Himself, not an angel relaying a message. God begins speaking by first declaring His own name: "I am the LORD God of Abraham thy father, and the God of Isaac." To whom is this said? To a man who has just deceived his father. God did not first say, I am the God who judges you, nor did He say, I am the God who watches your performance. God first spoke the identity from the family line: I am your grandfather's God, I am your father's God. This blessing was originally transmitted along this covenant, and now God personally reaffirms it to the third generation. The blessing stolen away was fought over at home until heads were bloodied; the real blessing is God personally saying it again in the wilderness. What Jacob seized from his father by trickery, God here gives him freely again by grace. Would you say this grace is great? It is so great it makes one ashamed. Does he deserve it? He does not. But the transmission of the covenant rests not on human worthiness, but on God's faithfulness.

God's word has three layers. The first layer is the land: "The land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed." The land where he now lies is the very ground where he used a stone for a pillow. He lies there with nothing at all, and God says, this land is yours. Note that God gives the land not for him alone to enjoy, but for him and his descendants.

The second layer is descendants: "And thy seed shall be as the dust of the earth, and thou shalt spread abroad to the west, and to the east, and to the north, and to the south: and in thee and in thy seed shall all the families of the earth be blessed." As numerous as the dust, spreading to the four directions, all families blessed — this is the Abrahamic covenant restated in its original flavor. The deception did not lose the covenant; the flight did not run away from the covenant. In the wilderness God reads the terms of the covenant to Jacob one by one, like a father putting the family deed back into the hands of a son who has run away from home.

The third layer is the most moving: His presence. "And, behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest, and will bring thee again into this land; for I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of." Four promise sentences: I am with you, I will keep you, I will bring you back, I will not leave you until it is fulfilled. What Jacob needs most at this moment is not land, nor descendants — those are too far away. What he needs most at this moment is: tonight may I not be eaten by wild beasts, tomorrow may my brother not catch up with me, and when I arrive in Haran may I not be driven out. God's word lands exactly on his need tonight: wherever you go — including going to live under someone else's roof in a household you do not know — I will keep you. And there is a time of return: I will bring you again into this land. Rebekah said, when your brother's anger subsides I will send for you; God said, I will bring you back. Human fetching can fail — in twenty years Rebekah sent no one; God's bringing back did not fail — twenty years later Jacob indeed returned to this land with his whole household and built an altar again at Bethel.

The Lord Jesus personally provided a footnote for this chapter in the Gospel of John. He said to Nathanael: "…Hereafter ye shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man." (John 1:51, excerpt, KJV) [6] The ladder here has a name: the Son of Man. Jacob's ladder was a vision; the Lord Jesus, the Son of Man, is the reality. The pathway between heaven and earth is no longer a ladder in a dream, but a real person: He came from heaven, returns to heaven, and sends the Holy Spirit to dwell among us. Today we no longer need to pillow on a stone and wait for a dream, because the ladder has become flesh and dwells among us. Heaven opened is not a one-night vision, but a new normal: through Christ, prayer can go up and grace can come down. What Jacob glimpsed in a dream, we live in every day in Christ.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The Man Who Wakes — Pillar, Oil, and Vow (28:16-22)

The dream is over, and the man must wake. The second half of this chapter is entirely about how a man responds to grace after he wakes. Jacob's response has four steps, and every step is worth learning.

The first step is awakening: "And Jacob awaked out of his sleep, and he said, Surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not!" The Bible contains few confessions of faith more honest than this sentence. Surely the LORD is in this place — that is fact; and I knew it not — that is admitting fault. He did not pretend he had always been devout; he admitted his ignorance. How often God is right beside us, yet we see only the stone, only the wilderness, only bad luck. This confession today may be the very sentence you need to say: Lord, You truly were in these days of mine, and I knew it not. I thought You were not here; in fact I was asleep.

The second step is fear: "And he was afraid, and said, How dreadful is this place! this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven." Fear is not merely trembling and then being done; fear is re-evaluating this place and re-evaluating God. He gives this place two definitions: the house of God — in Hebrew, Bet-el (בֵּית־אֵל, H1008), the house of God, the temple of God [3]; and the gate of heaven, a door, a door to heaven. That nameless place [1] from then on had a name. Jacob later named that city Bethel; its former name was Luz. Luz was a human name; Bethel is the name after meeting God. A person's life is the same: some years, when you look back, were originally called failure, called wandering, called a low valley; after meeting God they are renamed Bethel. It is not that the experience changed; it is that you recognized God in the experience, and the name changed.

The third step is remembrance: "And Jacob rose up early in the morning, and took the stone that he had put for his pillows, and set it up for a pillar, and poured oil upon the top of it." Pillar — the original word is matstsevah (מַצֵּבָה, H4676), an erected stone pillar, a memorial pillar [4]. The stone he had pillowed on, stood upright, became a memorial. The thing that made his neck ache last night, stood up today, reminds him never to forget this night for the rest of his life. Pouring oil on it is an act of consecration: this stone is no longer an ordinary stone, this place is no longer an ordinary place. Brothers and sisters, faith needs memorials. Our memorials need not be stones; they can be a page of a journal, a hymn, a testimony before others. What is to be feared is receiving grace and then forgetting it as soon as we turn around, and when days go smoothly, returning to the old ways. Jacob returned to Bethel many times later in his life, and each time he returned, he returned following this pillar. People forget; pillars do not forget.

The fourth step is the vow: "And Jacob vowed a vow, saying, If God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat, and raiment to put on, So that I come again to my father's house in peace; then shall the LORD be my God." When the word "If" appears, many people frown: is Jacob bargaining with God? We must read fairly. In the dream God had already unconditionally promised presence, keeping, return, and never forsaking. Jacob's vow is almost a restatement of God's promise sentence by sentence: You said You will be with me, so I say if God will be with me; You said You will keep me, so I say keep me in this way; You said You will bring me back, so I say bring me again to my father's house in peace. This is not negotiation; this is a new believer clutching the promise and learning to pray word by word. His faith is still tender, still carrying an "if," still thinking of the most basic things like bread and raiment. God does not despise his tenderness. God knows best that for a fugitive, faith grows starting from bread and clothing.

The vow lands on the tenth: "And of all that thou shalt give me I will surely give the tenth unto thee." The tenth — the original word is ma'aser (מַעֲשֵׂר, H4643), a tenth, a tithe offering [5]. Notice his logic: I now have nothing; what can I offer? What he offers is a future promise: of all that Thou shalt give me — first acknowledging that everything is given by You — I will surely give a tenth to You from it. This is an early example of personal tithing in the Bible, hundreds of years before the Law. His grandfather Abraham once gave a tenth of all to Melchizedek; now the grandson, in the wilderness, standing at a starting point of emptiness, fixes the proportion first. The lesson of giving is best learned when one has nothing. If one waits until one has much to learn it, the heart will first be tied by money. Jacob learned this lesson beside the stone: when having nothing, first acknowledge that what God gives, I will set apart a tenth for Him. This pillar and this vow accompanied him through twenty years of living under another's roof. When he returned twenty years later he said, with my staff I passed over this Jordan, and now I am become two bands. That vow he did not forget; and that God did not forget either.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: those on a road of flight. You may not be fleeing for your life, but you are fleeing: fleeing a failure, fleeing a relationship, fleeing a result you dare not face. Today's word says to you: you have come to "that place," the sun is about to set; lying down first is not resignation — God wants to speak to you in the place where you lie down. Presence, keeping, bringing back, never forsaking — these four promises come to you today just as they were.

The second kind: those who grasped blessings by the wrong method. Jacob grasped the blessing, but had to pay the price of twenty years away from home. At Bethel God did not withdraw the blessing, but He also did not condone the means. Grace is not a stamp approving trickery; grace is leading a person out of trickery. From today, loosen the grasping hand and change it into a receiving hand: what God promises with His own mouth is more secure than what you snatch.

The third kind: those who think God is not here. "Surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not" — please paste this sentence onto your hardest days. That you cannot see does not mean He is not here. Often it is not that God is absent; it is that we are asleep: asleep with busyness, asleep with bitterness, asleep with discouragement. Ask God to open your eyes to see that the place where you live is in fact the gate of heaven.

The fourth kind: new believers whose faith is still tender. Your prayers still contain an "if," and what you ask for is still only bread, clothing, and a safe return home — do not feel inferior. Jacob's starting point was like this, and God still accepted him and still led him. Tender faith is not sin; not growing is the problem. Pray by restating God's words sentence by sentence, learn sentence by sentence, and twenty years later when you look back, you will be amazed how far you have come.

The fifth kind: those without memorials who easily forget grace. Learn from Jacob to set up a pillar: record concretely what God has accomplished for you, write it down, speak it out. Faith without memorials cannot withstand the next wilderness. Your pillar may be a testimony you make today before others: in that year, in that place, how God treated me — I set this up as a witness.

The sixth kind: those who have not yet known the true ladder. The reality of the ladder is the Lord Jesus Christ. He said heaven is open, and the angels ascend and descend upon the Son of Man. You no longer need to rely on luck, on visions, or on searching everywhere for miracles; you can come directly before Him. Trust in Him, and the ground under your feet becomes the house of God, and the heaven above your head is no longer shut.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Responsive Prayer

Today we spent a night with Jacob in the wilderness: he fled, slept in the open, pillowed on a stone, yet in a dream he saw heaven open, saw the ladder reaching to heaven, heard God reaffirm the Abrahamic covenant, and when he woke he set up a pillar, poured oil, made a vow, and named a nameless place Bethel — the house of God. Heaven opened, and it turns out the first place a fugitive arrives is not a dead end, but the doorway of God's house.

Let us pray together: Dear Lord, thank You that when we fled You did not abandon us, and when we slept You Yourself spoke. Open our eyes to see that You are truly here; strengthen our hearts to believe that You will keep us, will bring us back, will never forsake us, until Your promise is fulfilled. And let us respond to You as Jacob did when he woke: set up a memorial, offer a tenth, and place our whole lives in Your hands. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, who became the ladder to heaven for us, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] maqom: Hebrew מָקוֹם (H4725), place, location, standing place; in Genesis 28:11 it appears three times — "he lighted upon a certain place," "he took of the stones of that place"; in 28:16-17 Jacob refers to it as "this place"; the meaning follows BDB and HALOT. The observation in Part One of this manuscript, that a nameless place becomes a holy place because God comes to it, is a homiletic observation based on the repeated occurrence of this word in chapter 28.

[2] sullam: Hebrew סֻלָּם (H5551), ladder, stairway; it occurs in the entire Old Testament only in Genesis 28:12, a hapax legomenon; scholars generally consider it related to the Akkadian silla (stairway, ramp) and it may refer to a temple-tower-like stairway; the meaning follows BDB and HALOT. The statement in this manuscript that a word used only once in the whole Bible is used in a unique dream is based on this.

[3] Bet-el: Hebrew בֵּית־אֵל (H1008), house of God, temple of God, compounded from bayith (house) and El (God); in Genesis 28:17 Jacob calls the place where he slept "the house of God" and "the gate of heaven," and in 28:19 he names that city Bethel; its former name was Luz; the meaning follows BDB and HALOT. Note the distinction: in 28:17 Jacob says "the house of God" (the common-noun phrase beth Elohim), whereas Bet-el (H1008) is the place name he gives the city in 28:19; the two are related in sense but are not the same form, and this note covers the place name.

[4] matstsevah: Hebrew מַצֵּבָה (H4676), an erected stone pillar, a memorial pillar; in Genesis 28:18, 22 Jacob sets up the pillow stone as a pillar, pours oil on it, and says this pillar shall be God's house; the meaning follows BDB and HALOT. See also Genesis 31:45 and 35:14, where Jacob later sets up pillars again.

[5] ma'aser: Hebrew מַעֲשֵׂר (H4643), a tenth, a tithe offering; Genesis 28:22, "of all that thou shalt give me I will surely give the tenth unto thee"; the meaning follows BDB and HALOT. See also Genesis 14:20, where Abraham gave a tenth of all to Melchizedek, an earlier tithe example in the Bible.

[6] On the cross-reference to John 1:51: The Lord Jesus said to Nathanael, "…Hereafter ye shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man," wording that directly echoes Genesis 28:12; Part Two of this manuscript connects the ladder with the Son of Man on this basis, with the source noted here, without presenting a New Testament interpretation as the original meaning of the Old Testament.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it. And, behold, the LORD stood above it, and said, I am the LORD God of Abraham thy father, and the God of Isaac: the land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed." (Genesis 28:12-13) "And, behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest, and will bring thee again into this land; for I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of." (Genesis 28:15)

That night Jacob had nothing in his hands and only a stone under his head. He thought he was running for his life, yet the place where he lay down was the very place where God would speak. The stairway in his dream told him: heaven was not shut, the earth was not abandoned, and God stood above, beyond his reach, repeating the promise in His own voice. Perhaps today you too are sleeping in a wilderness: the problem is unsolved, the road is long, and your heart has no certainty. Remember this night. You did not climb up to find God; God opened heaven and came to you. His word has not changed: I am with you, I will watch over you wherever you go, I will not leave you until I have done what I promised. Stand that stone upright, and write this night down. A nameless place where you meet God receives a new name: Bethel, the house of God.

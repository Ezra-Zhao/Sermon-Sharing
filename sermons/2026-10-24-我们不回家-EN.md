## Summary

The war with Midian has been fought and its spoil divided (chapter 31, preceding-text layer), and the camp is still pitched in the plains of Moab on the east side of Jordan when the question of land is laid on the table at once. The children of Reuben and the children of Gad had a very great multitude of cattle, and they saw the land of Jazer and the land of Gilead, that the place was a place for cattle (32:1). They came to Moses, to Eleazar the priest, and to the princes of the congregation, and set out nine place-names, ending with one sentence: "if we have found grace in thy sight, let this land be given unto thy servants for a possession, and bring us not over Jordan." (32:5) The words were barely out when Moses' answer came — not negotiation but rebuke: "Shall your brethren go to war, and shall ye sit here?" (32:6) He then brought out the old matter of forty years before in full: the spies went up from Kadesh-barnea and discouraged the heart of the people; the LORD swore in His anger, and that generation fell in the wilderness; if you now discourage the people again, you are risen up in your fathers' stead, augmenting the fierce anger, and the whole people will be left in the wilderness again (32:7-15). Under the rebuke the two tribes came near and handed over a clarified plan: first build sheepfolds here for the cattle and cities for the little ones, so that the families dwell in fenced cities; they themselves will go ready armed before the children of Israel until they have brought their brethren to their place — "We will not return unto our houses, until the children of Israel have inherited every man his inheritance." (32:17-18) Only then did Moses recast the plan as the conditions of a covenant: if you go armed before the LORD over Jordan to war, until the land is subdued, and only then return, you shall be guiltless before the LORD and before Israel, and this land shall be yours for a possession; "But if ye will not do so, behold, ye have sinned against the LORD: and be sure your sin will find you out." (32:22-23) The close is permission, and also an urging: "Build you cities for your little ones, and folds for your sheep; and do that which hath proceeded out of your mouth." (32:24) The two tribes assented again and again (32:25-27, 31-32); Moses entrusted the conditions face to face to Eleazar, to Joshua, and to the chief fathers, to be kept on record (32:28-30), and at once gave the kingdoms of Sihon and Og to Gad, to Reuben, and to half the tribe of Manasseh (32:33). The chapter ends with three lists: the eight cities and the folds built by Gad, the six cities built by Reuben, and the three movements of the children of Manasseh in Gilead (32:34-42). There is not one battle in this chapter, and its eye is not on the land but on words — on what it takes for words once spoken to count.

## Main Text

### Part One: The Request East of Jordan — Nine Place-Names and One "Bring Us Not over Jordan" (Numbers 32:1-5) (00:00-10:00)

(Fact) Verse 1, the origin of the request, quoted verbatim in full from the base text: "Now the children of Reuben and the children of Gad had a very great multitude of cattle: and when they saw the land of Jazer, and the land of Gilead, that, behold, the place was a place for cattle;" (32:1) The items registered one by one: first, the subject is two tribes — Reuben and Gad; from beginning to end of this chapter half the tribe of Manasseh is not among the requesters and appears only at the grant (32:33), and the two registers are kept distinct as they stand. Second, the origin is cattle — "a very great multitude" is this chapter's only description of the two tribes' property, and the eye with which they look at the land is tied to it: the land of Jazer and the land of Gilead are, in their statement, first of all "a place for cattle." Verse 2, those to whom the request is made, registered: they came to Moses, to Eleazar the priest, and to the princes of the congregation — the occasion of the request is public, laid before the leaders of the whole camp, not privately negotiated at the edge of the camp, recorded as it stands. Verse 3, the list of names, registered in its own wording: they set out nine places — Ataroth, and Dibon, and Jazer, and Nimrah, and Heshbon, and Elealeh, and Shebam, and Nebo, and Beon (registered by the base text; the identification of individual names with modern sites belongs to the discussion layer). Verse 4, the ground of the request, registered in its substance: this is the country which the LORD smote before the congregation of Israel, it is a land for cattle, and thy servants have cattle — the grounds are two: this land has already been won (the defeat of the two kings Sihon and Og stands in chapter 21, preceding-text layer), and this land suits their livelihood. Verse 5, the request itself, quoted verbatim in full: "wherefore, said they, if we have found grace in thy sight, let this land be given unto thy servants for a possession, and bring us not over Jordan." (32:5) Registered: the request is twofold — give this land to us for a possession, and bring us not over Jordan; its form is the pleading form, "if we have found grace in thy sight," not an ultimatum, recorded as it stands. As for the fact that their possession would thereby fall east of Jordan, separated from their brethren west of it, this verse offers not one word of comment, and says nothing about crossing over to fight — and that silence is exactly where the rebuke of the next part takes hold.

(Argument) First, a fair word for this passage: looking only at these five verses, the request itself is grounded at every point. The land has already been taken; the whole camp is not being asked to fight another war for them. The land suits their livelihood; a very great multitude of cattle is fact, not an excuse anyone invented. The occasion is public too — Moses, the priest, and the princes are all seated; no land is being taken behind the brethren's backs. A man who, according to his own livelihood, lays a grounded request openly before those who lead him — that act in itself, in any camp, should not be condemned first. If we read this chapter by turning the two tribes at once into small men greedy for ease, we lose the weight of Moses' rebuke: the rebuke is heavy precisely because the request is on its face unimpeachable — the problem is not in what they said, but in what they did not say. They set out nine place-names, argued the land's origin and use, and arranged where their own possession would fall; the one thing they never mentioned was this: the war west of Jordan — who will fight it? Their brethren's land — who will help take it? A request that never mentions the whole camp's future, however grounded word by word, has already lost half its case in Moses' ears. When we bring requests to the church or to a team today, we should let this question examine us too: in my request, besides my land, my cattle, and my settling, is there a share for my brethren? The half that goes unmentioned is often the half the leader is truly listening for.

(Argument) Look again at that sentence, "bring us not over Jordan." In this book Jordan is not only a river: it is the threshold of the promised land, the finishing line of the whole camp's forty years of journeying. Whatever the two tribes meant by the sentence, objectively it says: our possession is enough on this side of the threshold (the meaning they themselves spell out later at 32:19, registered in Part Three). A man may sit down before the promise is complete — the land is real, the grass is green, the cattle are his own — and the fuller his reasons for sitting, the smaller his chance of rising. The danger Moses heard lies exactly here: not that these two tribes had seized anything, but that they modelled a way of living — see what suits you and stop there; while the whole camp's road is unfinished, those who arrive first turn "arrived early" into "arrived." This lesson does not wait for great matters to be used: how many people leave the community not in failure but by slowly fading out inside "my side is already settled." Settling itself is not sin; settling early and never turning back to walk with the brethren is what Moses moves to stop.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Cattle" is miqneh (H4735), the word of this chapter's origin, denoting livestock property in herds. "Possession / inheritance" is ʾāḥuzzâ (H272), a possession held and inherited; the same Hebrew word stands behind the request of verse 5, the self-statement of verse 19, the promise of verse 22, and the clauses of verses 29-30 (see Endnote 5) — in the KJV letter it is rendered "possession" at 32:5, 22, 29, and 32 and "possessions" at 32:30, while at 32:19 the KJV reads "inheritance," and each verse is registered by its own rendering. "Over (Jordan)" rests on ʿābar (H5674); the same root stands behind the request of 32:5, the condition of 32:21, and the assents of 32:27 and 32:32 — one river's width, and everything hangs on this one word "over."

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The motives of the two tribes' request are set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one reads it as a livelihood reading — the two tribes live by herding, the plateau east of Jordan has water and grass that suit them, the request seeks land according to livelihood and has nothing to do with faith, and Moses' rebuke is aimed at timing and unity, not at motive itself. Another reads it as an ease reading — the request comes hard after victory and just before entering Canaan, and those who sit down first risk breaking away from the whole camp's common future; later readings (as in the alarm at Joshua 22, when the tribes east of Jordan built an altar, following-text layer) show that the whole camp's doubts about the unity of the eastern tribes never went away. This sermon registers only the points of view of the two readings and the textual facts of this chapter: the request does not mention going to war (32:1-5), and the pledge to go to war is added only after the rebuke (32:16-19); the main text builds its argument only on that sequence, and does not adjudicate the original intentions of the two tribes, good or bad. Registered in addition: among the nine place-names, Ataroth, Dibon, Jazer, Heshbon, Elealeh, and Nebo reappear in the building lists at the chapter's end (32:34-38); Beon does not reappear in the final section — this textual fact only is registered, and no reason is extrapolated from it.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of Part One: the requesters are two tribes (Reuben and Gad); the origin is one item (a very great multitude of cattle, and Jazer and Gilead seen to be a place for cattle); those addressed are three parties (Moses, Eleazar, and the princes of the congregation); the list of names has nine places (Ataroth, Dibon, Jazer, Nimrah, Heshbon, Elealeh, Shebam, Nebo, Beon); the request is two sentences (give this land for a possession; bring us not over Jordan); the grounds are two (the land is one the LORD has already smitten; it suits herding, and the servants have cattle). And one blank is registered: from first to last the request never mentions crossing over to fight, nor the brethren west of Jordan receiving their land — and that blank is the direct occasion of the rebuke in the next part.

### Part Two: Moses' Rebuke — "Shall Ye Sit Here?" (Numbers 32:6-15) (10:00-24:00)

(Fact) Verse 6, the question of rebuke, quoted verbatim in full: "And Moses said unto the children of Gad and to the children of Reuben, Shall your brethren go to war, and shall ye sit here?" (32:6) Registered: Moses' answer comes before all negotiation — he does not ask about the details of the land or the number of the cattle; his first sentence translates the request into a picture: your brethren go to war, and ye sit here. The contrast of "go to war" and "sit" is Moses' characterization, not the two tribes' own words, and the two are registered distinctly. Verse 7, the second question, registered in its substance: wherefore discourage ye the heart of the children of Israel from going over into the land which the LORD hath given them? — from this verse the centre of Moses' charge shifts from "sit" to "discourage": the problem is not only that the two tribes themselves will not cross, but that their not crossing will discourage the heart of the whole camp. Verses 8-9, the old matter brought up again, registered: Moses states that he himself sent their fathers from Kadesh-barnea to see the land; they went up to the valley of Eshcol, saw the land, and discouraged the heart of the children of Israel, so that they would not go into the land which the LORD had given them — this chapter's retelling here corresponds to the events themselves in chapters 13-14 (preceding-text layer); the details rest on the events themselves, and this chapter takes only the outline. Verses 10-12, the retelling of the oath, registered: on that day the LORD's anger was kindled and He sware — none of the men who came up out of Egypt, from twenty years old and upward, shall see the land sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, because they have not wholly followed Him; save Caleb the son of Jephunneh the Kenezite and Joshua the son of Nun, for they have wholly followed the LORD. Verse 13, the retelling of the consequence, registered: the LORD's anger was kindled against Israel, and He made them wander in the wilderness forty years, until all that generation that had done evil in the sight of the LORD was consumed. Verses 14-15, the landing-point of the rebuke, registered in its substance: Moses calls the present requesters a generation risen up in their fathers' stead, and warns — if the camp turns away from following Him, the LORD will yet again leave this people in the wilderness, and so you shall destroy all this people. At this point the rebuke rises from the two tribes' question of property to a question of the whole people's survival; its order of argument is registered as it stands, and whether it is fair to the two tribes is not adjudicated here (the several accounts stand at the discussion layer).

(Argument) This speech of Moses is worth every leader's study, sentence by sentence. He does not argue the request on the request's own terms — you want land, that can be discussed; at one stroke he sets the request back inside the whole camp's history: this thing happened once, forty years ago, and the whole camp is still paying for it. The leader's eye lies precisely in recognizing the new edition of an old matter: the people look at the present and see grassland and cattle; Moses looks at the present and sees the spies of Kadesh. The same sentence, in the ears of those who see only the present, is an arrangement of livelihood; in the ears of one who remembers history, it is the first scene of a disaster being staged again. This is not Moses being suspicious: a community's survival often hangs not on how strong the enemy is, but on what example its own people set at the decisive moment. If the two tribes sit down east of Jordan now, the thought of imitation comes alive at once in the rest of the tribes — "they can stop; why can we not?" Discouragement is contagious, and so is settling. What Moses blocks is not the ownership of two pieces of land but a possible collapse of the whole camp; he speaks up to "ye shall destroy all this people" because he is speaking by the true scale of the consequences, not by the surface size of the request.

(Argument) Hear also the measuring rod inside Moses' words: he measures the two tribes not by how much they lose, but by how much they will discourage their brethren. A man's weight in a community is never decided only by his own gain and loss, but also by what his decisions stir up in the hearts of others. However well the two tribes' property is arranged, if it plants in the brethren's hearts the thought "we need not cross," it stands as a loss in Moses' account. So it is today: your withdrawal, your sitting down first — each by itself is your right; but the more prominent your place, the less your "sitting down" is your affair alone — the people in the back rows are watching the front rows to decide whether they themselves will rise. Moses sets a boundary for everyone with influence: before exercising a right, reckon the account of example first.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The verb of "discourage" is the causative of nûʾ (H5106), to hinder, to make lose heart; the question of verse 7 and the retelling of verse 9 use the same word. "Sware" is šābaʿ (H7650). The "anger" of "the LORD's anger was kindled" is built on ḥārâ (H2734), anger flaring up. The expression "wholly followed" is set in contrast at verses 11 and 12 — the many "have not wholly followed me," Caleb and Joshua "have wholly followed the LORD" (see Endnote 5). The word-form behind "wander" at verse 13 ("he made them wander in the wilderness forty years") is marked pending verification in this sermon (see Endnote 5); only the base text's rendering is recorded.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Whether Moses' characterization of this request by the old matter of the spies is fair is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one holds that the analogy stands — the common point of the two events lies not in motive but in effect: both occur on the eve of entering Canaan, both begin with the drawing back of a part of the people, and either is enough to discourage the whole camp, so Moses rebukes by effect, and the rebuke is grounded. Another holds that the analogy is too heavy — the spies' sin was to bring an evil report and deny the promise, whereas the two tribes have not denied the land west of Jordan; they have only applied for the east as their possession, in a public setting and in a pleading tone, and to charge them with the old account of destroying the whole people risks excessive pressure. A third looks to narrative function — the severe rebuke raises, in the narrative, the cost of the pledge that follows: precisely because they have first been held before the mirror of the old matter, the two tribes' clarification must answer by going ready armed at the front; the rebuke is the threshold of the covenant. This sermon registers only the points of view of the three readings and the order of speaking in this chapter (rebuke first, clarification after), and does not adjudicate the right or wrong of Moses' analogy at the fact layer; the main text builds only on what the rebuke states of itself: the contrast (32:6), discouraging the heart (32:7), the old matter and the oath (32:8-13), and the warning of survival (32:14-15).

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of Part Two: two questions of rebuke (your brethren go to war and ye sit here; wherefore discourage ye the heart of the children of Israel); four sections of retelling (the sending to spy, the discouraging at the valley of Eshcol, the anger and the oath, the forty years of wandering); two exceptions (Caleb and Joshua); one warning (if the people are left in the wilderness again, the whole people is destroyed). And the distribution of the right to speak is registered: all ten verses of this part are Moses' words alone, and the two tribes have no recorded answer in this part — their answer begins in the next part (32:16), and the manner of its opening is to come near, first to state the arrangements, then to give the pledge, recorded as it stands.

### Part Three: Clarification and Covenant — Those Who First Build Folds and Cities Themselves Cross Over at the Front (Numbers 32:16-24) (24:00-34:00)

(Fact) Verse 16, the coming near and the first move, registered: they came near unto him and said that they would first build sheepfolds here for their cattle, and cities for their little ones — the first sentence of the clarification is not a defence of motives but the handing over of an order for settling the families: settle first, then go out to war, and those settled are the livestock and the women and children, not themselves. Verse 17, the first pledge, quoted verbatim in full: "but we ourselves will go ready armed before the children of Israel, until we have brought them unto their place: and our little ones shall dwell in the fenced cities because of the inhabitants of the land." (32:17) The items registered one by one: first, "we ourselves" is set against "our little ones" — those who remain are only the women and children; everyone who can fight crosses over, in full. Second, the position is "before the children of Israel" — not bringing up the rear with the host, but at the front. Third, the finishing line of the task is "until we have brought them unto their place" — the brethren's receiving their land is the point at which their own task is complete. Verse 18, the second pledge, quoted verbatim in full: "We will not return unto our houses, until the children of Israel have inherited every man his inheritance." (32:18) Registered: the condition of returning home is not that the war is over, but that the children of Israel have inherited every man his inheritance — only when every man has his possession does their turn to go home come. Verse 19, the third pledge, registered in its substance: they will not inherit with their brethren on yonder side Jordan, or forward, because their inheritance is fallen to them on this side Jordan eastward — giving up a double portion west of Jordan is the counter-value of the pledge, recorded as it stands. Verses 20-21, Moses lays down the conditions, registered in their substance: Moses said, if you will do this thing, if you will go armed before the LORD to war, and all of you who are armed will go over Jordan before the LORD, until He has driven out His enemies from before Him — from here the subject of the conditions changes from the two tribes' willingness to clauses promulgated by Moses. Verse 22, guiltlessness and the land given as possession, quoted verbatim in full: "and the land be subdued before the LORD: then afterward ye shall return, and be guiltless before the LORD, and before Israel; and this land shall be your possession before the LORD." (32:22) Registered: the order is first subdued, first returned, and only then "guiltless," only then "your possession" — the formal belonging of the possession comes after performance, not at the time of the request. Verse 23, the penalty clause, quoted verbatim in full: "But if ye will not do so, behold, ye have sinned against the LORD: and be sure your sin will find you out." (32:23) Registered: breach is characterized as having "sinned against the LORD," and its consequence is stated as "be sure your sin will find you out" — this verse sets out no specific punishment, recorded as it stands; the warning sentence is neither softened nor enlarged. Verse 24, permission and urging, quoted verbatim in full: "Build you cities for your little ones, and folds for your sheep; and do that which hath proceeded out of your mouth." (32:24) Registered: Moses' close is twofold — the building of cities and folds is approved as asked, and what has proceeded out of your mouth is simply to be done; in the KJV letter the building stands first and the doing second, where the Chinese base text reverses that order, and each manuscript follows its own text (see the Base-text notes). This sentence also joins head to tail with the ordinance on vows in chapter 30 (preceding-text layer): once the words are out of the mouth, act on them.

(Argument) The two tribes' clarification is the passage of this whole chapter most worth learning as speech. Under so heavy a rebuke, they did not do the three most natural things: they did not defend their original intention (we are not greedy for ease), they did not accuse Moses of misunderstanding (you have classed us wrongly), and they did not bargain (we will send half our men over). They did the hardest thing: they translated Moses' concern into their own plan of action and answered it item by item — you fear we will sit down? We will stand at the very front of the ranks. You fear we will discourage others? We make our return home conditional on every one of the brethren receiving his inheritance. You fear we are taking advantage? Of the possession west of Jordan we take not a pennyworth. The highest form of clarification is never explaining oneself; it is revising the plan, so that the item the other side worries about has nowhere to live in the new plan. Whether a conflict escalates or not — in family, workplace, or church today — very often turns on whether the party rebuked will take this road: not hurrying to prove itself guiltless, but hurrying first to make the other side's concern fall empty. What Moses wanted was never a sentence of "we are not that kind of people"; he wanted "we ourselves will go ready armed before" — the former is words, and the latter is words too, but the latter is words that can be made into a covenant and verified.

(Argument) Look then at how Moses receives this clarification: he does not soften the conditions because their attitude is good. He rewrites the voluntary pledge clause by clause: the number who cross — all who are armed; the finishing point of the fighting — until the enemies are driven out and the land is subdued; the certification of performance — only after returning do they count as guiltless before the LORD and before Israel; the characterization of breach — sin against the LORD, and your sin will find you out. Attitude is attitude and clauses are clauses; that is the hand of a mature leader: receive the sincerity, and at the same time nail the sincerity into a verifiable order. A man's sincerity is discounted by the days; clauses are not. Three years on, when the war is half fought and someone wants to go home, what stops him is not the emotion of that year but the finishing point agreed that year. When the church and individuals make covenants, set boundaries, and arrange repayment and service, they should keep this same clear-headedness: when the words are settled, settle the finishing point, the certification, and the breach along with them — not because persons are not trusted, but to leave the man who will one day be weak a reason that will stand.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The word for the state of being "armed" comes from the root ḥālaṣ (H2502), to draw oneself out and go armed to war; the self-statement of verse 17 and the conditions of verses 20-21 are of the same family. "Subdued" is kābaš (H3533), the same word in verse 22, "the land be subdued," and in the clause of verse 29. "Guiltless" is nāqî (H5355), without fault in both directions, toward the LORD and toward Israel. The verb of "ye have sinned" is ḥāṭāʾ (H2398); the "will find you out" of "be sure your sin will find you out" is a future form of māṣāʾ (H4672) — the sin finds the man (see Endnote 5). The word-form behind "folds" (gĕdērâ) is marked pending verification in this sermon (see Endnote 5); only the base text's rendering is recorded.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The readings of land granted under conditions are set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one reads it as order within grace — the land is promised and given before the war is finished (32:33), and what the conditions govern is the certification of being "guiltless" and the peaceful enjoyment of the possession, not God watching first and showing grace afterwards; trust comes first, verification after. Another reads it as the counter-value structure of a covenant — the possession east of Jordan is exchanged for service west of it; the two tribes trade labour for land, and the penalty clause of verse 23 shows this is no unconditional gift but a binding arrangement. A third looks to the phrase "before the LORD," which in the Chinese base text's reckoning occurs at six places in this chapter (32:20, 21, 22, 27, 29, 32 — registered as the Chinese base text's count). Recounted on the KJV letter itself, the phrase "before the LORD" occurs eight times in six verses — 32:20, 21, 22 (three times), 27, 29, and 32 — the difference in count is registered in Endnote 4, and neither text is conformed to the other. On either reckoning the force of the observation stands: the first witness of the conditions is the LORD, and only then the congregation of Israel; this covenant's court sits first before God, then before men. This sermon registers only the points of view of the three readings and the text of the clauses in this chapter (conditions, guiltlessness, and penalty clause all quoted in full from the base text above), and does not decide the theological structure of the grant at the fact layer; the main text builds only on the three items the clauses state of themselves: the finishing point is the land subdued, the certification is guiltlessness before the LORD and before Israel, and breach is sin against the LORD. The warning sentence of 32:23 is preached in this sermon only within the bounds the verse itself sets: it characterizes breach of this covenant, and is neither softened into a general proverb nor enlarged into a specific punishment the verse does not name.

(New Testament Reading) The Lord Jesus' words on honesty run: "But let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay: for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil." (Matt 5:37, KJV, cross-reference quoted directly from the KJV at that place; reading layer) One comparison is registered at the reading layer: the covenant-making of this chapter carries the "yea" to the point where it can be verified — having said cross over, cross to the very end; having said we will not return home, take the brethren's receiving their inheritance as the term; the New Testament words gather the honesty of speech back into daily life — there is no need to prop up credibility with heavy oaths; yea is yea and nay is nay. Read together, the two places show the Bible's one consistent attitude toward "words": the weight of a word lies not in how loudly it is spoken, but in how far it is walked when it is done. The reading layer registers the comparison of the two texts only, and does not read the New Testament words back into the facts of this chapter's clauses; this chapter's clauses still rest on their own original wording.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of Part Three: three pledges of clarification (we ourselves will go ready armed at the front; we will not return home until every one of the brethren has his inheritance; of the possession west of Jordan we take nothing); two orders of settling (sheepfolds built for the cattle, cities built for the little ones, the women and children dwelling in fenced cities); four clauses of the covenant (all who are armed to cross over; until the enemies are driven out and the land is subdued; only after returning are they guiltless and the land theirs for a possession; breach is sin against the LORD and your sin will find you out); one sentence of permission (do that which hath proceeded out of your mouth; the building of cities and folds is approved as asked). And it is registered: from this part on, the pleading words of the request (found grace, let it be given) disappear, and conditional sentences take their place (if … then afterward … but if ye will not …) — a pleading has by now become a covenant, and the change of literary form is recorded as it stands.

### Part Four: Assent, Entrusting, and Land Given First (Numbers 32:25-33) (34:00-48:00)

(Fact) Verse 25, the first assent, registered: the children of Gad and the children of Reuben spake unto Moses, saying, Thy servants will do as my lord commandeth — the first sentence of assent is a full acceptance of the clauses, with no condition attached. Verse 26, the arrangements for those who remain, registered: our little ones, our wives, our flocks, and all our cattle shall be there in the cities of Gilead — the list of those remaining has four items (little ones, wives, flocks, and all our cattle), answering to the command to build cities and folds at verses 16 and 24. Verse 27, the assent to go out to war, registered in its substance: thy servants will pass over, every man armed for war, before the LORD to battle, as my lord saith — the scope of those who go out is "every man armed for war," with no exemption. Verses 28-30, Moses' entrusting, registered: Moses commanded concerning them Eleazar the priest, and Joshua the son of Nun, and the chief fathers of the tribes of the children of Israel, writing the conditions as a command to those who would execute them — if the children of Gad and the children of Reuben will pass with you over Jordan, every man armed to battle, before the LORD, and the land shall be subdued before you, then ye shall give them the land of Gilead for a possession; but if they will not pass over with you armed, they shall have possessions among you in the land of Canaan. Two items are registered: first, among those who receive the entrusting is Joshua — the man who would execute this covenant after Moses' death (Joshua was appointed successor in chapter 27, preceding-text layer); the conditions are thus placed on record with the next generation's leadership and do not lapse with Moses' departure. Second, the handling of breach is supplied here: not expulsion from the camp, but the cancelling of the possession east of Jordan and a possession given instead in Canaan among all the tribes — the one who breaks the covenant still has a possession, only not the portion he chose first. Verses 31-32, the second assent, registered in its substance: they answered, As the LORD hath said unto thy servants, so will we do; we will pass over armed before the LORD into the land of Canaan, that the possession of our inheritance on this side Jordan may be our's — with this the assent is finalized; the KJV's "our's" at 32:32 is preserved exactly as it stands in the anchor fetched from bible.com. Verse 33, the land given first, quoted verbatim in full: "And Moses gave unto them, even to the children of Gad, and to the children of Reuben, and unto half the tribe of Manasseh the son of Joseph, the kingdom of Sihon king of the Amorites, and the kingdom of Og king of Bashan, the land, with the cities thereof in the coasts, even the cities of the country round about." (32:33) The items registered one by one: first, the time of the grant is before the crossing and the fighting — the clauses are not yet performed when the land is already handed over. Second, the recipients are enlarged to two and a half tribes: half the tribe of Manasseh is not seen speaking in the request or the covenant-making (in 32:1-32 the speakers are only the two tribes), and this verse does not state the reason for its receiving land; only its standing in the same list of recipients is registered (its movements in Gilead stand in the final section, 32:39-42). Third, what is given is the kingdoms of the two kings, land and cities together — the defeat of Sihon and Og stands in chapter 21 (preceding-text layer); this verse registers the grant only and does not retell the battles.

(Argument) Look first, in this part, at Moses' act of placing the matter on record. Once the conditions are settled he does not stop at "settled"; before everyone's face he repeats the clauses to Eleazar, to Joshua, and to the chief fathers — which is to turn an oral agreement into a formal document with witnesses, executors, and a provision for breach. Why so solemn? Because the term of this covenant's performance lies beyond Moses' lifetime: the war will take years to fight, and the certification must be made after his death. If it rested only on the words at the camp gate, after three or five years, with the men changed and memories faded, who would still honour the account? By storing the covenant in the ears of the next generation's leadership, Moses settles in advance the disputes of the future, for the two tribes and for the rest alike. In every arrangement of ours that spans time — repayment, handover, a term of service — we should learn this move: an important agreement does not rest on good memory; it rests on having witnesses when it is made and someone to check it against when it is executed. Putting the account in the open does not wound feeling; it protects feeling — when the reckoning comes, what is reckoned against is the clauses, not men's hearts.

(Argument) Look then at the order in which the land is given: the land is given before performance. It is a risky move — give the land first, and if the men change their minds, what does the camp have to claim it back with? Moses dares to give this way because the constraint does not lie in a deed of land but in that heavier sentence: "But if ye will not do so, behold, ye have sinned against the LORD: and be sure your sin will find you out." He hands the right of recovery to God: the cost of breach is not as simple as losing a piece of land; it is sin finding a man out, and not being able to stand before the LORD. Nor have the two tribes gained an advantage by receiving the land first: every year of enjoyment afterwards reminds them that west of Jordan there is still an unfinished account. That is the normal shape of a faithful relationship: trust goes first, verification follows. When we are entrusted by others today — a sum of money, a post, a charge laid on us — we should recognize this account: trust given first is not a pass exempt from checking; it is the warrant by which you are to cross over to the very end in the place where no one watches.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Commanded" is ṣāwâ (H6680), Moses' commanding of Eleazar, Joshua, and the chief fathers at verse 28 — a verb this book commonly uses for laying down and handing on commands. "Possession" is ʾāḥuzzâ (H272), occurring in this KJV section at three places in its own renderings: 32:29 "for a possession," 32:30 "possessions among you," and 32:32 "the possession of our inheritance" — the same word as the request of the first part: within one chapter the possession is first requested (32:5), then promised (32:22), and finally granted (32:33); the word is the same and its rank changes three times, and this sermon registers the word and its places only, without expanding it. "The land of Gilead" serves in this part as the general name of the possession east of Jordan (32:26, 29); it bears the same name as the "land of Gilead" named alongside Jazer in the first part (32:1), and whether their extent is wholly the same this chapter does not state; only the sameness of name is registered.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The readings of half the tribe of Manasseh "receiving land without having requested it" are set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one holds that the grant to half the tribe of Manasseh is connected with the movements of the house of Machir in Gilead (32:39-40 states of itself that the children of Machir took Gilead and that Moses gave Gilead to Machir), so that its receiving land has another origin, in deeds of war; this chapter registers it only at the two places of grant and movement, and does not mix it with the two tribes' request. Another holds that the chapter's arrangement in any case gathers the apportioning east of Jordan into one place, the two tribes' negotiation being the representative scene of the whole eastern arrangement, with half the tribe of Manasseh included within the gathering and needing no separate record of negotiation. This sermon registers only the textual facts: the speakers of the request and the covenant-making are throughout only Gad and Reuben (32:1-32); half the tribe of Manasseh appears first at the grant (32:33) and next at its movements (32:39-42), with no record of speech in between; the main text builds no argument that fills this blank from either reading.

(Fact) This part closes by registering the account of Part Four: two assents (Thy servants will do as my lord commandeth; As the LORD hath said unto thy servants, so will we do); a list of those remaining in four items (little ones, wives, flocks, and all our cattle, remaining in the cities of Gilead); an entrusting to three parties (Eleazar, Joshua, and the chief fathers) with the clauses in two branches (if performed, the land of Gilead is given for a possession; if not performed, a possession in Canaan together with the tribes); one verse of grant (the kingdoms of the two kings, land and cities together, given to two and a half tribes, at a time before performance). And it is registered: up to this point in the whole chapter, Moses' words occupy the greater part of the speaking — rebuke, covenant-making, and entrusting — the two tribes' words stand in four sections (request, clarification, first assent, second assent), and the narrator's grant occupies only the final verse — the proportion of negotiation to grant is inverted, which itself shows that this chapter weighs words more than land.

### Part Five: The Lists of Built Cities — Possession Landing on Brick and Stone (Numbers 32:34-42) (48:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verses 34-36, Gad's building, registered: the children of Gad built Dibon, and Ataroth, and Aroer, and Atroth Shophan, and Jaazer, and Jogbehah, and Beth-nimrah, and Beth-haran — eight places in all — fenced cities, and folds for sheep; the order of the list and its registration follow the base text as they stand. One count is registered with its ground: at 32:35 the anchor reads "and Atroth, Shophan, and Jaazer, and Jogbehah" with a comma, but "Atroth Shophan" (in the Chinese base text a single four-character name) is one built place, and Gad's list is counted at eight on that basis, as the Chinese manuscript counts it; counted by commas alone it would read as nine, and the difference is registered here and in Endnote 4 rather than silently resolved. The hyphenated names are preserved as in the anchor: "Beth-nimrah" and "Beth-haran" (32:36). Read against the request list (32:3): Jazer reappears; Dibon recurs with its order changed; Ataroth in the request list appears in the building list as Ataroth, and beside it stands Atroth Shophan — the difference of wording between the two lists is registered by each list's own text (see Endnote 3). Verses 37-38, Reuben's building, registered: the children of Reuben built Heshbon, and Elealeh, and Kirjathaim, and Nebo, and Baal-meon (their names being changed), and Shibmah — six places in all; the base text notes at Nebo and Baal-meon that their names were changed, and records that they gave other names unto the cities which they builded — as to the renaming, this chapter records only that it happened, not what each new name was, and that blank is registered only. Note also the spelling: the request list (32:3) reads "Shebam," the building list (32:38) reads "Shibmah"; each list is registered by its own letter, and the two are not conformed. Verses 39-40, the house of Machir, registered: the children of Machir the son of Manasseh went to Gilead, and took it, and dispossessed the Amorite which was in it; and Moses gave Gilead unto Machir the son of Manasseh, and he dwelt therein — the origin of half the tribe of Manasseh's receiving land is supplied at this point: first the movement, then the grant, and the object of the grant is first the house of Machir. Verse 41, Jair, registered: Jair the son of Manasseh went and took the small towns thereof, and called them Havoth-jair — naming the land with his own name. Verse 42, Nobah, registered: Nobah went and took Kenath, and the villages thereof, and called it Nobah, after his own name — the whole chapter closes with a renaming. Recounted by this sermon's own hand: the building lists are eight places for Gad and six for Reuben, fourteen places in all; the movements in the Manasseh section are three (Machir takes Gilead, Jair takes the small towns, Nobah takes Kenath); the details stand in Endnote 4.

(Argument) These nine verses of lists at the chapter's end read most like a running account, yet they are the landing-point of the whole chapter: the words spoken in the thirty-three verses before have here become brick and stone. The two tribes said, settle the families first — the lists are the documentary proof of the settling: the cities are fenced, the folds are built, the women and children have a place to dwell, and only then can the men who fight get free to go. The lists also divide settling from escape for us: the man who escapes settles himself; the man who settles his family does so in order to go farther. We often hear it said, "When I have finished dealing with things at home, I will come and serve, I will come and help" — whether those words are true is judged not by their tone but by their order: the truly settled man, once settled, crosses over; the falsely settled man, once settled, produces further settling. The reason the two tribes' lists can remain in the Bible is that, having built their cities, they really crossed over (the retelling of the performance stands in the book of Joshua, following-text layer) — the lists are the warrant: their first sentence (build the cities first) was done, which makes men the readier to believe that their second sentence (cross over to the very end) would be done too. A man's credibility in words is accumulated exactly this way: redeem the small places first, and only then will anyone dare to trust the great ones.

(Argument) Notice also the renamings in the lists. Reuben gave other names to the cities he built, and Jair and Nobah named land with their own names — for those who receive land to give it a new name is the plainest mark of possession: from now on the land is called by my name. Set at the very end of the chapter, this carries a reminder: the moment a possession is received is precisely the moment a man most easily forgets how the land was received — forgets that it was given at a negotiating table, under conditions; forgets that across the river there is still a war; forgets that his name can be written on the land because he first pledged his name in a promise. The Bible records the renamings not wholly in celebration: it lets later readers — including the eastern tribes' own descendants — remember that beneath every new name there lies pressed down an old covenant. Later, the descendants of the tribes east of Jordan built an altar by Jordan and nearly stirred up civil war (Joshua chapter 22, following-text layer), and what was contested was precisely whether the men east of the river still acknowledged the covenant west of it. Every possession in our hands that is "already received" — a degree, a post, a family estate, a reputation — likewise has an old covenant pressed beneath it: who made it possible, what was promised to whom, and whom it is to serve. Let the name be remembered, and let the covenant be remembered too; the man who remembers only the name will not hold the land long.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Built" is bānâ (H1129); both building lists of verses 34-38 use this verb, in mutual reference with the "build cities" commanded at verses 16 and 24. The word rendered "small towns" at verse 41, of Jair's taking, has its original word-form marked pending verification in this sermon (see Endnote 5); only the base text's rendering is recorded. The composition of the place-name "Havoth-jair" (towns plus a personal name) is registered by the base text's rendering, and no assertion about its etymology is made.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The long-term gain or loss of receiving land east of Jordan is set out side by side without adjudication. The readings run in more than one direction: one holds that the eastern arrangement was a blessed completion — the two tribes received land according to their livelihood and performed the covenant faithfully, and Joshua later commended them face to face for keeping the charge and not forsaking their brethren for many days (Joshua 22:2-3, following-text layer), the possession east of Jordan being reckoned Israel's possession throughout the canonical retellings. Another holds that the east was the beginning of a slow loss — the tribes east of Jordan lay on the border, and in later generations were carried away by enemies before those west of it (the retellings in 2 Kings and 1 Chronicles, following-text layer); sitting down first in geography and losing ground first in history become a contrast, and the reading warns by it of the cost of settling early. A third looks to the symbolism of Jordan — in this book the river is both a geographical boundary and a boundary of communal identity, and the narrative of the whole canon after this chapter returns again and again to this river to test oneness (the building of the altar in Joshua chapter 22 being the nearest instance, following-text layer). This sermon registers only the points of view of the three readings and the existence of the later texts; the main text builds only on the three facts this chapter states of itself — grant, building, and renaming — and passes no judgment within this chapter on the eastern tribes' later generations.

(Fact) The whole chapter's data are gathered in closing, every item already counted (for the details see Endnote 4): the whole chapter has 42 verses; the five parts have, in order, 5 verses (32:1-5), 10 (32:6-15), 9 (32:16-24), 9 (32:25-33), and 9 (32:34-42) — 42 in all. The request list has nine places; the building lists have fourteen places (eight for Gad, six for Reuben); the movements are three (Machir, Jair, Nobah); the kingdoms are two (Sihon and Og); the recipients are two and a half tribes; the exceptions in the retelling of the oath are two men (Caleb and Joshua); the years of wandering are forty years; and "before the LORD," recounted on the KJV letter, occurs eight times in six verses (32:20, 21, 22 three times, 27, 29, 32) — the Chinese base text's count of six places (32:20, 21, 22, 27, 29, 32) is that base text's own reckoning, and the difference is registered in Endnote 4. The account of speeches: the two tribes speak in four sections (the request, 32:2-5; the clarification, 32:16-19; the first assent, 32:25-27; the second assent, 32:31-32), Moses in three movements as the Chinese manuscript's closing register counts them in its final reckoning (the rebuke, 32:6-15; the covenant-making, 32:20-24; the entrusting, 32:28-30 — with the receipt of the request in Part One standing outside the count), and the narrator in three sections (the origin, 32:1; the grant, 32:33; the building and the movements, 32:34-42). This chapter's joins with the chapters before and after: it follows the dividing of the spoil after the Midianite war in chapter 31 (preceding-text layer) — the war fought and the spoil divided, the apportioning of land is next laid on the table; and it leads into the review of the journeys in chapter 33 (following-text layer) — whose existence only is registered: after the prior apportioning east of Jordan, the whole camp's journeys will be recounted in full, and none of it is read back into this chapter.

## Devotional Excerpt

Moses finished stating the conditions and left only one closing sentence: "Build you cities for your little ones, and folds for your sheep; and do that which hath proceeded out of your mouth." (32:24, in the KJV order: building first, doing second; the Chinese base text places the doing first — see the Base-text notes) The two tribes spoke many words in this chapter: first a request, then a pledge, and at the end assent upon assent. What the Bible values is not which sentence was spoken most earnestly, but whether the words spoken afterwards became cities, became folds, became the footsteps of crossing the river. Examine today, too, the words that have proceeded out of your mouth: that promise to your parents, that pledge to your spouse, that word spoken to the Lord in prayer — do they still count? Words are not costly when they leave the mouth; they are costly when, after the days have passed, they still stand where they stood, waiting for you to redeem them. Moses did not ask them to swear a heavier oath; he only charged them to do according to what had proceeded out of their mouths. Do not hurry to speak new words; first do the old ones: a telephone call owed to someone, a small thing promised, a prayer you said you would begin — begin repaying from the smallest sentence; to repay the smallest first is also faithfulness. And remember the tenderness behind Moses' warning: sin will find a man out, and grace will too — every small promise you earnestly carry out today will one day find you out, and become the warrant by which you can stand. Do that which hath proceeded out of your mouth; words carried out are your true possession.

English (KJV-based):

"We will not return unto our houses, until the children of Israel have inherited every man his inheritance." (Num 32:18, KJV) The two tribes spoke many words in this chapter — a request, a pledge, and a promise repeated. Moses answered with one rule for them all: do that which hath proceeded out of your mouth. Look today at the words that have proceeded out of yours — the promise made to a parent, a spouse, or the Lord himself. Do not rush to speak new words; go and do the old ones, beginning with the smallest. Words kept are the only possession that cannot be taken from you.

"Jordan" in the KJV occurs six times in five verses (32:5; 32:19 twice, "on yonder side Jordan" and "on this side Jordan"; 32:21; 32:29; 32:32), answering to the five verses of the Chinese register. The list of those remaining has four items: little ones, wives, flocks, and all our cattle (32:26). The account of speeches: the two tribes speak in four sections as counted in the Chinese manuscript's note (request, clarification, first assent, second assent — 32:25-27 and 31-32 counting as two assents), Moses in three movements (rebuke, covenant-making, entrusting), and the narrator in three sections (origin, grant, building and movements); Part Four's closing register counts the two tribes' speaking in the same four sections.

The process: origin (32:1), request (32:2-5), rebuke (32:6-15), clarification (32:16-19), covenant-making (32:20-24), assent and entrusting (32:25-32), grant (32:33), building and movements (32:34-42) — eight steps, all completed within this chapter; only the performance itself (crossing over to help in the fighting, and its certification) does not stand in this chapter, its retelling standing in the book of Joshua (following-text layer); this chapter registers only the placing of the conditions on record, and does not extrapolate the details of the performance.
8. Blanks of this chapter, registered as blanks: Why half the tribe of Manasseh received land east of Jordan with the others, and whether it made a separate request, this chapter does not say (it appears only at the grant, 32:33, and at the movements, 32:39-42); the number of the two tribes' men who crossed over to fight, this chapter gives no figure; the new names after the renaming at 32:38 this chapter does not carry; Beon, a place in the request list, does not reappear in the building lists, and this chapter does not say why; the sequence in time between Machir's taking of Gilead and the two tribes' negotiation this chapter does not state (only the order of narration is registered: grant first, retelling of the movements after); the reaction of Eleazar and the chief fathers at the time of Moses' rebuke this chapter does not record; the concrete manner of executing the penalty clause of verse 23 this chapter does not set out (the handling of breach appears only at 32:30, in the entrusting clauses, in the form of a possession apportioned instead in Canaan). The above are registered as blanks only, and are not filled by extrapolation.

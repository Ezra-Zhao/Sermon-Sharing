## Synopsis (about 100 words)

Caesar issued a decree that all the people should be registered. Joseph took Mary, who was with child, back to Bethlehem. The child was born on a night when there was no room in the inn, wrapped and laid in a manger. Heaven first announced the good news of great joy to shepherds keeping watch in the fields at night, telling them to go and see the sign, and to hear glory to God and peace to men. When the shepherds saw it, they spread the word, and all who heard it wondered. This sermon looks at three things: how God comes to the lowly, how heaven's glory falls upon those who keep watch on earth at night, and how people who hear good news should rise, go and see, and go and tell.

## (00:00 - 06:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace on this Christmas Eve!

Tonight let us not begin with the lights, nor with the tree. Let us begin with a story of a night shift. A group of people were keeping watch in the fields, staffs in their hands, eyelids heavy, the flock bleating now and then in the darkness. Their work was unremarkable, their clothes smelled of sheep, and not one of their names was left behind. That night, heaven's most weighty news was not sent first into a palace, nor first into the temple. It was sent first to their ears. Why them? Tonight let us follow Luke, step by step, into that night.

Let us read together the key places in the text:

"And it came to pass in those days, that there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that all the world should be taxed." (Luke 2:1)

"And Joseph also went up from Galilee, out of the city of Nazareth, into Judaea, unto the city of David, which is called Bethlehem; (because he was of the house and lineage of David:) To be taxed with Mary his espoused wife, being great with child. And so it was, that, while they were there, the days were accomplished that she should be delivered. And she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn." (Luke 2:4-7)

"And there were in the same country shepherds abiding in the field, keeping watch over their flock by night. And, lo, the angel of the Lord came upon them, and the glory of the Lord shone round about them: and they were sore afraid. And the angel said unto them, Fear not: for, behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people. For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord. And this shall be a sign unto you; Ye shall find the babe wrapped in swaddling clothes, lying in a manger. And suddenly there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly host praising God, and saying, Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men." (Luke 2:8-14)

"And it came to pass, as the angels were gone away from them into heaven, the shepherds said one to another, Let us now go even unto Bethlehem, and see this thing which is come to pass, which the Lord hath made known unto us. And they came with haste, and found Mary, and Joseph, and the babe lying in a manger. And when they had seen it, they made known abroad the saying which was told them concerning this child. And all they that heard it wondered at those things which were told them by the shepherds. But Mary kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart. And the shepherds returned, glorifying and praising God for all the things that they had heard and seen, as it was told unto them." (Luke 2:15-20)

Let us pray: Lord, tonight open our ears, as You opened the ears of the shepherds. Our night is also deep, and our watch is also weary. Let the good news of great joy come into our hearts, that we may hear glory to You and peace to men. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (06:00 - 17:00) Part One: The Days of the Registration — A Great King's Decree and an Infant in a Small Town (2:1-7)

Luke does not begin with an angel. He begins with a government decree. There went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that all the world should be taxed. This was the Roman emperor counting heads, checking households, and fixing the tax base. In his eyes, the world was a table, and every person was a number on that table. That decree carried great force; it moved a whole empire. Yet it was precisely that decree that drove a carpenter from Galilee and his espoused wife, great with child, from Nazareth to Bethlehem. God's work often hides in folds like this: the people on the stage think they are directing the world, while below the stage God uses their directing to bring His promise, step by step, to the place where it must arrive. Micah had said centuries earlier: But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel. The emperor did not know that prophecy, and Joseph may not always have remembered it, but once the decree was issued, people took to the road. Brothers and sisters, look back over your own year. How much of it did you plan, and how much of it were you pushed along through? Being pushed along does not mean being abandoned by God. Sometimes the road you were least willing to take is exactly the road that brings you to the place of promise.

When they arrived in Bethlehem, the time of delivery also arrived. Luke writes with extreme restraint. There is no clamor of a delivery room, no details of a midwife, only three things: she brought forth her firstborn son, wrapped him, and laid him in a manger. Why? He adds the reason himself: because there was no room for them in the inn. Read that sentence slowly. It was not that there was no hospital, nor that the journey was too far. It was that there was no room in the inn. The fullest, busiest fullness of the human world pushed the most important guest to the side of a trough. A manger, in the original phatne [3], is a trough for feeding livestock. The angel later pointed out that very trough on purpose, as a sign. The sign was not a golden crown, nor a throne. It was a trough for feeding livestock. God's sign for people is low, down in the dust, so that poor night watchers too can recognize it and can walk in. If you imagine the Savior as One who can only be found in high places, change that tonight: the first place where He lay down was the place where livestock ate, a place no one contested and no one seized.

Notice Mary as well. She was not a passive vessel. She walked the whole road, gave birth along the way, and wrapped the child herself. Young, weary, far from home, she became a mother on a night in a strange place. She wrapped the child. That action could not be more ordinary, yet it was the first care that the Son of God received in this world. God did not send an angel to wrap the cloth. He entrusted that small task to an obedient woman. Many great spiritual things, when they land on earth, look just like this: a piece of cloth, a pair of hands, a willing person. If tonight you feel that what you can do is too small, remember: the hands that wrapped the cloth are also in the story of salvation.

The quiet of this section and the heavenly clamor of the next form a deliberate contrast. When the child was born, no one on earth applauded, yet heaven had already prepared a whole host of praise. The quieter the earth, the clearer it is that heaven's news was not made by men. Tonight's message is not news that the people of Bethlehem deliberated and produced. It is a proclamation sent down from heaven.

Look once more at Joseph. He did not speak a single word on this whole road, and in the text he is silent throughout. But a silent person is not a person without faith. He received Mary, he accepted the command to be registered, he traveled for days, he arrived, and then he had to arrange a place for the birth. There was no room in the inn, and he left no recorded word of complaint. He simply settled the child in the place where the child could be settled. Brothers and sisters, the church has a large group of people like this: they do not preach, they do not lead singing, they do not stand in front. They simply, quietly, bring their families to the place where they need to arrive, wrap the child, and keep daily life steady. God sees such people. That night's angel did not appear to Joseph, yet every step in the fulfillment of the promise bears his footprints. Tonight, let such brothers and sisters hear this well too: God counts your silence, and heaven remembers the road you led.

Notice also this place, Bethlehem. The city of David — Luke points it out twice. The Savior was born in the king's city, but not in the king's palace; born in the place the prophecy specified, yet lying in a trough that the prophecy did not describe in detail. Not one word of God falls empty, but the way it is fulfilled often surprises people. If you too hold in your hand a word of God, a promise of God, and the way it is fulfilled differs from the picture you drew, do not rush to say that God has forgotten. Bethlehem was right, and the manger was right too. It was only that the way it was right was lower, quieter, and closer to the poor than people had imagined.

## (17:00 - 30:00) Part Two: Night Watchers in the Fields — Why Was the Good News of Great Joy Given to Them First (2:8-14)

The camera turns, from the small town to the fields. There were in the same country shepherds abiding in the field, keeping watch over their flock by night. Keeping watch in turns means working in shifts through the night, one watch after another, the kind of work where you do not sleep. Shepherds ranked very low in the society of that time: they were in the fields all year, could not keep many rules of cleanness on time, and the weight of their testimony was often looked down upon. They were the group most cut off from news; whatever happened in the city, they were always the last to know. Yet that night the order was reversed: they knew first.

Lo, the angel of the Lord came upon them, and the glory of the Lord shone round about them. Glory, in the original doxa [4], is that overwhelming brilliance and honor when God appears. What the night watchers experienced first was not joy. It was fear: they were sore afraid. When people truly meet the things of heaven, they first know their own smallness and their own unworthiness. That reaction is real. The angel's first words were not rebuke but comfort: Fear not. The next sentence is the heart of Christmas: I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people. To bring good news, in the original euangelizomai [1], is the verbal form of the word for preaching the gospel. The word gospel was spoken first in the fields to shepherds, and only then entered the city, the temple, and all nations. The angel did not say, I bring you a piece of news. He said good tidings of great joy. The greatness of the joy lies not in the spectacle but in whom it concerns: it shall be to all people. It is not one family's happy event, nor one nation's privilege. It is for all people. The first lesson the night watchers heard was that this news is far larger than their flock, far larger than Bethlehem, and would be carried to the ends of the earth.

For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord. Saviour, in the original soter [2]. Hear this order clearly: first it says that He was born, then it says who He is. Unto you is born — the birth of the Savior was first of all for you. The angel did not first teach a set of doctrines. He first connected the child with the people listening: this child has to do with you. He is the Saviour, the One who saves people from sin, from fear, and from death; He is Christ the Lord, the One whom God established and anointed. A baby lying in a manger, yet the titles the angel gave Him were greater one than another. That is precisely the tension of Christmas: the title is extremely great, and the lying place is extremely low. If you look only at the lying place, you will miss Him; if you hear only the titles, you will think He is far from you. The angel gives you both together: look at the sign, and recognize Him.

What is the sign? Ye shall find the babe wrapped in swaddling clothes, lying in a manger. That is the sign. God giving a sign is grace: He does not make people guess. He gives people a mark that can be verified. Later the shepherds arrived at the manger and saw exactly what the angel had said, and their faith landed on solid ground. Brothers and sisters, the sign God gives us today is just as concrete: His word is written clearly, and what He has done is placed there, for us to go and see, to compare, and to believe. Faith is not jumping with closed eyes. It is recognizing the sign with open eyes.

Then heaven opened: suddenly there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly host praising God and saying, Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men. Notice that this host sang only two lines, one upward and one downward. The upward line, glory to God, is because He took the initiative to come to earth; the downward line, peace to men, is because the glorious God was willing to come among men. Glory and peace were joined that night: God received glory, men received peace, and standing in the middle was the child in the manger. The cross, later, would sing those two lines to the end. Tonight let us first learn this tune: every time we give glory to God and not to ourselves, the earth gains a measure more of the peace that comes from Him.

Notice also that after singing, the heavenly host left. The angel finished announcing the news, the host finished singing the song, and heaven withdrew its team. The remaining road had to be walked by the shepherds themselves. God gives visions, and He also gives quiet; He gives a sign, and He also leaves a journey. Many people wait for a voice from heaven every day before they are willing to set out. The shepherds heard for only one night, and walked for a lifetime.

Consider again that line about peace. On earth peace, good will toward men — this peace is not empty talk about the absence of war, nor a feeling of being in a good mood. The root of this peace is that the wall between God and men has begun to be torn down: the Savior was born precisely in order later to bring men and God back into reconciliation. The shepherds that night may not have understood the cross, but the peace they heard would one day be paid in full at Calvary. When we sing of a silent night today, we are not singing about a quiet evening. We are singing about the beginning of reconciliation: God reached out His hand first, men received peace first, and only then do men learn to be reconciled with one another and to forgive one another. If this year there is a relationship of yours that has long been stuck, the peace of Christmas is not sung only to heaven. It is sung to that phone call you have never made, and to that apology you have never spoken.

Do not miss the direction of those words either: on earth peace, good will toward men. The peace travels from God toward men, carried by His good will. Behind the King James wording stands a Greek phrase that most modern versions read as "peace to those on whom his favor rests" — the people He delights in (so also the Chinese Union Version from which the Chinese original of this sermon preached; see note [6]). Under either wording the point is the same: this peace bears the character of favor. It is not wages men have earned, but a gift God delights to give. The shepherds had paid nothing for this news, and their night-watch wages did not include this item. It was received freely. A grateful heart comes from here: I was not at the front of the list; I was only keeping watch at night, yet grace arrived at my ears first. Anyone who turns Christmas into his own achievement cannot sing this tune; anyone who knows he has received freely — before his mouth has opened, his heart has already knelt.

## (30:00 - 39:00) Part Three: They Came with Haste, and They Spread the Word — How Those Who Saw Responded (2:15-20)

As the angels were gone away from them into heaven. That sentence is the watershed of the whole chapter: heaven's part ends, and earth's response begins. The shepherds said one to another, Let us now go even unto Bethlehem, and see this thing which is come to pass, which the Lord hath made known unto us. First, they said to one another. Response is not one person being moved in silence. It is companions reminding one another and urging one another. Tonight, when you have heard, please also turn to the person beside you and say: let us go and see. Second, they said they would go and see this thing which is come to pass. They believed that what the angel said had already come to pass, and now they would go and see it with their own eyes. Faith has ears, and it also has feet: having heard, it must also walk over there.

And they came with haste. With haste is the speed word of this section. Before dawn, the flock was still in the fields, the road was not near, yet they did not schedule it for tomorrow, did not wait for a shift change, did not first go back to sleep. Some things can wait. Seeing the Lord cannot wait. Think about it: how many times have we placed important things behind unimportant ones — prayer can wait, confession can wait, reconciliation can wait, seeing the Lord can wait too. The shepherds teach us another ordering: when heaven's word arrives, the feet move at once. Haste is not panic. It is putting the order right.

And they found Mary, and Joseph, and the babe lying in a manger. Found means they searched and found. The sign matched: wrapped in cloth, lying in the manger, not one word different from what the angel had said. And when they had seen it, they made known abroad the saying which was told them concerning this child. Those who had seen naturally became those who told. They did not first go to take classes, nor first receive an office. What they spread was not how marvelous their own experience was. What they spread was the saying told them concerning this child. The starting point of preaching the gospel has always been like this: spread, truthfully, the word concerning Him. And all they that heard it wondered at those things which were told them by the shepherds. Wonder is the first step. The people of the city were first amazed, and in that amazement was buried the faith that would come later.

One person among the crowd was different: But Mary kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart. The shepherds spread outward; Mary kept inward. One outward, one inward — both are right responses. Some words must be spread at once, and some things must be weighed again and again in the heart. As a mother, she, more than anyone, needed time to understand who it was she held in her arms. The church also needs these two kinds of people: shepherds who hurry to tell, and a Mary who quietly keeps things in her heart. Tonight, whichever kind you are, be that kind first, and do not look down on one another.

At last, the shepherds returned. Returned where? To the fields, to the flock, to the night shift. They did not stay by the manger and make a home there, nor did they from then on leave daily life behind. They carried glory and praise back to the place where they had originally kept watch. For all the things that they had heard and seen, as it was told unto them, they returned, glorifying and praising God. On the road back, they were already a different group of people: their posts had not changed, but their hearts had changed; their watches had not changed, but now there was a song. This is also for us: when Christmas is over, we all have to return — to the company, to the kitchen, to the hospital ward, to our own watch. The question is not whether you can stay beside the manger. The question is whether, when you return, there is a song in your mouth and glory given to God in your heart.

There is another layer, about the people in the city who heard. All they that heard it wondered at those things which were told them by the shepherds. And after wondering? The text does not record that they followed, nor that they did not believe. It records only that they wondered. Wonder is an open door: some walk from wonder into belief, and some wonder and then close the door and go on with the festival. Every Christmas, tens of millions of people in the world wonder for a moment at this story: what a beautiful night, what a moving baby. Then the lights go out, the gifts are unwrapped, and life goes on as before. Brothers and sisters, wonder does not save people. Seeing saves people. Being moved does not count as a response. Setting out counts as a response. After tonight's sermon ends, will you be a person in the city who wonders, or a person who sets out and finds? The shepherds have already walked the road clearly for us: hear, hurry to go, see, spread the word, return, give glory. Six steps in one night, none of them hard. What is hard is whether the first step is willing to be taken.

May the Lord also remember tonight the families of every night watcher seated here. When the shepherds returned, they surely told that night to their wives, to their children, to their old fathers. One person's encounter becomes a whole family's story. Your encounter is the same: what you hear and see tonight, when you go home and tell it, becomes seed planted in your family's hearts. Do not belittle a stretch of sharing at the dinner table, nor the one telling of the manger to a child before sleep. The angel told it once, and the shepherds told it for a lifetime; the one time we tell it to our families may be the first watch of their faith.

Finally, let us set that night beside our own night. The night we keep may have no wind from the fields, but the weariness is the same: keeping watch for parents, keeping watch for children, keeping watch for a job, keeping watch for an answer that has not yet come. The angel's word did not first ask the night watchers whether they were tired. He directly delivered the great joy: Fear not. Tonight those words must also fall upon every one of us here. What you fear, He knows. The people you keep watch over, He holds in mind. The dawn you wait for, He is already on the road toward it. The child in the manger, when He had grown up, said that the gospel of the kingdom would be preached to the poor. His word has not changed from the first night: those who hear first are always those who, in the night, are still willing to lift up their heads. May our way of keeping watch be different after tonight: the staff still in our hands as before, our eyes still watching the flock as before, but in our ears a word of good news of great joy, and in our hearts a song that the heavenly host once sang.

One more word is left for those who have brought children tonight. What the shepherds found was a babe lying in a manger. A child's knowing of the Lord also begins from a low place like this: do not wait until he has grown up to speak of faith. First, at a height he can reach, introduce the Lord to him. A story before sleep, a prayer before a meal, a short song while keeping the year's watch — these are a child's version of the manger: not high, not far, within reach of a hand. If we place the Lord too high, a child will think He belongs only to adults; if we speak of Him truthfully and closely, then in a child's small heart there will first be a sign. May every family that goes out from here tonight set up a little Bethlehem at home: a fixed corner, a fixed passage of Scripture, a family's fixed prayer. Many years later, when the child has gone far away, that sign will still be there, and he will recognize the road back home.

## (39:00 - 43:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: people still keeping watch. You keep watch outside a hospital ward, outside a child's door, over the accounts of a business, in a relationship where you cannot see dawn. The angel's word is spoken to you first: Fear not. The good news of great joy shall be to all people, and you are among them. He has not left you out. The ones He seeks first are those who keep watch at night.

The second kind: people who feel they are ranked last. Education, accent, status, work — all make you feel that good news will not reach you first. The sign of the manger was set up for people like you: the threshold is as low as dust, so low that you too can enter. Tonight do not stand outside the door guessing. First come with haste and see this thing which is come to pass.

The third kind: people whose inn is full. Your schedule is full, your heart is full, your phone is full — there is simply no room. What Christmas asks is not whether you have time to attend an activity. It is whether there is still an empty place in your heart to set down this Savior. Clear out one place this year. Even if it is as small as a manger, He is willing to lie down there.

The fourth kind: people who hear and wonder, but do not set out. Every year you hear the Christmas story, every year you are moved for a moment, and then you return to the way things were. The shepherds walked one step more than wonder: they came with haste. Tonight here is a concrete setting out for you: spread the word concerning this child to one person, or go and complete a reconciliation you have delayed for a long time.

The fifth kind: people who keep things in their hearts, like Mary. This year you have experienced too much, and for the moment you cannot say it clearly. Do not force yourself to produce a complete testimony at once. To keep all these things in your heart and ponder them repeatedly is itself worship. God does not hurry you. He stays with you as you slowly come to understand.

The sixth kind: people who must return to the night shift. The festival will disperse, the lights will go out, and you will return to your original post. Learn the way the shepherds returned: glorifying God and praising Him. The post does not change, but the song has changed, and your daily life becomes the scene of witness.

## (43:00 - 45:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Tonight we have walked a whole road: a decree came down to a small town, a child was born in a manger, good news arrived first in the fields, night watchers hurried to see, and then returned with a song. The shepherds heard it first — this was not an accident. This is the heart of God: His good news of great joy comes first to lowly people who keep watch.

Let us pray together: God, to whom glory in the highest belongs, thank You that, while we were keeping watch at night, You were not silent. You caused a Savior to be born for us, who is Christ the Lord; You placed the sign where we could reach it. Lord, remove our fear, hasten our feet with haste before You, and teach us, on the road back, to give glory to You and to bring peace to men. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] euangelizomai: Greek εὐαγγελίζω (G2097), to announce good news, to proclaim the gospel; in Luke 2:10 the angel's words "I bring you good tidings of great joy" use precisely this word, and the proclamation of the gospel came first to the shepherds; for the meaning see BDAG: announce good news, proclaim the gospel. The second part of this manuscript, "the gospel was spoken first in the fields," develops its exposition from observation of this word.

[2] soter: Greek σωτήρ (G4990), Savior, deliverer; Luke 2:11, "unto you is born ... a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord"; for the meaning see BDAG: savior, deliverer, preserver. This manuscript's statement that "the Savior is the One who saves people from sin and fear" is a sermonic exposition based on this word and on this Gospel's theme of salvation.

[3] phatne: Greek φάτνη (G5336), manger, feeding trough for livestock; it appears three times, in Luke 2:7, 12, 16, and the angel made lying in a manger the sign; for the meaning see BDAG: manger, feeding trough. This manuscript's "the sign is low, down in the dust" is a sermonic development based on this word and on its repetition in the narrative.

[4] doxa: Greek δόξα (G1391), glory, radiance; Luke 2:9, "the glory of the Lord shone round about them," and 2:14, the heavenly host sang "Glory to God in the highest"; for the meaning see BDAG: glory, radiance, majesty. This manuscript's "glory and peace were joined" develops its exposition from the juxtaposition of the two lines in 2:14.

[5] On the registration and the Bethlehem promise: Luke 2:1-5 records that Caesar Augustus issued a decree for registration, and that Joseph went up to Bethlehem because he belonged to the house of David; this manuscript's connection to the prophecy of Bethlehem in Micah 5:2 is cross-exegesis, a Scripture-to-Scripture cross-reference at the sermonic level. This is noted here expressly; it does not claim that Luke's original text directly quotes that verse.

[6] On "good will toward men" (Luke 2:14): the KJV follows the Greek reading εὐδοκία in the nominative — "good will toward men." The better-attested reading is the genitive εὐδοκίας, "(peace) to men of His good pleasure," which underlies modern renderings such as "those on whom his favor rests" (ESV; similarly NIV) and the Chinese Union Version's "men He delights in," on which the Chinese original of this sermon preached. The body of this manuscript now states the difference openly. Its exposition — peace as unmerited favor, not wages — rests on the genitive reading, and the KJV's wording does not contradict it: in the KJV too, peace flows from God's good will toward men, not from men's merit. This is stated expressly.

## Devotional Excerpt

On that night, the first to hear the good news were not priests serving in the temple or officials on duty in a palace, but shepherds keeping watch over their flock in the fields. Their hands carried the smell of sheep, and their eyes were tired from the night shift, yet the glory of the Lord shone around them, and the angel said to them: do not be afraid, I bring you good news of great joy. If you are keeping watch today, beside a hospital bed, outside a child's room, over work that shows no fruit, or through a season that seems never to reach dawn, remember that God does not place night watchers last. A Savior has been born to you, and the sign is placed within your reach: not high, not far, but low, like a manger. You do not need to make yourself presentable before you are worthy to hear the gospel. Like the shepherds, hear it, hurry to see it, tell what was said about this child, and then return to your post giving glory to God. Tonight, may this great joy come to you first, and may His peace rest on you and your household.

Learn also from the pace of the shepherds. They heard, and they hurried. They saw, and they spread the word. They returned, and they glorified God on the road back to ordinary work. Nothing about their post changed that night, yet everything about their hearts had changed, because the Savior who lay in the manger had stepped into their darkness and would never leave it again. So let this good news quicken your steps as well. Go and see what the Lord has done. Tell someone what was spoken about this child. Then walk back into your daily watch with a song, giving glory to God, until the long night itself begins to shine with the peace He promised to His people.

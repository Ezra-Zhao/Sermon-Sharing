# Offering Even the Bronze Mirror

Preacher: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries

Status: Reviewed · Published. Passed cross-review on 2026-10-23 (independent Gemini review + two rounds of self-review); published in Sermon-Sharing. English version translated from the Chinese original.

Review: Passed cross-review on 2026-10-23 (independent Gemini review + two rounds of self-review), with minor fixes per Group Y review record.

Scripture: Exodus 38:1-31 (KJV)
Theme: Offering Even the Bronze Mirror — The Bronze Altar, the Laver, the Courtyard, and the Full Accounting of Materials
Connection: The previous chapter (Exodus 37) records Bezalel making the ark, the table, the lampstand, and the golden altar of incense, and the furnishings inside the holy place have taken shape; this chapter moves the camera out into the courtyard, and leaves a counted, audited total account for the whole work.

## Abstract

Exodus 38 first makes the altar of burnt offering, then the laver — noting that the laver and its base were made from the bronze mirrors of the women who served at the entrance of the tent of meeting — then records the courtyard with its hangings, pillars, and screen, and finally closes the chapter with the totals and uses of the gold, the silver, and the bronze. This sermon follows that line: from sacrifice and washing in the courtyard, we watch a company of people hand over even the objects of their adornment; and from the publicly reckoned account, we see how an offering is recorded item by item and put to use place by place.

## I. Entering the Courtyard, We Meet the Bronze Altar First (00:00-08:00)

Brothers and sisters, in the last chapter the camera stayed inside the holy place: the ark, the mercy seat, the table, the lampstand, the golden altar of incense — piece after piece of gold, piece after piece close to the centre. In chapter 38 the camera moves one step outward, into the courtyard, and before we have found our footing we run into an altar. The first verse says, in the KJV: "And he made the altar of burnt offering of shittim wood" (Exod 38:1). In the order of the tabernacle, this altar is the first great thing an ordinary Israelite could approach. A person brought an offering in through the courtyard gate and, before entering the holy place, came first to the altar. Sin had to be dealt with here; sacrifices were offered here; blood flowed here. The way into the tabernacle does not bypass the altar and go straight in to draw near; the way begins at the altar. Let us make that clear first, lest we read the laver, the courtyard, and the account that follow as a craftsman's inventory and forget that they all gather around one thing: how sinful people are brought before a holy God.

The making of this bronze altar is recorded in concrete detail. The altar was made of acacia wood, five cubits long, five cubits wide, foursquare, and three cubits high. On its four corners he made horns, joined to the altar in one piece. He overlaid the altar with bronze — its body, its horns, and its sides. He made its utensils: the pots, the shovels, the basins, the flesh hooks, and the firepans, all of bronze. Below the rim of the altar he made a bronze grating of network, reaching halfway up the altar. He cast four rings for the four corners of the bronze grating, as holders for the poles. He made the poles of acacia wood, overlaid them with bronze, and put them into the rings on the sides of the altar to carry it. The altar was made hollow, with boards. This passage corresponds to the pattern given in Exodus 27:1-8: chapter 37 reports the execution of the holy place furnishings, and the opening of chapter 38 reports the execution of the courtyard furnishings. The word was spoken on the mountain, and the hands made it below the mountain just as it was spoken; read side by side, the dimensions, materials, and construction agree point by point. This is not literary waste; it is the proper duty of an execution narrative — to let later readers verify that what was made is precisely what was commanded.

In preaching this altar, one structural observation may be set down lightly here; this chapter does not expand it, it serves only as a contrast. There are two altars in the tabernacle: this bronze altar, standing in the courtyard, large in size, overlaid with bronze, receiving burnt offerings and the various sacrifices; and the golden altar of the previous chapter, standing in the holy place near the veil, small in size, overlaid with gold, burning incense. Different materials, different positions, different offices — Scripture does not merge them into one. The bronze altar is outside, the golden altar inside; one handles the sacrificial animals, the other holds up the smoke of incense. This contrast helps us read the theological order of the space, but it must not be pressed too far: the text does not say bronze is baser than gold, nor does it ask us to match every material to a virtue. We keep only what the text gives: near the courtyard gate, there is first an altar. Everyone who would draw near to God must pass here first, must first acknowledge his own condition, must first let the place of the substitute offering be established, and only then speak of going further in.

That the altar was hollow, made with boards, should not be read too quickly either. It was hollow in order to be light, in order to be carried, in order to travel with the tabernacle on the wilderness road. The four rings and the poles have appeared again and again in this book — the ark has them, the table has them, the incense altar has them, and the bronze altar has them too. Portability tells us this altar was not one of those fixed, immovable hulks in an Egyptian temple; it belonged to a people on the move. Set up in the camp today, tomorrow the camp breaks and the altar must be lifted and follow the pillar of cloud forward. From the very beginning, worship in the wilderness was not a memorial shut behind settled doors; it was a presence travelling with the people. People carried the altar as they walked, and the altar reminded them at every step: wherever you arrive, first set up the place of sacrifice, first put your fellowship with God in order, and only then pitch camp and live your life.

That the horns were joined to the altar in one piece is likewise a construction repeated on several furnishings. The horn was not a decoration nailed on afterwards; it was one with the altar. In the Old Testament context the horns of the altar were among its most sacred parts: the blood of the sin offering was put on the horns of the altar, and a fugitive once caught hold of the horns of the altar. Other texts ground those connections; this chapter only records how the horns were made, and we do not expand those stories here. We hold only to the main line: the craftsman made even the horns according to the pattern, without shrinking or omitting. At the very first furnishing of the courtyard, faithfulness still uses one measuring line. The same measure Bezalel used in the last chapter on the golden mercy seat, he uses in this chapter on the heavy bronze altar. Furnishings have sizes, posts have insides and outsides, but the measure is one. Many people are willing to be careful with the work at the centre and careless with the heavy work in the courtyard, as if what everyone can see and what is not finely wrought does not matter. This chapter puts the bronze altar first in the chapter, as if to say: the work of the outer court must also be done as commanded; the utensils that get carried, burned, and dirtied must also render their account before God.

## II. Offering Even the Bronze Mirror: The Laver and the Women at the Entrance (08:00-24:00)

Verse 8 is only one short sentence, yet it is the place where the whole chapter makes us stop. The KJV reads: "And he made the laver of brass, and the foot of it of brass, of the lookingglasses of the women assembling, which assembled at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation" (Exod 38:8). This one sentence presses two things together: the laver, and where its bronze came from. The laver was bronze, its base was bronze, and this bronze was no bronze from elsewhere — it was the women's mirrors, melted down. The laver stood between the tent and the bronze altar, and before serving, the priests were to wash their hands and feet there, as Leviticus and other parts of Exodus command; this chapter records only the making of the laver. Its position is between the altar and the tent, and its order is there too: first past the altar, then washing, then in to serve. Sacrifice and cleansing stand in the courtyard one before the other, so that those who serve remember every day, in their bodies, that they do not enter on the strength of their own natural cleanness.

First, the mirror itself, lest we imagine today's glass mirror. Mirrors in the ancient Near East were mostly polished metal surfaces; a bronze mirror was bronze polished bright enough to show a face. In that world a mirror was an article of a woman's adornment — valuable, personal, used every day. A person who offers gold offers from what is stored up; a person who offers a mirror offers from daily life. What you use when you rise in the morning, what you look into before you go out — now handed over, melted down, made into a laver, and never again will your own face be seen in that mirror. The text does not record how the women felt as they offered their mirrors; it records no tears and no struggle, and in preaching we will not supply their inner states for them. That is restraint at the level of fact. But the text deliberately records where the material came from, and that deliberateness is itself the narrative's intention: where the bronze of a holy vessel came from, Scripture refuses to pass over vaguely. Other things can be recorded simply by what was made; when we reach the laver, we must be told whose things made it. That deliberateness is where the title of this sermon comes from: offering even the bronze mirror.

Set this verse back into the whole book, and a narrative arc of women and ornaments can be seen. This belongs to structural observation, and its level must be named. In chapter 32, at the making of the golden calf, Aaron told the people to break off the golden earrings, and the people broke them off and brought them to Aaron, who cast them into a calf. That is the book's most painful scene: ornaments taken off, and made into an idol. In chapter 33, God commanded the people to take off their ornaments, and the people, hearing the distressing word, mourned, and no one put on his ornaments — a taking-off in grief after sin. In chapter 35, at the gathering of materials for the tabernacle, everyone whose heart was willing came with an offering: men and women brought gold objects, earrings, and rings, and the women spun yarn with their hands and brought what they had spun, and goats' hair. That was a willing offering; gold ornaments were used for the right purpose for the first time. By Exodus 38:8 the arc reaches a quiet end point: the women hand over their bronze mirrors, and the mirrors no longer reflect faces; they are turned into the laver at which priests wash their hands. Four times things are taken off, four times handed over, and the direction grows clearer each time. A gold earring can be cast into a calf, and it can also be brought into the tabernacle; a mirror can reflect only oneself, and it can also be turned into a holy vessel. The thing itself is neither good nor evil; the good and the evil lie in where it is handed over and by whom it is used. This layer comes to us from the narrative structure; it is not a lesson read directly out of a single verse, and in preaching the distinction must be kept.

Notice also who gave the mirrors. The text says, the women who served at the entrance of the tent of meeting. The wording of this service is discussed in scholarship; we set the views out honestly and reach no conclusion — the detailed lexicography is in the endnotes. Here we take only the picture the surface of the text gives us: a company of women gathered at the entrance of the tent, holding some place of service there. Women appear publicly only rarely in the Old Testament, and this verse suddenly names them: the laver was made from their mirrors, so that this company at the entrance is bound forever to the laver used every day in the courtyard. Every time a priest washed his hands and feet, he walked up to a laver made out of mirrors. No one would mention their names again — the text does not leave a single name — but their mirrors remained in the daily life of the ritual. This is a very Exodus way of remembering: Pharaoh's daughter has a name, the midwives have names, but these women have no names, only mirrors. Only mirrors are enough. Sometimes God remembers a person not by carving her name on a monument, but by placing the thing she handed over in the most-used spot in His house, to be used generation after generation.

Nor should this verse be preached as a rule that women ought to give up all adornment; that would press one verse into a law it never speaks. The text does not forbid looking into a mirror, nor does it praise carelessness; it records one concrete offering: at the moment when the tabernacle was being built, materials were scarce, and everyone brought what they had, a company of women handed over the most personal bronze object they owned. When preaching takes up this layer, it must say it precisely: the question is never whether a mirror may be kept, but whether, when the house of God has need and when worship needs preparing, there is something in our hands that, because it is too personal, too daily, too hard to part with, never makes it onto the list of what we offer. Gold can be offered, because the gold sits in a box; a little time can be offered, because there is plenty of time left; but at the mirror's level, what is offered is the small dignity we use every day, the very tool by which we set ourselves in order. To offer even the bronze mirror is to offer not what is valuable, but what is personal. This layer is the sermon's appropriation, not a direct command of the text; keeping the distinction is what makes the preaching steady.

Return now to the laver itself. A mirror turned into a laver: the change of shape is also a change of use. Once it faced inward and reflected one's own face; now it holds water facing outward and washes the hands of others. The bronze of adornment has become a vessel of cleansing; the bronze that showed a person has become the bronze of service. This contrast is a matter of the vessels themselves, set before us by the text: the laver's use is stated clearly elsewhere in the text, and the mirror's use was understood by everyone of that age. Placing the two side by side is a legitimate act of reading. What a person hands over into God's hand, God often does not discard; He melts it down and recasts it, and gives it another use: from being for oneself, to being for others; from reflecting one's own face, to washing the hands of those who serve. The thing in our hands we can least bear to give, if we are willing to hand it over, will most likely not simply vanish; it will change its master and its use, and be used every day in the house of God. The bronze is still the same bronze, but the mirror has become a laver. What is offered does not disappear for nothing; Scripture here, in the quietest way, casts that sentence into bronze.

Finally, the place: the entrance. Where these women served was before the entrance of the tent, at the thoroughfare where people came and went through the gate of the courtyard and the door of the tent. They were not in the Most Holy Place, and they were not somewhere invisible; they were at the entrance. The entrance is where people are welcomed and seen off, where offerings and worshippers come in, and the place most easily overlooked. Much of a church's service happens at the entrance: those who open the door, those who prepare, those who tidy up, those who stand there and catch everything. Exodus 38:8 places the most moving stroke of the whole chapter on this company at the entrance, and places their mirrors between the altar and the tent. It is as if Scripture wants us to see on purpose: the most-used vessel of cleansing in God's house came from the most personal offering of those who served at the entrance. Do not despise the entrance, and do not despise the little mirror in your hand. You think it is only enough to reflect one face; God can use it to wash the hands of generation after generation of those who serve.

## III. The Courtyard: A Circle of Hangings That Sets Apart (24:00-42:00)

From verse 9 the text turns from the furnishings to the courtyard itself: he made the court, that is, the hangings of the court. This passage corresponds to Exodus 27:9-19; the preaching takes only the main line and does not guess at symbolism for each side. The main line is this: on the south side, hangings of fine twined linen, one hundred cubits, with twenty pillars and twenty bronze sockets, and the hooks and fillets of the pillars of silver. On the north side likewise one hundred cubits, twenty pillars, twenty bronze sockets, hooks and fillets of silver. On the west side fifty cubits, ten pillars and ten sockets. On the east side also fifty cubits: fifteen cubits on one side, three pillars and three sockets, fifteen cubits on the other side, three pillars and three sockets, and in the middle the gate of the court, its screen twenty cubits wide. All the hangings of the court were five cubits high. The pillars were joined with silver fillets, their capitals overlaid with silver, and their sockets were of bronze. The screen for the gate was of blue, purple, and scarlet yarns and fine twined linen, worked by an embroiderer. All the pins of the tabernacle and of the court were of bronze. A circle of fine linen hangings marked out a piece of ground, one hundred cubits by fifty, from the wilderness sand. What was marked out was holy ground; outside it remained the camp and the wilderness.

Look first at the order in the pairing of materials. The hangings were fine linen, the pillar sockets bronze, the hooks and fillets silver, the capitals overlaid with silver. This matches exactly the uses of silver and bronze in the account that follows: silver was cast into the sockets of the tabernacle, and bronze made the court's sockets and pins. Each material has its place; nothing was grabbed at random. The fine linen hangings, five cubits high — somewhat taller than a person — meant that standing outside the court you could not see the altar and the laver within, and standing inside, the camp outside was screened away. Separation here is not an abstract word; it is a circle of hangings that can be measured, touched, and counted. People often preach being set apart as a mood, as if feeling different in the heart were enough. The separation of Exodus has a boundary: it has length, width, height, and a gate. The boundary is not there to boast that we are inside; it is there so that worship has its proper order, so that what is holy belongs to the holy, so that the way in is only through one gate, and so that every approach passes through the places it should pass through.

The gate screen is the only part of this whole circle of hangings made with embroidery and with the blue, purple, and scarlet colours. The hangings of the court are white fine linen throughout; only the gate screen has colour, and embroidery. This contrast is a fact the text itself displays: the entrance is marked out on purpose. A person could not slip in under the hangings or cut his own opening in the wall; he could only enter by the gate. The screen of the gate was made beautiful not because God loves pomp, but because the entrance itself is a great matter: the one who enters here must first come to the bronze altar, must first have his sin dealt with, before anything else is spoken of. In preaching this layer we still keep measure, and do not assign a meaning to each colour one by one; we say only what the structure gives us: in the wall of separation only one gate is left, and the gate is made conspicuous and solemn. There are so many paths in the wilderness, but arriving at the tabernacle, the path narrows to one. This is still a reminder for us today: drawing near to God is not each person finding a crack according to his own convenience; it is entering by the gate God Himself has opened and the order He Himself has set. That the gate stands open is grace; that there is only one gate is holiness. The two together are the shape of the gospel in the courtyard.

Then look at the pins — the tent pegs. For the hangings all around to stand, the pillars must rest on sockets and the cords must run down to pegs driven into the ground. At the very end the text adds on purpose that all the pins of the court were of bronze. Wind blows over sand, and if the hangings had no pegs and the pillars no sockets, the first gust would bring it all down. That Scripture records even the pegs is again the consistent stroke of the execution narrative: great and small alike are on the account. Preaching may let this land lightly on us. In the life of a church everyone can see the hangings and the embroidery of the gate screen, but few notice the pegs. A peg's work is to be buried in the dirt, stepped around, holding its cord taut. Without pegs, everything above is decoration. Many among us spend their whole service as one bronze peg: unremarkable, unshining, placed at the very bottom, yet holding tight the corner where they stand. God writes the pegs into the text, and writes down that their material was bronze, to tell us that on His account nothing is too small to record. The total account that begins at verse 21 will say this even more plainly: even the pegs have a source; even the pegs are counted in the materials. Hold the picture of the courtyard first, and then listen to that account with a settled heart.

With the courtyard made, the space of the tabernacle is complete: the Most Holy Place, the Holy Place, and the courtyard, from the inside outward, layer by layer. We have watched this order for two chapters; today we add the outermost layer. That God dwells among His people is not a slogan but an arrangement with walls, a gate, an altar, and a laver. He is willing to move in, and He sets boundaries according to His holiness; He sets boundaries, and He Himself opens a gate. If the two sides are split apart, faith goes astray: speak only of presence and not of boundaries, and worship becomes careless; speak only of boundaries and not of presence, and worship becomes distant. Exodus nails both sides to the same circle of hangings: one hundred cubits of fine linen, five cubits high, and one embroidered gate. In the next chapter, chapter 39, the making of the priestly garments will be recorded — that is another passage, and this chapter gives it only one sentence of structural notice, without reaching ahead. We stand in the courtyard and finish looking at what this chapter has for us: the altar is set up, the laver is ready, the hangings are hung round, the gate screen is in place. One thing remains: to count, in public, every material that was used for all of this.

## IV. An Offering Must Have an Account: The Total of the Materials (42:00-56:00)

From verse 21 the whole chapter changes its tone. Until now it has been verb after verb — he made the altar, he made the laver, he made the court; here the narrative stops and begins to count. The KJV reads: "This is the sum of the tabernacle, even of the tabernacle of testimony, as it was counted, according to the commandment of Moses, for the service of the Levites, by the hand of Ithamar, son to Aaron the priest" (Exod 38:21). The man who handled the counting has a name, the command to count has a source, and the sum is to be shown to all. Then the text names the makers again: all that Moses commanded, Bezalel made; and with him was Aholiab, son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan, an engraver and a designer, who worked in blue, purple, and scarlet yarns and fine linen. Only after the names of the two craftsmen have been read once more at the head of the account do the numbers begin. This arrangement is worth hearing slowly: the account is not an anonymous account, and the work is not anonymous work. Who commanded, who handled, who made — all are written before the sums. Public, named, traceable: that is the genre of this account.

First, the level of fact. This is a total account of materials, recording the total amount and destination of the gold, silver, and bronze the people offered. The gold: twenty-nine talents and seven hundred and thirty shekels, all by the shekel of the sanctuary. The silver: one hundred talents and one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five shekels. Verse 26 states the source of the silver most clearly: it came from those of the congregation who were counted, each man paying half a shekel; all those twenty years old and upward who could go to war, who were counted, were six hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty men. This account can be checked, and today we shall check it on the spot: six hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty, multiplied by half a shekel each, is three hundred and one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five shekels; at one talent equalling three thousand shekels, that is exactly one hundred talents, with one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five shekels remaining — not one digit different from what the text records. This is not a theological inference; it is arithmetic, and the factual level should be presented in a factual way: the numbers are internally consistent, and their source is clear. The man who set up this account more than three thousand years ago knew that others would do the sums when they read it, so he wrote down all three things — the number of men, what each man paid, and the total — so that anyone could verify it for himself.

The uses of the silver are accounted for item by item. With the hundred talents of silver the sockets of the sanctuary and the sockets of the veil were cast: one hundred sockets from the hundred talents, one talent for each socket. The remaining one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five shekels were made into hooks for the pillars, their capitals were overlaid, and silver fillets joined the pillars. The hundred talents became exactly one hundred sockets, one talent to a socket, as neat as a summary sentence: the silver of the whole congregation, half a shekel a man, held up the place where the entire tabernacle stood. The offering with the largest number of givers did not turn into the most conspicuous vessel; it was cast into the sockets at the very bottom. The socket is underneath, bearing the pillar, and the pillar carries the veil and the hangings. Walking through the tabernacle you would not look down at the sockets, but without sockets nothing could stand. This meaning still belongs to the distribution of uses the text itself displays, and we state it as it is: the ransom silver became the foundation, and the number of the people became the ground on which the sanctuary stood. As for drawing further symbolism out of the numbers, this sermon does not do it. Theological readings of numbers, matching some figure to some doctrine, are not written in this chapter, and we will not write them in its place. The narrative function of the account itself is powerful enough: the house of God is held up by everyone, one share apiece, and the way it is held up is precisely the least conspicuous work of all — the foundation work.

The total of the bronze was seventy talents and two thousand four hundred shekels. Its uses are listed in the last three verses: with the bronze were made the sockets for the door of the tent and the gate of the court, the bronze altar and its bronze grating, all the utensils of the altar, the sockets all around the court, the sockets of the court gate, all the pins of the tabernacle, and all the pins of the court. Read the bronze list back against the first half of the chapter and it closes: the bronze altar, the grating, the sockets and pegs in every place — all find their source in this account. The gold was least, the silver next, the bronze most, and what the bronze made was precisely what everyone in the courtyard used and touched every day: the altar for sacrifice, the laver for washing, the pegs that held the hangings taut. That materials have prices but uses have no rank — the text does not say this sentence outright; it is a structure the account lays out, and we hear it as structural observation. The women's bronze mirrors are inside these seventy talents, not listed separately, not specially marked; their bronze has been merged with the bronze of the altar and the bronze of the pegs into one total. Once a thing offered has entered the total account, it is no longer my bronze; it has become the bronze of God's house, and the account decides where it is used. Only when an offering reaches this point has it truly been handed over: not only is the thing surrendered, but the say over where the thing goes is surrendered too, asking only that it be clean on the account and right in its use.

Now let the application layer be marked separately: this is the sermon's appropriation, not the direct sense of the text. This chapter leaves the church a lasting lesson: an offering must have an account. The people offered willingly, and chapter 35 wrote that out in full; but willingness does not replace counting, and zeal does not replace openness. Moses commanded the counting, the Levites did the counting, Ithamar the priest handled it, the names of Bezalel and Aholiab stand at the front, the totals and uses stand behind, and the silver even comes with arithmetic anyone can verify. Why such solemnity? Because an offering is the many placing precious things into the hands of the few, and the place where trust is most easily wounded is exactly the place where the numbers are unclear. An open account is not distrust of people; it protects trust — it protects the cleanness of the giver and the cleanness of the handler alike. Everyone in the church who handles money or goods should love this account, and like Ithamar be willing to have his name written beside the sums; and everyone who gives should rejoice in this account, because it proves that what you offered was not blurred away, but became a socket, became a hook, became a peg, standing place by place in the house of God. We preach this application with a straight back, but we must remember that its foundation is the account at the factual level, not our borrowing of a theme. First the text's account, then the sermon's application; the order cannot be reversed.

There is also a quiet close. Verse 21 calls the tabernacle the tabernacle of testimony; what is counted is the whole estate of the house where the tablets of the covenant are kept. At the end of the count, it is not the inventory of a workshop that has been counted, but the whole household of the covenant between God and man. The gold has its number, the silver its number, the bronze its number; at the end of chapter 39 all the work will be brought to Moses for inspection; and in chapter 40 the tabernacle will be raised, the cloud will cover it, and the glory will fill it. That is for later; this chapter stops first at the account. Stopping at the account is fitting: before glory fills, there are clean numbers; before the cloud descends, someone has accounted clearly for every talent. The presence of God and the clean accounting of men have never been in conflict in Exodus. May our offering be like this too: one hand willing, one hand clean; when we offer, we keep no say over the thing; when we record, we keep no blurred account.

## V. Settle the Account, Offer the Mirror (58:00-60:00)

In the last two minutes let us gather this chapter into two sentences. One stands in the courtyard: offer even the bronze mirror. The thing in your hand that is most personal, most daily, most in use — when the house of God has need, will you hand it over, to be turned into a laver that washes others? One stands in the account book: settle the account clearly. Everyone who handles the money, the goods, or the good name of God's house — will you let your name be written beside the sums, so that all can be checked, calculated, and matched? When what is offered arrives, and what is counted arrives, the courtyard stands firm and the laver can hold water. The next chapter will record the priestly garments, the glory worn on a person's body; today let us first offer what should be offered, and count clearly what should be counted. Let us pray: Lord, thank You that in the courtyard there is first an altar, teaching us that to draw near to You we must first pass through sacrifice; thank You that the laver was made from mirrors, teaching us that the most personal things can also become holy vessels. Teach us to offer willingly, and teach us also to record cleanly: may those who offer not cling, and those who record not blur. May every offering in our church, like this account, have a number, a name, and a destination; and may the little mirror in each of our hands, when You have need of it, be willing to become a laver. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

On the bronze altar and its correspondence with chapter 27: Exodus 27:1-8 first gives the pattern of the altar of burnt offering — acacia wood, foursquare, five cubits in length and breadth, three cubits high, four horns, bronze overlay, utensils, bronze grating, four rings, and bronze-overlaid poles; Exodus 38:1-7 is the execution report, restating each item. This correspondence between command and execution is the basic structure of the two great blocks Exodus 25-31 and 35-40; see Childs on the relation of the two blocks, and Durham on chapter 38 as a passage of execution and reckoning. This sermon calls the contrast between the bronze altar and the golden altar of incense (30:1-10; 37:25-28) a structural observation, taking only the difference of material and position, and drawing from it no doctrine of rank that the text does not state.
2. Word anchor 1: mar'ah, Hebrew מַרְאָה, plural מַרְאֹת, transliteration mar'āh / mar'ôt, Strong's number H4759; according to BDB and HALOT the meaning here is a mirror (a polished metal mirror-surface). The word's other common sense is a vision, a sight seen, and the senses must be distinguished by context. In 38:8, since the material is bronze and the owners are women, commentators agree on the sense mirror, denoting the cosmetic implement of the day that showed the face in polished bronze.
3. Word anchor 2: tzava', Hebrew צָבָא, transliteration ṣābā', Strong's number H6633, a verb; according to BDB and HALOT, to march in ranks, to serve, to wage war, to go out on campaign. Its participial form in 38:8 is הַצֹּבְאֹת (haṣṣōvə'ôt, feminine plural), rendered "served" in some versions and "assembling" in the KJV. Scholarship discusses what exactly the word denotes here: one view takes it as a company of women who bore some service at the entrance of the tent; another takes the sense of ranks or assembly, women gathered at the entrance. First Samuel 2:22 uses the same participial form for the women who assembled at the entrance of the tent of meeting, in the negative context of the sins of Eli's sons; the usages in the two places may be compared, but their contexts and evaluations differ and must not be read back into one another. This sermon sets the views out side by side and reaches no conclusion.
4. Word anchor 3: kiyor, Hebrew כִּיּוֹר, transliteration kîyôr, Strong's number H3595; according to BDB and HALOT, a basin, a laver, also a hearth or a stand. In the tabernacle context it denotes specifically the laver between the bronze altar and the tent (cf. 30:17-21, where the priests must wash their hands and feet there when they enter the tent or approach the altar to minister). Exodus 38:8 records that the laver and its base were both of bronze, and both from one source: the women's bronze mirrors.
5. Word anchor 4: nechoshet, Hebrew נְחֹשֶׁת, transliteration nəḥōšet, Strong's number H5178; according to BDB and HALOT, copper, bronze. The ancient Near East made no strict distinction, in the modern metallurgical sense, between pure copper and bronze; versions render it variously, and this sermon follows the KJV's "brass"/bronze. In Exodus 38 bronze is the most-used metal (70 talents and 2,400 shekels), used for the bronze altar, the grating, the laver, the sockets of the court, and all the pins.
6. Word anchor 5: kikar, Hebrew כִּכָּר, transliteration kikkār, Strong's number H3603; according to BDB and HALOT, a round thing, a loaf, and a unit of weight, the talent. As a weight, one talent equals 3,000 shekels (cf. the computation in 38:25-26: 603,550 men at half a shekel each make 301,775 shekels, recorded as 100 talents and 1,775 shekels). Commentators' estimates of the talent in modern weights differ slightly; this sermon uses only the internal conversion given by the text.
7. Word anchor 6: pequdê, Hebrew פְּקוּדֵי, transliteration pəqûdê, Strong's number H6485; according to BDB and HALOT, numberings, appointments, charges, offices, from the same root as the verb פָּקַד (pāqad, to count, to inspect, to appoint). The phrase in 38:21, פְּקוּדֵי הַמִּשְׁכָּן (pəqûdê hammiškān), is rendered "the sum of the tabernacle" in the KJV and denotes the numbered reckoning of all the materials and work of the tabernacle. The application in Section IV of this sermon, that an offering must have an account, belongs to the level of sermonic appropriation; its factual ground is the reckoning genre of this verse.
8. On the narrative arc of women and ornaments (chapters 32, 33, 35, 38): Exodus 32:2-4 records the people breaking off their gold earrings to cast the calf; 33:5-6 records the people taking off their ornaments in mourning at the command; 35:22 records men and women bringing gold objects, and 35:25-26 the women spinning yarn. This sermon reads the four places together as a narrative arc, at the level of structural observation; each passage has its own historical and ritual context, the arc does not replace passage-by-passage exegesis, and no inference is drawn from it about the inner state of the women who offered their mirrors, on which the text says nothing.
9. On the courtyard's correspondence and the gate screen: Exodus 27:9-19 gives the pattern of the court's hangings, pillars, bronze sockets, silver hooks and fillets, and gate screen; 38:9-20 is the execution report. The gate screen, of blue, purple, and scarlet yarns and fine twined linen, worked by an embroiderer (38:18), forms a contrast within the text with the plain fine linen hangings; this sermon observes only that the entrance is specially marked, and does not expound the symbolism of the colours one by one, on which scholarship has no agreed reading.
10. On the arithmetic of the silver account: Exodus 38:26 states plainly that those counted were 603,550 men, each paying half a shekel (by the shekel of the sanctuary). 603,550 × 0.5 = 301,775 shekels; at 1 talent = 3,000 shekels, 301,775 = 100 × 3,000 + 1,775, that is, 100 talents and 1,775 shekels, agreeing with the total recorded in 38:25. This is a check at the level of fact. The uses of the silver are in 38:27-28: the 100 talents were cast into 100 sockets (the sockets of the sanctuary and of the veil, one talent per socket), and the remaining 1,775 shekels made the hooks of the pillars, overlaid their capitals, and made the silver fillets joining them.
11. On the accounts of gold and bronze: the gold was 29 talents and 730 shekels (38:24), summed up in the text as the gold of the offering used in all the work of the holy place; this verse does not distribute it item by item, and this sermon does not distribute it in its place. The bronze was 70 talents and 2,400 shekels (38:29), and its uses are listed item by item in 38:30-31: the sockets for the door of the tent and the gate of the court, the bronze altar and its grating and all its utensils, the sockets around the court and of the court gate, and all the pins of the tabernacle and of the court. The bronze of the women's mirrors is merged into the bronze total; the text does not list its amount separately, and this sermon records the fact without estimating the amount.
12. On the handlers and the list of craftsmen in the total account: Exodus 38:21 records that the reckoning was carried out by the Levites and handled by Ithamar (son of Aaron, a priest), at the command of Moses; 38:22-23 names Bezalel (of the tribe of Judah, son of Uri, son of Hur) as the one who made all of it, with Aholiab (of the tribe of Dan, son of Ahisamach) alongside him, an engraver and a designer. This practice of naming may be compared with the signing conventions of ancient Near Eastern building accounts; see the relevant discussions in Propp and Houtman. This sermon's description of the account as public, named, and traceable is a description at the level of fact, drawn from the genre of the text.
13. On the boundary of chapter 39: Exodus 39 records the making of the priestly garments (the ephod, the breastpiece, the robe, and the rest) and the presentation of the finished work, and does not belong to this chapter's scope; this sermon gives it only one sentence of structural notice at the end of Section III. In addition, the reading of the tabernacle furnishings in Hebrews 9 in the New Testament belongs to the level of New Testament reading; this sermon has not read it back into the direct sense of chapter 38, and notes the level here.

## Devotional Excerpt

Look first today at that bronze mirror. It once reflected one face every morning; now it has been handed over, melted down, and made into the laver, standing between the altar and the tabernacle. From now on it will reflect no one's adornment; it will only hold clear water and wash the hands of those who serve. Each of us holds such a mirror: the thing closest to us, most ordinary, hardest to surrender — a little time, a little dignity, a small plan kept only for ourselves. The Lord does not ask us to give up everything; He asks only whether, when His house has need and when another person needs to be lifted up, we will loosen our grip on this one thing. Offering is not loss. The bronze is still the same bronze; it has only changed its master and its use, and is now used daily in the house of God. Put that mirror down quietly today, without announcement, without waiting to be seen. You will find that no longer seeing your own face is no pity at all: to hold a basin of clear water and wash another's hands is the most honourable destination this piece of bronze could ever have. And do not wait for some great occasion to make the offering: the laver was used daily, and the mirror was looked into daily, and faithfulness hides in the daily things. To reserve a little time for prayer today, to speak a kind word to your family first, to finish the small task you have been putting off — these too are ways of laying the mirror down. What the Lord values is not how much bronze there is, but whether we will let Him decide where it goes.

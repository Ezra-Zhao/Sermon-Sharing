# Love Your Neighbour as Yourself

## — A Sermon on Leviticus 19: You Shall Be Holy

> Text: Leviticus 19:1-37

**Abstract**: Leviticus 19 carries holiness out of the sanctuary into the field, the courtroom, the market, and the human heart. Revering parents and leaving the corner of the field, judging righteously and rebuking frankly, loving the neighbour and loving the sojourner are not separate filing drawers of rules; they are one interleaved poem in which love of God and love of neighbour meet in a single verse. The chapter's title-word is the command of verse 18, and its foundation is the call of verse 2: you shall be holy, for the LORD is holy.

## I. 00:00-08:00 Holiness Is Not a Wall; It Is an Open Corner of a Field

Brothers and sisters, it is easy to put Leviticus down halfway. Sacrifices, purity, regulations, one after another — like walking into a room full of vessels, not knowing where to begin. But chapter 19 is different. It opens with words addressed not to priests but to the whole congregation. (Summary) In verses 1-2 the LORD tells Moses to speak to all the congregation of Israel and proclaim a call laid on the whole people. Verse 2 in the KJV reads:

"Speak unto all the congregation of the children of Israel, and say unto them, Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy." (Lev 19:2, KJV)

(Fact) The word "holy" here is the Hebrew adjective qadosh (Strong's H6918; BDB and HALOT both give "set apart, consecrated to God" as its core sense); the command uses the plural form qedoshim, "you (all) shall be holy." Notice two things. First, the ground of holiness lies on God's side, not ours: not "be holy, for you are better than your neighbours," but "for I the LORD your God am holy" — holiness is imitation, a family likeness, not a qualifying race. Second, the audience is "all the congregation," not the priestly class. Earlier in Leviticus, holiness clings mostly to the sanctuary, the priests, and the offerings; in chapter 19 it is placed into the hands of everyone who farms, hires, trades, and raises children.

(Application) This chapter therefore corrects a deep misunderstanding: holiness is not a wall. A wall's logic is to keep the unclean out and lock ourselves in. But the holiness of chapter 19 has an open face — it opens at the corner of the field, where the poor come in to glean; it opens in the courtroom, where poor and mighty receive the same justice; it opens at the city gate, where the foreign sojourner is loved as a native. Holiness that produces no mercy is not this chapter's holiness; and mercy without holiness as its root will not last. The thirty-odd verses that follow translate the seemingly abstract word "holy," verse by verse, into a life you can touch: how to treat your parents, how to work, how to speak, how to trade, and how to treat the person you cannot get along with.

(Fact) Scholars commonly call Leviticus 17-26 (or 27) the "Holiness Code," because the theme of holiness and the self-declaration "I am the LORD" recur throughout; many commentators regard chapter 19 as the central chapter of that collection (see Milgrom's placement of the chapter; Wenham likewise treats it as one of the Holiness Code's peak passages). It is central not only in position: only here are ritual and ethical commands woven together so densely that "piety" and "being human" cannot be pulled apart.

## II. 08:00-24:00 The Interleaved Arrangement: God and Neighbour Are Not in Two Drawers

(Fact) Look first at the structure — one of the chapter's most important observations. Walk through the sequence (the following is a (summary) paraphrase, not quotation): verse 3, revering parents alongside keeping Sabbaths; verse 4, no idols; verses 5-8, how the peace offering is eaten and its deadline; verses 9-10, leaving the field's corner at harvest; verses 11-12, no stealing or deceiving, no swearing falsely by God's name; verses 13-14, no withholding a labourer's wages, no mocking the deaf or the blind; verses 15-16, righteous judgment, no talebearing; verses 17-18, no hatred in the heart, love your neighbour as yourself; from verse 19, statutes again — mixed breeding and mixed cloth; verses 23-25, the years of a new fruit tree; verses 26-28, no eating blood, no divination, no cutting or tattooing for the dead; verses 29-31, no prostituting a daughter, keep the Sabbath and revere the sanctuary, no mediums or wizards; verse 32, honour the elderly; verses 33-34, love the sojourner; verses 35-36, just balances; verse 37, the total conclusion — observe all the statutes and judgments.

(Application) Do you see it? The editor could easily have filed these commands by theme: one drawer for God, one for neighbour, one for ritual, one for taboos. He did not. He puts parents and Sabbath in one verse, the field's corner right after the peace offering, false oaths next door to stealing, honouring the aged after the ban on necromancy and before loving the sojourner. This alternation is not disorder; it is itself a theological statement: for the one who gave this chapter, "revering God" and "loving people" were never two departments but one act of breathing — inhale reverence for God, exhale justice and mercy toward people. You cannot live by inhaling only; nor can you keep the Sabbath, withhold a worker's wages, and still call yourself holy.

(Fact — scholarly discussion) A second structural observation is the echo of the Decalogue. Many commentators note a recognisable correspondence between this chapter's contents and order and the Ten Commandments (Exod 20:1-17; Deut 5:6-21): honouring parents, keeping the Sabbath, no idols, no taking God's name in vain (false oaths), no stealing, no false witness (talebearing and unjust judgment) all reappear here in expanded form; Jewish tradition early read chapter 19 as a footnote to the Decalogue. But mark this clearly: it is an "echo," a correspondence, not a one-to-one alignment, and the order is not identical. This is a consensus-level observation in scholarly discussion, not a cipher left by the editor. This sermon takes only its secure conclusion: chapter 19 does restate the Decalogue's ethical centre — God first, neighbour with him, the two interwoven — in the language of daily life.

(Fact) A third structural observation is the formula that runs through the chapter: "I am the LORD." Counted by a checkable method, it stands as the closing signature in verses 3, 4, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 25, 28, 30, 31, 32, 34, 36, and 37 — fifteen verses in all; verse 2, "I the LORD your God am holy," is a variant of the same self-declaration and serves as the chapter's heading, counted separately. Commentaries differ slightly in total because their counting rules differ (whether variants and half-verses are included); readers may verify the list verse by verse. This sermon takes the most conservative, verse-checkable count: fifteen closing declarations, plus one heading.

(Application) Why does the formula matter? Because of where it appears. It is nailed not only behind sacrifices and festivals, but also behind "wages shall not stay overnight," "do not put a stumblingblock before the blind," and "use just balances." In other words, every seemingly secular ethical demand is signed with God's own name. This is not religious packaging on morality; it is a declaration: how you treat a hired hand and how you weigh goods for a customer stand in the same ledger, in God's eyes, as how you offer sacrifice. The signature is short — I am the LORD — yet it draws the whole of daily life inside the sanctuary's boundary.

(Fact) One more easily missed textual detail: the address alternates between "you" plural and "you" singular. Verse 2 addresses the whole congregation in the plural; verses 9-10 move from plural to singular; from verse 13 the singular predominates; verse 37 closes in the plural again. Milgrom discusses this alternation in his commentary and relates it to the sources and editing of the material (his redactional explanation belongs to scholarly discussion). But in the finished text the alternation preaches: holiness is a call to the whole community and a summons naming each person — "you all" leave the field's corner, but it is "you," this one employer, who must not keep wages overnight. No one can hide behind the group and call it everyone's business; no one can call it merely private either.

## III. 24:00-42:00 From the Field's Corner to the Courtroom: Love Has a Shape

(Fact) The chapter's middle commands of neighbour-love begin in the field. Verses 9-10 in the KJV read:

"And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corners of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleanings of thy harvest. And thou shalt not glean thy vineyard, neither shalt thou gather every grape of thy vineyard; thou shalt leave them for the poor and stranger: I am the LORD your God." (Lev 19:9-10, KJV)

(Fact) The words must be made clear. The "corners" of verse 9 render the Hebrew pe'ah (H6285), literally an edge or corner; in law it names the strip of field deliberately left unharvested. The "gleanings" render the noun leqet (H3951), grain dropped and left behind at harvest, from the verb laqat (H3950), to pick up, to gather. Verse 10 turns to the vineyard: (summary) do not strip it bare or pick up what has fallen; leave it for the poor and the sojourner — the related term 'olelot (H5955) denotes the gleanings left for picking in a vineyard. Together the words point to one action: at harvest, deliberately do not harvest clean.

(Application) Notice the economics of this law. It is not a charity system — charity takes a little from the surplus after everything is gathered and stored. The corner system marks out, inside production itself, a portion that is not yours. The poor man is not a beggar at your door; he walks into your field and bends down to glean: he receives food and keeps a worker's dignity, while you lose at most a corner's yield and gain a society that does not turn gifts into shame. Milgrom notes that such harvest laws write protection for the poor and the sojourner directly into the order of production, a rare arrangement among ancient Near Eastern laws (its uniqueness is a matter of scholarly discussion; this sermon follows Milgrom and says so). Most of us today have no field corner, but everyone has the instinct to "reap clean": schedules filled to the brim, budgets spent to the last coin, relationships calculated to the last favour. The lesson of holiness is to leave a corner in your own "field" first — for the person who can give you nothing back.

(Fact) From the field the camera moves to the market and the worksite. (Summary) Verse 13: do not defraud or rob your neighbour; a hired man's wages must not stay with you overnight until morning. (Summary) Verse 14: do not curse the deaf — he cannot hear your curse; do not put a stumblingblock before the blind — he cannot see who put it there; but fear God. The sharpness of this verse is that both evils are evils a victim cannot testify to. People restrain themselves mostly for fear of being seen; this verse sets the examination where no one sees, and gives a single reason — God sees. (Application) Levitical ethics never governs behaviour only; it governs who you are when there is no audience. Holiness that holds only in front of an audience is performance.

(Fact) Next comes the courtroom. Verse 15 in the KJV reads:

"Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment: thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty: but in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour." (Lev 19:15, KJV)

(Application) This verse blocks both exits: do not honour the mighty — that is flattery; do not favour the poor either — that is another unrighteousness. Bending the law out of sympathy does not count as justice in this chapter; justice is what treats everyone the same. Immediately after, (summary) verse 16 forbids going about as a talebearer among your people and forbids standing against the life of your neighbour. Talebearing looks like a sin of the mouth, yet the law places it in the same verse as a neighbour's life — because in that world, a slander was enough to kill a man at trial. Hartley stresses the social character of this chapter's ethics: speech, judgment, wages, and harvest all hang on one line of holiness, and personal piety cannot be cut away from social justice (see Hartley's survey of the chapter).

(Fact) A further group of commands must be accounted for, since it decides how we read the chapter's "ritual" material. (Summary) Verses 5-8 concern the peace offering: it must be offered willingly; its meat is eaten on the day of offering and the next day; what remains to the third day is burned; eaten on the third day it is an abomination, not accepted, and the eater bears his iniquity, having profaned the holy thing of the LORD, and is cut off from his people. Note where this rule stands: not isolated in an altar chapter, but set between "You shall be holy" and "leave the corner for the poor." (Application) The peace offering is the most communal sacrifice in the Bible — eater, family, and friends at one table, at peace with God and with each other. Yet the rule sets a deadline in the middle of the feast: holy things may not be hoarded. What was willingly offered must not rot in private storage; the token of peace with God, delayed or kept back, turns into profanation. The deadline answers the field's corner across the page: one says, what is offered to God must not be hoarded; the other, the land's produce must not be reaped clean. In this chapter the opposite of holiness is often not lust or idols, but keeping everything for yourself.

(Fact) A later cluster is summarised together here: (summary) verse 29 forbids prostituting one's daughter, lest the land fall into whoredom and wickedness; verse 30 repeats the Sabbath and reverence for the sanctuary; verse 31 forbids turning to mediums and wizards, lest one be defiled; verse 32 commands rising before the aged and honouring the old man, with fear of God. The sequence interleaves again: family ethics (v. 29), worship (v. 30), the boundary of faith (v. 31), social ethics (v. 32) — three switches in four verses. (Application) Verse 29 must not be passed over lightly: it condemns not the daughter but the logic of power and poverty that pushes her into selling herself — the law lays responsibility on the one who holds authority. Read today, it asks a holy community not only about private morals but about who is forced to sell themselves within our arrangements, and who profits. Verse 32 gives the other side: how a society treats those who no longer produce is a test of its holiness. The aged man has no corner to leave you; he can only receive. Yet the law writes "rise up" as a command — the body practises reverence before the feelings arrive, which is this chapter's constant ethical order: action first, feeling after — just as loving one's neighbour is written first as the concrete acts of not avenging and not bearing a grudge, not as a sentiment.

## IV. 42:00-56:00 Do Not Hate in Your Heart: From Rebuke to Loving Your Neighbour as Yourself

(Fact) Verse 17 is the door to verse 18. In the KJV it reads:

"Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him." (Lev 19:17, KJV)

(Fact) "Rebuke" renders the Hebrew verb yakhach (H3198), to reprove, correct, decide a dispute; its noun form is tokhechah (H8433), reproof, correction. Watch the psychology of the verse: hatred first hides "in thine heart," and the law refuses to let it ferment there in storage; it provides an exit — reprove to his face. Silently bearing hatred and frankly rebuking are the two options this verse sets out; the third option, telling a third party, has just been closed off in the previous verse — that is talebearing.

(Application) This is a very practical word for us. Many relationships are destroyed not by quarrels but by silence: accounts kept in the heart, courtesy on the face, the ledger growing thicker until one day it explodes over something small. Leviticus prescribes something disarmingly plain: if you have something to say, say it to his face — say it so he stops going wrong, and so you do not bear the sin that silence breeds. In this chapter rebuke is not a licence to vent; it is an obligation of love — its opposite is not gentleness but hatred in the heart.

(Fact) Then comes the peak of the chapter, verse 18, the source of this sermon's title:

"Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD." (Lev 19:18, KJV)

(Fact) Before building on it, the title-word itself must be checked. In the Chinese Union Version (New Punctuation) text of this verse, the phrase is rendered "love others as yourself" — its word for the one loved corresponds to the Hebrew rea' (H7453), whose range includes neighbour, companion, fellow countryman, and friend. Within this chapter rea' itself occurs in verses 13, 16, and 18; the "neighbour" of verses 15 and 17 in the same version renders not rea' but 'amit (עָמִית, H5997), a word near it in sense — the two words are distinct but close in meaning, and the standard versions render both alike as "neighbour." This sermon's Chinese title follows the Union Version's actual wording of verse 18, and the English title follows the KJV's "thy neighbour." The order of the command also deserves notice: first two prohibitions — do not avenge, do not bear a grudge — then one command — love. In the Hebrew, avenging is the outward act and bearing a grudge the inward residue; love is both act and disposition. The law first removes revenge's fuse, then puts love in its place.

(Fact — both views presented, no verdict) The range of rea', "neighbour," is this chapter's most famous dispute, and this sermon presents it on two levels without deciding it. First, within the text itself: verse 18 speaks above of "the children of thy people," and rea' most directly denotes the fellow member of the covenant community; some scholars therefore hold that the command's original scope was chiefly the kin-community (the fellow-Israelite view). Second, within the same chapter, verse 34 applies the identical formula, "love him as thyself," to the foreign sojourner (see below); other scholars argue that the chapter's own arrangement already pushes love's range outward (the textual basis of the universal-humanity view). At the level of later reading: from the Second Temple period onward, rabbinic tradition and the New Testament writers alike read the verse more broadly. This sermon judges the real question to be not the dictionary width of rea', but whether the reader takes verse 18 alone as the boundary, or the correspondence of verses 18 and 34 together as the boundary. That is an academic question; this sermon issues no ruling — except to note that on either view, verse 34 makes "love only our own" an impossible reading of this chapter.

(Fact) Verse 34 in the KJV reads:

"But the stranger that dwelleth with you shall be unto you as one born among you, and thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God." (Lev 19:34, KJV)

(Fact — textual-structure observation) The parallel between verses 18 and 34 is the clearest pair of bookends in the chapter's arrangement: one formula, "love … as thyself," turned inward first (the children of your own people) and outward second (the stranger dwelling with you); signed "I am the LORD" in the first, and "I am the LORD your God" in the second, with the chapter's most moving reason attached — for you were strangers in the land of Egypt. Wenham's structural analysis likewise lists verses 33-34 among the climactic units of the chapter's latter half (see Wenham's outline of the chapter). (Application) Hear the logic of that reason: it appeals neither to the stranger's loveliness nor to the profit of welcoming him, only to your own memory — you have tasted what it is to live under someone else's roof. In this chapter, a people's memory of suffering is forged into an engine of ethics: a community that has suffered must not pass the suffering on to the next sojourner. (Summary) Verse 33 first forbids vexing the stranger who sojourns with you; verse 34 then commands loving him as yourself — the law's demand climbs step by step: first, do no harm; at the top, active love.

(Fact — scholarly discussion) This section must also deal with verse 19. (Summary) Verse 19 commands keeping the statutes: do not let cattle breed with a different kind, do not sow a field with mixed seed, do not wear a garment of mixed material. The word for these mixtures is kil'ayim (H3610; BDB: "two kinds, hybrids," a dual form denoting the mixing of two kinds); the garment in verse 19 is further specified by sha'atnez (H8162; BDB: "mixed stuff," probably a loanword, defined in Deut 22:11 as wool and linen together). The explanations of these bans are presented side by side as scholarly discussion, with no verdict: first, the symbolic-order view — the bans teach Israel, through everyday non-mixing, to guard created distinctions and the boundaries of holiness, so that food, clothing, and farming all become instruction (both Milgrom and Wenham discuss this symbolic reading); second, the anti-Canaanite-custom view — some mixed practices were tied to Canaanite religion, and the bans mark separation (the strength of its evidence is unevenly judged by scholars); third, the mercy-and-stewardship view — the breeding bans imply restraint in the use of animals and land (this view appears mostly in ethical exposition and its historical evidence is thinner). All three are presented; this sermon's only position is that verse 19 must not be preached as a higher doctrine than verses 18 and 34 — within this chapter's arrangement, its boundary-keeping finally serves the love of God and neighbour.

(Fact — scholarly discussion) Likewise verse 26 (summary) forbids eating anything with blood and forbids enchantment and observing times; the verb behind "observe times," me'onen, derives from 'anan (H6049) and denotes soothsaying, seeking omens of fortune. The chapter sets such practices beside eating blood, tattooing, and necromancy, inside one boundary: do not go elsewhere for answers. This sermon states only the text and the word-meanings for such commands; the detailed reconstruction of their ancient background remains disputed among scholars and is left open here.

## V. 58:00-60:00 Just Balances: Holiness Can Be Weighed

(Fact) The chapter closes at the market scales. (Summary) Verse 35 forbids unrighteousness in judgment, in measure of length, weight, or volume; verse 36 commands just balances, just weights, a just ephah and a just hin, grounding the signature in the Exodus: I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of Egypt. Verse 32 has already said (summary): rise before the hoary head, honour the aged, fear God. Honouring the old and keeping honest scales — one soft, one hard — are both what holiness looks like in public life. Verse 37 gathers everything into the command to observe all the statutes and judgments, and ends with the same short signature: I am the LORD.

(Application) Brothers and sisters, the last picture Leviticus 19 leaves us is not a sanctuary but a pair of scales. Holiness, it turns out, can be weighed: weighed in how much corner you left, in whether wages were settled the same night, in whether poor and mighty fared alike in your judgment, in the account in your heart against your brother — recorded as hatred, or spoken as one honest word of rebuke, and turned into love. It is weighed also in how we treat sojourners — many of us have known what it is to be strangers in a foreign land; this chapter hands that memory back to us, not for self-pity, but so that we recognise the person standing today at the edge of our own field.

So read this chapter as one sentence: you shall be holy, for the LORD is holy — and the shape holiness takes on the ground is called loving your neighbour as yourself: the people of your own nation, yes, and the stranger who dwells with you, also. Next time you harvest the field of your own life, leave a corner first. Next time your heart reaches for its ledger, open your mouth first. Next time you lift the scales, remember first the God who brought a people out of Egypt and who still audits every balance. The signature is short — I am the LORD — and all thirty-seven verses of this chapter are its echo.

(Fact — New Testament reading layer) One later layer of reading must be marked, so that it is not confused with the chapter's own meaning: verse 18's "love thy neighbour as thyself" is cited repeatedly in the New Testament — by Jesus in answering the question of the law's greatest commandments (Matt 22:39; cf. Mark 12:31); by Paul, who says the whole law is fulfilled in this one word (Gal 5:14; cf. Rom 13:9); and by James, who calls it the royal law (Jas 2:8). This is the New Testament authors' reading and authoritative use, belonging to the New Testament reading layer; in preaching Leviticus itself, this sermon rests on this chapter's own text and arrangement, and uses the New Testament citations only as intertextual reference, without reading their settlement of the scope question back into the chapter.

## Devotional Extract

**Text (Lev 19:18, KJV)**: "Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD."

Do one small thing today: open the ledger in your heart. Leviticus 19 does not treat hatred by telling us to endure it; it gives two steps — lay down revenge and the grudge, then go to the person and speak plainly. A rebuke is not losing your temper; it is refusing to let hatred spend the night in your heart, and refusing to let a relationship rot in silence. If the words cannot yet be spoken today, at least confess the hatred honestly before God, and ask him to take the hand of revenge out of yours — the signature is "I am the LORD": the right of judgment and vengeance is his, not ours. Loving your neighbour as yourself sometimes begins simply with not avenging: not returning that sentence, not withholding that wage, not touching the scales. Leave a corner of the field for the poor, and leave a corner of your heart for the person you least want to leave one for. And remember that verse 34 gives the same sentence to the sojourner: love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt. If you are living far from home just now, tasting what it is to sojourn, hear this verse first as comfort — God remembers sojourners, and commands his people to remember them — and then as a commission: when one day you have a field of your own, leave its corner first for those who come after you. May we be people who can leave that corner today.

## I. The Scripture

"Blessed are the merciful, For they shall obtain mercy." (Matt. 5:7, NKJV)

By the time we reach the Fifth Beatitude, the flavor of the Beatitudes has changed. The first four beatitudes mostly speak of what a person is like before God: poor in spirit, mourning over sin, hands opened, heart hungry. The Fifth Beatitude turns the camera for the first time toward how a person treats other people — how you treat those who have fallen into difficulty. Jesus did not reverse the order. If a person has not first been poor before God, wept before God, and been fed by God, his kindness toward others easily turns into a transaction, a performance, or condescending charity. Mercy is not like that. Mercy is given outward by one who has first received it.

## II. Exegesis of the Original Text

The Greek text: Μακάριοι οἱ ἐλεήμονες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ ἐλεηθήσονται.

**1. "The merciful" (οἱ ἐλεήμονες, hoi eleēmones)**

ἐλεήμων (eleēmōn) is an adjective, derived from ἔλεος (eleos, mercy). This adjective appears only twice in the New Testament: once here, and once in Hebrews 2:17, describing Christ the High Priest as a "merciful" High Priest. Its occurrences are few, but its weight is not light — it is first used to describe the Lord Himself, and only later used of disciples. The mercy of disciples is not kindness squeezed hard out of their own hearts; it is learned after first tasting the mercy of that High Priest, and learning to be like Him.

In the Septuagint, ἔλεος often corresponds to the Hebrew hesed, that covenantal, steadfast love spoken of again and again in the Old Testament — love bound by covenant and not lightly withdrawn. God's mercy toward Israel was not a momentary softening of the heart; it was covenant-keeping. So the mercy spoken of in the Bible is never merely feeling distressed for a moment. If the heart is moved but the hand does not move, that does not yet count as mercy in the Bible. Mercy must always land in action: a debt owed is forgiven, help is given in difficulty, and the person held in your grip — you let him go.

The object of mercy must also be seen clearly. In the Bible, mercy is mostly spoken of toward those who have no ability to repay, and even no standing to plead: the poor, debtors, those who have done wrong and fallen into your hands, the sick and the weak. Being good to useful people is management; being good to those who can repay is investment; still reaching out to those who cannot repay, cannot help you, and have even offended you — that is what is called mercy.

**2. "They shall obtain mercy" (ἐλεηθήσονται, eleēthēsontai)**

This is the future passive form of the verb ἐλεέω (eleeō): they shall be shown mercy. As in the preceding beatitudes, it is again passive. Who will show them mercy is not stated explicitly in the text, but the reader understands in his heart — it is God. This way of writing is very common in Jewish expression: not naming God directly, yet referring to Him everywhere. Those who show mercy shall obtain mercy, and the mercy they obtain ultimately comes from God.

A misunderstanding must be guarded against here: this saying is not a transaction. It is not that I first show mercy to a few people, accumulate enough merit, and then go to God to exchange it for mercy. In the gospel, the order is always reversed: a person first receives God's freely given mercy, his heart is softened by this mercy, and only then can he show mercy. If a person refuses to loosen his grip at all on those who have fallen into his hands, James puts it very heavily: "For judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy" (James 2:13, in substance). It is not that God is stingy; it is that the hard heart itself shows that it has not yet truly received mercy. The hand of one who has received it is soft.

The rendering of this verse in the Lü Zhenzhong Translation is broadly the same in meaning as the Chinese Union Version, and the promise likewise rests on the blessing of obtaining mercy (the Lü translation uses the wording "怜悯" for this verse, whose meaning is the same as the Union Version's "怜恤"). The English versions also do not differ greatly at this verse; the difference lies only in the tone between "obtaining" and "being shown."

English versions for comparison:
- KJV: "Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy."
- NIV: "Blessed are the merciful, for they will be shown mercy."
- NRSV: "Blessed are the merciful, for they will be shown mercy."

## III. The Place and Logic of This Beatitude in the Sermon on the Mount

Reaching the Fifth Beatitude, the Beatitudes are exactly halfway through, and its position is crucial.

The first four beatitudes move inward: poor in spirit, mourning, meekness, hungering and thirsting for righteousness — speaking of how a person is emptied before God, and fed by God's word and God's righteousness. From the Fifth Beatitude onward, the camera begins to turn outward: showing mercy (5:7) speaks of how to treat people, purity of heart (5:8) speaks of how to see God, making peace (5:9) speaks of how to do, among people, what a child of God ought to do. The order cannot be read in reverse. A person who has not been fed by God, when he gives outwardly, gives his own face and calculation; a person who has been fed by God's righteousness and forgiven by God's mercy, when he gives outwardly, can give true mercy.

Jesus Himself spoke of this beatitude more than once in the Gospel of Matthew, and returned to it again and again afterward. In chapter 9 He quotes Hosea: "I desire mercy and not sacrifice" — "mercy" there is ἔλεος, understood in the line of the Lü Zhenzhong Translation mostly as "怜悯" (Matt. 9:13, quoting Hos. 6:6). In chapter 12 He quotes it again (Matt. 12:7). By chapter 23, He rebukes the Pharisees, saying that the weightier matters of the law are justice and mercy and faith, but they had neglected them (Matt. 23:23). It is clear that in Jesus' heart, mercy is not a small matter of good or bad temperament; it is the skeleton of faith. However neatly sacrifices are performed, if the hand refuses to show mercy to people, in His eyes the heavy things have been thrown away and the light things picked up.

That line in the Lord's Prayer, "And forgive us our debts, As we forgive our debtors" (Matt. 6:12, NKJV), is the same truth, and after teaching the prayer Jesus deliberately added an explanation (Matt. 6:14–15). The Fifth Beatitude is not merely a pleasant saying; it has echoes in the Sermon on the Mount, with roots before it and fruit after it.

## IV. Cross References

- Hos. 6:6 — "For I desire mercy and not sacrifice." This is the saying Jesus quotes twice in the Gospel of Matthew (Matt. 9:13; 12:7). The mercy God desires has mercy at its core: He values how a person treats other people more than how completely rituals are performed.
- Mic. 6:8 — The prophet states God's requirement with utmost simplicity: to do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God. "To love mercy" is to love mercy itself, not to do it once on a passing impulse.
- Ps. 41:1 — "Blessed is he who considers the poor; The LORD will deliver him in time of trouble." (NKJV) Its structure is almost identical to the Fifth Beatitude: the one who considers others will himself be considered. This is not a truth that only appears in the New Testament; David had already sung it long ago.
- Prov. 19:17 — "He who has pity on the poor lends to the LORD, And He will pay back what he has given." (NKJV) For this account of showing mercy to the poor, it is God who keeps the books; there is no need to fear that the other party cannot repay.
- Matt. 18:21–35 — The parable of the unforgiving servant is the longest footnote to the Fifth Beatitude. Forgiven a debt of ten thousand talents, he turned around and seized by the throat a fellow servant who owed him a hundred denarii and would not let go. That question of the master is one every reader should hear: "Should you not also have had compassion on your fellow servant, just as I had pity on you?" (Matt. 18:33, NKJV)
- Luke 6:36 — "Therefore be merciful, just as your Father also is merciful." (NKJV) The standard of mercy is not on other people; it is on the heavenly Father. As He treats us, so we learn to treat others.
- James 2:13 — "Mercy triumphs over judgment." (NKJV) Everyone fears the day of judgment, yet the Bible says mercy can triumph on that day. A person willing to show mercy to others need not hide from that day.

## V. Life Application

**Scene One: Someone has fallen into your hands — will you let him go?** There are always such moments in life: a subordinate has botched a matter, and the right to explain it rests with you; a family member has said the wrong thing, and his apology comes out stammering; a partner owes you a sum of money, cannot repay it for the time being, and stands before you not daring to lift his head. At such a time, what you hold in your hand is the power to dispose of him. Mercy does not ask you to pretend nothing happened; wrong is still wrong, a debt is still a debt, and what needs remedying should still be remedied. Mercy is that when you have reason, have the right, and have the opportunity to step down on him, you hold back your foot and leave the other person a road on which he can stand up and live as a person again. Think of that servant in Matthew 18: his problem was never that he calculated the account wrongly; it was that he forgot how his own ten thousand talents had been forgiven. Once a person forgets grace, his hand hardens.

**Scene Two: Help for the poor — do not stop at being moved.** Swiping past a message asking for help, feeling a pang in the heart, tapping share, and counting that as having done one's best — this is the most common "mercy" of our generation. Proverbs says that he who has pity on the poor lends to the LORD; that requires real money and real action. To be concrete: for people truly in difficulty nearby — in the church, among relatives, among old colleagues — first take good care of such near people, then talk about great matters far away. The amount need not be large, but it should be regular, it should be given willingly, and it should still be given when the other party cannot repay and cannot even thank you aptly. There is another point: when helping people, protect the other person's dignity: do not take photographs, do not publicize it, do not make the recipient feel shamed in public. If mercy carries a camera with it, its flavor changes.

**Scene Three: In the church and at home, that old account you refuse to forgive.** Some accounts are not money but words: a hurtful sentence remembered for ten years, a decision resented to this day, not even willing to sit at the same table during festivals. People who pray the Lord's Prayer every day recite "And forgive us our debts, As we forgive our debtors," while in their hearts they keep a ledger they refuse to cancel. The Fifth Beatitude here does not reason in the abstract; it speaks of order: weigh together the forgiveness you receive from God every day and the portion in your hand that you refuse to release. Releasing is not indulgence, still less saying the harm never happened; releasing is returning the right of judgment to God, and first coming out of bitterness oneself. Very often, the person unwilling to show mercy is precisely the one first tormented by it himself — the account is recorded under another person's name, but the interest is paid by himself day after day.

## VI. Response Prayer

Lord, I first confess: the mercy I have received is much, and the mercy I have given is little. What You have forgiven me is not a small sum; yet with those who have fallen into my hands, I often first calculate their faults, calculate them clearly, and am unwilling to give even a single word that loosens my grip.

Please make me remember the end of that servant in Matthew 18, and also remember the heart his master had at first. Do not let me receive Your forgiveness on one side while seizing another person by the throat on the other. Where I should let go, please give me the strength to let go; where I should reach out to help, please give me the willingness to reach out; those old accounts recorded for many years, please help me hand them over to You one by one — I will no longer collect the interest myself.

You have promised that the merciful shall obtain mercy. I believe that on the final day of judgment, this saying will still hold true. Let me live in this promise today, with a softer hand and a broader heart, treating those who have fallen into my hands as You have treated me. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ I pray, Amen.

## I. The Scripture Text

"Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted." (Matt. 5:4, NKJV)

The first beatitude speaks of "seeing that I cannot do it" (poor in spirit); the second beatitude speaks of "grieving over it in the heart" (mourning). To see and yet not grieve is indifference; to grieve and yet not act is sentimentality. The mourning of the kingdom of heaven is real weeping before God after seeing clearly one's own bankruptcy and the breaches of the world — and to people who weep like this God gives a promise: they shall be comforted.

## II. Exegesis of the Original Text

The Greek original: μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται.

**1. "Mourn" (πενθοῦντες, the present active participle of pentheō; the verb πενθέω, Strong G3996)**

The New Testament has nine words to describe "sorrow," and the one Jesus uses here is the heaviest of them. πενθέω refers to intense grief that cannot be held down and cannot be hidden — in the Septuagint it is used to describe the weeping of mourning for the dead (see Gen. 37:34; and the mourning context of 2 Sam. 13:37). This is not a frown of displeasure; it is weeping torn out from the depths of the heart.

Note the tense: the participle is in the present tense, describing a continuing state — this kind of person is "one who is mourning." What Jesus refers to is not the experience of having cried once, but a continuing state of life of the citizens of the kingdom of heaven: toward sin, toward brokenness, toward the lost, always keeping a heart that can still feel pain.

The Lü Zhenzhong Translation renders it: "Blessed are those who mourn! For they shall be comforted." Rendering "blessed" with an exclamation gives it a sighing, declarative tone, closer to the declarative force of the original μακάριοι.

**2. "They shall be comforted" (παρακληθήσονται, the future passive of parakaleō; the verb παρακαλέω, Strong G3870)**

The literal meaning of this word is worth examining closely: παρά ("from beside") + καλέω ("to call") — "to be called to come alongside," referring to one who is summoned to your side and steps forward to support you. It shares the same root as "Helper" (παράκλητος, Strong G3875, John 14:16, 26) in the Gospel of John. In other words, the "comfort" Jesus promises is not a sentence of "try to look on the bright side"; it is the nearness of a Person: the Holy Spirit Himself comes alongside the one who mourns to be his Supporter.

The passive voice makes clear: this comfort is not manufactured by man himself; it is "bestowed" from God. The future tense makes clear: this comfort is certain — it will surely come in God's time.

English versions for comparison:
- KJV: "Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted."
- NIV: "Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted."
- NRSV: "Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted."

The three versions agree in meaning: the mourning is real pain, and the comfort is a certain promise.

## III. The Place and Logic of This Beatitude in the Sermon on the Mount

The second beatitude follows closely after the first. It is not a second maxim standing side by side, but the necessary result of the first beatitude:

- The first beatitude (5:3): admitting bankruptcy in spirit (seeing);
- The second beatitude (5:4): grieving and repenting over this bankruptcy and all sin (heartache);
- The third beatitude (5:5): no longer wrestling with God, handing one's strength back to God (meekness);
- The fourth beatitude (5:6): turning to long for God's righteousness (hungering and thirsting for righteousness).

This is a "road of repentance": seeing → heartache → surrender → longing. Without the "heartache" of the second beatitude, the "seeing" of the first beatitude is only cold knowledge, and the "meekness" of the third beatitude is a put-on good temper.

It is worth noting that Isaiah 61:1–3 (the prophecy of the Messiah's mission) corresponds directly to the first two beatitudes: 61:1 speaks of the Messiah being anointed "to preach good tidings to the poor" and to proclaim liberty to the captives (corresponding to the first beatitude — the poor in spirit receive the kingdom of heaven); 61:2–3 immediately follows with "to comfort all who mourn" and to give those who mourn in Zion beauty instead of ashes (corresponding to the second beatitude — those who mourn receive comfort) — what verse 3 speaks of is still the comfort given to those who mourn in Zion; it is not the source of the third beatitude, "inherit the earth" (the third beatitude comes from Ps. 37:11; see chapter three). The passage Jesus read in the synagogue was precisely this one (Luke 4:18–19). When He spoke the eight beatitudes on the mountain, He was declaring: the Messiah's work of comfort has already begun.

## IV. Cross-References

- Isa. 61:2–3 — "To comfort all who mourn, to console those who mourn in Zion, to give them beauty for ashes." The direct Old Testament root of the second beatitude.
- Ps. 34:18 — "The LORD is near to those who have a broken heart, and saves such as have a contrite spirit." The reason those who mourn are comforted: God Himself draws near.
- Ps. 51:17 — "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and a contrite heart — these, O God, You will not despise." David's psalm of confession after his sin is a living sample of the second beatitude.
- 2 Cor. 7:10 — "For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death." The dividing line between two kinds of sorrow: sorrow over sin brings life; sorrow over face brings death.
- Luke 6:21 — "Blessed are you who hunger now, for you shall be filled. Blessed are you who weep now, for you shall laugh." Luke's parallel text, emphasizing the reversal of mourning and laughter.
- Rev. 21:4 — "And God will wipe away every tear from their eyes; there shall be no more death, nor sorrow, nor crying. There shall be no more pain." The ultimate promise of the second beatitude: the full realization of comfort is in the new heaven and the new earth.

## V. Life Application

**Scene One: No longer covering up when confessing sin.** Many people's confession of sin is like crisis public relations: they make big things sound small, and small things are explained away as "a personality issue." The person of the second beatitude is different — having offended God, he says before God, "I have offended You," with no embellishment and no excuses. The Book of Proverbs says it clearly: "He who covers his sins will not prosper, but whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy" (Prov. 28:13, NKJV). Mourning is not self-torment; it is honesty.

**Scene Two: Not forcing oneself to hold up in the face of brokenness.** A family member falls ill, a crack appears in a marriage, a child goes astray, a job is lost — these pains are real. The world teaches us two roads: either numb ourselves (busyness, scrolling on the phone, drinking), or grit our teeth and hold on, saying "I'm fine." The second beatitude gives a third road: bring your tears before God. God does not despise a broken and contrite heart, and the Holy Spirit, the Helper, will come alongside in person. Entrusting through tears is not weakness; it is the proper road for the citizens of the kingdom of heaven to deal with brokenness.

**Scene Three: Grieving in the heart over the breaches of others.** The mourning of the second beatitude is not only for oneself. When we see people around us in sin and in suffering, the heart aches, and we are willing to intercede for them, and even willing to walk a stretch of road with them. The one who mourns receives comfort, and the one who has been comforted turns around and becomes a comforter of others (2 Cor. 1:4: "who comforts us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort those who are in any trouble, with the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted by God").

## VI. Response Prayer

Lord, I confess that my heart is numb much of the time. When I see my own sin, I make excuses; when I see the pain of others, I walk around it; in the face of brokenness, I pretend nothing is wrong. Today I ask You to restore in me a heart that can still feel pain.

Thank You for Your promise: those who mourn shall be comforted. Thank You for sending the Holy Spirit, the Helper, to stand beside me and support me. I bring before You all that I have not dared to weep out, all that I have hidden away, all that I have been forcing myself to hold up. Comfort me, I pray, and let me become a comfort to others. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ I pray, Amen.

# Once a Year, Two Goats — Blood Sprinkled on the Mercy Seat, Sin Borne into the Wilderness

## Abstract

The Day of Atonement stands at the centre of Leviticus' ritual calendar: once a year the high priest enters the Holy of Holies, first offering a bull for himself and his house, then a goat for the people, sprinkling blood upon the mercy seat, and laying the sins of the congregation on the head of a live goat sent into the wilderness. The chapter neither hides its warning of death nor softens the cost of sin. It lets the congregation see two things at once: sin must be dealt with, and no one walks into God's presence by his own way; yet God himself has appointed a way, so that an unclean people and an unclean sanctuary may together be cleansed.

## I. Not at All Times: Death Written at the Head of the Chapter (Lev 16:1-6)

### 00:00-08:00

(Fact) The chapter opens not with festival instructions but with a bereavement: "And the Lord spake unto Moses after the death of the two sons of Aaron, when they offered before the Lord, and died;" (Lev 16:1, KJV). Readers who look back to chapter 10 know this refers to Nadab and Abihu and their strange fire; this chapter does not retell the details, but places death before all ritual. The arrangement itself is interpretation: the ordinances of the Day of Atonement were not given in untroubled air, but after the priestly family had paid a grievous price. Drawing near to God has never been a matter for carelessness; the higher the office, the less holiness may be treated as routine.

(Fact) The command that follows draws the boundary: "And the Lord said unto Moses, Speak unto Aaron thy brother, that he come not at all times into the holy place within the vail before the mercy seat, which is upon the ark; that he die not: for I will appear in the cloud upon the mercy seat." (Lev 16:2, KJV) The point is not whether Aaron may enter, but that he may not enter at his own time and in his own way. "That he die not" recurs through the chapter and makes entry into the Holy Place a matter of life and death; the reason is not divine caprice, but presence itself: "for I will appear in the cloud upon the mercy seat." Where God himself appears, presence itself is the boundary.

(Application) This opening speaks to today's hearers in at least two ways. First, familiarity in worship must not cancel reverence. Familiarity with liturgy, songs, and pulpits can make people imagine that holiness has been tamed by habit; the chapter instead writes death at its head, like a sign nailed to the door: the nearer you come, the greater the care required. Second, God sets a boundary and a way at the same time. He does not say "never," but "not at all times," and then specifies in this chapter exactly how entry is to be made. Judgment and grace run in parallel within the same passage: the door is narrow, but the door is open — only its opening is determined by God, not invented by man.

(Fact) Before entering, the high priest must prepare himself. His clothing is prescribed: "He shall put on the holy linen coat, and he shall have the linen breeches upon his flesh, and shall be girded with a linen girdle, and with the linen mitre shall he be attired: these are holy garments; therefore shall he wash his flesh in water, and so put them on." (Lev 16:4, KJV) Compared with the glorious vestments of gold and of blue, purple, and scarlet in Exodus 28, this day's dress is linen throughout, plain and unadorned; the text calls it simply holy garments and makes no show of glory. Washing precedes dressing, and the order cannot be reversed: first the man is washed, then clothed; first the person is dealt with, then the rite.

(Fact) The order of the offerings is likewise irreversible: "And Aaron shall offer his bullock of the sin offering, which is for himself, and make an atonement for himself, and for his house." (Lev 16:6, KJV) Before the high priest acts for the people, he confesses that he himself needs atonement. The bull is no stage prop but his own confession: the one standing at the door of the Holy of Holies is not a sinless functionary, but a sinner first covered by the rite of atonement himself. (Application) This corrects a common illusion in religious life — that the minister stands by nature on the far side of sin. The chapter's order returns everyone to the same place: first for his own house, then for the people; first cleansed, then cleansing. Whoever skips this order repeats the error at the head of the chapter — dying not of ignorance, but of presuming he need not draw near by the rule.

## II. Two Goats, One Casting of Lots (Lev 16:5-10)

### 08:00-24:00

(Fact) The people's offering is taken from the congregation: two male goats for a sin offering and one ram for a burnt offering (Lev 16:5). Ritually, the two goats are first treated as a single unit: together they constitute the sin offering, yet at the door of the tabernacle, before the Lord, they are to receive entirely different treatment. What decides their treatment is neither the high priest's preference nor the goats' appearance, but the lot: "And Aaron shall cast lots upon the two goats; one lot for the Lord, and the other lot for the scapegoat." (Lev 16:8, KJV)

(Fact) The terms must first be verified. The Hebrew goral (Strong's H1486), rendered "lots," denotes a lot itself and also the portion determined by lot, as in the allotment of land; BDB gives the lot and its assigned portion as the basic sense. In this chapter its use is a ritual decision procedure: two lots bearing two destinations — one for the Lord, one for Azazel — which the high priest may not alter on the spot. The "male goat," sa'ir (H8163), literally a hairy one, a he-goat, frequently serves in Leviticus as a sin offering. Here the two sa'irim form a pair in contrast: one slain, one kept alive, and together they complete the single narrative of the Day of Atonement; neither can be dispensed with.

(Fact) The goat for the Lord is slaughtered, and its blood is brought within the veil (Lev 16:9, 15). The goat for Azazel receives precisely the opposite fate — not slaughtered, no blood shed, not placed on the altar: "But the goat, on which the lot fell to be the scapegoat, shall be presented alive before the Lord, to make an atonement with him, and to let him go for a scapegoat into the wilderness." (Lev 16:10, KJV) Note two details. First, this goat too is first "presented alive before the Lord": its destination is named Azazel, but its starting point is before God. Second, the text expressly says it is "to make an atonement with him" — being sent alive into the wilderness is no appendage to the rite, but part of the act of atonement itself. One dies, one lives; one on the altar, one in the wilderness: the two goats display the two faces of atonement side by side — sin's penalty borne, and sin's record removed.

(Fact) As for 'Azazel (H5799), this sermon sets the views side by side and reaches no verdict. First, that it is the name of a wilderness demon or evil spirit — a view with ancient roots, developed in Second Temple literature such as 1 Enoch into the name of a fallen angel. Second, that it is a place name, denoting a cliff or desolate region in the wilderness, with later Jewish practice of casting the goat down a precipice associated with it. Third, that it is an abstract formation meaning the complete removal of sin, so that the goat's destination is not a person at all but a disposal. Fourth, an etymological proposal connecting it with a formation meaning "complete removal," stressing utter elimination. The KJV renders the word "scapegoat" in this chapter — a rendering that is itself an interpretation — while the Chinese Union Version transliterates it, leaving the interpretive space to the reader. The coexistence of these views reminds the preacher: what the text has not settled must not be preached as settled. What is certain is the rite itself — the goat lives, the sins are confessed, the goat is sent away, and no one may bring it back.

(Fact) One more arrangement is easily missed: besides the two goats, the congregation provides a ram for a burnt offering, and the high priest provides a ram of his own as well (Lev 16:3, 5). The burnt offering is not the protagonist of the chapter, yet it frames the whole, beginning and ending with sacrifice (Lev 16:24). This tells the reader that the Day of Atonement is no isolated special ceremony, but one day embedded in Israel's daily sacrificial life: the smoke that rose daily from the altar and the blood taken this one day within the veil belong to a single order of communion between God and man. The day's distinctiveness is not that it negates the ordinary, but that once a year it penetrates where ordinary offerings cannot go — within the veil, before the mercy seat. The chapter sets up no second faith; it presses the same faith to its greatest depth.

(Fact) The place of the casting matters as well: the two goats are set at the door of the tabernacle, before the Lord (Lev 16:7); there the lots are cast and there the goats are divided. The whole congregation could see it: two goats alike in appearance standing side by side at the door, until the lot fell, and only then moving toward utterly different roads. This was no administrative decision taken in a closed room, but a ritual proclamation performed in public. Its public character has its own effect: when the people later recalled the Day of Atonement, what rose first in memory was not the unseen action within the veil, but this visible separation at the door — the dealing with sin was from its first step no private affair, but a public event witnessed and received by the whole congregation together.

(Application) The casting of lots is not foreign to modern hearers; we simply call the outcome luck in daily speech. The lots of this chapter dismantle the place of luck: the two goats differ in no quality, and their fates hang not on anything in the goats, but on the words on the lots. Applied to our own condition, the picture first blocks two wrong roads — self-righteousness, imagining oneself cleansed because one is better than others, and self-abandonment, imagining oneself forsaken because one is worse. The chapter leaves neither thought any ground: it is God's appointment that decides the destination, not the quality of the one chosen. Likewise, setting the views on Azazel side by side without a verdict is the pulpit's duty: honestly marking what is unknown, rather than filling the text's silences with imagination, makes hearers trust all the more what the text plainly says.

## III. Incense Cloud and Blood: The Line of Life and Death before the Mercy Seat (Lev 16:11-19)

### 24:00-42:00

(Fact) The central action takes place within the veil. The high priest first slaughters the bull for his own sin offering, then takes the censer and the fine incense into the Holy of Holies: "And he shall put the incense upon the fire before the Lord, that the cloud of the incense may cover the mercy seat that is upon the testimony, that he die not:" (Lev 16:13, KJV) The cloud of incense is no decoration but a covering: the mercy seat is the place of God's appearing (Lev 16:2), and a sinner facing that glory with no covering at all faces the consequence the text has repeatedly named. The cloud here becomes a means of grace — God commands the man to make the cloud, and the cloud lets the man live; on the road of approach, even the covering is prepared by God himself.

(Fact) Then comes the sprinkling of blood: "And he shall take of the blood of the bullock, and sprinkle it with his finger upon the mercy seat eastward; and before the mercy seat shall he sprinkle of the blood with his finger seven times." (Lev 16:14, KJV) The action is astonishingly precise: with his finger, sprinkle, eastward, before the mercy seat, seven times. After the goat for the people is killed, its blood is likewise brought within the veil and sprinkled upon the mercy seat and before the mercy seat (Lev 16:15).

(Fact) Lexically, kapporet, "mercy seat" (H3727), and kipper, "to make atonement" (H3722), belong to one root system. In BDB and HALOT alike, kipper's semantic field centres on covering, atoning, and propitiating; in priestly literature it denotes the ritual removal of sin and uncleanness so that a relationship is restored. Kapporet denotes specifically the cover upon the ark, the place where atonement is performed; hence the KJV's "mercy seat." That the two words share a root is no wordplay but the theological skeleton of the chapter: atonement is no abstraction — it has a place, a set of actions, an officiant, and a timetable of once a year.

(Fact) Verse 16 gives the reason, and must be quoted in full: "And he shall make an atonement for the holy place, because of the uncleanness of the children of Israel, and because of their transgressions in all their sins: and so shall he do for the tabernacle of the congregation, that remaineth among them in the midst of their uncleanness." (Lev 16:16, KJV) Observe the sequence of objects: atonement is made first for the holy place, then for the tabernacle, and only afterwards for the altar and the people (Lev 16:18-19, 33). (Fact) Two readings stand side by side in scholarship, and this sermon adjudicates neither. The traditional reading stresses that the Day of Atonement makes atonement for people, the blood sprinkled for the sins of the congregation. The reading represented by Milgrom holds that, by the grammar and sequence of verse 16, atonement's primary object is the sanctuary itself: the people's sins and uncleannesses accumulate through the year and pollute the sanctuary that dwells among them, and the Day of Atonement is the sanctuary's annual great purgation; only with the sanctuary cleansed can God's dwelling among them continue. The two are not wholly opposed: the same chapter expressly makes atonement both for the sanctuary and for the priests and all the congregation (Lev 16:33); the dispute concerns priority of emphasis. The pulpit should hand the dispute to hearers as it stands, and not preach one scholar's view as the text's verdict.

(Fact) After the work within the veil is finished, the high priest goes out to the altar in the court and makes atonement for it: blood of the bull and of the goat is put on the horns of the altar round about, and blood is sprinkled on it seven times to cleanse it (Lev 16:18-19; summarized here, not quoted). The route of the action deserves attention: from the Holy of Holies outward — mercy seat, tabernacle, altar — the circle of cleansing widens, like ripples spreading from a centre to the edges. This inside-out sequence runs opposite to the direction of uncleanness: on the chapter's own account, and as Milgrom argues, uncleanness erodes inward from the people's life, accumulating year by year into the depths of the sanctuary, while the Day of Atonement begins its cleansing at the deepest point and works layer by layer back among the people. To cite this spatial pattern is interpretation, and it is marked as such (Application); yet the pattern is not invented: verse 16 says the tabernacle remains "in the midst of their uncleanness," and verse 33 lists the objects of atonement again in the order sanctuary, tabernacle, altar, priests, congregation — the two lines, space and people, run in parallel through the chapter.

(Fact) The number of sprinklings is equally unambiguous: seven times before the mercy seat, seven times upon the altar. Seven recurs throughout Leviticus' ritual language; the cleansing of the leper and the sprinkling before the veil are likewise sevenfold. The preacher need only state the fact: the chapter's prescriptions of number, direction, and order are exacting to the point of severity, and this exactness is itself the source of its solemnity. Atonement is not improvised emotional expression but a procedure to be carried out as commanded; before the procedure, the high priest's own discretion recedes to its minimum — all he can do is act as he was told. This answers the chapter's opening, "not at all times": the time may not be chosen at will, and the actions may not be altered at will, because the one he faces is the God who appears in the cloud.

(Fact) During the sprinkling, the tabernacle is emptied: "And there shall be no man in the tabernacle of the congregation when he goeth in to make an atonement in the holy place, until he come out, and have made an atonement for himself, and for his household, and for all the congregation of Israel." (Lev 16:17, KJV) This once-a-year, one-man-alone entry is the loneliest scene in the chapter: no congregation watching, no assistant handing him anything; atonement is completed where no one sees, and the people outside can only wait for him to come out alive. (Application) For us, this picture corrects at least one expectation: the most important cleansing usually happens away from the spotlight. The chapter arranges its order slowly, minutely, quietly — washing, dressing, burning incense, sprinkling blood, not one step omissible. What instant worship cannot give, this chapter performs slowly over a whole day: because sin is heavy, the actions that deal with sin must be heavy; because life is precious, neither the covering cloud nor the sprinkled blood can be spared.

## IV. Both Hands on the Goat's Head: Sin Sent to a Land Not Inhabited (Lev 16:20-28)

### 42:00-56:00

(Fact) When atonement has been made for the holy place, the tabernacle, and the altar, the live goat is brought before the high priest. The heaviest passage in the chapter stands here: "And Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions in all their sins, putting them upon the head of the goat, and shall send him away by the hand of a fit man into the wilderness:" (Lev 16:21, KJV) Note the words "both his hands." Where Leviticus speaks of laying a hand on a sacrifice, it ordinarily uses the singular, one hand (e.g., Lev 1:4; 4:4); here alone it says "both hands," and adds confession — the high priest must speak, confessing item by item all the sins of Israel, not glossing them over. The Hebrew samakh (H5564), "to lay," is glossed by BDB as to lean, lay, rest upon; in sacrificial contexts it marks the offerer's identification with the offering. Here both hands are laid and confession is added, and the weight of the act plainly exceeds the ordinary — though any inference from this contrast, such as speaking of a "total transfer," must be labelled interpretation drawn from the textual contrast between two hands and one, not a statement the text itself makes (Application).

(Fact) What is confessed is listed in three layers: iniquities, transgressions, sins — not an abstract "wrongness," but itemised, countable debts. The destination to which they are assigned is stated just as concretely: "And the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited: and he shall let go the goat in the wilderness." (Lev 16:22, KJV) "Bear" is the operative verb of this verse: sin is not forgotten but transported; not denied existence but sent to an uninhabited land, never to return into the camp. The man who leads the goat away and the man who burns the carcasses must afterwards wash their clothes and bathe before returning to the camp (Lev 16:26, 28); the high priest himself takes off the linen garments, leaves them in the holy place, washes, puts on his ordinary garments, and offers the burnt offerings (Lev 16:23-25). The rite's closing movements are still full of water and fire: every step that has touched the transport of sin must end in cleansing.

(Fact) One geographical detail remains. The two animals whose blood was brought into the holy place — the bull and the goat for the Lord — have their skins, flesh, and dung carried outside the camp and burned, nothing of them left within (Lev 16:27; summarized here, not quoted). Inside the camp, blood before the holiest place; outside the camp, the fire of burning: one and the same animal has its blood carried to the innermost place and its body taken outside. This completes the chapter's spatial pattern: the dealing with sin allows no middle ground — either covered before the mercy seat, or removed beyond the camp gate; no offering is half in and half out. People often want a middle ground for sin too — to keep a little, hide a little, postpone a little. The geography of this chapter permits no such arrangement; in the plainest spatial language it declares that sin has no lawful dwelling in God's camp: it is either covered by blood, or burned by fire, or carried by the live goat to an uninhabited land. Three paths, and all three are removal; not one of them is coexistence.

(Application) This passage sets the measure for confession. First, confession must be specific. The confession here is no vague apology but an itemised reckoning of iniquities, transgressions, and sins; a vague confession often treats only feelings, while a specific confession deals with facts. Second, confession must have somewhere to go. Both hands on the head, confession item by item, and watching the goat led into the wilderness — God appointed these actions so that the people would see with their own eyes: sin has left. For those haunted by the past this is great comfort: in this chapter's picture, forgiveness is not God's reluctant forgetting, but sin borne, transported, and set down in an uninhabited land. Third, confession must not be performance. What the high priest confesses is not his own spiritual feelings but the sins of all Israel; the centre of confession is sin itself, not the image of the one confessing. All three applications arise from the actions of the text and do not step outside the boundaries the text itself sets.

(Fact — New Testament reading layer) How the New Testament reads this chapter must be kept on a separate layer, unmixed with the Old Testament's own sense. Hebrews 9-10 takes the Day of Atonement as its backdrop, contrasting the high priest who entered the second tabernacle alone, once a year, with blood, with Christ, who entered once into the holy place and obtained eternal redemption; and in Romans 3, where God set forth Christ as a propitiation, the Greek word hilasterion belongs to the same line of usage by which the Septuagint rendered kapporet, a connection scholarship widely discusses. This sermon only notes the existence and the textual basis of this New Testament reading: it is the apostolic text's reinterpretation of Leviticus 16 and belongs to the New Testament reading layer; on the layer of the Old Testament's own sense, this chapter concerns the annual rite under Aaron's office. Set side by side, hearers can see for themselves the distance and correspondence between shadow and substance; the preacher neither pours later light back into the text's original meaning, nor erases the contrast the New Testament itself establishes.

## V. The Tenth Day of the Seventh Month: Afflicting the Soul under an Everlasting Statute (Lev 16:29-34)

### 58:00-60:00

(Fact) The chapter closes with a statute, its date fixed precisely: "And this shall be a statute for ever unto you: that in the seventh month, on the tenth day of the month, ye shall afflict your souls, and do no work at all, whether it be one of your own country, or a stranger that sojourneth among you:" (Lev 16:29, KJV) "Afflict your souls" renders the Hebrew expression 'innah nefesh: the verb 'innah (H6031), to humble, afflict, bring low, with the noun nefesh (H5315), life, soul, self — to afflict and humble oneself, concretely embodied in tradition as fasting, and in this chapter placed alongside "do no work at all." Native-born and sojourning stranger alike are included: the discipline of the Day of Atonement makes no distinction of status, because the need for cleansing makes none.

(Fact) The day's character is marked with the heaviest Sabbath vocabulary: "It shall be a sabbath of rest unto you, and ye shall afflict your souls, by a statute for ever." (Lev 16:31, KJV) "Sabbath of rest" is shabbat shabbaton (H7676 plus H7677) — two words of one root set side by side to denote complete, solemn rest. The Day of Atonement is no working day and no ordinary festival: man stops his work, and God completes his atonement; man's part is to afflict the soul and wait quietly, God's part is to cleanse. This division of labour is itself a rehearsal of the gospel — on this day the people can do nothing to assist the atonement, not even qualify as helpers; they can only receive.

(Fact) The statute also provides for its own succession across generations: a later high priest, anointed and consecrated in his father's place, is likewise to put on the linen garments and make atonement for the holy sanctuary, the tabernacle, and the altar, and for the priests and all the people (Lev 16:32-33; summarized here, not quoted). Readers often skip this, yet it determines the chapter's nature: the Day of Atonement is not Aaron's private exercise but an institution for a community continuing generation after generation. Aaron will grow old and die; his sons already lie under the shadow at the chapter's head. But the statute does not lapse with a man's death, because the God who appointed it remains: the officiant may change, while the need for atonement is the same every year, and God's appointment is the same every year. The "for ever" of the everlasting statute hangs not on any high priest's lifespan but on God's continuing remembrance — he knows that every generation brings the same sins to this day, and so for every generation he has reserved the same way.

(Fact) The statute's effect is stated in full: "For on that day shall the priest make an atonement for you, to cleanse you, that ye may be clean from all your sins before the Lord." (Lev 16:30, KJV) Clean "from all your sins" is no rhetoric of partial pardon; paired with the "statute for ever" of verse 29, it declares that every year, on this day, it is to be announced again: sin can be dealt with completely. The chapter closes on a record of obedience: "And this shall be an everlasting statute unto you, to make an atonement for the children of Israel for all their sins once a year. And he did as the Lord commanded Moses." (Lev 16:34, KJV) "Once a year" is both comfort and limit. Comfort, because God remembers human weakness and appoints a way that can be followed year by year; limit, because the way must be walked again every year — the high priest must enter again, the blood be sprinkled again. On the layer of the Old Testament's own sense, the Day of Atonement never claims to be a once-for-all finish; by its very repetition it testifies that it points to a completion that has not yet come.

(Application) The sermon closes with three things to take away. First, treat drawing near to God as a weighty matter: "not at all times" is not obstruction but protection; to draw near in the way God has appointed is this chapter's lesson for every worshipper. Second, make confession specific: as with both hands on the goat's head, confess item by item — not vaguely, not theatrically, not confessing one's own sins for another, nor pushing one's own sins onto another. Third, receive cleansing as grace: on the tenth day of the seventh month, the only thing the people could do was afflict their souls and cease from work and wait; cleansing comes from before the mercy seat, not from human effort. Two goats: one died before the altar, one walked alive into the wilderness; blood sprinkled on the mercy seat, sin sent to an uninhabited land. The Old Testament rite of once a year is finished; the question it leaves is answered elsewhere, in the New Testament — but standing on this side of it, we first learn, on this day, to stop, to confess, to receive, and to stand in awe.

## Endnotes

1. On Lev 16:1 and the Nadab and Abihu episode of Lev 10: this chapter says only "the two sons of Aaron," without names or retelling; commentators regularly note the warning function of opening with the bereavement (see Wenham's discussion in his introduction to the chapter).
2. "Not at all times" (Lev 16:2, in the Chinese base text's wording; KJV: "come not at all times") restricts the time and manner of entry, not entry itself; the chapter proceeds to give the once-a-year right way.
3. The linen garments (Lev 16:4): Wenham observes that on this day the high priest lays aside his usual glorious vestments and wears linen only, matching the day's note of humiliation; the text itself calls them simply holy garments, and application should not outrun the text.
4. kipper (H3722): BDB glosses cover, atone, propitiate; HALOT, in priestly contexts, marks the ritual removal of sin and uncleanness. In Lev 16 the verb's objects include the holy place, tabernacle, altar, priests, and congregation (cf. Lev 16:16, 18, 20, 24, 33).
5. kapporet (H3727): the cover of the ark, the place where atonement is performed; the Septuagint generally renders it hilasterion, which is the textual basis for its association with Rom 3:25 — a matter for the New Testament reading layer; see the end of Section IV.
6. goral (H1486): a lot, and the portion determined by it; used in the Old Testament also for apportioning land (Josh 18) and for decision (the "lot" of Prov 16:33 belongs to the same word-field).
7. 'Azazel (H5799): the four views are set out in Section II. Hartley lists the options in his commentary without forcing one conclusion; Milgrom inclines toward a proper name connected with the wilderness. This sermon follows the side-by-side presentation and reaches no verdict.
8. Milgrom's sanctuary-purgation thesis: see his extended treatment of chapter 16 in Leviticus 1–16; its core claim is that sin and uncleanness pollute the sanctuary and that the Day of Atonement is its annual great purgation. The traditional reading, atonement for people, is presented alongside it; Lev 16:33, where both classes of objects stand in one verse, is the shared textual starting point of both.
9. samakh (H5564) with both hands (Lev 16:21): the hand-laying texts of Lev 1:4 and 4:4 use a single hand; this chapter expressly says "both hands" and adds the confession of all sins. The contrast is textual fact; the inference of "total transfer" drawn from it is application, as marked in the text.
10. "Bear" (Lev 16:22): the goat bears the iniquities to an uninhabited land; its imagery stands near the language of the Servant bearing iniquities in Isaiah 53. The relation between the two is cross-textual interpretation; when cited, its layer should be named, and Isaiah 53 should not be used directly to define the original sense of Lev 16.
11. 'innah nefesh (H6031 plus H5315): the wording of Lev 16:29, 31 agrees with Lev 23:27, 32 and Num 29:7; later Jewish tradition took fasting as its chief form, while this chapter itself pairs it with abstention from all work.
12. shabbat shabbaton (H7676 plus H7677): a doubling of cognate words denoting complete rest; Lev 23:32 uses the expression for the Day of Atonement as well. The Chinese base text renders Lev 16:31 with the sense "solemn Sabbath"; this sermon's argument rests on the Hebrew doubling together with that rendering, not on either alone.
13. The reinterpretation of the Day of Atonement in Hebrews 9-10 (once a year, alone, with blood) and the hilasterion of Rom 3:25 both belong to the New Testament reading layer; the sermon has kept them on that layer in Section IV, unmixed with the original sense of Leviticus 16.

## Devotional Excerpt

On the tenth day of the seventh month, Israel could do nothing. No work, no helping, no sharing even one step of the high priest's task; the only thing commanded of them was to afflict their souls and wait in quiet. Cleansing was not something they produced. As the text says: "For on that day shall the priest make an atonement for you, to cleanse you, that ye may be clean from all your sins before the Lord." (Lev 16:30, KJV) The atonement was accomplished inside the great veil, where no one saw; what the people received in the camp was only the announcement: you are clean. In our lives today, we too are tempted to do something to make up for our debts, and busyness is sometimes only a way of making the conscience feel better. But the Day of Atonement holds our hands still: first stop, first confess, first receive. The confession with both hands on the goat's head was specific; the blood sprinkled on the mercy seat was real; and the goat sent into the wilderness does not come back by itself. May we today exchange specific confession for vague self-reproach, and quiet receiving for hurried compensation. Try praying this way: name this year's debts to him one by one, without explanation and without embellishment, and when you have finished, stop, and leave the outcome with the Lord who shows mercy before the mercy seat. To stop is not to give up; it is to confess that the source of cleansing is not in our hands. He has appointed the way — so let us draw near, in his way, with confidence.

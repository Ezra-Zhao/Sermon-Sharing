# What God Putteth in My Mouth, That Shall I Speak — A Sermon on Numbers 22

Author: Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and the commentaries
Status: Translated in full from the finalized Chinese manuscript, 25 October 2026; draft translation pending native-speaker proofreading
Text: Numbers chapter 22 (41 verses in full; the verse numbering is the same in the Chinese, English, and Hebrew canonical divisions of the chapter — see Endnote 1)
Base texts: English quotations are embedded verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), checked verse by verse against the fixed anchor text for this chapter (sacred-texts and Bible Gateway KJV, cross-verified from two sources); the companion Chinese manuscript quotes the Chinese Union Version, New Punctuation Edition (Simplified, "Shen" edition; hereafter "the Chinese base text"). The English text is not a back-translation of the Chinese quotations: every quotation below is KJV wording, and where the two base texts differ in wording, the difference is registered, not harmonized.
Title verification: The title is taken from Balaam's declaration to Balak on his arrival in Moab at verse 38, in the KJV's own wording at that verse — "the word that God putteth in my mouth, that shall I speak" (22:38). The chapter's three movements — Balak's two summonings of Balaam (22:1-20), the ass and the angel on the road (22:21-35), and Balak's welcome and the prospect from the high place (22:36-41) — are threaded by one and the same boundary: what this mouth may say is decided neither by the man who offers the price nor by the man who opens the mouth, but only by the God who puts the word in it. God issues this boundary first (22:12, 20), the angel restates it on the road (22:35), and Balaam repeats it at verse 38 to the king who offered the price, face to face. The candidates were checked verse by verse: "for they are blessed" (22:12) covers only the prohibition of the first movement; "What have I done unto thee, that thou hast smitten me these three times?" (22:28) governs only the road section; "thy way is perverse before me" (22:32) is the angel's word of condemnation and governs only the ass episode; only the sentence at verse 38 is the chapter-wide restatement of every section's boundary, and it lands in the first person on the speaker himself — it is therefore the title. The Chinese title is taken from the same verse in its own base text; each manuscript follows its own base text.
Base-text notes (where the two base texts or common paraphrase differ, each base text is recorded as it stands): At 22:3 the KJV reads "And Moab was sore afraid of the people, because they were many: and Moab was distressed because of the children of Israel" — not a loose paraphrase on the order of "Moab was very afraid of the Israelites." At 22:5 Balaam's home is "to Pethor, which is by the river of the land of the children of his people," and his father is Beor. At 22:7 what the messengers carry is the "rewards of divination" — not a generic "payment" or "gift." At 22:12 God's prohibition reads "Thou shalt not go with them; thou shalt not curse the people: for they are blessed" — the ground lies in the people's blessed state, not in Balaam's willingness. At 22:18 the KJV reads "If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the word of the LORD my God, to do less or more": the words "my God" are Balaam's own designation and are recorded as they stand; and where the companion Chinese base text phrases the limit as doing neither great things nor small beyond the LORD's command, the KJV phrases it as doing "less or more" beyond the word — each manuscript keeps its own wording, and the difference is registered here. At 22:22 the KJV reads "And God's anger was kindled because he went" — not the softened "God was displeased that he went." At 22:28 the ass's question reads "What have I done unto thee" — not a colloquial expansion. At 22:32 the angel names his conduct "perverse" ("thy way is perverse before me") — not "rebellious." At 22:31 the KJV reads "Then the LORD opened the eyes of Balaam": the construction is a direct opening of the eyes, whereas the companion Chinese base text renders the effect as making Balaam's eyes "bright" — the underlying construction (an uncovering of the eyes, so that they see) is registered in the (Summary) of Part Three, and each manuscript keeps its own rendering. At 22:40 the KJV reads "And Balak offered oxen and sheep": the sacrificial verb stands in the KJV's own main text, whereas the companion Chinese base text's main text reads "slaughtered," with its own footnote "the original reads 'offered'" — a cross-version difference of placement, registered here and in Endnote 3, not harmonized.
Form: A verbatim script of about sixty minutes. The main text is divided into five parts with verse ranges and time codes (00:00-08:00 / 08:00-24:00 / 24:00-44:00 / 44:00-52:00 / 52:00-60:00). Paragraphs are marked (Fact) / (Argument) / (Summary) / (New Testament Reading) / (Scholarly and Historical Discussion) to keep the layers distinct. Original-language words appear only in the (Summary) paragraphs as philological statements; material from other chapters enters only as cross-reference, with its layer marked, and is never read back into this chapter's fact layer. Endnotes and a bibliography are appended.

## Summary

In Numbers 21 Israel gained the land east of the Jordan; in Numbers 22 they encamp in the plains of Moab, opposite Jericho (22:1). The new enemy does not send out an army first; he sends out an offer. Balak king of Moab sees all that Israel had done to the Amorites and is afraid; he sends messengers far away to Pethor, by the river, to summon the prophet Balaam to curse Israel (22:2-6). At the first summoning God says plainly that he is not to go and not to curse, "for they are blessed" (22:12). At the second summoning the messengers are more numerous and more honourable and the honour offered is greater still; Balaam keeps them for the night and asks again, and God permits him to go, on one condition: he is to do only the word that God speaks (22:15-20). In the morning Balaam saddles his ass and sets out, but God's anger is kindled because he went; the angel stands in the way with a drawn sword as an adversary; the ass sees three times, turns aside three times, is smitten three times, and opens her mouth and speaks; Balaam's eyes are opened, he sees the angel, bows down and confesses his sin, and the angel lets him pass and restates the condition (22:21-35). Balaam arrives; Balak comes out to meet him at the border city; and Balaam declares to his face: what God puts in my mouth, that shall I speak (22:36-38). At the chapter's end Balak brings him up into the high places of Baal, and he sees the utmost part of the camp of Israel (22:41) — the curse has not yet left his mouth, and the boundary has already been set first.

## Main Text

### Part One: Moab's Fear and Balak's Calculation (Numbers 22:1-6) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) Verse 1 continues from the previous chapter, quoted verbatim from the base text: "And the children of Israel set forward, and pitched in the plains of Moab on this side Jordan by Jericho." (22:1) The company is by now on the far side of the promised land, facing Jericho across the river; yet the conflict of this chapter is not launched against them, and they do not have so much as one line of dialogue — the whole chapter is other people busying themselves about them. Verses 2-4 are the fear on Moab's side, quoted verbatim: "And Balak the son of Zippor saw all that Israel had done to the Amorites. And Moab was sore afraid of the people, because they were many: and Moab was distressed because of the children of Israel. And Moab said unto the elders of Midian, Now shall this company lick up all that are round about us, as the ox licketh up the grass of the field. And Balak the son of Zippor was king of the Moabites at that time." (22:2-4) Registered item by item: Balak's fear has a ground — what he saw was the double defeat of Sihon and Og in chapter 21; the elders of Moab and Midian are here in allied council; and the figure of "lick up" is on the lips of the Moabites — it is the rhetoric of fear, registered as speech, not stated as fact.

(Fact) In verses 5-6 Balak's countermeasure is not to mobilize an army but to send messengers far away to summon a curser; his words are quoted verbatim: "Behold, there is a people come out from Egypt: behold, they cover the face of the earth, and they abide over against me: Come now therefore, I pray thee, curse me this people; for they are too mighty for me: peradventure I shall prevail, that we may smite them, and that I may drive them out of the land: for I wot that he whom thou blessest is blessed, and he whom thou cursest is cursed." (22:5-6) Registered item by item: the man summoned is "Balaam the son of Beor," whose home is Pethor, by the river, Balaam's own country (22:5); Balak's intelligence on Israel has only three items — they have come out of Egypt, they cover the face of the earth, they are too mighty for him; his order of warfare is curse first, then smite — the curse is the first procedure of the war; and the foundation of his confidence is one sentence: "he whom thou blessest is blessed, and he whom thou cursest is cursed" — Balak firmly believes that words have real efficacy, and it is precisely this efficacy that he means to buy. Register also this: nowhere in the whole passage does Balak mention the name of Israel's God; he treats Balaam as a source of power independent of any god — the first miscalculation in his reckoning.

(Argument) The bone of this section is how fear chooses its tool. A man afraid to the extreme grabs whatever is at hand; what Balak grabbed was not a sword but a mouth. He understood one thing better than many men: in some battles, victory and defeat are settled in words before the fighting ever starts — in his eyes blessing and cursing were not ritual but weaponry. But he understood only half of it: the efficacy of words is real, yet the sovereignty over words is not in the speaker's hand. He supposed a curse to be a machine that could be hired — pay enough, engage the right man, and it would start up; in the forty verses that follow, God will correct the ownership of that machine in person. The church has its own Balak-style calculations: treating prayer as technique, blessing as a resource, and spiritual people as tools to be dispatched. Before you open your mouth, ask one question first: what I am trying to buy — God's presence, or a man to speak for me?

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. Balak is Bālāq (H1111); Balaam is Bilʿām (H1109). "Curse" in this section is ʾārar (H779); "bless" is bārak (H1288). For Balaam's father "Beor," Bəʿôr, and his home "Pethor," Pəṯôr, this sermon marks the Strong's numbers as pending verification; it does not fill them in from memory — see Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) On Balaam's survival outside the Bible: the plaster inscription found at Deir ʿAlla in Jordan in 1967 (about the eighth century BC) mentions "Balaam son of Beor, a seer of the gods"; scholarship mostly takes this to refer to the same figure as this chapter's, or to a common source. Its date, its character, and its relation to the biblical narrative are much discussed — stated here, not adjudicated. The main text of this sermon establishes only this chapter's narrative facts: Balaam lived at Pethor by the river and practised cursing by divination for a living (cross-checked by the "rewards of divination" of 22:7), and was the joint invitee of Moab and Midian.

### Part Two: Two Sets of Messengers — Refusal, a Raised Price, and a Conditional Permission (Numbers 22:7-20) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) The messengers of the first summoning arrive, quoted verbatim: "And the elders of Moab and the elders of Midian departed with the rewards of divination in their hand; and they came unto Balaam, and spake unto him the words of Balak." (22:7) The words "rewards of divination" are entered in the record: this was no courtesy visit; it was a transaction that arrived carrying its price. Balaam's reply, quoted verbatim: "And he said unto them, Lodge here this night, and I will bring you word again, as the LORD shall speak unto me: and the princes of Moab abode with Balaam." (22:8) He neither refused on the spot nor accepted on the spot — he kept the men for the night, to ask God in the night. In verse 9 God comes of His own initiative, quoted verbatim: "And God came unto Balaam, and said, What men are these with thee?" (22:9) God's question is not ignorance; He means Balaam himself to give an account of where this transaction comes from. After Balaam relays Balak's words (22:10-11), verse 12 is God's verdict, quoted verbatim in full: "And God said unto Balaam, Thou shalt not go with them; thou shalt not curse the people: for they are blessed." (22:12) Two prohibitions and one ground: do not go, do not curse — for they are blessed. "Blessed" here is not a statement of Israel's merit; it is a state God has already fixed; a curse is impossible not because Balaam is unwilling but because its object does not stand among the curseable. In verse 13 Balaam passes the answer on, quoted verbatim: "And Balaam rose up in the morning, and said unto the princes of Balak, Get you into your land: for the LORD refuseth to give me leave to go with you." (22:13) The envoys go back and report only one sentence: "Balaam refuseth to come with us." (22:14) — God's refusal, by the time it reaches Balak's ear, has become Balaam's refusal; the refusal changes flavour in the relaying, and that changed flavour is precisely what leads into the second summoning.

(Fact) In the verse where Balaam relays Balak's words to God there hides an omission, registered here by reading the two passages of the base text against each other. At the end of Balak's original words stands a letter of credit: "for I wot that he whom thou blessest is blessed, and he whom thou cursest is cursed." (22:6) When Balaam relays the message (22:10-11), that sentence is gone; what he reports is the facts — a people come out of Egypt, covering the face of the earth, an invitation to curse, perhaps to drive them out — and he omits, alone, the sentence that complimented his own power. This chapter does not state the motive of the omission; only the textual fact is registered: relaying the message before God, Balaam removed the part that praised himself. Whether out of reverence or out of rhetoric, this omission gives God's question in verse 9 its footing all the more: "What men are these with thee?" — what God wants to hear is precisely how Balaam himself narrates this transaction. God registers the omissions men make when they pray to Him: when we omit the part that compliments ourselves, that is honesty; when we omit the part that tells against ourselves, that is concealment. Balaam's omission here cut a compliment; a pity that by verse 19 the "more" he kept back for himself would cost far more than a compliment.

(Fact) The second summoning raises the price on every front, quoted verbatim: "And Balak sent yet again princes, more, and more honourable than they." (22:15) The messengers' words, quoted verbatim: "And they came to Balaam, and said to him, Thus saith Balak the son of Zippor, Let nothing, I pray thee, hinder thee from coming unto me: For I will promote thee unto very great honour, and I will do whatsoever thou sayest unto me: come therefore, I pray thee, curse me this people." (22:16-17) Three items in the raise: the number and rank of the delegation upgraded; the honour raised from nothing to "very great honour"; and the price raised from the rewards of divination to the blank cheque of "whatsoever thou sayest unto me." Balaam's answer falls into two halves. The first half is the most dignified sentence in the chapter, quoted verbatim in full: "And Balaam answered and said unto the servants of Balak, If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the word of the LORD my God, to do less or more." (22:18) — a house full of silver and gold is produced, and he answers that he cannot go beyond. But the second half is a crack, quoted verbatim: "Now therefore, I pray you, tarry ye also here this night, that I may know what the LORD will say unto me more." (22:19) The word "more" is entered in the record: God has already finished speaking — do not go, do not curse, for they are blessed — yet Balaam keeps the men for another night, to hear what the LORD will say to him "more." In verse 20 God comes in the night, quoted verbatim in full: "And God came unto Balaam at night, and said unto him, If the men come to call thee, rise up, and go with them; but yet the word which I shall say unto thee, that shalt thou do." (22:20) Permission and condition are issued in the same sentence: go, you may; but what you say is not yours to decide — "the word which I shall say unto thee, that shalt thou do" is the rule for everything that happens in this chapter from here on.

(Argument) This section must expound the word "more" thoroughly. The most dangerous moment in a man's hearing of God's word is not open resistance; it is when he has heard clearly, and repeated it correctly, and asks once more. Balaam's first refusal was beyond reproach: God does not permit it, I will not go. The problem came in his response to the second raise — if his heart had truly agreed with his first answer, when the second delegation came through his door he should have dismissed them with the same sentence, and not even needed to keep them the night. He kept the night. Keeping the night is itself an action: it leaves a door-ajar for a matter already settled, in case it can be renegotiated — what if God changes His mind? A house full of silver and gold and very great honour need not buy his mouth first; it is enough if they first buy his ears — and make him willing to listen once more. People today deal with what God has already said in the same way: knowing the answer, they pray again, "Lord, look at it once more"; knowing the boundary, they ask again, "May this once be an exception?" God's second answer did not alter the boundary; it only put Balaam on the road he had begged for himself, and nailed the condition down tighter: go wherever you like — you have no control over what you say. A road a man insists on walking, God sometimes permits — permission is not approval; it is handing a man over to his own choice while tightening the one rope that truly matters.

(Argument) Read verses 18 and 19 side by side and you have the sharpest character sketch in the chapter; it deserves a lesson of its own. The first half of the sentence takes the words to the summit: a house full of silver and gold, and I will not go beyond — if the sentence stopped there, Balaam would be a man proof against any price. The ruin is in the turn of the second half: having spoken the summit words, he did not show his guests out; he kept his guests. Verbal resolution and practical lingering in the same frame show that a man's corruption is often not that he lacks the right words; it is that after the right words there is still a "but." We should measure ourselves the same way: do not only listen to what you said in the face of temptation; look at what you did after you said it — did you close the door and show them out, or did you keep them for the night? Balaam's two nights also form a contrast: on the first night God came, and the word closed the door; on the second night God also came, and the word opened a crack. God's words do not contradict each other front and back — the prohibition was aimed at the curse, the permission at the journey — what contradicts itself is the hearer: he took the permission he heard on the second night as a loosening of the first night's prohibition. The commonest self-deception of hearers of the word is to take a later sentence and dilute an earlier one with it. God's word must be heard whole: there is a condition inside the permission, and the condition is the prohibition continued; take only the half-sentence that suits you, and even the most complete revelation will go perverse in our hands.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The original of "rewards of divination" is qəṣāmîm, from qesem (H7081), divination, the price obtained for divination; it occurs only here in this chapter (cross-reference: the same word at Numbers 23:23 denotes divination itself). The "curse" in the second delegation's words is qābâ (H6895), a different word from the ʾārar (H779) of Balak's original words in verse 6: Balak's first words speak of cursing in general terms; the messengers and Balaam's relay (22:11, 17) switch to qābâ, more specifically cursing by name; and when God answers at verse 12 He uses the ʾārar prohibition again. This sermon registers each verse's own word separately and does not merge them. The original of "honour" belongs to the kābôd family (the verb family H3513): verse 17 promises "very great honour," and at verse 37 Balak asks again, "am I not able indeed to promote thee to honour?" — the same root at both places, the head and tail of the chapter's price-line answering each other.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) The tension between the permission of verse 20 and the anger of verse 22 has always been the centre of discussion for this section. One reading takes the conditional clause "If the men come to call thee" as the key — the permission carried the order of waiting to be called, and Balaam's saddling his ass in the morning and going off with the princes of his own motion stepped over the condition of waiting. Another reading takes the motive as the key — God was angry not at the act of going but at the heart that went. The scholarly and commentary traditions set out both readings side by side, stated here, not adjudicated. The main text of this sermon establishes only the two facts the base text expressly records: the permission carried a condition (22:20), and God's anger was kindled because he went (22:22); the base text adds no explanation of the reason for the anger, and every explanation belongs to the discussion layer and is not asserted as fact.

### Part Three: The Ass and the Angel with the Drawn Sword on the Road (Numbers 22:21-35) (24:00-44:00)

(Fact) In verse 21 Balaam sets out, quoted verbatim: "And Balaam rose up in the morning, and saddled his ass, and went with the princes of Moab." (22:21) In verse 22 the narrative gives God's reaction at once, quoted verbatim in full: "And God's anger was kindled because he went: and the angel of the LORD stood in the way for an adversary against him. Now he was riding upon his ass, and his two servants were with him." (22:22) Registered item by item: the object of the anger is "he went," this act; the base text does not state a deeper reason for the anger (for the discussion layer, see the note appended after the (Summary) of Part Two); and the angel's position is "in the way" and his posture that of an adversary — God's angel bars, in an adversary's identity, the very road God permitted: this is the chapter's greatest surface contradiction, and the main text sets both facts out as they stand, without harmonizing them. In the three acts that follow, the positions of the ass and the angel tighten act by act. Act one, on the road: "And the ass saw the angel of the LORD standing in the way, and his sword drawn in his hand: and the ass turned aside out of the way, and went into the field: and Balaam smote the ass, to turn her into the way." (22:23) Act two, on a narrow path: "But the angel of the LORD stood in a path of the vineyards, a wall being on this side, and a wall on that side. And when the ass saw the angel of the LORD, she thrust herself unto the wall, and crushed Balaam's foot against the wall: and he smote her again." (22:24-25) Act three, at the place of no escape: "And the angel of the LORD went further, and stood in a narrow place, where was no way to turn either to the right hand or to the left. And when the ass saw the angel of the LORD, she fell down under Balaam: and Balaam's anger was kindled, and he smote the ass with a staff." (22:26-27) Read the gradient of the three acts as it stands: the road goes from wide to narrow to no road at all; the ass's turning aside goes from going into the field to thrusting against the wall to falling down; the smiting goes from smote to smote again to smote with a staff — at every escalation of the blocking, Balaam's anger escalates too; the one thing never seen is his stopping to ask one question: why?

(Fact) In verse 28 the ass opens her mouth, quoted verbatim in full: "And the LORD opened the mouth of the ass, and she said unto Balaam, What have I done unto thee, that thou hast smitten me these three times?" (22:28) The subject of the opening is the LORD — the base text reads "the LORD opened the mouth of the ass"; the one who speaks is the ass, and the one who makes her open her mouth is God. The ass has only one question: what have I done unto thee, to deserve these three smitings? Balaam's answer, quoted verbatim: "And Balaam said unto the ass, Because thou hast mocked me: I would there were a sword in mine hand, for now would I kill thee." (22:29) — he wishes there were a sword in his hand; he does not know that right across the road stands one with a sword already drawn in his hand. In verse 30 the ass answers with a lifetime of service, quoted verbatim: "And the ass said unto Balaam, Am not I thine ass, upon which thou hast ridden ever since I was thine unto this day? was I ever wont to do so unto thee? And he said, Nay." (22:30) "Ever since I was thine unto this day" — the ass's record is long years without fault; "was I ever wont to do so unto thee?" — the turning aside has no precedent. Balaam's "Nay" is a compelled honesty: he admits with his own mouth that this ass has never done so before. The abnormality is by now plain to see: an old ass that has never once turned aside turns aside three times in a row — if a man would only ask one question in the second act, the staff of the third act might never need to fall.

(Fact) In verse 31 his eyes are opened, quoted verbatim in full: "Then the LORD opened the eyes of Balaam, and he saw the angel of the LORD standing in the way, and his sword drawn in his hand: and he bowed down his head, and fell flat on his face." (22:31) On the wording: the KJV reads "opened the eyes," a direct opening; the companion Chinese base text renders the effect with the word "bright" — each is registered from its own base text (see the Base-text notes at the head of this sermon); the bowing down is the first time in the whole chapter that Balaam bows before God's power. In verses 32-33 the angel opens his mouth and settles the case, quoted verbatim in full: "And the angel of the LORD said unto him, Wherefore hast thou smitten thine ass these three times? behold, I went out to withstand thee, because thy way is perverse before me: And the ass saw me, and turned from me these three times: unless she had turned from me, surely now also I had slain thee, and saved her alive." (22:32-33) Registered item by item: the angel's first question is in the same form as the ass's first question — "Wherefore hast thou smitten thine ass these three times?" God asks first on the ass's behalf, then answers Himself; the word of condemnation is "perverse" — the charge lies not in having travelled this road but in the perverseness of the way he went about it; and the heaviest sentence falls on the ass: "surely now also I had slain thee, and saved her alive" — the ass smitten three times is the one who saved her master, and the one her master wished he could kill is precisely the one who kept her master from going to his death. In verse 34 Balaam confesses, quoted verbatim: "And Balaam said unto the angel of the LORD, I have sinned; for I knew not that thou stoodest in the way against me: now therefore, if it displease thee, I will get me back again." (22:34) In verse 35 the angel lets him pass and restates the condition, quoted verbatim in full: "And the angel of the LORD said unto Balaam, Go with the men: but only the word that I shall speak unto thee, that thou shalt speak. So Balaam went with the princes of Balak." (22:35) The condition of the release has the same sense as God's word in verse 20 — the road he may go has not widened; the words he may speak have been locked down first.

(Argument) This section speaks first of the asymmetry of seeing. The ass saw the angel; Balaam could not see; the ass had no theology — what she saw was the danger itself; Balaam was a professional seer — his trade was precisely seeing what others could not see — and this time he was so blind that his mount had to save his life. Spiritual dullness never lacks knowledge; what it lacks is stopping. Balaam did not not know that God could block him; he wanted too much to press forward, so much that he would not even investigate his old ass's abnormal behaviour, and only laid on harder. Three smitings of the ass are the instinctive reaction of everyone in a hurry to every obstruction: the obstruction must come from below, from the tool, from the disobedient mount — never from above. But this chapter writes it plainly: the obstruction stood in the way, sword in hand — it was God Himself. Hear the sentence "thy way is perverse before me" clearly: the angel did not settle the ass's account first; he settled the man's conduct first. The ass's three turnings aside were righteousness; the man's three swings of the staff were perverseness — on God's account, the one who kept deviating from the direction was never that ass. Today we do the same: a plan obstructed, we first blame the environment, blame our fellow workers, blame the one at hand least able to argue back; if the ass did not open her mouth, most of us would go to our deaths believing the fault was the ass's. To be willing, when obstructed, to ask first, "Is it God standing in the way?" — that is this section's first lesson for everyone on a road.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. The original of "adversary" is śāṭān (H7854), here a common noun denoting an adversary, one who withstands (the same word twice, at 22:22 and 22:32); this chapter does not read it as a proper name — for the reading layer, see Endnote 6. "Ass" is ʾāṯôn (H860), denoting specifically a she-ass; "smote" is nāḵâ (H5221); "angel" is malʾāk (H4397). The original of "perverse" is yāraṭ (H3399), occurring in the whole Bible only here and at Job 16:11, meaning to rush headlong, to deviate from the right way; this sermon registers it by the base text's own word, "perverse." The "opened the eyes" of verse 31 rests on gālâ (H1540, to uncover) used together with "eye," ʿayin (H5869) — to uncover the eyes is to make them see; the companion Chinese base text renders the effect with the word "bright," while the KJV renders the act itself as "opened the eyes" — this sermon registers only the original construction, and its wording follows its own base text. The numbers above were each checked before writing (for the method of checking, see Endnote 5).

(New Testament Reading) The New Testament reads Balaam back in three places, with a consistently severe verdict. In key points: 2 Peter says that he loved the wages of unrighteousness, and states expressly that the dumb ass, speaking with man's voice, forbade the madness of the prophet (2 Pet 2:15-16, key points); Jude lists "the error of Balaam" among the patterns of false teachers (Jude 11, key points); and Revelation rebukes the church in Pergamos for holding "the doctrine of Balaam" (Rev 2:14, key points). The common point of the three readings back: the New Testament does not deny that Balaam spoke God's words; its condemnation lands on the motive and the outcome — the man who loves the wages may have his mouth held by God for a time, but his way ends perverse all the same. At this chapter's fact layer only this chapter's own events are registered: God held this mouth for this chapter's journey; the New Testament's reading back of the man and of what followed is set out at the reading layer, side by side, and is not filled back into this chapter.

(Fact) This section has two more figures who are silent throughout, registered from the base text: when Balaam set out, "his two servants were with him" (22:22). The three acts of the ass, the ass's opening her mouth, the angel's appearing, Balaam's falling flat on his face — all of it happened before the eyes of two servants, yet this chapter gives them not one sentence, not one reaction. Their silence is a blank left by the narrative (see Endnote 8); this sermon registers only their presence and does not supply their inner thoughts for them. Yet the blank itself carries weight: Balaam's humiliation did not happen where no one was — the objects of his everyday command were present, his mount was present, the angel from heaven was present. When God corrects a man, He does not necessarily send the bystanders away; some lessons are to be learned precisely in front of one's followers. When a leader's dignity cannot be preserved, the prophet's practice here can serve as a mark: he did not silence witnesses, he did not invent a story; he fell flat on his face, confessed his sin, and then went on up the road according to the angel's word — a sin confessed in front of one's servants is harder than one confessed in private, and truer.

(Argument) There is one more scene of human nature, hidden in the exchange of verse 29: the moment the ass opened her mouth, Balaam at once began to argue with the ass — "Because thou hast mocked me" — he did not cry out in amazement, he did not flee; his first reaction was a quarrel. A man blinded by anger is like that: a thing without precedent since the beginning of the world — an ass speaking — is set before his eyes, and all he still cares about is his own grievance and his own face; he cannot spare even the effort of being astonished. Here is where anger wastes a man most: it shrinks a signal of the first magnitude down into a private feud. The angel stood three paces away with a drawn sword, and the prophet's whole attention was on an ass; God had already put the microphone to his ear, and he used it to bicker with his mount. Do not laugh too soon as you read: how often God speaks to us through the humblest of mouths, and our first reaction is also to argue, to talk back, to save face — win this quarrel of words first, and as for the signal itself, think about it tomorrow. The ass's words were not long, yet every sentence was fact; Balaam's words were not long either, and every sentence was emotion. This chapter sets these two speeches side by side as a mirror: before you open your mouth, distinguish first — this sentence of mine, is it fact, or is it emotion?

(Fact) Gather up the space of the three acts into one final account (registered within this chapter itself): the angel stands three times — in "the way" (22:23), in "a path of the vineyards" (22:24), and in "a narrow place, where was no way to turn either to the right hand or to the left" (22:26) — the place of the blocking narrows act by act, and the room the ass has to retreat narrows in step: into the field she could still go, against the wall she could still thrust herself, at the place of no escape she could only fall down. Balaam's road was not sealed off with a single stroke; it was narrowed stretch by stretch — God's patience with the perverse, measured out, is precisely the width of these three acts. Register one more contrast: the sword in the angel's hand was "drawn" from the first act onward (the same wording twice, at 22:23 and 22:31), yet it never fell — a sword out of its sheath and not striking is where all the tension of this section lies: judgment was really present, mercy was really deferred, and what stood between them was exactly that smitten ass.

### Part Four: The Border City — Balak's Reproach and Balaam's Declaration (Numbers 22:36-41) (44:00-52:00)

(Fact) In verse 36 Balak comes out in person to meet him, in key points from the base text: Balak heard that Balaam had come and went out to meet him to a city of Moab, on the border of Arnon, at the utmost coast (22:36). The Arnon is the southern boundary of the land Israel gained in chapter 21 (21:24, cross-reference layer) — the place of meeting presses right on the new boundary between the two armies. Verse 37 is the reproach, quoted verbatim in full: "And Balak said unto Balaam, Did I not earnestly send unto thee to call thee? wherefore camest thou not unto me? am I not able indeed to promote thee to honour?" (22:37) The order of the three questions is: I pressed urgently enough, you came too slowly, and do you not believe I can afford the price? — at the moment of meeting face to face, the account in Balak's heart is still price. Verse 38 is Balaam's declaration, the verse on which this sermon's title rests, quoted verbatim in full: "And Balaam said unto Balak, Lo, I am come unto thee: have I now any power at all to say any thing? the word that God putteth in my mouth, that shall I speak." (22:38) Registered item by item: Balaam first acknowledges the fact of his arrival and explains nothing about the delay — of what happened on the road he does not say one word; the hinge of the declaration is "have I now any power at all to say any thing?" — he admits that this mouth has no power of its own decision; and the object of the declaration is "the word that God putteth in my mouth" — the source, the content, and the moment of the word are all God's, and Balaam occupies only the one word "speak." In verses 39-41 the procedure begins to run, quoted verbatim: "And Balaam went with Balak, and they came unto Kirjathhuzoth. And Balak offered oxen and sheep, and sent to Balaam, and to the princes that were with him. And it came to pass on the morrow, that Balak took Balaam, and brought him up into the high places of Baal, that thence he might see the utmost part of the people." (22:39-41) On "offered": the KJV's own main text reads "offered," while the companion Chinese base text's main text reads "slaughtered" with its own footnote "the original reads 'offered'" — registered in Endnote 3. The chapter ends on a looking: from the high place Balaam sees only the "utmost part" of the camp of Israel; the whole camp has not come into view, and the curse has not left his mouth — the chapter closes in the surveying.

(Argument) The sentence of verse 38 has its weight only when it is read after the reproach of verse 37. Balak asked about price; Balaam answered about power: you ask whether I believe you can afford the honour — I tell you, this transaction was never a question of price from the beginning — it is not that you do not offer enough; it is that I have no power to open my mouth on my own authority. A man tempted by a house full of silver and gold and intercepted by an angel with a drawn sword finally speaks the clearest sentence in the chapter. But it must also be said as it stands: a clear declaration is not the same as a clean heart. Balaam stated the boundary correctly, yet the man was still standing at the edge of the high places of Baal; his mouth was held by God, but his feet walked, step by step, into every place Balak had arranged. Many servants' failure does not begin with changing their words; it begins with keeping the appointment: not one word said wrong, and the man already standing on a high place where he should not stand. This chapter's final picture freezes here — a man standing on a high place the enemy chose for him, looking at the border of God's people, with God's words prepared in his mouth. What this mouth will speak in the next chapter, this chapter does not advance; this chapter only sets the boundary: the word is God's — and whether the man has first stood firm is still in the undecided heaven.

(Argument) The matter of the high place requires one more thorough word on Balak's tactics — why he let Balaam see only the "utmost part" of the camp. By Balak's reckoning, a curse is to be aimed at the part that can be seen: see it clearly first, lock onto it first, begin from one corner first. This is also the common way of fearful men: not daring to face the whole, they deal with one corner first; not daring to face the God who made the covenant, they lay hands first on the edge of His people. But God's blessing was never distributed by corners — blessedness is a judgment on the whole ("for they are blessed," 22:12), and it neither increases nor decreases according to whether what is seen is the border or the centre. The man standing on the high place supposed that the field of vision decides the efficacy; what this chapter goes on to prove is exactly the opposite: where you stand and how much you see cannot change that one sentence already fixed. If a man truly wants to deal with God's people, the pass he must get through first is not the border — it is the sentence of verse 12; and that pass the man who offers the price cannot get through, and neither can the man who is bought.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Kirjath-huzoth," Qiryat Ḥuṣôt, and "Bamoth-baal," Bāmôt-Baʿal (the KJV's "the high places of Baal"), are recorded in transliteration only, without Strong's numbers. The construction behind "have I now any power at all to say any thing?" is a rhetorical question of the form "can I say anything (at all)?"; the companion Chinese base text renders the sense with a word meaning "on my own authority," and this sermon registers each base text as it stands and sets up no separate etymology. "That God putteth in my mouth" rests on the original image of God placing the word in a man's mouth; this sermon registers only its own base text's wording.

(Fact) At the chapter's very end, settle the account of the right to speak. Within this chapter the initiative over "speaking" changes hands four times and returns at last to one Lord: Balak tries to buy words with a price (22:6); Balaam tries with his "more" to renegotiate words (22:19); God locks the words down with a condition (22:20, 35); the ass corrects the man by opening her mouth (22:28); and at the end Balaam admits, to the face of the man who offered the price: the words are not mine (22:38). In the whole chapter not one Israelite opens his mouth, and not one battle is fought, yet a declaration of sovereignty is completed — on the far side of the promised land, at the foot of a high place chosen by the enemy, God first takes back into His own possession the mouth of a foreign prophet, and only then does the next chapter's prophecy come round. Men read this chapter and look at the ass; God wrote this chapter and wrote about the mouth.

### Part Five: Before Standing on the High Place — The Whole Chapter in Review and Application (Numbers 22:41 and the Whole Chapter) (52:00-60:00)

(Fact) The chapter's data are gathered up; every item has been counted (for the details, see Endnote 4): the chapter has 41 verses in full; God comes to Balaam twice (22:9, 20); the messengers are sent in two batches (22:5, 15; the first batch arrives at 22:7); the title "the angel of the LORD" occurs ten times (22:22-27, 31-32, 34-35); the ass opens her mouth and speaks once, in two speeches (22:28, 30); the ass is smitten three times (22:23, 25, 27); the angel stands in the way in three acts (22:23, 24, 26); and Balaam falls flat on his face once (22:31). The narrative's geographical arc: Pethor (by the river) lies outside; the plains of Moab lie on this side of the Jordan; the meeting is at the border city by the Arnon; and the chapter closes at the high places of Baal — the man walks step by step from outside territory into enemy territory, and God's boundary does not retreat one step.

(Argument) The chapter closes, leaving one sentence for each of four kinds of people. First, for those summoned at a high price: Balaam's tragedy did not begin with greed; it began with "more" — God had already spoken clearly, and if you keep your guests for one more night you are leaving your own heart room to bargain; on matters where the boundary is clear, do not hold a second meeting. Second, for those obstructed on their road: an obstruction is not necessarily an enemy; sometimes it is an angel with a drawn sword. A mount behaving abnormally, fellow workers blocking you, a plan shelved three times — do not swing the staff first; ask first: is it that my way is perverse? The ass's three turnings aside saved her master; our three obstructions may be the same. Third, for those who speak — preachers, witnesses, advisers: Balaam's declaration is our duty: what God puts in my mouth, that shall I speak. The hardest part is not the speaking; it is the not speaking on our own authority: not adding on our own authority one flattering sentence, not cutting on our own authority one offending sentence, not turning on our own authority a conditional sentence into an approval. Fourth, for those standing in fear: every sentence of Balak's fear had its ground, yet he handed his kingdom over to a calculation; the Israelites sitting across in the plains of Moab said not one word and encamped as usual — the blessed need not defend themselves; God will, outside the enemy's camp and below the high place on the far side, hold the mouth of the curse first. Your duty is reduced to one thing: encamp, as usual.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) What Balaam did after the Moab narrative (Numbers 31:8, 16 records his being slain and his counsel) belongs to the fact layer of the later narrative; this chapter does not carry it, and this sermon does not read this chapter backwards from the ending — the Balaam of this chapter is the Balaam whose mouth was held, who stands on the high place, and whose prophecy has not yet left his mouth; read this chapter keeping this chapter's undecidedness: do not condemn this chapter's figure in advance on the strength of his later ruin, and do not whitewash the man on the strength of this chapter's declaration.

(Fact) Close with one paired reading of this chapter against the previous chapter (cross-reference layer). On the battlefields of Numbers 21 God's people drew the sword themselves, and two kings fell; on the battlefield of Numbers 22 the people did not move a sword and did not make a sound, and God took the battle into His own hands outside their camp — first He locked the curse with one sentence (22:12), then He intercepted the prophet with an angel (22:22), and at last He declared His sovereignty with the prophet's own mouth (22:38). The two chapters are two forms of victory: one is a victory in which God and His people work together; the other is a victory in which the people rest entirely and God is alone. Walking to the end of forty years on the wilderness road, Israel had two lessons to learn, and neither may be lacking: when it is time to lift the sword, do not flinch (Numbers 21); when it is time to encamp, do not be clever on your own account (Numbers 22). In this chapter they have not one line of dialogue, yet they are the chapter's greatest gainers — the highest evidence of blessedness is sometimes not what you have won a victory over, but that the enemy's calculations of a whole night have not, by morning, landed on your head to the value of one cent.

## Devotional Excerpt

The angel of the LORD stood in the way with his sword drawn; the professional seer could not see him, and an ass saw him. Three times she turned aside, three times she was smitten, and when she opened her mouth she asked only one thing: "What have I done unto thee, that thou hast smitten me these three times?" (22:28) The ass did not accuse her fate; she set out a lifetime of service: ever since she was his unto this day, she had never done so. Balaam was compelled to answer with one word: "Nay." Very often, when God blocks our road, the one He uses is precisely the most familiar one beside us, the one least able to speak: the delay that embarrasses you, the fellow worker who will not comply, the plan that suddenly falls down and will not walk. The moment we are obstructed we swing the staff — blame the tool first, blame the person first, blame the ass that has "mocked" us — and seldom stop to ask: is it that my way is perverse before God? (22:32) The angel's word shows that the ass's turning aside was life-saving and our persistence is the danger. At the moment of obstruction, do not strike first; look first — the drawn sword may be exactly the proof that God has not given up on you. That He is willing to stand in the way and withstand you is because He has not yet let go and let you walk through to your death. And there is one more layer of comfort, for those who have been smitten and are not understood: the ass did not succeed in vindicating herself, and Balaam did not apologize to her, yet the angel's first sentence was spoken on her behalf — "Wherefore hast thou smitten thine ass these three times?" If the wrong you have suffered has for the moment no one to hear it, there is One in heaven who opens His mouth for you first. Put the staff down and lift up your eyes: the God who stood in the way and asked the question for the ass knows likewise how you have come through these three times.

English (KJV-based):

"Then the LORD opened the eyes of Balaam, and he saw the angel of the LORD standing in the way, and his sword drawn in his hand: and he bowed down his head, and fell flat on his face." (Num 22:31, KJV) The seer could not see; the donkey could. Three times she turned aside, three times she was beaten — and every blow fell on the very creature who was saving his life. When the road narrows and everything under you refuses to move, do not strike first. The drawn sword may not mean God has abandoned your journey; it may mean He has not yet given up on stopping you.

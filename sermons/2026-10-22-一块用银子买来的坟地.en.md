## Executive Summary (Approx. 100 words)

Sarah lived to be one hundred and twenty-seven years old, and died in Kirjath Arba, which is Hebron, in the land of Canaan. Abraham mourned and wept for her, and subsequently asked the Hittites for a piece of land to bury his wife. The Hittites honored him as a prince of God, urging him to choose the best of their burial plots; Abraham insisted on purchasing the field and cave of Machpelah facing Mamre at the full price—Ephron asked four hundred shekels of silver, and Abraham weighed the silver and settled the deed in the presence of all, securing the field and cave as his possession, and there he buried Sarah. In the Promised Land, the very first parcel of land Abraham acquired by legal deed in his entire life was a burial plot. This sermon examines three things: believers weep just the same; the sojourner first buys a tomb, staking hope on the next generation; conducting business according to propriety, faith does not take petty advantage.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Peace to all brothers and sisters!

In today's chapter, there are no visions, no angels, no miracles. What we have is an old man over one hundred and thirty years old, weeping beside his deceased wife; then wiping away his tears, walking into the crowd at the city gate, and negotiating a business transaction. Many people skip this chapter when reading the Bible, thinking it trivial—measuring land, weighing silver, mutual polite demurrals. Yet the Bible devotes an entire chapter to record it. An event as monumental as Mount Moriah took nineteen verses, whereas this chapter takes twenty verses. God regards "burying a person" just as weighty as "offering up a person."

Sarah is the only woman in the Bible whose age, death, and burial are all specifically recorded. Her one hundred and twenty-seven years: following her husband out of Ur, going down to Egypt, being taken into royal palaces twice, laughing behind the tent door, and giving birth to Isaac at ninety. This woman completed her journey through life. Today, we accompany Abraham in seeing to her funeral.

Let us first read the key scripture passages:

"Sarah lived one hundred and twenty-seven years; these were the years of the life of Sarah. So Sarah died in Kirjath Arba (that is, Hebron) in the land of Canaan, and Abraham came to mourn for Sarah and to weep for her." (Gen. 23:1-2)

"Then Abraham stood up from before his dead, and spoke to the sons of Heth, saying, 'I am a foreigner and a visitor among you; give me property for a burial place among you, that I may bury my dead out of my sight.'" (Gen. 23:3-4)

"Then Abraham stood up and bowed himself to the people of the land, the sons of Heth. And he spoke with them, saying, 'If it is your mind that I should bury my dead out of my sight, hear me, and meet with Ephron the son of Zohar for me, that he may give me the cave of Machpelah which he has, which is at the end of his field. Let him give it to me at the full price, as property for a burial place among you.'" (Gen. 23:7-9)

"Ephron answered Abraham... 'My lord, listen to me; the land is worth four hundred shekels of silver. What is that between you and me? So bury your dead.'" (Gen. 23:14-15)

"So the field of Ephron which was in Machpelah, which was before Mamre, the field and the cave which was in it, and all the trees that were in the field, which were within all the surrounding borders, were deeded to Abraham as a possession in the presence of the sons of Heth, before all who went in at the gate of his city." (Gen. 23:17-18)

Let us pray: Lord, teach us a quiet lesson today: how to handle arrangements after death, how to be sojourners on earth, and how to stake our hope upon Your promise. Comfort the brokenhearted among us, and strengthen our faith. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part 1: Mourning and Weeping for Her—Believers First Weep Properly (23:1-2)

"Sarah lived one hundred and twenty-seven years; these were the years of the life of Sarah." The Bible gives a summary number for her life. One hundred and twenty-seven years—this is a long life, a blessing. Yet no matter how beautiful the number, it cannot stop the next sentence: "So Sarah died in Kirjath Arba (that is, Hebron) in the land of Canaan." And then: "Abraham came to mourn for Sarah and to weep for her."

Mourn and weep—the original Hebrew is *safad* (H5594), to mourn, to beat the breast and wail; weep, *bakah* (H1058), to shed tears, to weep. Abraham is the father of faith, a man who had just come down from Mount Moriah; yet he weeps, and he weeps right before the lens of the biblical text, with the Scripture making no attempt to conceal it. Brothers and sisters, this corrects a common misunderstanding many people have about faith: supposing that those who believe in God should not cry, and that crying indicates a lack of faith. Not so. The Lord Jesus, standing before the tomb of Lazarus, wept as well (John 11:35). Tears are not the opposite of faith; tears are the outward expression of love—you do not weep for those who mean nothing to you. Abraham's faith is displayed not in his not weeping, but in his being able to stand up after weeping.

There is another detail often overlooked: Sarah died in Hebron, and Abraham "came" to mourn for her—the word "came" hints that he was not present by her side at that moment. Based on this, some commentators surmise that after Moriah, father, son, and wife had their respective itineraries. We will not dwell deeply on this; we need only grasp one point: not being present for the final journey does not mean love is absent. Abraham hurried back, mourning, weeping, and handling the funeral arrangements, missing not a single thing. The final tribute to the departed lies not in pomp, but in the sincerity of the heart, the completeness of propriety, and seeing the matter through to the end.

"Then Abraham stood up from before his dead"—this plain sentence is the turning point of this chapter. A person cannot stay bent down before the dead forever; mourning has its hour, and standing up to attend to matters is also godliness. First Thessalonians tells us that we do not "sorrow as others who have no hope" (1 Thess. 4:13). The sorrow that possesses hope knows how to weep, and also knows how to rise.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part 2: I Am a Sojourner Here—A Self-Designation from the Lips of a Prince (23:3-16)

Abraham stood up and walked among the Hittites. The title the Hittites gave him was exceedingly noble: "Hear us, my lord: You are a mighty prince among us." Prince, in Hebrew *nasi* (H5387), an exalted one, a ruler; they went on to say, simply bury your dead in the choicest of our burial places, none of us will withhold it from you. Yet the way Abraham addressed himself was of an entirely different nature: "I am a foreigner and a visitor among you." Foreigner and visitor, *ger* (H1616) and *toshav* (H8453) used together—a temporary resident, a rootless outsider.

A man seen as a prince in the eyes of the locals identified himself as a sojourner. This was not false modesty; this was Abraham's self-understanding: this land was promised by God, but at present it did not yet belong to him; he lived here as a guest. Hebrews penetrates straight to the core: "These all died in faith... and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth" (Heb. 11:13). Brothers and sisters, how you define yourself determines how you live this life: if you view yourself as the owner, you will strive and fight over everything; if you view yourself as a sojourning pilgrim, you will know how to travel light, how to wait, and how to stake your true inheritance on the future.

The process of this negotiation is worth examining frame by frame. Abraham refused a free burial site. The more generous the Hittites were—choose the best, go ahead and bury—the more he insisted on buying, specifically naming the cave of Machpelah and requesting that the seller, Ephron, sell it at full price. Why? A favor received for free is a debt: accepted today, it can be revoked tomorrow with a single word. A purchased property comes with a deed, with witnesses, and endures. He wanted to give Sarah a resting place that no one could take away, and he also wanted to leave a legal root in this land for his descendants. Taking advantage of others for the sake of faith is the worst kind of testimony; Abraham did the reverse: for the sake of faith, the accounts must be settled all the more clearly.

Ephron’s asking price was couched in the polite etiquette of Eastern negotiation: "The land is worth four hundred shekels of silver. What is that between you and me?"—fine words, but not a single penny knocked off the price. Four hundred shekels was an enormous sum (later, when David bought the threshing floor of Araunah as the site for building the temple, it was merely fifty shekels of silver by comparison; Jeremiah bought a field for seventeen shekels). How did Abraham counteroffer? He did not haggle. He "listened" to Ephron and weighed out the silver before all the people—silver at that time was not minted coinage, but was weighed. Current silver used among merchants, not a fraction short. Before all the people, at the city gate, silver weighed, deed established: everything was done so as to withstand any future scrutiny.

Here shines the light of worldly wisdom: matters of intense emotion, matters of sorrow, matters of faith—all must be handled coolly and properly. When emotions run highest, procedures must be most scrupulous. How many family disputes arise from ambiguous accounts when an elder passes away; Abraham, on his most sorrowful day, completed the most meticulous legal procedures in the entire chapter. Godliness is not the opposite of clear-headedness—a godly person keeps the clearest accounts of all.

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: A Tomb Acquired First—The First Estate in the Promised Land (23:17-20)

Once the procedures were complete, the text itemizes the transaction in the tone of a legal document: "So the field of Ephron which was in Machpelah, which was before Mamre, the field and the cave which was in it, and all the trees that were in the field, which were within all the surrounding borders, were deeded to Abraham... as a possession." "And after this, Abraham buried Sarah his wife in the cave of the field of Machpelah, before Mamre (that is, Hebron) in the land of Canaan." (23:19)

Pause and think about what a tremendous irony, and yet what profound faith, this was: God had promised him that all the families of the earth would be blessed through him, that his descendants would be as numerous as the stars, and that they would possess this entire land. Yet throughout Abraham's whole life, the only piece of land he acquired with deed and cold, hard silver was this one—a cemetery. The very first piece of property he possessed in the promised land was a place to lay the dead. By human calculation, this deal was an utter loss: spending an enormous fortune to buy a tomb, without the living enjoying a single inch of it.

Yet Abraham was not calculating the accounts of this present life. In burying his wife in this land, he was effectively setting up a boundary stone: our people are buried here; our descendants will return to live here. This burial ground became the anchor for the entire family: later, Abraham himself was buried here (25:9); Isaac and Rebekah were buried here (49:31); Leah was buried here; and finally, Jacob was brought back from Egypt and also buried here (50:13). The cave of Machpelah became the ancestral tomb of the patriarchs. A man on the verge of death in Egypt charged his children and grandchildren to carry him back to this cave—because where you are buried shows where you recognize as home.

The Book of Hebrews says that Abraham "waited for the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God" (Heb. 11:10). He bought not a palace, but only a burial plot, precisely because he knew: the true city is not on this earth; this piece of land was merely a pledge. Brothers and sisters, this is how hope operates: it does not evade reality—it still weeps, it still attends to procedures; nor is it confined by reality—it wagers its greatest stake on the future. How we spend our money today, how we acquire property, how we arrange our final affairs—all of these silently proclaim: where, in the end, do we truly recognize as home?

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

The first kind: those who are currently grieving. Weep fully. Faith does not abolish tears; it gives tears an appointed time to rise up. When you are done weeping, taking care of the final arrangements one by one is your final service to the one you love.

The second kind: those who are careless about end-of-life affairs. While your mind is still clear, make clear what needs to be entrusted, and establish properly the procedures that need to be established. Abraham settled the accounts on his most painful day—loving your family includes leaving no disputes behind for them.

The third kind: those who consider themselves "masters." Striving so fiercely on this earth comes from forgetting that you are a sojourner. Set your identity right today: we are pilgrims; loosen your grip a little, broaden your heart a little—the true inheritance lies ahead.

The fourth kind: those who look for shortcuts in getting things done. Refuse the land offered freely out of human obligation; buy it at full price. Faith does not give us the privilege of taking advantage of others; procedures that can withstand scrutiny are the most enduring testimony.

The fifth kind: those who are anxious because they have no guarantee for the future. The pledge of Abraham's whole life began with a single tomb. If you have only a tiny pledge in hand, first hold it fast and bury it in the right place—God's promises are not fulfilled according to the size of what we possess.

The sixth kind: those who leave something behind for their children. The most weighty thing Abraham left to Isaac was not the four hundred shekels of silver, but this piece of land in Machpelah and the hope behind it. On the title deed you leave to your children, is the direction toward home written upon it?

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Responsive Prayer

Today, this quiet chapter tells us: those who believe still weep, but they rise up from before their dead; sojourners receive a tomb first in the promised land, yet they bury the hope of the entire family into the future. Transacting at full price, keeping procedures transparent, getting one's identity right—this is the ordinary godliness that Abraham has left to us.

Let us pray together: Lord, You are the God who raises the dead. You do not rebuke us when we weep for those we love; we pray that You would lift us up from our sorrow to attend faithfully to what must be done. Teach us to live as pilgrims on the earth, neither greedy nor grasping, staking our hope entirely upon Your promises. One day, all that we have buried will hear Your voice and rise—we pray that the glory of that day would become our strength for doing our work today. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Chapter Notes

[1] safad: Hebrew סָפַד (H5594), to mourn, wail lamenting; bakah: Hebrew בָּכָה (H1058), to weep, shed tears; Gen. 23:2 "Abraham came to mourn for Sarah and to weep for her"; definitions per BDB, HALOT.

[2] ger and toshav: Hebrew גֵּר (H1616, sojourner, stranger) used together with תּוֹשָׁב (H8453, resident alien, sojourner); Gen. 23:4 "I am a foreigner and a visitor among you"; definitions per BDB, HALOT. Ps. 39:12 also uses these two words in David's self-description, though not expanded in this manuscript.

[3] nasi: Hebrew נָשִׂיא (H5387), prince, ruler, exalted one; Gen. 23:6 the Hittites address Abraham as "a mighty prince" (the original contains the sense of "a prince of God," the Chinese Union Version renders it "a mighty prince"); definitions per BDB, HALOT.

[4] Makpelah: Hebrew מַכְפֵּלָה (H4375), Machpelah; Gen. 23:9, 17, 19; scholarly views suggest the toponym may mean "double," but this remains unsettled; this manuscript does not adopt that view and merely records the name; see entries in BDB, HALOT.

[5] Regarding the magnitude of the four hundred shekels price: Chinese Union Version Gen. 23:15-16; compared with Jer. 32:9 (Jeremiah bought a field for seventeen shekels, excerpted for comparison) and 2 Sam. 24:24 (David bought the threshing floor and the oxen for fifty shekels of silver), four hundred shekels is clearly a high price; this manuscript's description of it as an "enormous sum" is a conclusion drawn from textual comparison. The shekel (sheqel, H8255) at that time was a unit of weight, not a minted coin, which is why 23:16 states that he "weighed out" the silver, as already noted in the body of this manuscript.

[6] Regarding subsequent burials in the cave of Machpelah: Gen. 25:9-10 (Abraham); 49:29-32 (Jacob's own account that Isaac, Rebekah, and Leah were buried here); 50:13 (Jacob's body brought back and buried); Part Three of this manuscript is stated on this basis.

[7] Regarding Abraham "coming" to mourn for Sarah (23:2): the original narrative implies that he may not have been in the immediate vicinity of Hebron at that time; this manuscript only highlights it as an application of "not accompanying her in her final moments," clearly noting that it is an interpretation conjectured by some commentators and remains inconclusive.

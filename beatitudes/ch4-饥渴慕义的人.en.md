## I. The Scripture Text

"Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be filled." (Matt. 5:6, NKJV)

The first three beatitudes all speak of "not having": no stock in the spirit, no comfort for sin, no longer a fist clenched in the hand to avenge oneself. By the fourth beatitude, Jesus speaks of a "wanting" — and wanting it intensely, like a hungry person wanting food, like a thirsty person wanting water. The spiritual life has a very plain mark: appetite. When a person is gravely ill, he cannot eat; when the spirit is numb, it is the same — anything will do, anything can be made do with. Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, because their appetite has awakened first.

## II. Exegesis of the Original Text

The Greek original: Μακάριοι οἱ πεινῶντες καὶ διψῶντες τὴν δικαιοσύνην, ὅτι αὐτοὶ χορτασθήσονται.

**1. "Hunger and thirst" (πεινῶντες καὶ διψῶντες, peinōntes kai dipsōntes; πεινάω, Strong G3983; διψάω, Strong G1372)**

Both of these words are present active participles in the nominative masculine plural, speaking not of being hungry for one occasional meal, but of a continuing state: being hungry, being thirsty. Jesus does not say "those who once hungered for righteousness," nor "those who have hungered for righteousness before." The longing of the citizens of the kingdom of heaven for righteousness is not a spell of youthful zeal; it is a lifelong appetite — having eaten, still wanting more; having drunk, still thirsty.

There is another detail worth noting, and the key lies in the case. In Greek usage, when verbs such as "to hunger" and "to thirst" are followed by a noun, the genitive case is often used (here the partitive genitive), indicating taking only a part of it — a hungry person wants to eat something to tide himself over; he does not want to swallow the whole table of dishes. But here "righteousness" (τὴν δικαιοσύνην) is in the accusative case (also called the objective case), singular, serving as the direct object of the verb; the object of longing set before us is righteousness itself, the whole of righteousness. That is to say, this hunger and thirst is not "I want to be right a little in one particular matter," but "I want God's righteousness, completely — in me, and also in the world where I am." A picky-eater kind of hungering for righteousness does not count as hungering for righteousness.

**2. "Righteousness" (δικαιοσύνη, dikaiosynē, Strong G1343; in this verse τὴν δικαιοσύνην is accusative singular)**

This word carries great weight in the Gospel of Matthew; it appears again and again throughout the book, and will be spoken of further at 5:20 and 6:33. It has at least three layers of meaning, and when reading this beatitude they must be held together:

The first layer is God's own righteousness — the way God does things, what kind of God He is. The second layer is the right relationship between man and God — the sinner is counted righteous by God and stands upright. The third layer is the rightness that is lived out — speech and conduct, treatment of others, and the doing of things all according to God's heart.

Missing any one layer makes it wrong. Speaking only of the third layer, hungering for righteousness becomes accumulating points for oneself through good conduct — the more one hungers, the prouder one becomes; speaking only of the second layer, hungering for righteousness becomes a certificate that is collected and put away, while life goes on as before. The hunger and thirst Jesus speaks of here is hunger for the whole of righteousness: I want God, I want to be right with Him, and I also want to live rightly.

The rendering of this verse in the Lü Zhenzhong Translation is very close to the Chinese Union Version, likewise using "filled" [in Chinese, "satisfied to the full"] to correspond to the promise of the original. This "being filled" is not tasting one bite; it is eating until full — see below.

**3. "They shall be filled" (χορτασθήσονται, chortasthēsontai; the verb χορτάζω, Strong G5526)**

This is the future passive form of χορτάζω. This word was originally used of feeding livestock: χόρτος is fodder, and χορτάζω is feeding livestock full with fodder; only later was it used of people eating their fill. Using it to speak of spiritual satisfaction gives a very down-to-earth picture — not politely sipping a mouthful at a banquet, but a truly hungry person being fed full by real food.

Look again at the voice: the passive. "They shall be filled," literally, is "they shall be fed full." Being filled is not an achievement earned by oneself; it is being fed by someone. As for whose hand does the feeding, Jesus does not say it explicitly, but those who read the whole Bible know who it is. Hungering is man's business; feeding full is God's business. What man can do is come with an appetite; what man cannot do is conjure food out of himself.

Finally, look at the tense: the future. Unlike the first beatitude's "theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (present tense), here is a promise still on the road: tasted first now, surely eaten full at the end. Today's satisfaction is real, but it is not the whole feast; the whole feast lies ahead. This tension of "already tasted, not yet finished eating" is precisely the reason the hunger and thirst keep continuing.

- NIV: "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled."
- NRSV: "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled."

## III. The Place and Logic of This Beatitude in the Sermon on the Mount

Reaching the fourth beatitude, the eight beatitudes come to a turning point.

The first four beatitudes can be seen this way: poor in spirit (5:3) is admitting I have nothing; mourning (5:4) is being grieved that I have nothing and that I have done wrong; meekness (5:5) is that I no longer snatch with my own hands or avenge myself; arriving at hungering and thirsting for righteousness (5:6), the emptied heart begins to want something — and to want the right thing. The deepest change in a person often does not begin with "what I no longer want," but with "I have finally come to want the right thing."

Nor can the order be reversed. Without poverty of spirit, hungering for righteousness will become self-righteousness — the Pharisees also "hungered for righteousness"; what they hungered for was a righteousness for people to see. Without mourning, hungering for righteousness will become picking out the faults of others — anxious for the state of the world and for other people, only never anxious for one's own sin. Without meekness, hungering for righteousness will become an impatient project of transformation — wanting to hammer the world right with one's own fists. For those who have first been emptied, the appetite is clean.

This beatitude also lays a thread for what follows. Later in the same sermon, Jesus says: "But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness" (Matt. 6:33, NKJV) — what is to be sought first is precisely what is hungered and thirsted for here. And 5:20 says that the righteousness of the disciples must exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees. The fourth beatitude speaks of the source of the motivation: righteousness is not a list carried on the back; it is a pursuit hungered out. With this hunger that has been fed by God, the mercy (5:7) and purity of heart (5:8) that follow have a root — only a person who has eaten God's food is willing to share food with others.

The Gospel of Luke has a parallel beatitude, which is a formal quotation: "Blessed are you who hunger now, for you shall be filled" (Luke 6:21, NKJV). Luke speaks of the hunger of the body; Matthew makes explicit the "hungering for righteousness." Reading the two places together is just right: God cares both for the hunger of the stomach and for the thirst of the soul; in the satisfaction He promises, neither of the two falls short.

## IV. Cross-References

- Ps. 42:1–2 — The psalmist compares himself to a deer panting for the water brooks, saying his soul thirsts for God, for the living God. Hungering and thirsting for righteousness is not a cold pursuit of doctrine; it is longing for a Person.
- Ps. 107:9 — The psalmist says that God satisfies the longing soul and fills the hungry soul with goodness. Note the subject: the one who fills is God, not the progress of man's own cultivation.
- Isa. 55:1–2 — The prophet calls the thirsty to come, buy, and eat, and asks: why spend money on what is not food? Man's greatest waste is using a real appetite to feed on false food.
- Amos 8:11–12 — The prophet foretells a particular kind of famine: not a lack of bread or water, but not hearing the words of the LORD, so that people run everywhere and cannot find them. Hungering and thirsting for righteousness shows at least one thing: God's word is still warm in your ears.
- John 6:35 — Jesus declares that He Himself is the bread of life; the one who comes to Him shall never hunger, and the one who believes in Him shall never thirst. The hunger and thirst of the fourth beatitude finally point to a Person, not to a set of standards.
- John 7:37–38 — On the last day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried out, saying that if anyone thirsts, let him come to Him and drink; out of the heart of the one who believes in Him will flow rivers of living water. The person who has been fed full himself becomes a source of water.
- Rev. 7:16–17 — At the end, that great multitude "shall neither hunger anymore nor thirst anymore," because the Lamb who is in the midst of the throne will shepherd them and lead them to living fountains of waters. The future tense of the fourth beatitude finally lands on this scene.

## V. Life Application

**Scene One: Scrolling until midnight, and the heart is emptier.** This is how many people use their appetite for a day: screen after screen of news scrolled, short videos swiped until the fingers ache, messages answered without stopping — and when they lie down, they cannot say what they actually took in today; they only feel empty. That question in Isaiah chapter 55 is for nights like these: why spend your most precious attention on things that cannot fill you? The practice of hungering and thirsting for righteousness is very concrete: every day, let God's word enter the stomach first, then let the world's feed enter the eyes — even if only for ten minutes, read Scripture first, pray first, then turn on the phone. Appetite is cultivated: what you feed first, you gradually come to love eating.

**Scene Two: Seeing injustice, only getting angry and never taking action.** Seeing something unfair — forwarding it, getting angry, sighing — and then life goes on as before. This is not called hungering for righteousness; this is called spectating. A person who hungers for righteousness takes one step more: within the range he can reach, he does one wrong thing rightly — the money that should be returned is returned, the account that should be acknowledged is acknowledged, no cutting in line, no hidden traps in small print when signing a contract, full wages paid to those who help him. If the appetite never lands in the hands, it is most likely a false appetite. Righteousness must first live in one's own home, in one's own accounts, and in one's own mouth, before one can talk of thirsting for a greater justice.

**Scene Three: Disappointed in oneself to the point of not wanting to grow.** After many years of believing in the Lord, what most easily appears is not a great fall, but numbness: prayer like handing in an assignment, gatherings like clocking in; clearly knowing some habits are wrong, yet too lazy to change them anymore. At such a time, that little bit that occasionally surfaces in the heart — "I do not want to go on like this anymore" — do not look down on it: that is hunger and thirst; it is evidence that the appetite has not yet died. Do not use entertainment, eating and drinking, or shopping to press it down; a suppressed appetite does not disappear, it only breaks out on other things. Bring this disappointment before God just as it is: "Lord, I am hungry; feed me." The good news of the passive voice is right here: you do not need to fix yourself first and then come; you come with your hunger, and He is responsible for feeding you full.

## VI. Response Prayer

Lord, I first confess that my appetite is often wrong. I have patience for endless messages to scroll, but when it comes to Your word, I cannot sit still for even ten minutes; I lose sleep over missing out on a small benefit, yet I am seldom grieved over how far I am from Your righteousness.

Give me again a clean hunger and thirst, I pray. Do not let me be satisfied with a little religious atmosphere, and do not let me pass off anger and commentary as hungering for righteousness. What I want is Yourself; what I want is to be right with You; and what I want is also to live rightly — right at home, right in the accounts, right in the places no one sees.

You have promised that those who hunger and thirst for righteousness shall be filled. I believe this filling is fed by You, not earned by me. Give me today today's portion; and on that day, let me stand in that crowd that hungers no more and thirsts no more, looking back on the hunger of this road, knowing that every meal was fed by You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ I pray, Amen.

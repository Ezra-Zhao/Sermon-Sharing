> Reading notes — five things must be nailed down before teaching this chapter. First, Abner's defection: Scripture states his motive plainly — the trigger was Ish-bosheth's rebuke over Rizpah and Abner's anger at being shamed; it was not repentance and not awe of David's God. The preacher must not beautify Abner into a righteous man who suddenly saw the light, nor conclude from this that his defection was worthless — on the battlefield he had twice warned Asahel to turn back (see 2 Samuel 2, preached earlier in this series), and he was the one man who could bring the eleven tribes over at this moment. This manuscript's position: a defection with impure motives is still to be received by the rules of the covenant, but the reception must not be preached as an endorsement of the motive. Second, Joab's killing of Abner is defined by Scripture as a private vendetta and an assassination, not an execution of justice: Abner killed Asahel in open battle after warning him twice; and Hebron was one of the cities of refuge appointed in Joshua's day (see Josh 20:7, registered) — at the gate of a city of refuge, for the blood-avenger to set up his own court and stab the man who had come over, in the abdomen, is murder, and this manuscript teaches it as murder. Hearers today must not use Joab to justify any private revenge, gang retaliation, or answering violence with violence. Third, David's innocence in this chapter is real, and so is his powerlessness: he declared himself publicly, mourned, and fasted — the right way to clear himself; yet in the end he did not punish Joab, but entrusted judgment to the LORD — and on his deathbed he still charged Solomon to deal with Joab (see 1 Kings 2, registered), which shows he carried this account all his life and endured all his life. The preacher must not present David's entrusting as a model of indulgence, nor erase his weakness: this manuscript states both sides as they are — the king's words entrusting judgment to God are true, and the king's pain of being unable to control his commander is also true. Fourth, in the passage where Michal is brought back, Paltiel's tears must be preached: he followed his wife weeping all the way to Bahurim, and at a single "Go back" he went back. On the ledger of a change of power, this entry records a nobody whose marriage was torn apart whole; the preacher must not keep only the royal accounts and skip Paltiel's tears, and must never use this chapter to excuse taking what another loves by power. Fifth, the list of the six sons born in Hebron must not be skipped as a genealogy: Amnon, Absalom, Adonijah — one will rape Tamar, one will rebel against his father and seize the throne, one will set himself up as king while his father is old (see 2 Sam 13, 15; 1 Kings 1, registered) — this birth announcement is the cast list of every family tragedy in the second half of 2 Samuel; the preacher should register this shadow already in this chapter, without unfolding it early, yet must not let hearers read these six lines of names as a simple list of blessings.

## Synopsis

2 Samuel 3 is a chapter with no battlefield, in which the most important man of all dies. It opens with the general ledger: a long war between the two houses; David's house growing stronger and stronger, Saul's house weaker and weaker — and the weakening house immediately splits from within: its commander Abner, shamed by Ish-bosheth's question over a former king's concubine, sends to Hebron in a rage to transfer the kingdom to David. The precondition of the negotiation is that David names Michal to be brought back first — the daughter Saul had once broken his promise over and married to another; so Michal's second husband Paltiel follows her weeping all the way to Bahurim and is dismissed with a single sentence — the tears nobody in the great work of unification has time for. Abner persuades the elders, promises at the feast in Hebron to gather all Israel, and David sends him out of the city in peace; unification is one step away when Joab returns from the raid, hears the news, angrily rebukes David, secretly sends after the man, brings Abner back from the well of Sirah, and at the gate of the city of refuge drives a blade into his abdomen — to avenge his brother Asahel. David hears of it and does three things in a row: he declares his innocence publicly, entrusts judgment to the LORD, follows the bier himself in mourning and makes a lament, and swears he will not eat before sunset, so that all Israel knows the killing was not the king's will; yet at the chapter's end the king admits with his own mouth: this day I am weak in Israel, and the sons of Zeruiah are too hard for me. This sermon treats three things: when strength and weakness change places, the defector's motive and the receiver's rules must be accounted separately; once justice is taken over by private vengeance, even the brightest cause will be stained with blood at the city gate; and how a king, in a situation he cannot control, first guards the boundary of justice with mourning and declaration, and then hands the final judgment back to God.

## Opening Prayer

Lord, today we stand at the gate of Hebron. Inside the city the feast of reconciliation has just been set; at the gate a man who had been sent away in peace has fallen. Teach us not to relax the rules when the situation turns in our favor: when someone comes over to us, we receive him by the covenant, but we do not endorse impure motives; when the enemy's hand is loosened, we do not reach out the hand of private vengeance again. Restrain the Joab among us — the heart that keeps the blood-debt, waits for its chance, and wants to settle justice in the dark; teach us to hand judgment back to You, not because we do not care about justice, but precisely because justice is too heavy for a private blade to carry. For Paltiel's tears along that road, remember him, Lord: every little person crushed beneath a great situation — You see them all. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Introduction: Between the Feast and the City Gate, Only a Short Step

First get the terrain of this chapter clear: almost everything happens within the one city of Hebron and on the roads not far outside it. Inside the city are the palace, the feast, and the grave; outside are the well of Sirah and the road to Bahurim. Abner was still at the table just now, promising to gather all Israel; in a moment he falls at the city gate. The door of unification has been pushed open a crack, and on its hinge is splashed blood. The chapter has three movements. First, the long war and the six sons (vv. 1-5): the general ledger of strength and weakness, and a birth list with a mine buried in it. Second, Abner's defection (vv. 6-21): a submission ignited by a rebuke, a wife taken away, and a feast of reconciliation. Third, blood at the city gate and the king's tears (vv. 22-39): Joab's private execution, David's declaration and lament, and a closing in which the king confesses his own weakness. The chapter has three inner threads. The thread of momentum: David's house growing stronger, Saul's house growing weaker — strength and weakness do not flip in a day; they shift day by day through a long war, and every defection, negotiation, and assassination in this chapter happens along that slope. The thread of tears: Paltiel weeps on the road, David weeps at the grave — one is a little man weeping as he sends off his wife, the other a king weeping as he buries an enemy commander; this chapter's tears are not on the battlefield but in the corners of power. The thread of blood: in chapter 2 Asahel's blood flowed on the mountain road; in this chapter Joab says he will settle the account for that blood, and Abner's blood flows at the city gate; blood demanded for blood, the account grows thicker the more it is collected — this chapter asks every person who keeps a blood-account, with the tightest treatment of blood-revenge in the whole Bible: hand that demands blood, when will you finally stop?

Before reading this chapter, settle three measures. First, do not preach Ish-bosheth as a comic fool of a king: his question to Abner about Rizpah may not have been entirely without ground in royal propriety (the former king's concubines touch the dignity of the throne — commentators' readings registered, not adjudicated); his tragedy is not that he asked the wrong question, but that apart from this one question he had no strength left to support his throne — a king with nothing left but his title asked his commander one question, and questioned his kingdom away. Second, do not preach Joab as a one-dimensional villain: his loyalty to David is documented across the book, and his words even contain a measure of true insight — Abner's coming was indeed not wholly without deceit, and unification did carry risks (the details of Abner's negotiation with David, registered); what this manuscript condemns is his method and his timing in demanding blood: after the king had publicly received the man and sent him away in peace, to pursue and kill the envoy in secret crossed the line between justice and murder, and loyalty cannot pay for that step. Third, do not miss the geographic irony of verse 27: Abner died in Hebron — a city of refuge. The institution of the refuge cities was established precisely to stop the blood-avenger outside the gate and let judgment complete its process first (see Num 35, cross-read in the cross-references) [26]; Joab carried out his private execution right beside the gate of the refuge city — he stabbed the very heart of the institution. The preacher should preach this layer through: what Joab killed was not only a man, but Israel's whole apparatus for stopping blood-revenge.

## Part One: The Long War and the Six Sons Born in Hebron (2 Sam 3:1-5)

### 1. David's House Grew Stronger and Stronger, Saul's House Weaker and Weaker (2 Sam 3:1)

There was a long war between the house of Saul and the house of David; David's house grew stronger and stronger, while Saul's house grew weaker and weaker (see 2 Sam 3:1, condensed rendering of the gist).

"War" in the original is מִלְחָמָה (milḥāmâ, H4421), war, battle. See BDB, s.v. מלחמה. [1] "Grew stronger and stronger" corresponds in the original to הלךְ וְחָזֵק (hālok wəḥāzēq), "going on and growing strong"; ḥāzaq is H2388, to be firm, strong. See BDB, s.v. חזק. [2] This verse is the chapter's general ledger, and a measure no reader of 2 Samuel can bypass: God's promise was not fulfilled on David by lightning, but by the "day by day" of a long war. Through the seven years and six months at Hebron (see 2 Sam 2:11, registered) there was no new anointing and no voice from heaven — only this slope: a little stronger each day, a little weaker on the other side each day. Many believers' trouble lies exactly here — the promise is real, but the process is measured in years; people are most prone, midway through a long war, to suspect the ledger has gone wrong. This chapter sets the word at its head first: the ledger is not wrong, and the slope has been moving toward the promise all along. The preacher should preach this verse to everyone worn down in a long situation: the long situation of a marriage, of a sickbed, of work and family — do not overturn the whole slope with one day's rise or fall; the question is not who won today, but over these many years, is my house on the side that grows stronger day by day, or on the side that grows weaker day by day? Direction is more honest than speed.

### 2. Six Sons, Six Mothers (2 Sam 3:2-5)

Sons were born to David in Hebron: his firstborn Amnon, by Ahinoam of Jezreel; his second Chileab, by Abigail, the widow of Nabal of Carmel; his third Absalom, by Maacah, daughter of Talmai king of Geshur; his fourth Adonijah, by Haggith; his fifth Shephatiah, by Abital; and his sixth Ithream, by David's wife Eglah; these six were born to David in Hebron (see 2 Sam 3:2-5, condensed rendering of the gist).

Amnon in the original is אַמְנוֹן (ʾAmnôn, H550), a personal name. See BDB, s.v. אמנון. [3] Absalom is אַבְשָׁלוֹם (ʾAḇšālôm, H53), a personal name. See BDB, s.v. אבשלום. [4] Adonijah is אֲדֹנִיָּה (ʾĂdōniyyâ, H138), a personal name. See BDB, s.v. אדניה. [5] At first reading this list is good news: the fugitive of many years now has children in a row and a flourishing household. But by the fifth reading note, this list must later be checked name by name: the firstborn Amnon will rape his half-sister Tamar and be killed by Absalom at a feast (see 2 Sam 13, registered); the third son Absalom will avenge his sister, then rebel against his father and die at Joab's hand (see 2 Sam 15, 18, registered); the fourth son Adonijah will rush to set himself up as king while David is old and failing (see 1 Kings 1, registered). At this chapter the preacher may only register this far, without unfolding it, but the position must be declared first: when Scripture wrote down these six lines of names, its tone was as calm as an ordinary family register — tragedy never falls out of the sky; it first enters the house in the form of good news. And one more layer must not be missed: six sons by six mothers — the polygamous pattern is already fixed in this chapter; Deuteronomy already has the plain command that the king must not multiply wives for himself (see Deut 17:17, registered), and on the relation between David's family tragedies and this pattern, commentators' readings are only registered, not adjudicated; here this manuscript drives in one sentence: while the king was growing stronger day by day on the battlefield, his home was burying the most painful defeats of his life. You who are winning: read your list twice — once counting grace, once checking for hidden dangers.

### Ignition Point One: In a Long War, Watch the Slope, Not the Single Day

Here I stop and speak to those worn down in a long situation: you have endured three years, five years, ten years, the situation has still not turned, and you have begun to wonder whether you misheard the promise at the start. Hear 2 Samuel 3:1 carefully: there was a long war between the house of Saul and the house of David — Scripture does not say the war was brief, does not say the turn came overnight; it speaks of a slope: stronger day by day, weaker day by day. Today — not after the situation clears up, before you believe — lay out your accounts of these years and compare them year by year: is your family a little steadier than last year, is your faith a little more able to bear things than the year before, is the situation in your hands moving inch by inch toward the promise? If it is, do not be driven off by one defeat last week; those who quit in the middle of a long war lose not for lack of strength but because they mistook one day's rise and fall for the general ledger. Stay, and walk the slope to its end.

## Part Two: Abner's Defection — A Submission Ignited by a Rebuke (2 Sam 3:6-21)

### 1. "Do You Question Me Because of a Woman?" (2 Sam 3:6-11)

At that time Abner had made himself strong in the house of Saul. Saul had a concubine named Rizpah, daughter of Aiah, and Ish-bosheth said to Abner, "Why have you gone in to my father's concubine?" Abner was very angry over Ish-bosheth's words and said, "Am I a dog's head? I have shown kindness to the house of Saul and have not delivered you into David's hand, yet today you charge me with a fault concerning a woman." And he swore: if he did not do as the LORD had sworn to David — deposing Saul's house and establishing David's throne — may God do so to him, and more also (see 2 Sam 3:6-10, condensed rendering of the gist). Ish-bosheth could not answer Abner another word, because he feared him (see 2 Sam 3:11, condensed rendering of the gist).

Rizpah in the original is רִצְפָּה (Riṣpâ, H7532), a personal name, daughter of Aiah. See BDB, s.v. רצפה. [6] "Concubine" is פִּילֶגֶשׁ (pîlegeš, H6370), concubine. See BDB, s.v. פילגש. [7] "Dog's head" corresponds to רֹאשׁ כֶּלֶב (rōʾš keleḇ), the head of a dog; keleḇ is H3611, dog. See BDB, s.v. כלב. [8] "Kindness" is חֶסֶד (ḥesed, H2617), covenant love, kindness. See BDB, s.v. חסד. [9] First hear both sides in full. Ish-bosheth's question concerned the commander's lying with the former king's concubine — in an ancient royal house, whoever touched the former king's concubines was suspected of aiming at the throne (later Absalom openly went in to David's concubines as proof of seizing the kingship; see 2 Sam 16:21-22, registered), so in point of title this question was not entirely groundless. Abner's anger, however, kept a different account: he was angry not at being wronged, but at being asked — a man who propped up the whole regime could not bear being questioned once by the king in name. Every line of his self-defense is a true account: he truly had shown kindness, he truly had not delivered Ish-bosheth into the enemy's hand; but he left one thing out of his reckoning: merit, however great, does not mean exemption from accountability. This anger produced the chapter's greatest turn: he swore to transfer the kingdom to David. Note the ground he cites: as the LORD had sworn to David — he had known all along that God's will was on David's side; for all these years propping up the house of Saul, he had been fighting a war whose ending he himself already knew. The preacher should preach this layer through: many people do not fail to know God's will; it is just that when the will conflicts with their position, they prefer to protect the position first; only when the position has been shamed and face can no longer be saved do they remember obedience. Abner's submission was a real submission, but its timetable was set by face. This manuscript does not cancel his submission for that reason; it only reminds hearers to examine themselves: does my obedience move when I hear the will, or only when I have been wronged? Both ways of walking can reach Hebron, but the number of people who die on the road is not the same.

### 2. "First Bring Michal to Me" (2 Sam 3:12-16)

Abner sent messengers to David, saying, "To whom does the land belong? Make your covenant with me, and behold, my hand shall be with you to bring all Israel over to you." David said, "Good, I will make a covenant with you; but one thing I require of you: you shall not see my face unless you first bring Michal, Saul's daughter, when you come to see my face." David also sent messengers to Ish-bosheth, saying, "Give me my wife Michal, whom I betrothed for a hundred foreskins of the Philistines." So Ish-bosheth sent and took her from her husband Paltiel, son of Laish. Her husband went with her, weeping as he went, as far as Bahurim. Then Abner said to him, "Go, return," and he returned (see 2 Sam 3:12-16, condensed rendering of the gist).

Michal in the original is מִיכַל (Mîkhal, H4324), a personal name, Saul's younger daughter. See BDB, s.v. מיכל. [10] Paltiel is פַּלְטִי (Palṭî, H6406), a personal name, son of Laish; in verses 15-16 of this chapter the spelling Paltiel also occurs — the detail is only registered. See BDB, s.v. פלטי. [11] "Weep" is בָּכָה (bākhâ, H1058), to weep. See BDB, s.v. בכה. [12] David's condition has three layers on its face: Michal is Saul's daughter, and taking her back declares to the north that the old covenant between Saul's house and David's house is renewed; she was his betrothed wife, and reclaiming her has a basis in propriety; and her return is also the most direct humiliation of Ish-bosheth. All three layers add up — yet Scripture does not stop at the abacus; it turns the camera to Paltiel. This man, who appears only twice in Scripture (see 1 Sam 25:44 and this chapter, vv. 15-16, registered), has not one line and not one scene — only one verb: weep. He sent his wife on her way all the way to Bahurim, following in tears; then Abner turned and said, "Go back," and he went back. He did not argue one word and did not stretch out one hand. The preacher must slow down here: the unification of an empire, the changing of a dynasty, the accomplishment of God's will — under these great words lies crushed one ordinary man's whole marriage. Scripture does not defend this move of David's, nor cover Abner's sentence with shame; it simply records Paltiel's tears, so that every later reader of the great work of unification cannot bypass the tears along that road. This manuscript's position is nailed down here: those who accomplish great things, if they turn a blind eye to the tears in the corners of the ledger, will sooner or later pay for their great work on another account. We who make decisions today should also learn to ask one question before signing: when this thing is done, who will be weeping on the road?

### 3. At the Feast He Promises to Gather All Israel (2 Sam 3:17-21)

Abner spoke with the elders of Israel, saying, "For some time past you have been seeking David as king over you. Now then bring it about, for the LORD has promised David, saying, 'By the hand of my servant David I will save my people Israel from the hand of the Philistines, and from the hand of all their enemies.'" Abner also spoke with the Benjaminites, and then went to Hebron to tell David all that Israel and Benjamin had agreed to do. He came with twenty men, and David made a feast for Abner and the men with him. Abner said to David, "Arise and let me go and gather all Israel to my lord the king, that they may make a covenant with you, and that you may reign over all that your heart desires." So David sent Abner away, and he went in peace (see 2 Sam 3:17-21, condensed rendering of the gist).

"Gather" corresponds to קָבַץ (qāḇaṣ, H6908), to gather, assemble. See BDB, s.v. קבץ. [13] "Feast" is מִשְׁתֶּה (mišteh, H4960), feast, banquet. See BDB, s.v. משתה. [14] This is the chapter's bright summit, and every stroke is right: the elders' hearts were moved (the text pointedly notes they had long wanted this, and Abner only spoke the people's will aloud); God's word was brought out ("By my servant David's hand I will save my people" — the will Abner had refused to acknowledge for years, he now acknowledged in public); the feast was set — in the narrative of Scripture, eating at one table is the seal of a covenant; enemies at one table means the old account ends here; and the man was sent away in peace — the "in peace" of verse 21 is rendered with weight in the Union Version, like a stamped guarantee. The preacher should let hearers look at this scene long enough, because the darkness of the next movement will be set against its whiteness: David did everything right — he did not detain the envoy, did not force a treaty dictated at swordpoint, did not humiliate the surrendered general at the table; with a king's dignity he received a defector of impure motives, and with a king's credit he sent the man out of the city gate in peace. The rules for receiving one who comes over were made complete by David in this chapter: the motive is God's to judge, the rules are mine to keep. What Joab is about to smash with his own hands in the next scene is precisely this set of rules.

### Ignition Point Two: Rules Are Kept Precisely When the Great Situation Is About to Be Completed

Here I stop and speak to those who hold a great situation in their hands: the more the situation improves, the more easily rules are treated as a burden. When a negotiation is about to close, someone always says: extraordinary times — skip the formalities; when an enemy comes over, someone always says: keep a reservation first, detain the man and talk later. On the day David came closest to unification, he kept the rules most completely: the covenant word had to be spoken first, the feast had to be set, the man had to be sent out of the gate in peace. Why? Because rules are not decoration for smooth days; rules set the boundary precisely for such days — how you treat the man who comes over today decides whether the people of the world will dare to come over to you tomorrow. Today — not after you hold all the power, before you speak of dignity: finish that nearly settled matter by the rules; send that man who has bowed his head away with dignity. Those who keep the rules do not lose out on account of the rules; the great losses always come on the day someone thinks he has already won and can stop keeping them. The blood of the second half of chapter 3 begins to flow from one man's thinking the rules could be waived.

## Part Three: Blood at the City Gate and the King's Tears (2 Sam 3:22-39)

### 1. Brought Back from the Well of Sirah, Stabbed Beside the Gate (2 Sam 3:22-27)

Just then the servants of David and Joab came from attacking the enemy, bringing much spoil. Joab heard it said that Abner had come to the king and the king had sent him away in peace, and he said to the king in anger, "What have you done? Abner came to you; why have you sent him away? Do you not know that he came to deceive you, to know your going out and your coming in, and to know all that you are doing?" After leaving David, Joab secretly sent messengers after Abner, and they brought him back from the well of Sirah — but David did not know it. When Abner returned to Hebron, Joab took him aside to the gate to speak with him privately, and there he struck him in the abdomen, and he died — to avenge the blood of his brother Asahel (see 2 Sam 3:22-27, condensed rendering of the gist).

The well of Sirah corresponds to בּוֹר הַסִּרָה (bôr hassirâ), the cistern of Sirah; bôr is H953, pit, cistern. See BDB, s.v. בור. [15] "Abdomen" corresponds to חֹמֶשׁ (ḥōmeš, H2570), abdomen. See BDB, s.v. חמש. [16] "Blood" is דָּם (dām, H1818), blood. See BDB, s.v. דם. [17] Hear Joab's words in full before judging him: his charge was not wholly without shadow — Abner had been an enemy for half a lifetime and had suddenly come over; the risk of spying out the situation is one anyone would raise; Joab's heart to protect his lord need not be wholly erased either. But the text immediately lays his method open: behind the king's back (David did not know), under the flag of a recall (intercepting the man at the well of Sirah), in the posture of a conversation (taking him aside to the gate, speaking privately), he struck a killer's blow (one thrust into the abdomen). Not one of the four steps walked in the open. Then nail down the place: the city gate was where Israel judged right and wrong in ancient times (see Ruth 4, registered), and Hebron was also a city of refuge (see Josh 20:7, registered) — at the gate of judgment, in the city of asylum, Joab carried out an unjudged private execution. At the end Scripture itself gives the verdict on the motive: to avenge the blood of his brother Asahel. We have already settled chapter 2's account: Asahel's death was an act of battle, with two warnings refused before it, and Abner had cried from the hilltop, "Shall the sword devour forever?", and Joab himself had answered by blowing the trumpet to stop the battle; today's thrust is Joab unilaterally tearing up the sound of his own trumpet. This manuscript draws the line here, and the clearer the better: if a blood-debt may be collected by a private person on his own timetable and in his own way, then refuge cities, city gates, judgments, and the king's covenant are all waste paper; Joab's blade stabbed not a surrendered general, but the very order Israel was about to establish. Hearers today who keep a blood-account, listen well: if the justice you demand must bypass every procedure, be hidden from everyone, and be struck in the dark, then on Scripture's ledger it is not called justice; it is called murder.

### 2. "I and My Kingdom Are Forever Guiltless" (2 Sam 3:28-30)

Afterward David heard of it and said: "I and my kingdom are forever guiltless before the LORD for the blood of Abner son of Ner. May it fall on the head of Joab and on all his father's house; and may the house of Joab never lack one who has a discharge, or one who has a leprous disease, or one who leans on a staff, or one who falls by the sword, or one who lacks bread." Joab and his brother Abishai killed Abner because he had killed their brother Asahel at Gibeon in the battle (see 2 Sam 3:28-30, condensed rendering of the gist).

"Guiltless, innocent" corresponds to נָקִי (nāqî, H5355), innocent, clear. See BDB, s.v. נקי. [18] "Leprous disease" corresponds to צָרַעַת (ṣāraʿat, H6883), a severe skin disease, of the leprosy kind. See BDB, s.v. צרעת. [19] David's first reaction is a declaration, his second a curse — both must be seen clearly. The declaration is immediate, public, and spoken toward the LORD: I and my kingdom are forever guiltless. This is not evasion — of the scene of the bloodshed, the motive, and the hands involved, he had no part in any of them; what he declares is the boundary: the king's name and the kingdom's name do not endorse a private execution. When a leader's subordinate kills a man in the dark, the leader's first lesson is not to shield his own, nor to suppress the news, but to draw the line in public: this deed was not my will, and my name is not lent to it. The curse that follows must be preached on both sides, by the third reading note: on one side, it is the king's solemn declaration returning the right of judgment to God — "May the LORD repay the evildoer according to his wickedness" (see 3:39, condensed rendering) — David did not send troops to seize Joab and did not repay blood with blood; he left the execution to God. On the other side, this curse runs down a whole clan, heavy in every line, and it also exposes David's real situation at the time: he could curse Joab, but he could not deal with Joab. Declaring entrustment and being powerless to punish are, in this chapter, two sides of the same thing; the preacher must not preach only one side. The later account must be registered: Joab afterward also killed Amasa (see 2 Sam 20, registered), and David on his deathbed charged Solomon not to let Joab's gray head go down to Sheol in peace (see 1 Kings 2:5-6, registered) — God did not forget this account, and neither did David; only the date of repayment does not fall in this chapter. Justice that comes late is still justice, but this chapter first teaches us: entrusting judgment to God is not forgetting the matter; it is recording it on God's ledger, which holds it more firmly than recording it on one's own blade.

### 3. The King Follows the Bier and Weeps Aloud at the Grave (2 Sam 3:31-34)

Then David said to Joab and to all the people with him, "Tear your clothes and put on sackcloth and mourn before Abner." And King David followed the bier. They buried Abner at Hebron, and the king lifted up his voice and wept at the grave of Abner, and all the people wept. The king lamented for Abner, saying, "Should Abner die as a fool dies? Your hands were not bound, your feet were not put in fetters; as one falls before the wicked, so you fell." And all the people wept again over him (see 2 Sam 3:31-34, condensed rendering of the gist).

"Bier" in the original is מִטָּה (miṭṭâ, H4296), bed, bier. See BDB, s.v. מטה. [20] "Fool" is נָבָל (nāḇāl, H5036), foolish, base. See BDB, s.v. נבל. [21] "Bound" is אָסַר (ʾāsar, H631), to bind, imprison. See BDB, s.v. אסר. [22] "Fetters" corresponds to נְחֻשְׁתַּיִם (nəḥuštayim, H5178), bronze fetters, a pair of bronze chains. See BDB, s.v. נחשתים. [23] Every action in this scene answers Joab's thrust: Joab killed in the dark, so the king mourns in the open; Joab wanted the world to think Abner was an enemy chief who deserved to die, so the king walks behind the bier himself, weeps aloud, and buries him with a commander's honors; and the lament's first line is even more direct — die as a fool dies. In Scripture the fool is the kind of man Nabal was, who died by his own folly (see 1 Sam 25, registered); David says Abner should not have died like this: he was not a general fallen in battle, nor a prisoner condemned after trial (hands unbound, feet unfettered) — he died at the hands of the wicked, and that verdict in the lament names no name, yet everyone understood whom it meant. Then come the two lines that give this sermon its title: your hands were not bound, your feet were not put in fetters. Preach these two lines to every wrongfully killed and ambushed person: Scripture has kept a song for the innocent victim, and in the song it does not speak first of the killer, but of the treatment the victim should never have received. The order of heaven's justice is here: first clear the dead man's name, then wait for the killer to be brought to justice. And one more layer must not be missed: among those commanded to tear their clothes and put on sackcloth was Joab himself — David commanded Joab too to walk in the funeral procession. The killer is pressed by the king to walk before the bier, weeping: this is the heaviest public verdict David could render while powerless to deal with him — the man your blade killed, you shall send off weeping in person. This is not a performance of weakness; it is a verdict the king left in the liturgy.

### 4. "Before Sunset, I Will Not Eat" (2 Sam 3:35-39)

Then all the people came to persuade David to eat bread while it was still day, and David swore: "God do so to me and more also, if I taste bread or anything else before the sun goes down." All the people took notice of it and it pleased them, as everything the king did pleased all the people. So all the people and all Israel understood that day that it had not been the king's will to put Abner son of Ner to death. Then the king said to his servants, "Do you not know that a prince and a great man has fallen this day in Israel? And I, though anointed king, am weak this day, and these men, the sons of Zeruiah, are too hard for me. May the LORD repay the evildoer according to his wickedness" (see 2 Sam 3:35-39, condensed rendering of the gist).

"Weak, tender" is רַךְ (rakh, H7390), tender, weak. See BDB, s.v. רך. [24] "Hard, harsh" corresponds to קָשֶׁה (qāšeh, H7186), hard, obstinate. See BDB, s.v. קשה. [25] The oath of fasting bore fruit at once: the people knew the killing of Abner was not the king's will. The people's hearts were turned in the mourning, not in a proclamation — David's tears and empty stomach reached the people's hearts before any declaration could. The king's words at the chapter's end are among the most candid royal confessions in the whole book: this day I am weak. A king anointed for years, newly secure in Hebron, with unification in sight, admits in public that he cannot control his two nephews. This manuscript sets two readings on this sentence, and both are to be preached: first, confessing weakness is not dereliction; it is a leader's honesty — David did not pretend everything was in his grasp, nor, to save face, press Joab's affair down into a small matter; he stated his limit in the open and handed the part he could not handle to the Lord in the open. Those today who stand in high places yet cannot do something may learn this honesty: admitting weakness is closer to justice than whitewashing strength. Second, this weakness also left a real cost, and this manuscript does not hide it for him: Joab went unpunished all his life and added a second bloodshed (Abner and Amasa, registered), and David's deathbed charge is in effect an admission that this account was never settled within his reign. Between entrusting to God and failing in governance, this chapter gives no cheap answer; it gives an order — first clear the name, first mourn, first declare, first entrust, and then carry the account in memory for the rest of one's life. The preacher should preach by this order, skipping none of its middle steps to land directly on entrustment and be done.

### Ignition Point Three: Put the Blade Down and Hand the Account to God

Here I stop and speak to the Joab kind of person — perhaps to someone right here in front of the screen or in these seats: you too keep a blood-account in your heart, remembered clearly, down to the date. What that person owes you cannot be settled with an apology; you have waited for a chance for many years — your well of Sirah has been scouted, your lines rehearsed — all that remains is to bring the man back. Hear one straight word from me: today, put the blade down. Not because that person should not be judged, but because the judgment should not come from your hand, your darkness, your timetable. When Abner's thrust went in, what did Joab win back? His brother could not live again; he himself carried the king's curse for decades and in the end died beside the altar (see 1 Kings 2, registered). Blood demanded for blood — in the end the life of the one demanding is thrown in as well. Today hand your account over — to God; that is not swallowing it and pretending nothing happened: the account may be recorded, the tears may flow, justice may wait for God's day; only the blade must no longer be hidden in your sleeve. The day you let go is the first day you have truly obtained justice for the one you love, because you did not let the day of his remembrance be stained with another family's tears.

## Cross-References: Five Passages Read Against This Chapter

This chapter's events have echoes elsewhere in Scripture; read five passages against it and nail it fast. First, from the Old Testament: the refuge cities stop blood-revenge — the LORD commanded that cities of refuge be appointed east of the Jordan and in the land of Canaan, so that a manslayer might flee there, and the avenger of blood must not in hot anger pursue the manslayer and kill him; he must stand before the congregation to be judged (see Num 35:9-12, 19-25, condensed rendering; key sentences checked against the Union Version, final word-by-word verification completed through cross-review on 2026-11-03; Hebron is listed among the six cities of refuge in Canaan, see Josh 20:7, registered). Read the two together and the weight of Joab's sin can be measured in full: God established the refuge cities precisely to set a procedure between blood and blood; Joab, bypassing the procedure to kill beside the gate of a refuge city, struck at the institution itself. Second, from the Old Testament: Michal's old account — Saul first betrothed his daughter Michal to David at the price of a hundred Philistine foreskins (see 1 Sam 18:20-27), and later gave Michal to Paltiel son of Laish, of Gallim (see 1 Sam 25:44, condensed rendering). Read the two together against this chapter: twice in her life Michal was handed back and forth as a token by the power of father and husbands on both sides; Paltiel's tears in this chapter are no isolated case, but an old account — marriage used for years as political currency — falling due all at once on the day of unification. From the Psalms: the self-testimony of one who walks in integrity — "Vindicate me, O LORD, for I have walked in my integrity, and I have trusted in the LORD without wavering" (Ps 26:1, Union Version). The superscription of Psalm 26 attributes it to David (psalm title registered). Read this psalm back into this chapter, and David's "I and my kingdom are guiltless" gains the echo of prayer: innocence is not self-praise; it is a testimony brought before God and submitted to His judgment — only the one who dares to hand his innocence to God's tribunal is truly innocent. From Proverbs: the plain command not to repay evil with evil — "Do not say, 'I will do to him as he has done to me; I will pay the man back for what he has done'" (see Prov 24:29, condensed rendering); and the image of the pit-digger falling in himself: "Whoever digs a pit will fall into it" (Prov 26:27, Union Version). Read the two verses against Joab: every line of his reasoning was the arithmetic of "as he did to me," and Proverbs judged that arithmetic long ago: the arithmetic may add up, but the striking of the blow is wrong. From the New Testament: the blessing of the peacemakers — "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God" (Matt 5:9, Union Version); and the teaching of overcoming evil with good: "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Rom 12:21, Union Version). Read the two against this chapter's ending: David's feast receiving a surrendered general and his following the bier to bury an enemy commander are the Old Testament preview of these two words of the Lord; and the Lord's words also set the final tribunal for this chapter — how justice is settled in the end rests not on the blade at the city gate, but on the ledger of the Son of God.

## Application: Six Kinds of People

First, to those worn down in a long war, a long situation. This chapter's general ledger is your sedative: strength and weakness are reckoned day by day, not by the single day. Concrete assignment: this week make a simple three-year table of your long situation — write the circumstances at each year's end in one line, compare only the direction, not the speed; if the direction moves toward the promise, put the table away and keep enduring; do not overturn three years of slope with one week's setback.

Second, to parents. The list of the six sons born in Hebron is your lesson: when children are small they are good news, but the pattern is being fixed day by day. Concrete assignment: this week think through, for each of your children, one hidden danger now growing in them — not a fault, but the one place most likely to grow crooked if left unattended — and for each place set one gentle, concrete act of companionship; half of David's pain was that he discovered it too late — you should start while the names are still only names.

Third, to those receiving someone who has come over and negotiating reconciliation. Learn David's whole set of rules: the motive is God's to judge, the reception is mine to keep. Concrete assignment: write the reconciliation you are negotiating into plain text — the terms, the courtesies, the manner of sending the person off — line by line, and carry them out as written; do not, because the other side's motive is impure, privately lower the treatment, set a trap, or keep a reserve; only when the rules are kept in the open can reconciliation stand.

Fourth, to those who keep a blood-account in their hearts. This chapter asks of you only one act: hand over the right of execution. Concrete assignment: tonight write that account down honestly — who, when, what is owed you — and when you have finished, add one line on the paper: judgment belongs to God, the date belongs to God, my hand stays clean; then put the paper away in a fixed place, and stop carrying it with you rehearsing lines of revenge. Handing the account over is not ingratitude or betrayal; it is leading the rest of your life back from the well of Sirah.

Fifth, to leaders who have strong men under them. David's order is your assignment: when something happens, in the first moment draw the boundary publicly, clear the name, mourn, entrust — none of the four steps may be missing. Concrete assignment: write down that matter in your team which you have never been able to manage and fear people will talk about, and check yourself against the four steps one by one — has the boundary been drawn? Has the name been cleared? Have those who were harmed been remembered? Is the entrustment real entrustment or real abandonment? Whichever step is missing, make it up this week; do not cover it over again with one more "there was nothing I could do."

Sixth, to the Paltiel kind of little people — those crushed in a great situation, robbed of what they love, without even a chance to plead. This chapter has kept for you, especially, the tears of that whole road to Bahurim: Scripture has not forgotten Paltiel, and neither has God. There is only one concrete assignment: bring your tears before God as prayer, without dressing them up and without making them into a grand doctrine; and in the church find one trustworthy person and tell this unregarded grievance in full once — being heard is the first step of healing; the God of this chapter heard Paltiel's weeping before it was David's turn to sing.

## Closing Response Prayer

Lord, we gather 2 Samuel 3 into a response. Teach those in long situations to recognize the slope and not abandon the long course; teach parents to see the hidden dangers behind the list while there is still time; teach those negotiating reconciliation to keep the rules in full — detaining no one, setting no ambush; teach those who keep a blood-account to put the blade down today and place the account in Your hand; teach leaders to have the honesty to say "I am weak," and the responsibility to clear names first and mourn first. For Abner, for Paltiel, and for all who died at city gates and wept on roads home, we keep silence before You, and we resolve before You: our generation will be people who set feasts and follow biers, not people who chase others down at the well of Sirah. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt (for daily devotional use, 300–500 words)

In 2 Samuel 3, unification is one step away: at the feast in Hebron Abner promises to gather all Israel, and David sends him out of the city in peace (see 2 Sam 3:17-21, condensed rendering). But Joab secretly brings the man back from the well of Sirah and stabs him to death beside the gate of Hebron, the city of refuge, to avenge his brother Asahel (see 2 Sam 3:22-27, condensed rendering). When David hears of it, he declares that he and his kingdom are forever guiltless, hands judgment to the LORD, follows the bier himself, weeps aloud, and makes a song: your hands were not bound, your feet were not put in fetters (see 2 Sam 3:28-39, condensed rendering). Three questions for meditation today: the blood-account I keep — is the right of execution still in my hand? When I receive those who come over, are the rules kept in the open? That matter I cannot handle — is it true entrustment, or false abandonment? Prayer: Lord, teach me to put the blade down and hand the account to You; teach me to be, at the city gate, one who follows the bier, not one who chases a man down.

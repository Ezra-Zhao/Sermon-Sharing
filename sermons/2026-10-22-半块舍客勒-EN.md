## Summary (about 100 words)

The tabernacle blueprint reaches its last group of furnishings and rules: the golden altar of incense stands outside the veil, before the ark, and incense is burned morning and evening; when the people are numbered, each gives half a shekel as the ransom for his life, the rich and the poor at the same price; the bronze laver holds water for the priests to wash their hands and feet before entering the tent or approaching the altar; and the holy anointing oil and holy incense are each made by their appointed recipe, for holy use only, never to be copied or diverted. The half shekel is the smallest thing of all, and it says the weightiest word: a life numbered by God has a price, and all are priced the same.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The blueprint on the mountain in the latter half of Exodus has, with today's chapter, nearly come to its end. We have passed the ark, the mercy seat, the table of showbread, and the golden candlestick; the curtains, the boards, the court, and the altar of brass; the priestly garments and the rite of consecration. At the end of the last chapter God said, there I will meet with you, and speak with you; and the tent shall be sanctified by My glory. When the words have come to such nearness and such glory, chapter 30 suddenly draws the camera back to several very concrete, very daily things: a small altar one cubit square, a piece of silver weighing only half a shekel, a bronze basin holding water, a jar of compounded oil, and a packet of blended spices. Not one of these is earth-shaking; none is large in size or heavy in weight, and the actions are repeated day after day. Yet the nearer we go toward the inner sanctuary, the more the text is like this: great things are hidden in small things, and nearness is hidden in rules.

Let us first see the structure of the whole chapter clearly. Its thirty-eight verses fall into five groups. The first is the altar of incense, verses 1 to 10: its making, dimensions, materials, and place, and the rule that Aaron shall burn incense daily and make atonement upon its horns once a year. The second is the ransom money at the numbering, verses 11 to 16: when Moses numbers the people, each one numbered gives half a shekel, as money to make atonement for his life, appointed for the service of the tabernacle. The third is the laver of brass, verses 17 to 21: the making of the basin and its foot, the water in it, and the rule that the priests wash their hands and feet before entering the tent or approaching the altar, lest they die — a statute for ever. The fourth is the holy anointing oil, verses 22 to 33: its recipe, the furnishings and men it anoints, and the prohibition against copying it or pouring it upon an outsider. The fifth is the holy incense, verses 34 to 38: its recipe and making, and the prohibition against making the like for oneself. Read in this order, one sequence is very clear: first the incense upon the altar, then the ransom for a man's life, then washed hands and feet, then the set-apartness of oil and incense — in this matter of drawing near to God, there is first the daily service of prayer and offering, then the solemnity of a life being counted, then the daily cleansing, and last a boundary in which everything, from the furnishings to the very fragrance, is set apart as holy.

In preaching this chapter, this manuscript still keeps the rules of this series, stated honestly at the outset. First, workmanship and materials are taken up only where they relate to the main line, never spread out item by item: the detail of the golden rings and staves and the weights in the recipes have their own place in the text; this manuscript treats only the few strokes that carry the main line — the place of the altar of incense, the same price of the ransom money, the statute of washing, and the rule that oil and incense may not be diverted; the rest is left for verse-by-verse study. Second, every symbolic use is marked by its level: what the text states directly — this money makes atonement for the life, a man must wash before he approaches the altar — this manuscript preaches as direct statement; homiletical exposition — for example, that what stands nearest to God's service is hands and feet washed daily — is an induction drawn from the text's arrangement and statute and is marked on the spot; and any New Testament reading, such as the link in Revelation between incense and the prayers of the saints, is marked as the New Testament's reading, never poured back into what this chapter meant at the time. Third, where neither the text nor scholarship has made a matter clear, this manuscript says that it is discussed and does not expand: the botanical identification of several ingredients of the holy incense, for instance, is still disputed among scholars, and this manuscript states that honestly, without issuing a ruling.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And thou shalt make an altar to burn incense upon: of shittim wood shalt thou make it… And thou shalt put it before the vail that is by the ark of the testimony, before the mercy seat that is over the testimony, where I will meet with thee." (Exodus 30:1, 6, KJV, excerpt)

"This they shall give, every one that passeth among them that are numbered, an half shekel after the shekel of the sanctuary: (a shekel is twenty gerahs:) an half shekel shall be the offering of the LORD. Every one that passeth among them that are numbered, from twenty years old and above, shall give an offering unto the LORD." (Exodus 30:13-14, KJV)

"For Aaron and his sons shall wash their hands and their feet thereat: when they go into the tabernacle of the congregation, they shall wash with water, that they die not; or when they come near to the altar to minister, to burn offering made by fire unto the LORD." (Exodus 30:19-20, KJV)

"Moreover the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Take thou also unto thee principal spices… And thou shalt make it an oil of holy ointment… And thou shalt speak unto the children of Israel, saying, This shall be an holy anointing oil unto me throughout your generations." (Exodus 30:22-23, 25, 31, KJV, excerpts)

Let us pray: Lord, You set out the way of drawing near to You thing by thing, so concretely: incense, silver, water, and oil. Open our eyes today to see the weight of a life in half a shekel, the lesson of daily cleansing beside the laver, and how seriously You keep the boundary of what is set apart as holy. Teach us not merely to admire the glory of nearness, but to come before You as You have appointed. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Altar of Incense — Outside the Veil, Before the Ark, Incense Morning and Evening (30:1-10)

The first part looks at the altar of incense, verses 1 to 10. Verse 1 opens directly: "And thou shalt make an altar to burn incense upon: of shittim wood shalt thou make it." Altar of incense, in the original mizbach haqetoret (מִזְבַּח הַקְּטֹרֶת, H4196 + H7004) [1]: mizbach is an altar, qetoret is incense, smoke; together, the altar for offering incense. It is not large: a cubit its length, a cubit its breadth, foursquare, and two cubits its height. Its horns are of the same piece with it; its top and its sides all around are overlaid with pure gold; a crown of gold is made around it; and two golden rings are set under the crown for the staves with which it is carried, the staves themselves shittim wood overlaid with gold. A small altar, one cubit square, covered wholly in gold, answering in style to the ark and the table of showbread: shittim wood within, gold without, rings and staves complete, so that it can be carried on the journey. Of its workmanship this manuscript takes only one stroke related to the main line, and marks it: small as this altar is, its gold covers it completely and its rings and staves lack nothing — it is no ornament, but a furnishing for daily use; the daily service of the holy place is never improvised; it is prepared, portable, and used day after day as appointed. Small in size, it is not light in weight, because the place where it stands matters most.

Its place is verse 6, and the text is extremely precise: "And thou shalt put it before the vail that is by the ark of the testimony, before the mercy seat that is over the testimony, where I will meet with thee." Set the picture in your mind: within the holy of holies is the ark of the testimony, and upon it the mercy seat; the veil divides off the holy of holies; and outside the veil, on this side, directly facing the veil, stands the golden altar of incense. Between altar and ark there is only one veil. When Aaron stands before the altar burning incense, he is one veil away from the place where God meets with men. This manuscript states it directly: the text does not place the altar of incense inside the holy of holies, nor out in the court; its place is outside the veil, facing the mercy seat — the furnishing of the holy place that stands nearest to the holy of holies. The golden candlestick is to the south, the table of showbread to the north, and the altar of incense in the middle, at the front; of the three furnishings in the holy place, the altar of incense is nearest the veil. As a structural observation, marked as such: nearness has its order, and distance its distinctions; the altar's place shows that offering incense is the nearest place to God that a man can stand at daily. Incense may not be burned just anywhere; the altar has its place and man has his boundary, and that boundary itself is reverence.

The altar's use is in verses 7 and 8, and it is given in terms of time: "And Aaron shall burn thereon sweet incense every morning: when he dresseth the lamps, he shall burn incense upon it. And when Aaron lighteth the lamps at even, he shall burn incense upon it, a perpetual incense before the LORD throughout your generations." Two points of time, morning and evening, both bound to the lamps: in the morning, when he dresses the lamps; at evening, when he lights them — the incense is offered together with the lamps. This manuscript states the structure directly: incense and lamps belong together, in the holy place, to the morning and evening service. The light of the candlestick must be tended morning and evening, and the incense on the altar must be burned morning and evening; half of the priest's daily work in the holy place is in the light, and half in the incense. Verse 9 lays down a prohibition: "Ye shall offer no strange incense thereon, nor burnt sacrifice, nor meat offering; neither shall ye pour drink offering thereon." Each altar has its office: the altar of brass in the court receives the slain offerings; the golden altar in the holy place burns incense only; incense cannot be exchanged for another offering, nor another offering moved onto this altar. As direct statement, marked as such: being set apart concerns not only men and things set apart as holy; office and use are set apart too — the right thing, on the right altar, is an offering rightly made.

Verse 10 lifts the altar of incense to the most solemn moment of the year: "And Aaron shall make an atonement upon the horns of it once in a year with the blood of the sin offering of atonements: once in the year shall he make atonement upon it throughout your generations: it is most holy unto the LORD." Blood is put upon the four horns of the altar. Ordinarily there is only incense upon this altar; once a year, its horns see blood. This manuscript states it directly: even the altar on which incense is offered daily itself needs, once a year, to be cleansed by the blood of the sin offering; if the altar is so, how much more the man who stands before the altar. This verse connects with the rites of the Day of Atonement in Leviticus, cited here only as an intra-book context, without expanding its detail; what can be stated directly is that the altar's holiness lies not in its material being gold, but in this, that it is both used daily and cleansed yearly by blood — holiness is maintained, re-separated again and again by the blood of the offering. At this point one New Testament reading must be marked, and its line drawn clearly: in Revelation chapter 8, an angel takes a golden censer, and much incense is given him with the prayers of all saints, which he offers upon the altar before God, and the smoke of the incense with the prayers of the saints ascends before God — that is the New Testament author's reading, within a vision, of the relation between incense and prayer; this manuscript cites it as a New Testament reading, and does not say that the people of Exodus already understood it so at the time. What this chapter itself can state directly is only that Aaron burned sweet incense upon the altar morning and evening, a perpetual incense before the LORD throughout the generations. The New Testament reading naturally makes us think today of the fragrance of prayer; this chapter's own words rest first, honestly, upon the daily service: once in the morning, once at evening, day after day, generation after generation. The way of drawing near to God has, it turns out, this side to it: not an occasional single blaze of fervour, but a perpetual burning, morning and evening.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Ransom Money and the Laver — Lives at One and the Same Price, and Hands and Feet Washed Daily (30:11-21)

The second part looks at two groups: one tells how a man's life is counted, the other how the ministering man is washed clean. First, the ransom money, verses 11 to 16 — the focal point of the whole chapter. Verses 11 and 12 give the scene: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, When thou takest the sum of the children of Israel after their number, then shall they give every man a ransom for his soul unto the LORD, when thou numberest them; that there be no plague among them, when thou numberest them." Numbering, in the ancient world, was no small matter: it counted heads, reckoned soldiers and taxes, and behind it stood royal power and strength; and in Scripture, numberings that spring from human pride and trust in numbers often bring disaster. Here God Himself commands the numbering, and at the very moment of counting lays down the rule of the ransom. This manuscript first draws the line of the passage's own sense and states it directly: verse 12 says plainly that this ransom is bound up with deliverance from plague at the numbering — a man who is numbered is not simply counted over for nothing; every one numbered gives a ransom for his own soul, that no plague come among them. This is the ransom money within the rite of numbering, with its setting at the time; this manuscript does not draw it out into a purchase of salvation: salvation cannot be bought for half a shekel, and the text never says so. What is said here is that a man numbered by God acknowledges, by the ransom, that his life is in God's hand and not at his own disposal. With that measure kept clear, the true weight of this passage can be heard.

Verse 13 fixes the amount: "This they shall give, every one that passeth among them that are numbered, an half shekel after the shekel of the sanctuary… an half shekel shall be the offering of the LORD." Shekel of the sanctuary, in the original sheqel haqodesh (שֶׁקֶל הַקֹּדֶשׁ, H8255 + H6944) [3]: sheqel is the shekel, both a weight and a unit of money; haqodesh is "of the sanctuary." After the shekel of the sanctuary, a shekel is twenty gerahs, and half a shekel is ten gerahs. Why after the shekel of the sanctuary? This manuscript states it directly: the balance that weighs the ransom is not the market's balance but the sanctuary's — the price of a life is set neither by the market nor by favour, but by God's sanctuary. The amount itself is not large — only half a shekel, less perhaps than a craftsman's daily wage — yet the text insists on telling us this half shekel with such care: its unit, its reckoning, its use, each given one by one. Verse 14 fixes its range: "Every one that passeth among them that are numbered, from twenty years old and above, shall give an offering unto the LORD." Men of twenty years and above are within the numbering, and every one gives half a shekel; there is no list of exceptions. Verse 15 is the heart of the whole section: "The rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less than half a shekel, when they give an offering unto the LORD, to make an atonement for your souls." The rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less — read this sentence slowly. The rich man cannot give more, cannot add a premium to his own life with more silver; the poor man cannot give less, cannot discount his own life because his hand is tight. In this matter of the ransom money, rich and poor are at the same price. In the original, this ransom is called kofer nafsho (כֹּפֶר נַפְשׁוֹ, H3724 + H5315) [2]: kofer is a ransom, a price of covering; nephesh is the life, the soul, the whole person; together, the ransom for his life — and it is exactly this word that the text uses for making atonement for his own soul. As an induction, marked here as homiletical exposition: a man cannot be numbered for nothing; a life numbered by God has a price, and all are priced the same. A prince's life is worth no more than a farmer's; a rich household's life weighs no heavier than a poor man's. On the sanctuary's balance, on one side lies half a shekel, and on the other, every man's equally precious life. The world weighs men by their fortune, their ability, their usefulness; God numbers men by first having every one give the same portion, levelling the balance of high and low at the very door of the sanctuary.

Verse 16 gives the silver its destination: "And thou shalt take the atonement money of the children of Israel, and shalt appoint it for the service of the tabernacle of the congregation; that it may be a memorial unto the children of Israel before the LORD, to make an atonement for your souls." The ransom silver does not go into a treasury for military spending; it is appointed for the service of the tabernacle, and it is a memorial before the LORD. This manuscript states its twofold use directly: on one side, something entirely practical — the silver serves the ministry and upkeep of the tabernacle; later, in chapter 38, the silver sockets and hooks are cast from precisely this silver gathered at the numbering, cited here as an intra-book intertext. On the other side, a memorial — the silver lies before God like the voucher of a register: these men have been numbered, the ransom has been given, their lives are recorded with God. Numbering thus ceases to be man's tally of men; it becomes God's remembrance of men. Brothers and sisters, when we read this passage today, our hearts should grow quiet: to be written in God's register was never a cheap matter of being recorded for free, nor an auction in which the highest bidder wins; it is an order of grace in which all are priced the same, all are ransomed, and all are remembered. Half a shekel is small enough to be held in a fist, yet the word it speaks is large enough to correct a lifetime's habit of weighing men — weigh no man's life by his fortune again; before God, every one who is numbered is worth the same ransom.

Next comes the laver, verses 17 to 21. Verses 17 and 18: "And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Thou shalt also make a laver of brass, and his foot also of brass, to wash withal: and thou shalt put it between the tabernacle of the congregation and the altar, and thou shalt put water therein." Laver, in the original kiyor (כִּיּוֹר, H3595) [4], is a great bronze basin, a basin of water. Its place is in the court, between the tabernacle and the altar: a priest coming in from the court passes the altar first, then moves toward the holy place, and between the two he must pass this basin. Its material is brass, answering to the brass of the altar in the court; later, chapter 38 says that the brass of this laver came from the mirrors of the women who assembled at the door — cited here as an intra-book intertext, from which this manuscript takes only one stroke: the women gave up the mirrors in which they saw their own faces, and they were melted down into a basin that holds water — from looking at one's own face, to washing the hands that minister; the use of the brass was rewritten by being set apart as holy. Verses 19 and 20 give the basin its office: "For Aaron and his sons shall wash their hands and their feet thereat: when they go into the tabernacle of the congregation, they shall wash with water, that they die not; or when they come near to the altar to minister, to burn offering made by fire unto the LORD." Before two actions, washing is required: entering the tabernacle, and approaching the altar to minister. What is washed is the hands and the feet: the hands that work and offer, the feet that walk in and out. The text's tone allows no negotiation: they shall wash, that they die not. Verse 21 closes: "So they shall wash their hands and their feet, that they die not: and it shall be a statute for ever to them, even to him and to his seed throughout their generations."

Here this manuscript offers an exposition and marks it as such: what stands nearest to God's service is, precisely, hands and feet washed daily. The priest is not a man washed once and clean for ever, settled once for all; he is a man who offers daily and enters daily, and therefore bends daily at the basin to wash his hands and feet. Cleanness in the sanctuary is not a one-time ceremonial feeling; it is a daily, repeated, unwearying lesson. Hands gather dust and feet carry soil; the more frequent the ministry, the more frequent the washing. As a further homiletical use, marked as such: so it is with our service today — do not admire one great cleansing, one great renewal, and despise the small daily lesson of confessing sin and returning before God to be washed. The lesson at the basin's edge is inconspicuous, yet it is the lesson of "that they die not." The text states directly the bond between washing and death; the exposition concerns our attitude toward daily cleansing; keep the two levels distinct, and the word is preached steadily: the man who draws near to God is first a pair of washed hands, a man willing to stop daily at the basin. The ransom money tells of lives at the same price; the laver tells of daily cleanness; one writes a man into God's register, the other washes him and brings him to the altar — these two groups of the second part, at its head and its tail, together tell in full how a numbered man comes before God.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: The Holy Anointing Oil and the Holy Incense — What Is Set Apart May Not Be Copied, nor Diverted to Private Use (30:22-38)

The third part looks at the last two groups: the holy anointing oil and the holy incense. They are a pair: one is applied, the other smelled; one is anointed upon furnishings and men, the other burned upon the altar and dissolved into the air; both have a strict recipe, and both carry the same prohibition — they may not be copied, and may not be put to private use. First, the holy oil, verses 22 to 33. In verses 22 and 23 God commands Moses to take principal spices: of pure myrrh five hundred shekels, of sweet cinnamon half so much, two hundred and fifty shekels, of sweet calamus two hundred and fifty shekels, of cassia five hundred shekels, after the shekel of the sanctuary, and of olive oil an hin. Of the weights this manuscript gives a plain account, without pharmacological explanation: the myrrh is flowing, the cinnamon is taken for its sweetness, calamus and cassia are both spices, and the olive oil is the base into which all the spices are compounded as ointment. Verse 25 gives this oil its name and appoints its use: it is made, after the art of the apothecary, an oil of holy ointment, with which the tabernacle and the ark of the testimony are anointed, the table and its vessels, the candlestick and its vessels, the altar of incense, the altar of burnt offering and its vessels, and the laver and its foot. This oil, in the original shemen mishchat qodesh (שֶׁמֶן מִשְׁחַת־קֹדֶשׁ, H8081 + H4888 + H6944) [5]: shemen is oil, ointment; mishchah is anointing, an anointed thing; qodesh is holy; together, the holy anointing oil, the oil set apart as holy. The list in verses 26 to 28 counts up nearly every furnishing of the tabernacle, inside and out; this manuscript takes its structure and states it directly: where the oil reaches, the furnishing is sanctified — verse 29 says that these things shall be most holy, and whatsoever touches them shall be holy. This oil is no preservative, no protection against rust; it is the mark of separation: whatever place it has touched is set apart from common use and belongs to God's service.

In verse 30 the oil passes from the furnishings to men: "And thou shalt anoint Aaron and his sons, and consecrate them, that they may minister unto me in the priest's office." Furnishings anointed are set apart as holy; men anointed take up an office; oil poured upon head and body is a public, visible separation. Verse 31 declares to Israel: "This shall be an holy anointing oil unto me throughout your generations." Verse 32 then lays down the prohibition: "Upon man's flesh shall it not be poured, neither shall ye make any other like it, after the composition of it: it is holy, and it shall be holy unto you." And verse 33: "Whosoever compoundeth any like it, or whosoever putteth any of it upon a stranger, shall even be cut off from his people." This manuscript states the boundary directly: the recipe of the holy oil is God's giving; its patent is not in men's hands; no man may compound oil privately after this recipe, nor pour the oil upon a man who ought not to be anointed. What is set apart may not be copied, and may not be diverted to private use — that sentence is this manuscript's induction from verses 32 and 33, marked here as such. Why so severe? Because the moment it is copied, being set apart becomes something that can be mass-produced, bought and sold, and used to adorn oneself; God keeps the recipe within the sanctuary precisely to teach men that holiness is not a fragrance or an effect, but a belonging — what belongs to God cannot then belong to a man's private fancy. Men today most easily turn spiritual things into products, into styles, into personal brands; the prohibition of the holy oil blocks that road in advance: what looks holy is not thereby holy; the fragrance can be imitated, the separation cannot. This paragraph is homiletical exposition, whose foundation remains the text's own prohibition.

Then the holy incense, verses 34 to 38, answering the oil at the close as a pair. Verse 34: "And the LORD said unto Moses, Take unto thee sweet spices, stacte, and onycha, and galbanum; these sweet spices with pure frankincense: of each shall there be a like weight." Stacte is a gum that drops from the tree; onycha and galbanum are names of spices; frankincense is the best known of them. This manuscript states it plainly and marks it: of these spices, apart from frankincense, which is comparatively certain, the exact botanical or resinous source of stacte, onycha, and galbanum is still discussed among scholars to this day; this manuscript follows the received names and the text's weights, without expanding the identification and without issuing a conclusion. What can be stated directly is the manner of the recipe: each ingredient of a like weight, and, as with the holy oil before it, everything by the proportion God appointed, not to be increased or lessened at the compounder's pleasure. Verse 35: it is to be made, after the art of the apothecary, tempered together, pure and holy — salt is in it, keeping the incense pure and lasting, and the word the text uses is the "holy" of holiness itself. Verse 36: "And thou shalt beat some of it very small, and put of it before the testimony in the tabernacle of the congregation, where I will meet with thee: it shall be unto you most holy." Part of the incense is beaten very small and placed before the ark of the testimony — exactly where the altar of the first part stands. Join the beginning and the end: the first group treats the altar, the fifth treats the incense burned upon the altar; the altar has been given, the incense has been given, and altar and incense together are most holy.

Verses 37 and 38 give the prohibition of the holy incense, answering the prohibition of the holy oil word for word: "And as for the perfume which thou shalt make, ye shall not make to yourselves according to the composition thereof: it shall be unto thee holy for the LORD. Whosoever shall make like unto that, to smell thereto, shall even be cut off from his people." Note the motive verse 38 names: "to smell thereto." What does the man who copies the holy incense want? He wants that pleasant fragrance; he wants to move the sweetness offered to God into his own daily enjoyment. This manuscript states the text's judgment directly: even the fragrance is set apart; what is given to God may not be taken for daily enjoyment. Incense rising in the sanctuary is offering; incense lit in one's own house to be smelled is enjoyment; the same recipe, one offered and one enjoyed — the boundary lies exactly there. As a closing exposition, marked as such: these last two groups, an oil and an incense, teach one and the same lesson — the holiness of a thing set apart lies not in the rarity of its materials but in the clarity of its belonging; the moment it is copied after the recipe and diverted to private use, holiness has been quietly exchanged for human enjoyment and a human brand. Brothers and sisters, this reminder comes very close to us today: the forms of worship, a spiritual atmosphere, a pleasant fragrance, a beautiful oil — all can be imitated, copied, and consumed; belonging alone cannot be copied — whether a man, a furnishing, or a ministry belongs in the end to God, or to one's own reputation and enjoyment, God distinguishes with perfect clarity. The half shekel levelled the weighing of lives; the holy oil and holy incense drew the line of belonging clearly: a ransomed man no longer belongs to himself, and a thing set apart as holy no longer serves private use. These two lines, at the head and tail of chapter 30, are the measure a man who draws near to God should remember best.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 30. Remember first the golden altar of incense outside the veil: one cubit square, overlaid with gold, facing the mercy seat; morning and evening, when Aaron dresses and lights the lamps, he burns incense — incense and lamps together are the morning and evening service — and once a year the blood of the sin offering is put upon its horns. Remember next that half shekel: at the numbering every one gives half, after the shekel of the sanctuary; the rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less — a life has a price, and all are priced the same; and this silver is appointed for the service of the tabernacle, a memorial before the LORD. Remember also the laver of brass, standing between the tabernacle and the altar: before entering the tent or approaching the altar the priests must wash their hands and their feet, that they die not — a statute throughout their generations; the ministry nearest to God begins with daily washing. Remember last the holy anointing oil and the holy incense: an oil and an incense, each with its recipe, each with its prohibition — not to be copied, not poured upon a stranger, not made for oneself to smell: the belonging of what is set apart as holy allows no diversion. May we too come before God in this way today: with prayer as our daily service, viewing men by lives of the same price, serving with daily cleansing, and offering ourselves with a clear belonging — not copying, not diverting, not belonging to ourselves.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You for appointing personally even half a shekel, a basin of water, and a jar of oil. Teach us to value every person numbered by You, and not to weigh men by their fortune; teach us to wash our hands and feet before You daily, and not to despise the cleansing of ordinary days; and keep us from diverting what is set apart as holy to private use, from taking what is Yours to adorn ourselves. May our lives truly be remembered by You, and our ministry truly be holy unto You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "The rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less than half a shekel, when they give an offering unto the LORD, to make an atonement for your souls." (Exodus 30:15, KJV) "When they go into the tabernacle of the congregation, they shall wash with water, that they die not." (Exodus 30:20, KJV, excerpt)

Meditate today on two small things. The first is half a shekel. On God's balance, my life and another's life are of the same price: I need not prove myself worth more by giving more, nor think my life discounted because I am poor and lacking. To be numbered and remembered by God does not depend on the size of the offering; it rests on the ransom — the same price for all, and all ransomed. Ask the Lord to remove my old balance for weighing people, so that I look at my family, my colleagues, and the inconspicuous people in the church remembering that they are equally precious before God. The second is a basin of water. The priests entered the tent and approached the altar daily, and daily they first washed their hands and their feet. My hands do many things each day, and my feet walk many roads, and daily they gather dust and carry soil; do not wait for a great crisis to think of being cleansed — today, and before every drawing near to God, pause, confess your own faults, and ask the Lord to wash you clean. Half a shekel teaches me rest; a basin of water teaches me dailiness. My life has been ransomed, so I live gladly; my hands and feet must be washed, so I wash them honestly. A day lived so is a day numbered, remembered, and washed clean before God.

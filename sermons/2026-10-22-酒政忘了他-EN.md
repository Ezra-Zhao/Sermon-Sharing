## Summary (about 100 words)

Pharaoh's chief butler and chief baker offended the king and were put in prison, in the same prison as Joseph. The two of them each dreamed a dream on the same night and woke with troubled faces. Joseph said, do not interpretations belong to God, and interpreted their dreams for them: in three days the butler would be restored, and in three days the baker would be hanged. Both came to pass as he interpreted. Joseph pleaded with the butler to remember him, but once things went well for the butler, he did not remember Joseph — he forgot him.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

At the end of the previous chapter, Joseph was already in prison. He had done nothing wrong; he was falsely accused by his master's wife and thrown in anger into the prison where the king's prisoners were bound. Yet in that chapter the Bible repeats one sentence: the LORD was with him, and whatever he did, the LORD made it to prosper, until even the keeper of the prison committed all the prisoners into his hand. In this chapter the story takes one step forward: God did not let Joseph out at once; God first sent two men in — two men who came from the king's palace, Pharaoh's chief butler and chief baker.

A falsely accused prisoner, waiting in prison; two men who had served the king, anxious in prison. This chapter has no miracle, no opened heaven, no shaking vision — only one night of dreams, one morning of troubled faces, three days of fulfillment, and one cold final sentence: he forgot him.

Let us read several key passages from today's Scripture together:

"And it came to pass after these things, that the butler of the king of Egypt and his baker had offended their lord the king of Egypt. And Pharaoh was wroth against two of his officers, against the chief of the butlers, and against the chief of the bakers. And he put them in ward in the house of the captain of the guard, into the prison, the place where Joseph was bound." (Genesis 40:1-3, excerpt, KJV)

"And they dreamed a dream both of them, each man his dream in one night, each man according to the interpretation of his dream. ... And they said unto him, We have dreamed a dream, and there is no interpreter of it. And Joseph said unto them, Do not interpretations belong to God? tell me them, I pray you." (Genesis 40:5, 8, excerpt, KJV)

"But think on me when it shall be well with thee, and shew kindness, I pray thee, unto me, and make mention of me unto Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house." (Genesis 40:14, excerpt, KJV)

"Yet did not the chief butler remember Joseph, but forgat him." (Genesis 40:23, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today we are going to sit for a while with Joseph in the prison. Comfort every one among us who is waiting — waiting for news, waiting for a turnaround, waiting for someone to turn back and remember him. Let us hear clearly today: human remembrance breaks, but Your remembrance does not break. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Men from the Palace Enter the Prison — Joseph Still Serves People in Prison (40:1-8)

First look at the opening of this chapter. The butler and the baker of Pharaoh, king of Egypt, offended their lord. The butler was the man who handed the king his cup; the baker was the man in charge of the king's food — both men who served the king at close quarters. Those who serve at close quarters know the king's temper best, and they also know best how near the king's anger is: today you are still standing before the king, tomorrow you are put in prison. Pharaoh was angry, and these two officers were put in ward in the house of the captain of the guard. Who was the captain of the guard? Potiphar. The text deliberately adds a sentence: the place where Joseph was bound. The Bible's pen is sparing, yet thoughtful everywhere — into the house of the man who had wronged Joseph there now came men of the king; Joseph's prison suddenly had air from the palace moving through it.

The captain of the guard charged Joseph to serve them. Notice this arrangement: Joseph did not go to visit the prison, nor to listen to stories; he went to serve. Pouring water, bringing food, tidying up, watching faces — a prisoner serving prisoners, and two prisoners who had once had standing before the king at that. If a man's heart is filled only with his own grievance, he cannot do this kind of serving. My case has not been overturned, my future is nowhere in sight — why should I serve you? Joseph did not ask that. Wherever he was, he treated that place as his post of service: in his father's house he was the beloved son, in Potiphar's house he was the servant in charge of the whole household, in prison he was the man in charge of the prisoners, and when it came to serving the butler and the baker, he still served them conscientiously. Gifts and character do not pick their settings. The lower the setting, the more it shows what is actually inside a person.

They were kept in prison for some time. The text says, a season — how long exactly, it does not write. Days of waiting often have no number in the Bible, because the one waiting knows best himself: days without a number are the longest days.

Then one night, the two men each dreamed a dream. The butler dreamed of a vine, and on the vine were three branches; it budded, blossomed, and brought forth ripe grapes; he pressed the grapes into Pharaoh's cup and put the cup into Pharaoh's hand. The baker dreamed that he had three white baskets on his head, and in the uppermost basket all manner of baked food for Pharaoh, and the birds ate it out of the basket. The two of them dreamed on the same night, each dream with its own interpretation. This was no coincidence; this was the hand of God: the same night, two dreams, one life and one death, and the two men slept the most important night of their lives beside Joseph.

In the morning Joseph came in to them and saw that they were sad. He asked: Wherefore look ye so sadly to day? Please pause on this question. In a prison, whose face is not sad? Was there no sadness on Joseph's own face? Yet the first thing he saw early in the morning was the sadness on other people's faces. A man who has been wronged, has suffered, has been locked up and forgotten, and can still see at a glance the difficulty on someone else's face, and is still willing to open his mouth and ask — such a man's heart has not been eaten away by bitterness. The most frightening thing about suffering is not that it leaves wounds on you; it is that it makes you able to see only your own wounds from then on. Joseph was not like that. In these years in prison his eyes were still bright and his heart still soft; he entered the door looking at people first, and at himself afterward.

They answered: We have dreamed a dream, and there is no interpreter of it. Egypt was a great nation of dream interpretation, and Pharaoh's palace kept wise men and magicians, but there were none in the prison. Two men who had served the king could not now hire even one interpreter. Joseph heard them and spoke the sentence that anchors the whole chapter: "Do not interpretations belong to God? tell me them, I pray you."

This sentence has two layers of meaning. The first layer is humility: interpreting dreams is not my ability; it is God's grace; I have nothing to boast of, I only speak out what God reveals. The second layer is courage: in Egypt, a land full of false gods, in the king's prison, he openly confessed that the interpretation of dreams is in the hand of the one true God. He did not turn his faith to silent because he was in prison, nor did he speak auspicious words first because the other party were the king's men. He gave the glory to God first, and then opened his mouth to tell the dreams. Brothers and sisters, whether a gift is used rightly or not is seen in this one sentence: before using the gift, first make clear where the gift comes from. Later, before Pharaoh, Joseph still said this same sentence — it is not in me; God shall give Pharaoh an answer of peace. Consistent through a whole life — this is called character.

There is one more layer we must not miss: Joseph served them not for a day or two. The text says the captain of the guard committed them to Joseph, and after they had spent some time in prison, Joseph came to that night of dreams. That is to say, before the miraculous dreams appeared, there first came day after day of ordinary service: what was carried in was food, what was carried out was empty bowls; what was asked was whether they had slept well, what was heard was sighing and complaining. No one records merit for such days, and no one applauds such days. But brothers and sisters, God's work is often arranged this way: first the faithfulness of ordinary days, then the gift of the special moment; and when the special moment arrives, the ones who can receive it are precisely those who have already stood firm in the ordinary days. If Joseph's daily service had been careless, cold, and resentful, then on the morning after that night's dreams, the two men would not have told him the sadness in their hearts. The sadness was seen by him first; the dreams were heard by him afterward — the order cannot be reversed: people first regard you as someone approachable, and only then will they place their most important dream before you to interpret.

This also explains why gifts are often revealed not to the most capable person, but to the one most willing to bend down. Pharaoh's palace was full of wise men and magicians; in learning and in grandeur, which of them was not stronger than Joseph in prison? Yet God sent the two dreams into the prison to be interpreted. God's revelation does not follow grandeur; God's revelation follows faithfulness. If the place where you are today is low, small, and unseen by anyone, do not hurry to pity yourself: do today's bowl of food, today's sentence, today's small matter well. Who knows — perhaps on some morning, the person you have been serving all along will gently place before you the dream of his night.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Three Branches and Three Baskets — He Spoke Straight, and Did Not Pick the Pleasant Things to Say (40:9-19)

The butler told his dream first. Joseph listened and gave the interpretation: the three branches are three days. Within three days Pharaoh shall lift up thine head and restore thee unto thy place, and thou shalt deliver Pharaoh's cup into his hand, after the former manner. Branch, in the original sarig (שָׂרִיג, H8299), specifically a vine branch, a tendril of the vine [4]. The branches of a vine look withered in winter, but in their season they bud, blossom, and bear fruit all in one breath. In the butler's dream, that whole process took only a moment: budding, blossoming, grapes ripe, juice pressed, cup delivered. Joseph heard the rhythm in it: fast, and a return to the original place. This was a dream of grace — not slowly enduring one's way out, but within three days the king himself would lift him up.

Please note Joseph's word for interpretation: in the original pithron (פִּתְרוֹן, H6623), the interpretation of a dream, the solution of a riddle [2]; and the act of interpreting, in the original patar (פָּתַר, H6622), to interpret, to explain the meaning of a dream [1]. These two words appear again and again in this chapter. The Bible does not use words carelessly: anyone can dream a dream, but not everyone can give an interpretation. Egypt's magicians could read faces and say what the king loved to hear; the interpretation Joseph gave, however, carried a date and an outcome, and could be verified. Three days — if after three days it did not come to pass, no one in that prison would ever trust his mouth again. A true gift dares to be verified; a false gift dares only to say vague, pleasant things. Joseph stated it firmly, because the revelation was not something he had made up.

Then the baker told his dream. He had seen that the butler's interpretation was good, hope rose in his heart, and he told his dream as well: I had three white baskets on my head, and in the uppermost basket there was all manner of baked food for Pharaoh, and the birds ate it out of the basket on my head. Joseph's interpretation, however, was another scene altogether: the three baskets are three days. Within three days Pharaoh shall lift up thy head from thee and shall hang thee on a tree, and the birds shall eat thy flesh from off thee.

The same three days — one man restored to his place, one man hanged on a tree; the same mouth of Joseph — one report of joy, one report of sorrow. Joseph's uprightness is seen most clearly here. He could perfectly well have softened the baker's words a little: we are all in prison together, why state it so absolutely? If he had reported only joy and not sorrow, both men would have been grateful to him, and both would have spoken for him once they got out. But he did not. As he received, so he delivered; where it was restoration, he added no embellishment, and where it was a death sentence, he painted nothing over it. Why? Because he had just said, do not interpretations belong to God — since they belong to God, he had no right to alter God's words. Those who proclaim God's word still live by this one rule today: do not pick the pleasant things to say, and do not revise the message according to faces. To speak sorrow as joy is not grace; it is deception. To hide the tree that is three days away and not speak of it is to take away from that listener even the chance to prepare himself. Joseph's honesty toward the baker was precisely his last respect toward a man about to die.

There is one more detail: in the baker's dream, the birds ate the baked food in the basket; in Joseph's interpretation, the birds ate the flesh from off him. The dream hid the most terrible scene under one layer; the interpretation uncovered it. God's revelation to people is sometimes like this: He does not spread the whole truth out at once; through the mouth of His servant He uncovers, honestly, the layer that was hidden. When we listen to preaching we should be prepared for this too: God's word does not always come to comfort us; sometimes it comes to warn us, to stop us, and to save us from the outcome underneath that basket.

On the third day was Pharaoh's birthday, and he made a feast for all his servants. Among his servants he lifted up the head of the chief butler and restored him to his place, and he hanged the chief baker — everything came to pass exactly as Joseph had interpreted. One birthday, two outcomes. At the king's feast the cups were raised and exchanged; one door of the prison opened, and then closed again; on a tree there was one more man, and in the palace one more man handing the cup. Joseph stood in the prison, heard the news from outside, and in his heart understood one thing: my interpretation was entirely right; not one word of mine fell to the ground. His gift, in the lowest place, received its clearest confirmation.

But confirmation is not the same as release. The dreams were interpreted rightly, and the man had not yet gone out. The most piercing place in this chapter is not the baker's death, nor the butler's life, but the words Joseph himself spoke in between — that is the next part, and we will take it slowly.

Look also at the contrast of the two dreams set side by side, which is the craftsmanship of this chapter's structure. In the butler's dream, things grew upward: branches budded, flowers opened, fruit ripened, and his hand delivered the cup into the king's hand — everything toward the king, toward life, toward restoration. In the baker's dream, things pressed down on his head: three baskets stacked layer upon layer on top of his head, the most important food in the uppermost basket, pecked away by birds — he carried the king's food, yet got nothing of it himself. In one dream, a man delivers something into the palace; in the other dream, what is on a man's head is taken away by the birds of the air. When Joseph interpreted, both dreams used only one number: three. The three branches were three days, and the three baskets were also three days. The same measure measured out a difference as wide as heaven and earth. This reminds us: judgment and grace are sometimes separated by only a sheet of paper; of two men in the same prison, after three days one returned before the king and the other was hanged on a tree. When we hear someone else's good outcome, we should not assume our own outcome will copy it; when we hear someone else's warning, we should not hurry to fasten it onto our own heads. God's interpretation for each person is individual; those who come before God must each listen for their own portion.

Moreover, in telling these two dreams Joseph did not stop midway to pave a road for himself. If he had had a selfish motive, he could perfectly well have made only the butler's dream sound beautiful, passed vaguely over the baker's dream, and waited for the butler to repay the favor after his restoration. The Bible deliberately records both interpretations in full, so that we may see: he served God's word first, and calculated his own way out afterward. With the order set right, that later sentence, "think on me," stands upright — it was not a transaction; it was the frank, private request of a man who had handled the public matter with complete integrity.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: "Think on Me" and "Yet He Forgot Him" — The Lesson of Waiting (40:14-23)

Let us go back to the moment the butler received his interpretation. After Joseph had spoken the words of restoration, he immediately said the only words in the whole chapter that he spoke for himself: "But think on me when it shall be well with thee, and shew kindness, I pray thee, unto me, and make mention of me unto Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house. For indeed I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews: and here also have I done nothing that they should put me into the dungeon."

First let us say a fair word for Joseph: there was nothing wrong at all with this request. He did not bribe anyone, he did not conspire to break out of prison, he did not fabricate a story; he only asked a man who was about to be restored, a man who could speak before the king, to mention one sentence on behalf of a wronged man. This is reasonable, it is fitting, and it agrees with faith — that God uses people as channels has never been anything shameful. "Remember," in the original zakar (זָכַר, H2142), to remember, to keep in mind, to mention [3]. This is exactly the word Joseph used: think on me, and make mention of me unto Pharaoh. In the Bible, remembering is never merely thinking of something in the mind; remembering is meant to bring forth action: God remembered Noah, and caused the waters to recede; God remembered Rachel, and enabled her to bear children. The remembrance Joseph asked for meant this too: if you truly remember me, you will open your mouth, and you will act.

He also, unusually, told of his own wrong: I was stolen away, and here also have I done nothing. A teenager torn from his home, sold as a slave, and then imprisoned for keeping his integrity — such words he ordinarily did not say. In this chapter he said them, and said them to the butler alone, with excellent measure: he did not pour out his grievance to everyone in the prison, he did not use his wrong as capital; only to the most suitable person, at the most suitable moment, did he say the one most necessary sentence. To defend oneself is not shameful; to state one's wrong clearly is also part of waiting.

And then? Three days passed, the butler was restored, and went back to hand the cup before the king. The last sentence of the text, only a few words, is like a basin of cold water poured from head to foot: "Yet did not the chief butler remember Joseph, but forgat him."

Please note that the Bible used two words: first, did not remember; then, forgot him. "Forgot," in the original shakach (שָׁכַח, H7911), to forget, to neglect, to be oblivious [3]. Not remembering is taking no action; forgetting is that even the person himself has slipped out of the heart. The word "yet" carries the sigh of the narrator: how could he possibly forget? Your sadness that night, the dream he interpreted for you, the fulfillment exact to three days, his final sentence, "think on me" — all forgotten.

Why did the butler forget? The Bible does not explain, and we need not invent motives for him. What can be imagined is not difficult: who, having returned to the palace, still wants to remember the prison? To remember Joseph would be to remember his own days as a prisoner; and to speak to Pharaoh on behalf of a Hebrew slave would carry a little risk. When people get what is good for them, the disease they catch most easily is forgetfulness: the vows made in difficulty, the words spoken, the kindnesses owed — once the good thing is in hand, all of it becomes the past. The wise man in Ecclesiastes sighed over a similar scene: the shelf life of gratitude in the human heart is often pitifully short. This is not the butler's fault alone; it is the common disease of human nature: the one who receives kindness forgets quickly, and the one who showed kindness remembers long.

Then the question comes: what are we to make of Joseph's two years of waiting — from this chapter until Pharaoh dreamed, a full two years in between? His gift was confirmed, his request fell empty, and the man he had served forgot him. He still had to sit in prison for two more years. Of these two years the Bible writes almost nothing; it writes only the outcome: after two full years, Pharaoh dreamed, no wise man or magician in all Egypt could interpret it, and only then did the butler suddenly remember: I do remember my faults this day.

Brothers and sisters, please see this sequence clearly. The butler's remembrance was two years late, yet it was not one minute late: had he remembered a year earlier, Pharaoh had not yet dreamed, and mentioning it would have accomplished nothing; had he remembered a day later, Pharaoh would already have found someone else. When God's time arrived, that forgetful man suddenly remembered. This is not to say the butler's forgetfulness was right — forgetfulness is forgetfulness, and the Bible does not cover for him; it is to say that the one who held Joseph's future was never the butler's memory, but God's remembrance. Joseph entrusted his hope of release to a man, and there was nothing wrong with that; but his true release did not hang on a human memory. The psalmist once described the taste of being forgotten like this: I am forgotten as a dead man out of mind. Yet the prophet Isaiah brings God's own words: a mother may forget her nursing child, but God says, I will not forget thee. People forget; even the closest kin may forget; God's covenant remembrance does not forget.

So this chapter gives those who are waiting three applications, and I will state them one by one.

First, in the season of waiting, do not let your gift lie idle. The timetable of Joseph's release was not in his hand, but the gift of interpreting dreams was in his hand all along. He did not say, I will use it after I get out; he used it in the prison, and used it on two prisoners. Your season of waiting — waiting for work, waiting for status, waiting for recovery from illness, waiting for a child to turn back — that time is not a blank page in your life. God placed the gift in your hand not for you to unwrap only after you get out, but for you to use it wherever you are. The man who uses it accurately in prison is the man who will use it steadily in the palace.

Second, do not pick your setting for serving, and do not pick your objects either. Of the two men Joseph served, one lived and still forgot him, and one died three days later — calculated by return, this service was all loss. But God's accounts are not calculated that way: it was precisely this "losing" service that connected him to the palace. Do not decide whether to serve by the rate of return. Visit the person who will not thank you, help the person who will turn around and forget you, teach the person who may amount to nothing — the ledger in heaven remembers more clearly than people do.

Third, hand remembrance back to God. One may ask people, and one may entrust a favor, as Joseph did, openly and honorably; but after entrusting, hand the outcome back. If after asking you still cannot sleep, and after entrusting you still count the days every day, you have staked your peace on someone else's memory, and you have bet on the wrong horse. In those two years Joseph did not make a scene, did not complain, and did not entrust a second person — when we see him again in the next chapter, he still stands steady; he shaved, changed his clothing, and entering the palace he still said that same sentence: it is not in me; God shall answer. Two years of prison did not wear away his gift, nor did they wear bitterness into him. This is the man who has handed remembrance back to God: people have forgotten me, and I do not panic, because the One who remembers me has never forgotten.

One more word must be added, for those who at this moment, like Joseph, are sitting in some kind of "prison." Your prison may not have iron bars: it may be a job with no visible end, a case that has dragged on for a long time, a frozen relationship, a notice that is long in coming. What people fear most is not hardship; it is suffering while feeling that God, too, has forgotten them. This chapter is written precisely for that fear. In this chapter God does not speak a single word and does not perform a single miracle; He only quietly places two men, one night of dreams, and three days of fulfillment into Joseph's prison — and having placed them, He lets him wait two more years. God's silence does not mean God's absence; God's delay does not mean God's forgetfulness. Sometimes God is in no hurry to open the door because the dream outside the door has not yet been dreamed, and the time has not yet come; and before the time comes, He first makes the waiting man into a man who will not change his character even when he enters the palace. Later Joseph became ruler of Egypt, with power over the whole land, and he treated his brothers, his father, and the butler who had once forgotten him all without bitterness — that breadth of heart was not trained in the palace; it was waited out in those two years in prison. If waiting is waited rightly, when a man goes out he carries away not a bellyful of resentment, but a deeper steadiness.

So do not belittle this present stretch of days in which nothing seems to move. Use your gift well, serve people well, finish asking what ought to be asked of people, and for the rest, rest in handing it to the God who remembers people. His memory is the best memory in all the universe; in His ledger, not one entry is ever missed.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

This whole chapter, from beginning to end, tells only one thing: a man who could interpret dreams first learned to wait. In prison he served people, interpreted straightly, and did not pick the pleasant things to say; he asked, rightly, to be remembered, and people forgot him completely. The Bible was in no hurry to let him out, because on God's timetable his release had to wait for the night Pharaoh dreamed. Human remembrance breaks; God's remembrance does not break. The butler forgot him; God did not forget him. Two years later, when that prison door opened, what came in was not the butler's favor, but God's time.

Let us pray together: Lord who remembers people, thank You: You remembered Noah, You remembered Rachel, You remembered Joseph in the prison, and You remember us too. Comfort everyone who has been forgotten by people: forgotten by friends, forgotten by family, forgotten by those who made promises. Teach us not to let our gifts lie idle in the waiting, not to pick our settings in serving, and, after we have entrusted things to people, to hand the outcome peacefully back to You. We hand the forgetful people to You, and we hand our own future to You as well, because You have never forgotten one person who belongs to You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

Today's Scripture: "And Joseph said unto them, Do not interpretations belong to God? tell me them, I pray you." (Genesis 40:8, KJV) "But think on me when it shall be well with thee, and shew kindness, I pray thee, unto me, and make mention of me unto Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house." (Genesis 40:14, KJV) "Yet did not the chief butler remember Joseph, but forgat him." (Genesis 40:23, KJV)

What hurts most in this chapter is not the prison; it is being forgotten. Joseph interpreted the dream rightly, helped the right man, and made his request as clearly as it could be made — and once the butler was out and restored, he forgot him completely. Have you known that feeling? The people you helped never look back; the promises made to you are never mentioned again; you wait in the same place until you almost forget what you were waiting for. The Bible does not soften the sentence: the chief butler did not remember Joseph, but forgot him. Yet the Bible does not stop there. Two years later Pharaoh dreamed, the butler suddenly remembered, and then we see it: Joseph's way out was never tied to the butler's memory, but kept in God's remembrance. God remembered Noah, remembered Rachel, remembered Joseph — God's remembrance never lapses. If you are being forgotten today, do not rush to resentment: keep using your gift, keep serving, and hand the outcome back to God. Human remembrance breaks; God's remembrance does not. And on the day God brings you to mind, no one can hold shut the door He opens.

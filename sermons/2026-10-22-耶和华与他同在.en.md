## Summary (about 100 words)

Joseph was sold into Egypt and became Potiphar's slave, yet the LORD was with him, made all that he did to prosper, and gave him his master's trust. He refused his mistress's seduction, left his garment and fled, and was falsely accused and imprisoned; yet in prison the LORD was still with him, showed him mercy, and gave him favor and charge. God's presence is no guarantee against suffering; holding fast to holiness may cost dearly, yet behind the cost God's hand remains.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The end of the previous chapter still lingered in Canaan: Judah had left home, and the tear in the family kept widening. Today the pen turns, and the camera follows a caravan of Ishmaelites all the way into Egypt. In that caravan was a seventeen-year-old youth. Yesterday he was still the one held in his father's palm, wearing that coat of many colours, walking among his brothers; today the coat had been torn away, dipped in goat's blood, and sent back into his father's hands as false evidence, while he himself was bound with ropes, and his name in the slave traders' ledger was worth only a price. The first page of Genesis in Egypt ought first to have told us how this youth's days ahead would go, and whether he would ever rise. But the Bible did not write it that way. What the Bible gave first was four words: the LORD was with him.

Please note, these four words were not a seal stamped on only after he had turned his fortune around. The text says that as soon as he was brought down to Egypt, as soon as he was sold, this sentence was already written. So in today's message, I want us first to put away one expectation: God's presence does not first check whether your circumstances deserve it. A slave deserves it; a prisoner deserves it too. It was because God arrived first that the circumstances were slowly lifted up by His hand.

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several key passages:

"And Joseph was brought down to Egypt; and Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, captain of the guard, an Egyptian, bought him of the hands of the Ishmeelites, which had brought him down thither. And the LORD was with Joseph, and he was a prosperous man; and he was in the house of his master the Egyptian." (Genesis 39:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"And his master saw that the LORD was with him, and that the LORD made all that he did to prosper in his hand. And Joseph found grace in his sight, and he served him: and he made him overseer over his house, and all that he had he put into his hand." (Genesis 39:3-4, excerpt, KJV)

"How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?" (Genesis 39:9, excerpt, KJV)

"But the LORD was with Joseph, and shewed him mercy, and gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison." (Genesis 39:21, excerpt, KJV)

"Because the LORD was with him, and that which he did, the LORD made it to prosper." (Genesis 39:23, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today we are going to watch a man fall from a height into a pit, and be upheld by You in the pit. Speak to those among us who are in a valley, and speak also to those in smooth circumstances who easily forget themselves, and let us all learn one lesson: circumstances change; You do not change. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: In the Slave's House, God Arrived First (39:1-6a)

First, let us make the geography and the status clear. Joseph was sold by his brothers to the Ishmaelites, and the Ishmaelites brought him down to Egypt and sold him in turn to a man: "Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, captain of the guard, an Egyptian." The captain of the guard was a high official beside the king, in charge of the palace guard and also of prisons. Joseph fell into such a household, and his status was slave. In the ancient Near East a slave had no freedom and no wages; whatever his master told him to do, he did, and even where he would live tomorrow was not his to decide. Genesis records not one word of complaint from Joseph about these days — not because he did not suffer, but because the narrative's eye first fixed on something greater than the suffering: "And the LORD was with Joseph, and he was a prosperous man."

The word "prosperous" here, in the original tsaleach (צָלֵחַ, H6743), means to prosper, to succeed, to advance victoriously [1]. Please do not hear this word too small. It does not say Joseph had good luck, winning whatever he bought; it says that God's hand was on what he did, making things move toward completion. A slave's work was at first nothing more than sweeping floors, carrying things, running errands — yet even these small things were done properly, done reliably, done to a result. His master Potiphar was a thoroughly shrewd man, and he watched day by day; the text records his observation very concretely: "And his master saw that the LORD was with him, and that the LORD made all that he did to prosper in his hand." Note, it was the master who saw it first. An Egyptian official who did not know the LORD first saw, from the work of a Hebrew slave, that there was a God upon him. This is what witness looks like at its earliest: not first preaching a sermon, but first doing the work in your hand so that people can see the difference.

So trust was added step by step. At first Joseph only found grace in his master's sight, and his master was pleased with him; then he "made him overseer over his house" and handed the household over; then "all that he had he put into his hand." A foreign slave, within a few years, became steward of a great household; apart from the food his master himself ate, there was nothing great in the house that did not pass through his hand. The Bible does not say he climbed by any maneuvering — no gifts, no flattering words, no taking sides. The Bible gives only one reason: the LORD was with him. Presence in this passage appears in its most ordinary form: not a vision, not a marvel, but accounts kept clear, work done to completion, and a person who could be relied on. Brothers and sisters, when we wait for evidence of God's presence, we often wait for something great to arrive; Joseph teaches us that the evidence of God's presence often grows first in day-after-day small faithfulness. How you handle the small, unseen work in your hand at your company, at home, and in the church — given time, the people beside you will see it for themselves: this person has the Lord upon him.

There is another sentence we must not miss: "And it came to pass from the time that he had made him overseer in his house, and over all that he had, that the LORD blessed the Egyptian's house for Joseph's sake." The blessing overflowed Joseph himself and overflowed into the whole house: "the blessing of the LORD was upon all that he had in the house, and in the field." This is precisely the echo of God's promise to Abraham long ago: in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed. Joseph had not yet become the man who would bless all peoples; he first became the slave who blessed one household. God's promise does not pick its places to land: a palace will do, and a slave's courtyard will do as well. Your post today may be very low, so low that no one remembers your full name, yet if the God who is present is upon you, the small place where you stand will be different because of you from what it was before. Colleagues spared a little injustice because of you, a home given a little more peace because of you, a team given a measure more honesty because of you — these are all what blessing looks like when it lands.

At this point I must settle a heart first, lest we read this passage crooked. Joseph prospered; that does not mean Joseph did not suffer. When he prospered, his status was still slave; when he was trusted, the road home still had not even a shadow. Prosperity and deliverance are two different things. In this passage God had not yet turned him from a slave into a free man; what God did first was something else: on a slave's footing, He steadied him, formed him, and let the skill in his hands and the character in his heart grow inch by inch. Many people's prayers ask only one question: Lord, when will You move me out of here? Joseph's story first answers a different sentence: the Lord is here, with you. The order of these two sentences decides whether a person merely endures a valley or grows through it.

There is another layer: Joseph's age. When he was sold he was only seventeen, exactly the age for dreams — and he really had dreamed them: the sheaves bowing down to his sheaf, the sun, moon, and stars bowing down to him. Those dreams now looked like a joke: the people who would bow to him had not appeared, but the people who sold him had appeared first. God's presence did not prevent the dreams from first being pressed down into the soil. Not a few of us also had dreams in our youth, imagining what kind of person we would become and what kind of life we would live; then life struck blow after blow, the dreams broke, and we became realistic. What this passage tells us is how a person should live while the dream has not yet come true: not abandoning himself in despair in a slave's courtyard while clutching the dream, and not willing to do anything at all in order to turn his fortune quickly, but placing the dream in God's hand first and doing well the work in front of his eyes. The matter of the sheaves and the stars God had not forgotten; it simply was not yet time. The broom and the ledger in Joseph's hand were today's matter; hold today's steady first, and you will be fit for tomorrow's.

A word also for those at a height. Potiphar put everything into Joseph's hand, with one single thing he did not hand over, and could not hand over: his wife. However great the trust, there is a boundary; however deep the appointment, there is a forbidden zone. The temptation in the next passage enters precisely through this one exception. When a person is in smooth days, his ears are full of affirmation and his eyes full of convenience, and boundaries at such a time are most easily talked down into small matters. But the Bible's pen pauses here and deliberately writes that exception out plainly, as if ringing a bell in advance: what comes next is not an enemy from outside, but a voice from inside the house.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Cost of a Garment — Refusal, Flight, and False Accusation (39:6b-20)

When temptation came in, it first passed through the eyes. The text says Joseph was "a goodly person, and well favoured" — in the original, well-formed in body and fair in appearance. After some days, his mistress began to notice him. The Bible writes it very directly, hiding nothing: "his master's wife cast her eyes upon Joseph; and she said, Lie with me." This was no veiled hint; it was a daily siege: "she spake to Joseph day by day." "Day by day" is the most oppressive part of that phrase. Refusing once is easy; refusing every day is hard. What people fear most is not one great wave, but water rising a little every day: a sentence today, a step closer tomorrow, the boundary worn thin bit by bit in familiarity. How did Joseph answer? He "hearkened not unto her, to lie by her, or to be with her." Three refusals, each firmer than the last: he would not listen — the heart shut its door first; he would not lie by her — the body did not cross the line; he would not be with her — he avoided even the occasion. This is the clearest way a person can fight temptation: not by relying on willpower in the moment to hold out, but by keeping distance in ordinary days to stay alive.

Even more important is the reason he gave for refusing. He reckoned the account for his mistress: my master has committed all that he has to my hand, and he himself does not know what is in the house; only you he has kept back, because you are his wife. In these words there is gratitude, there is a sense of measure, and there is fear — and the last sentence is the nail of the whole chapter: "How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?" Please note what he did not say: he did not say, I am afraid my master will find out, afraid of losing my livelihood, afraid of ruining my future. He said, sin against God. "Sin," in the original chata' (חָטָא, H2398), to sin, to offend, to miss and deviate from the right way [4]. In Joseph's reckoning, the first victim of this act was not Potiphar, but God; the first boundary was not a rule made by men, but the holiness of God. Reverence like this is what can withstand daily entreaty: a person who only fears being found out loosens his hold the moment no one is watching; a person who fears sinning against God knows there is One who always sees. So when that day came and "there was none of the men of the house there within" — no one in the house at all, exactly the hour temptation loves to choose — Joseph's choice had already been made in his ordinary days.

That day's description is written by the Bible like a slow-motion scene. His mistress caught him by his garment, saying, "Lie with me"; and Joseph "left his garment in her hand, and fled, and got him out." Brothers and sisters, please pause for this word "fled." Joseph did not leave after winning an argument; he ran away. The Bible never treats fleeing as cowardice: Paul exhorts Timothy to "flee youthful lusts," and First Corinthians exhorts its readers to flee fornication. For this kind of temptation, the weapon God gives is not debate and not contest, but feet — turning around, even at the cost of leaving a garment behind rather than leaving yourself behind. Some scenes you simply cannot win; sitting down at the negotiating table is already half the loss. The only way to win is not to be there. The garment he left behind later became the evidence used to accuse him, but in that moment it proved only one thing: this young man would rather run out in disgrace than stay behind respectably and sin. Sometimes a person must choose between reputation and innocence, and Joseph chose innocence. What God remembers is precisely this choice.

Worth a second look is the echo of this garment detail through Joseph's whole life. Twice in his life he lost a garment: the first time was the coat of many colours, stripped off by his brothers, dipped in goat's blood, and made into false evidence of his death; this time it was his outer garment, left by himself in his mistress's hand, and made into false evidence of his crime. Both times it was false evidence, and both times it pushed him to a lower place: the first time made him a slave, the second time made him a prisoner. But between the two times there is one difference, and the difference lies in whether the man had grown: the first time, the garment was snatched away by others, and he could only weep and be led wherever they took him; the second time, the garment was let go by himself — he would rather lose it than lose his own person. Brothers and sisters, when a person is young, his fate is mostly written on him by other people: those who rob you, sell you, and define you are all others. But a person must grow to the day when he can make one choice for himself: some things can be lost — reputation can be lost, position can be lost, even the evidence of innocence can for a time be lost — but there is one line that cannot be lost; lose it, and a person is no longer himself. Joseph's growth is not written first in how high an office he later rose to, but in this letting go.

What followed was the scene of the bite turned back. His mistress kept the garment until the men of the house came home, and first told the servants a version: See, he has brought in a Hebrew unto us to mock us. She first shifted the blame onto her husband, then made use of his foreign identity, turning a private entanglement of desire into an insult by an outsider against the whole household. When Potiphar came home, she told him the same version again, adding only, "he fled out," with the garment as ironclad proof. Her husband heard it, and "put him into the prison, a place where the king's prisoners were bound." The Bible does not record Potiphar investigating deeply, nor does it record him turning on his wife; it records only that he was angry, and put Joseph in prison. There is a detail here worth pondering: by the rules of that time, a slave guilty of such an act would most likely have lost his life; Joseph was put in the prison of the king's prisoners, and in the end kept his life. We need not read this as a settled conclusion; we can only say the narrative leaves a crack: in Potiphar's anger there may also have been a measure of hesitation he could not voice — this steward's conduct on ordinary days, he had watched it with his own eyes every day. But in any case, the innocent man went to prison, and the lying woman remained in the house. The cost Joseph paid was real: the steward's position was gone, the trust accumulated over years was reset to zero, and his name carried a stain that could not be washed off. Holding fast to holiness has never been free. If the pulpit tells you only that obedience brings blessing and refusal brings an immediate reversal, it has made the Bible shallow. Joseph's lesson is heavy: do the right thing, and you may not be vindicated right away; flee in the right direction, and there may be no one to testify for you. Sometimes all you receive is one sentence — God saw — and a heart that can sleep at night. But the Bible asks us to read on, precisely because this is not yet the end of the story.

Brought into today, the picture is not unfamiliar at all. Temptation in the workplace does not always wear a mistress's face: it can be the glass pressed into your hand at a banquet that you are told you cannot refuse, a figure in the accounts you are silently permitted to make smaller, a superior's remark that everyone does it this way, so do not be so earnest. "Day by day" is its most common method — it does not ask you to become bad all at once; it asks you to yield one inch at a time. Joseph's three refusals fit today just as well: do not listen — give it no seat in your heart first; do not comply — let neither your body nor your hands cross that line; do not be there — the occasions you can avoid and the gatherings you can decline, avoid and decline first. And his reason matters even more today: behind a report no one else will see, in a chat record only you know about, ask first not whether you will be found out, but whether you would sin against God. Once reverence stands firm, your feet will know how to run.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: In Prison, Presence Was Spoken Again (39:21-23)

The Bible's pen, arriving at the prison, did not change its tone. The place where Joseph was confined was the prison "where the king's prisoners were bound" — its inmates were no ordinary criminals. The Psalms later looked back on these days and said his feet were hurt with fetters and iron entered his neck. In just such a place, the text spoke again the sentence we have already heard: "But the LORD was with Joseph." Brothers and sisters, please read that word "but" with weight. It could have stood without the "but" earlier: when things went well at home, God's presence was reasonable and expected. But now an innocent man sat in an unjust prison, and the story had fallen to a place lower than slavery; and precisely here the Bible adds a "but": But the LORD was with Joseph. Present when he rose, present also when he fell; present in his master's house, present also in the king's prison. The same sentence was stamped once on the first page of chapter 39 and stamped again on its last page, with an unreasonable false accusation sandwiched between. If God's presence counted only in favorable circumstances, it would be mere fair-weather talk; that it counts in a prison cell too is the very sentence we need today.

In prison, presence came bringing two things. The first was mercy. "And shewed him mercy" — "mercy" in the original is chesed (חֶסֶד, H2617), lovingkindness, steadfast love, covenant faithfulness [2]. This lovingkindness is not a passing softness of heart; it is God acknowledging His own covenant and acknowledging His own chosen one, all the way to the end. Immediately after, "and gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison" — "favour" in the original is chen (חֵן, H2580), grace, favor, finding acceptance in another's eyes [3]. The two words for grace stand side by side, and their order matters greatly: first God showed him mercy, then a man showed him favor. You cannot control people's attitudes toward you, but the source of those attitudes is in God's hand. The keeper of the prison worked in a hard, cold profession, looking at prisoners every day, yet Joseph found favor in his sight. This was not Joseph being socially skillful; it was God gently turning a human heart a few degrees. Many people in a valley ask: where is my benefactor? Joseph's answer is: before the benefactor appears, God's chesed is already underneath as the foundation.

The second thing was a charge entrusted. "And the keeper of the prison committed to Joseph's hand all the prisoners that were in the prison; and whatsoever they did there, he was the doer of it." Exactly the same rhythm as in Potiphar's house walked through once again: first favor, then entrustment; first small things, then great things. Even the closing appraisal reads as if written after the earlier text: "The keeper of the prison looked not to any thing that was under his hand; because the LORD was with him, and that which he did, the LORD made it to prosper." Please count: this is now the third appearance of "prosper" — tsaleach [1] — in this chapter: once in the slave's house, once after his appointment, and once again in prison. God seems afraid we might miss it, and deliberately uses the same word to remind us: what changed was only the place; what did not change was the fruit that presence brings. The prison did not first turn into a palace; Joseph was still a prisoner and the keeper still the keeper, yet right there in prison God first gave him a place where he could work, could serve people, and could prepare himself. The man who would later interpret Pharaoh's dreams and administer all the storehouses of Egypt learned his skill of managing people, inch by inch, in a household's counting room and a prison's cells. God's preparation is often inconspicuous: He did not first open the prison door; He first opened Joseph's hand.

Psalm 105, looking back on these prison days, gives a sentence of great weight: the word of the LORD tried him, until the time that his word came. This sentence sees straight through the days in prison: Joseph in prison was not only suffering; he was being tried, and what tried him was no one else but the very word God Himself had spoken — those youthful dreams, those promises not yet fulfilled. Every day the promise had not yet come true, it asked him one question: do you still believe? The hardest thing for a person is never not having heard God's word; it is having heard it, believed it, and waited for it, yet seeing no movement for a long time. Joseph's excellence was that while he waited, his hands did not stop: what the keeper entrusted, he did; the troubles of the prisoners, he asked after; later he was even willing to listen carefully to the dreams of fellow prisoners and interpret them. Waiting in him was not blankness, but preparation; not freezing, but a slow fire. He did not know on which day the door would open; he knew only that before the door opened, he himself must not collapse first. Brothers and sisters, when we wait on God we should wait like this too: the word still stands, so the heart does not die; the door has not opened, so the hands do not idle. God's word tries a person, and when the trying is enough, that word itself will bring the person out.

This chapter stops right here, and stops a little unwillingly: Joseph is still in prison; the word of presence has been spoken, the word of prosperity has been spoken, and the man has not yet gone out. We must honestly admit that not a few chapters of the Bible stop just like this: the promise has been spoken, and the day has not yet come. The Book of Acts, looking back on Joseph, summed him up in one very short sentence: "But God was with him, and delivered him out of all his afflictions." The deliverance was a later matter; the presence was a present matter. If today you are in a stretch of days you cannot get out of — a relationship that cannot be untangled, a situation with no visible end, a grievance that cannot be washed clean — please first fix this chapter's order firmly in mind: God does not wait until you are delivered to come; while you are still confined, He is already with you.

Finally, let it land on ourselves, with three applications to take home. First, regarding temptation in the workplace, recognize first its "day by day" shape. Do not wait for that day to draw your boundaries; draw them in ordinary days ahead of time: which gatherings you will not attend, which conversations you will not take up, which conveniences you will not take — settle them in advance, and in the moment you will not so easily go soft. Joseph's running was run out of his three ordinary refusals. Second, when you are innocent yet falsely accused, do not hurry to clear yourself by revenge. Not one sentence of self-defense from Joseph in prison is recorded; he still did the prisoners' work well and kept the days in his hand upright. Vindication sometimes has to wait a long time, but character cannot stop for a single day. How you work while under a grievance is the testimony you write for yourself — the slowest, and the hardest. Third, for those holding fast in a valley, look first at what is still in your hand. Joseph in prison still had prisoners, still had tasks, and still had a keeper willing to entrust him. The valley God gives you may not first come with an exit; it often first comes with a small patch of ground where you can be faithful. Do well and prosper on that small patch, until people can rest easy about you, and when the exit arrives, you will already be prepared. Not indulging yourself in smooth days, not lying down in adverse days, not abandoning yourself under grievance — in the end none of this rests on temperament, but on that seal stamped twice: the LORD was with him.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Genesis chapter 39 and seen a man change three identities: from a favored son, to a favored slave, to a falsely accused prisoner. The coat of many colours was torn away, the steward's place was lost, and his name was stained — yet one thing never changed from beginning to end: the LORD was with him. When he rose, this sentence was stamped at the head of Genesis 39; when he was put in prison, this sentence was stamped at the end of Genesis 39. It turns out that presence is not a guarantee of exemption from suffering; it is the solid ground within suffering. The cost of holding fast to holiness is real, and the hand behind the cost is real too.

Before we close, please keep the three place-names of today in your heart: the home in Canaan, the house of Potiphar, and the king's prison. Joseph walked down step after step, and God followed him down step after step, not halting His feet on any stair. Which stair you stand on today does not decide whether God is present; whether God is present rests only on this: He is the God who is with people. Take this sentence home and set it upon the hardest day of this week: at the height and in the pit, His presence counts.

Let us pray together: Lord who is with people, thank You; Joseph went down into a slave's courtyard, and You arrived; Joseph went down into the king's prison, and You arrived there too. Help us: not to indulge ourselves in smooth circumstances, to learn to flee before temptation, not to give up being upright people under grievance, and still to do well the small things in our hands in the valley. Let us, all our lives, whether we walk to high places or low places, hear the same sentence: the LORD is with you. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] tsaleach: Hebrew צָלֵחַ (H6743), to prosper, be successful, advance successfully, prevail; Genesis 39:2 "he was a prosperous man," 39:3 "the LORD made all that he did to prosper in his hand," and 39:23 "that which he did, the LORD made it to prosper" — this word appears three times in the chapter; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the fruit of presence does not change when the place changes" is a narrative observation and homiletical exposition based on the word's three appearances in the chapter; the word itself means only to prosper and to succeed, and does not in itself contain the sense of "regardless of circumstances"; this distinction is made clear here.

[2] chesed: Hebrew חֶסֶד (H2617), lovingkindness, steadfast love, kindness, covenant faithfulness; Genesis 39:21 "the LORD was with Joseph, and shewed him mercy," where "mercy" is this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "acknowledging His own covenant and His own man, all the way to the end" is an exposition based on the word's common sense in Old Testament covenant contexts; the original text here states only that God showed Joseph chesed, and does not mention covenant clauses word for word; this distinction is made clear here.

[3] chen: Hebrew חֵן (H2580), grace, favor, acceptance, finding favor in another's eyes; Genesis 39:21 "gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison," and the related expression in 39:4, where Joseph found grace in his master's sight, uses the same word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's observation of the order — first God shows mercy, then a man shows favor — rests on the sequence within Genesis 39:21 itself, "shewed him mercy, and gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison," and is a fact of the narrative text.

[4] chata': Hebrew חָטָא (H2398), to sin, to offend, to miss the right way; Genesis 39:9, where Joseph said, "How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?" — "sin against" is this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the first victim is God and the first boundary is God's holiness" is a homiletical exposition based on the fact that Joseph's own words name God as the one offended; the original sentence also contains the sense of disloyalty to his master (the context of 39:8-9); this distinction is made clear here.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And the LORD was with Joseph, and he was a prosperous man; and he was in the house of his master the Egyptian." (Genesis 39:2) "But the LORD was with Joseph, and shewed him mercy, and gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison." (Genesis 39:21) "Because the LORD was with him, and that which he did, the LORD made it to prosper." (Genesis 39:23)

The same sentence is spoken twice in Genesis 39: once in Potiphar's house, and once in the king's prison. The one in the house is easy to understand; the one in the prison carries the weight. Joseph did the right thing and was jailed for it; he fled temptation and lost his good name for it. If God's presence recognized only favorable circumstances, this sentence would have to change its wording in prison — yet the text does not change a single word. If you too are in a season today with no visible way out, do not rush first to ask where the exit is. Hear this sentence land on you first: the LORD is with you. The exit is tomorrow's matter; His presence is today's. Do well the small thing in your hand today, hold the line you must hold, and hand the injustice back to God — that is how Joseph in prison prepared himself while waiting for a door not yet opened. The door will open, in God's time; until then, His presence is already enough for you to stand firm.

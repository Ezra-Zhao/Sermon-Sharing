## Summary

Exodus chapters 28 to 29 gave the commands on the mountain; Leviticus chapter 8 carries them out, one by one, at the door of the tabernacle: the whole congregation is assembled, Aaron and his sons are washed, clothed, and anointed, the sin offering, the burnt offering, and the ram of consecration are offered, blood is put on the tip of the right ear and on the thumb of the right hand and on the great toe of the right foot, blood and oil are sprinkled on the garments, and finally they remain seven days at the door of the tabernacle, not daring to leave at will. This chapter is not about the pageantry of a rite; it is about the source and the weight of an office: before the hands are filled, the man is first washed; the one who is to stand between God and man must first act according to the command before the eyes of the congregation, and then learn obedience in seven days of waiting.

## I. When the Command Became the Perfect Tense: How to Read a Fulfilment Narrative 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, today we read Leviticus chapter 8. To read this chapter, first recognise its genre. This is a judgement at the factual level; hold it firmly first: the chapter is not new legislation, not the invention of a new rite, but a fulfilment narrative. Exodus chapter 28 gave the pattern of the holy garments; Exodus chapter 29 gave the procedure of the consecration rite — how to wash, how to clothe, how to anoint, what sacrifices to offer, how many days to keep. The commands were all delivered on the mountain. In Leviticus chapter 8 the camera moves from the mountain to the door of the tabernacle, and the imperative becomes the perfect tense: the text no longer says "you shall do," but "Moses did," and "Aaron and his sons did." The same set of actions we read in Exodus as a blueprint, we read in Leviticus as a completion: in one place the tone of command, in the other the tone of an account rendered. Its relation to the sermon on Leviticus 1 also lies here: that sermon asked how a layman brings an offering before God; this one asks how the men who stand beside the altar and handle the offerings are themselves first brought before God. Those who offer the sacrifices are first dealt with themselves; those who handle the offerings must first pass through washing, clothing, anointing, and sacrifice. This order is itself the first thing this chapter has to say.

The skeleton of the chapter is a refrain that recurs: "as the LORD commanded Moses." Here this sermon counts it verse by verse — the number must be counted accurately before it is preached. In its strict form the refrain occurs seven times in the chapter. Verse 4: "And Moses did as the LORD commanded him" (KJV) — the general opening of the whole fulfilment. Verse 9: the mitre and the golden plate are set in place, "as the LORD commanded Moses." Verse 13: Aaron's sons are clothed and girded, "as the LORD commanded Moses." Verse 17: what remains of the bullock of the sin offering is burned outside the camp, "as the LORD commanded Moses." Verse 21: the ram of the burnt offering is burned wholly on the altar, "as the LORD commanded Moses." Verse 29: Moses takes the breast of the ram of consecration as a wave offering, "as the LORD commanded Moses." And verse 36 closes the chapter with its summary variant: "So Aaron and his sons did all things which the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses" (KJV). Besides these seven there are three related expressions of command, not counted among the seven: verse 5, where Moses declares to the congregation, "This is the thing which the LORD commanded to be done" (KJV); verse 34, "as he hath done this day, so the LORD hath commanded to do" (KJV); and verse 35, "for so I am commanded" (KJV). Separating the seven from the three is not pedantry; it is the basic honesty of reading a fulfilment narrative: the seven refrains are like seven nails, fastening each great section back to the command itself; the other three are declaration and cross-reference, different in function, and must not be merged to make up a number.

Why does this repetition matter? What follows is at the level of homiletical exposition, and is marked as such: a fulfilment narrative reads almost with a ritual repetition — as the command spoke, so the narrative records; the procedure is written once, and then enacted once. Modern readers easily find it tedious, but this repetition is exactly where the theology of the chapter lies. In the narrative of Scripture, doing according to the command is not paperwork; it is the shape of obedience: the words God spoke enter time, one by one, and become the water of washing, the garments put on, the oil poured out, the blood sprinkled. The ritual repetition tells the reader: this rite was not Moses' invention, nor the self-coronation of Aaron's house; there is no place for improvisation, and no place altered by favour. The solemnity of the consecration rite lies not in the size of the spectacle, but in the fact that every step can be traced to its source. This sermon therefore does not spread itself over all thirty-six verses one by one, but follows the main line of the rite: assembling, washing, clothing, anointing, the three offerings, blood on ear, thumb, and toe, the wave offering, the sprinkling of the garments, and seven days at the door. Hold the main line, and the details fall into place. Where the text itself does not explain — for example, how Moses' performance of priestly actions is to be understood — this sermon sets the scholarly explanations side by side and draws no final conclusion.

One narrative observation must also be set down in advance, to be taken up later: the officiant of the whole rite is Moses. The one who washes is Moses; the one who clothes is Moses; the one who anoints is Moses; the one who slaughters the three offerings, sprinkles the blood, and waves the offering is Moses too. Yet Moses himself is not a priest. Aaron's office is set up by Moses' hands, but Moses' own office is not inside these garments. This transitional arrangement is a quiet anomaly in the chapter's narrative: the one who installs the priests is himself not a priest; when the rite is complete, Moses returns to his place as prophet and leader, and the standing order at the altar passes from this chapter onward to the line of Aaron. Keep this observation here; when the rite is finished, we shall return and look closely at what it shows.

## II. From Assembling to Anointing: Washed, Clothed, and Sanctified Before the Congregation 08:00-24:00

The first step of the rite's main line is assembling. In verses 1 to 5 the LORD speaks to Moses and sets out the list: Aaron and his sons, the garments, the anointing oil, the bullock for the sin offering, the two rams, and the basket of unleavened bread — bring them all — "and gather thou all the congregation together unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation" (Lev 8:3, KJV). The word for congregation will be dealt with in the notes; here grasp the plain sense of the scene: consecration is not a private affair conducted behind closed doors. All Israel is summoned to the door of the tabernacle as witnesses of the whole rite. From its first day the priestly office is public and verifiable: who is installed, according to what he is installed, and for what he is installed — the congregation stands at the door and watches. The first thing Moses says to the congregation is also a declaration, before any action: "This is the thing which the LORD commanded to be done" (Lev 8:5, KJV). The order is declaration first, execution afterward: the people first hear that the ground of this rite is the command, and only then watch the rite performed. One sentence of homiletical exposition, marked here: the credibility of an office comes not from the splendour of the rite itself, but from its being checked item by item, in public, against the command already given. When the church today ordains its workers, it likewise is not a decision of leaders behind closed doors, but must stand under the word of God and the witness of the congregation, able to bear examination point by point.

The second step is washing. Verse 6: Moses brought Aaron and his sons and washed them with water. Notice: their first act in receiving the office is not clothing, not anointing, but washing — and they do not wash themselves; they are brought, and they are washed. Before any glorious garment comes upon them, there is water first. This stroke answers, across the book, to Leviticus 1, where the offerer leads his animal to the door of the tabernacle: the one who offers a sacrifice brings the offering before God; the one who receives an office first surrenders himself before God. The plain sense of the washing is only what the text says — washed with water; this sermon does not add to it a list of symbols the text has not given. What can be stated directly is the order: cleansing comes before office. A man may have gifts, learning, and zeal, but the first lesson of the man who stands beside the altar in service is not display; it is to be washed. However precious the anointing oil, it is not poured on a man who has not been washed; however splendid the holy garments, they are not put on a man who has skipped the washing. The order of this chapter nails this down: washing before clothing, clothing before anointing.

The third step is clothing. Verses 7 to 9 give Aaron's holy garments, verse 13 gives the garments of his sons, with the anointing of the tabernacle set between them, to which we shall return. The order in which Aaron is clothed is recorded in fine detail: the coat, the girdle, the robe, the ephod with its skilfully woven band, the breastplate — with the Urim and the Thummim put in the breastplate — the mitre on his head, and on the mitre the golden plate, the holy crown. In the sermon on Exodus chapter 28 in this series, the bearing of the garments was treated piece by piece — the names borne on the shoulders, the names remembered on the breast, the golden plate engraved "HOLINESS TO THE LORD"; this sermon does not repeat that one, but takes only the perspective proper to this chapter: there we read the commands for making; here we read the act of putting on. With almost every piece named, the text attaches the sense of "as it was commanded," like the stitching of a garment: one piece is put on, and one note of verification follows. The sons' garments are far more briefly told: coats, girdles, and bonnets, closing at verse 13 with "as the LORD commanded Moses." This brevity is itself a narrative fact: the garments of the high priest and those of the priests differ; the office has its ranks, and the account has its proportions; the text does not write the secondary as if it were primary, nor the primary as if it were vague.

The fourth step is anointing. Verses 10 to 12 are a hinge of the whole chapter. Moses takes the anointing oil and first anoints the tabernacle and all that is in it, and sanctifies them; then he sprinkles some of the oil on the altar seven times and anoints the altar and all its vessels, and the laver and its foot, to sanctify them; and finally he pours some of the anointing oil on Aaron's head and anoints him, to sanctify him. First the vessels, then the man; first the space, then the office. The verb for anointing and the noun for the oil will be listed in the notes; here grasp the direction of the act: anointing is the marking action of being set apart as holy. What is anointed is no longer for common use; the man who is anointed no longer belongs to himself. The stroke of the oil poured on Aaron's head is written more weightily than elsewhere: elsewhere it is smearing; here it is pouring. Aaron is anointed alone, before his sons, answering to his singular place as high priest; for the sons no separate individual anointing is recorded — they are sanctified together in their father's anointing and in the later sprinkling of the garments. Note this proportion now; the sprinkling of blood and oil in verse 30 will supply the other half of the sons' sanctification.

Read these four steps together — this is a homiletical summary, and is marked as such: assembling makes the office public; washing makes it clean; clothing gives it form; anointing makes it God's. Not one of the four steps is seized by the men themselves: the congregation is summoned, the men are brought, the garments are put on them, the oil is poured upon them. Consecration, from beginning to end, is theology in the passive voice: a man does not climb up into the office; he is brought into it. This does not mean the men have no response: Aaron and his sons stand there and let the water wash, the garments be put on, and the oil be poured — this standing and submitting is their response. Only the order must not be reversed: first God commands, God prepares, God establishes; then comes man's standing. If a man reverses the order and speaks of office as his own achievement, the narrative of this chapter is the first to disagree.

## III. The Order of the Three Offerings: Sin Dealt With First, Then All Burned, Then Filled 24:00-42:00

After the clothing and the anointing, the rite enters the three offerings. In verses 14 to 29 the order is: the sin offering, the burnt offering, and the ram of consecration. State this order first at the factual level: the first offering deals with sin; the second expresses complete giving over to God; the third accomplishes the filling of the consecrated man's hands. The three offerings are not three parallel performances but one road with a direction: with sin undealt with, there is no speaking of complete offering; without complete offering, there are no filled hands. Disorder this sequence, and the weight of the chapter falls apart.

First, the sin offering, verses 14 to 17. Moses brings the bullock of the sin offering; Aaron and his sons lay their hands on its head; Moses kills it, takes the blood, and puts it with his finger on the horns of the altar round about, purifying the altar, and pours the blood at the bottom of the altar and sanctifies it, to make reconciliation upon it; the fat, the kidneys, and the caul above the liver he burns on the altar; the hide, the flesh, and the dung he burns outside the camp. Stop here and think: the principals of this rite, Aaron and his sons — their very first sacrificial act is to offer for their own sin. The hands laid on the bullock's head are the hands of men about to become priests; before they have sprinkled blood for the people even once, they must first confess that they themselves are sinners who need atonement. The sermon on Leviticus 1 discussed the sense of connection in the laying on of the hand, and that discussion is not repeated here; what can be stated directly in this chapter is the reversal of the identity of the one who lays on his hand: ordinarily the people bring the animal and the priests handle it; today the future priests themselves stand in the offerer's place and lay their hands on their own sin offering. With this first offering the text sets a boundary for every office: the man who stands between God and man stands first of all within the need of atonement himself. Whoever skips this offering and goes straight to speaking of the honour of office has not understood the order of this chapter. The blood is first put on the horns of the altar and poured at its bottom, sanctifying the altar — even the altar must first be cleansed and set apart before it bears the offerings that follow. If it is so with the vessel, how much more with the man.

Next, the burnt offering, verses 18 to 21. Moses brings the ram of the burnt offering; Aaron and his sons lay their hands on the ram's head; Moses kills it and sprinkles the blood round about upon the altar, cuts it into pieces, washes the inwards and the legs, and burns the whole ram on the altar: "it was a burnt sacrifice for a sweet savour, and an offering made by fire unto the LORD" (Lev 8:21, KJV). The procedure corresponds, point for point, to the rules for the burnt offering recorded in Leviticus 1; this sermon takes only the emphasis proper to this chapter: the burnt offering is the offering wholly burned, with no portion left to be shared as food. The whole ram goes up on the altar; the plain symbolic sense is the giving of the entire animal to God. Read within the sequence of the consecration rite, the homiletical exposition — marked here — is this: the sin offering deals with what a man has already committed; the burnt offering deals with what he is henceforth to become. First forgiven, then wholly given over; first the account of sin removed, then the resolve of offering established. In this offering Aaron and his sons are not merely offering a ram; they are setting the life of office they are receiving on the altar in the shape of a whole burning: keeping back no portion for their own calculations, no corner that does not go up on the altar. This level of exposition is drawn from the structure of the sequence of offerings and does not pretend to be the words of the text itself; but the structure stands here: if the second stage of the three offerings were not the wholly burned burnt offering, the consecration rite would lack the ring of self-offering.

Last, the ram of consecration, verses 22 to 29 — the longest and most densely acted offering of the chapter. Moses brings the second ram, the ram of consecration; Aaron and his sons lay their hands on its head; Moses kills it and takes some of its blood and puts it upon the tip of Aaron's right ear, and upon the thumb of his right hand, and upon the great toe of his right foot, and does the same for his sons — on the tip of the right ear, the thumb of the right hand, and the great toe of the right foot — and sprinkles the blood round about upon the altar. The places where the blood is put are the best-known stroke in this chapter, and they must be read slowly. The ear is the organ of hearing; the hand, the organ of work; the foot, the organ of walking. Blood on the tip of the right ear: this ear has first heard the voice of atonement before it is fit to hear the word of God and the pleas of the people. Blood on the thumb of the right hand: these hands will presently be filled with the offering and be called consecrated, but the hand must first pass through blood — a working hand that has not been redeemed does only man's work. Blood on the great toe of the right foot: the feet will go in and out of the tabernacle, stand beside the altar, and walk the seven days' road at the door — first let the blood mark every step they stand on. Right, not left, is taken throughout; the Hebrew term for the lobe of the ear will be listed in the notes. The homiletical summary goes this far, and is marked here: the blood circles a whole man in — from hearing, to doing, to walking. It does not cleanse only his heart, nor only his hands; the ear that obeys, the hand that works, and the foot that walks are together brought within the reach of redemption. Hereafter this family will sprinkle blood at the altar, arrange offerings, and go in and out of the sanctuary; this chapter first lets them bear the mark of blood on their own bodies: those who administer atonement are themselves first the atoned.

After the putting of the blood, Moses takes from the ram its fat, the fat tail, the fat covering the inwards, the caul above the liver and the kidneys, and the right shoulder; and from the basket of unleavened bread he takes one unleavened cake, one cake of oiled bread, and one wafer, and puts them on the fat and on the right shoulder, and puts all of it into the hands of Aaron and his sons, and waves them for a wave offering before the LORD; then he takes them from their hands and burns them on the altar upon the burnt offering. The centre of this chain of actions is the hand. The offering and the bread are placed in their hands; they themselves wave them with their own hands before the material is taken and burned. The very name "consecration" is, in its literal sense, connected with the hand being filled — the notes will set out the original term and the scholarly discussion side by side; the body of the sermon takes only the picture the narrative itself gives: their hands do not receive the office empty; into them are first placed the offering and the bread. Office is not an empty title; it is an entrusting. It is not first obtaining a position and then looking for something to do; the hand is first filled with the very things it is to handle. When the waving is done, Moses takes the breast of the ram as his own portion and waves it before the LORD. The refrain of verse 29 falls exactly here: "as the LORD commanded Moses." The fulfilment narrative notes the source even for Moses' own portion: at the consecration rite there is no place where the officiant may act on his own authority — even the officiant's portion is according to the command.

## IV. Blood and Oil Sprinkled on the Garments, Seven Days at the Door 42:00-56:00

After the three offerings there are two closing steps, and neither can be missing. The first is the sprinkling of the garments, verse 30: Moses takes some of the anointing oil and some of the blood which is upon the altar and sprinkles it on Aaron and on his garments, and on his sons and on his sons' garments with him, and sanctifies him and his garments, and his sons and his sons' garments. This verse supplies the proportion noted earlier: the anointing oil was poured on Aaron alone, but the blood and the oil together are sprinkled on two generations, Aaron and his sons — on the men and on their garments alike. Here the object of sanctification is not only the man but also the garment: the man serves wearing the garment; the garment serves stained with blood and oil. Office is not an identity that can be taken off after hours; the very shape of a man's daily standing is set apart with him. The blood is taken from upon the altar — the blood of the sacrifices just offered; the oil is the anointing oil. Sprinkled together on the garments, the two gather the three offerings and the anointing into one mark worn on the consecrated men's bodies: every time hereafter that they put on these garments to serve, the garments have a traceable origin for their blood and their oil. One sentence of homiletical exposition: the mark of the consecrated man is not a medal he has earned for himself, but the trace left on him by the blood of the altar and the oil of the sanctuary; what men see is the garment, and what the garment speaks of is its source.

The second step is eating and keeping, verses 31 to 36. Moses commands that the flesh of the ram of consecration be boiled in a holy place at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, and that Aaron and his sons eat there the flesh and the bread in the basket, and that what remains be burned with fire and not left over; and he commands them that for seven days they shall not go out of the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, until the days of their consecration be at an end; "As he hath done this day, so the LORD hath commanded to do, to make an atonement for you" (Lev 8:34, KJV); "Therefore shall ye abide at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation day and night seven days, and keep the charge of the LORD, that ye die not: for so I am commanded" (Lev 8:35, KJV). Note first a quiet fact: the consecration rite closes its three offerings with a meal eaten at the door. Most of the offering is burned on the altar, Moses' portion is taken, and the appointed flesh and bread are eaten by the consecrated men in the holy place. Eating here is part of receiving the office: what God has appointed, they not only watch and handle, but receive with their own mouths. Yet what follows the meal is not dispersal homeward, but seven days at the door. For seven days they shall not go out of the door — not reporting there daily, but abiding at the door day and night, a full seven days. The last lesson of the office is to remain where one is placed and wait: the rite is finished, and the man still may not leave; the actions are done, and the days still have to be kept in full.

Verse 35 carries the heaviest weight in the chapter, and the factual level must be stated first: keeping the charge of the LORD and "that ye die not" are directly joined in the sentence itself — keep, and live; the consequence of not keeping, the text plainly says, is death. This is not the preacher's exaggeration for the sake of atmosphere; it is the wording of the text itself. The seven days at the door are therefore not decorative tail of the rite, but a boundary on which life depends: the door of the tabernacle, ordinarily a passage for going in and out, becomes for these seven days a line that may not be crossed. The first lesson of men who have just been anointed and clothed turns out to be that they may not go out. The homiletical exposition, its level marked here: the maturity of an office often begins not with great displays of ability, but with willingness to keep the station where one has been placed; how much failure in service comes not from inability to act, but from inability to keep — to keep time, to keep bounds, to keep the measure of the command. Aaron and his sons kept these seven days, and only then does verse 36 set down the chapter's closing refrain: "So Aaron and his sons did all things which the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses" (KJV). Only here is the account of the consecration rite fully rendered — and its final entry is that they did all things, including the final keeping.

Now we may return to the narrative observation left in the first section. The officiant of the whole rite is Moses: washing the men, clothing them, anointing, slaughtering, sprinkling the blood, waving the offering, sprinkling the garments, and charging them to keep the door — all of it by his own hand throughout. But the notes must state honestly: Moses himself is not a priest; his office is prophet and leader; his performance of these priestly actions belongs to the transitional arrangement of the very occasion on which the rite is instituted. Scholarship explains this anomaly in different ways: some say Moses acts as covenant mediator, temporarily performing the office before the priestly system is formally in operation; some say the officiant of an institution rite of this kind need not himself be a priest, just as the installing of kings and prophets later is not necessarily performed by priests; and some bring into the discussion texts such as the psalm that lists Moses among the priests, for comparison. This sermon sets these views side by side and asserts no one of them as a final conclusion; the only narrative observation it makes is this single one: from this chapter onward, the standing order of sacrifice at the altar is performed by the line of Aaron, and Moses no longer stands at the altar in this capacity. The transitional man completes the transition and returns to his own place — and this manner of withdrawal is itself a lesson this chapter teaches to those who receive an office: an office is not a thing clutched and never released; the one who installed you will leave the scene, and you too must learn faithfulness in your own station, rather than leaning forever on the extraordinary arrangements of the day of installation.

## V. Conclusion and Response: Hands That Are Filled Must First Be Redeemed, and Must Be Able to Keep 58:00-60:00

To sum up this chapter, walk the main line once more. The whole congregation is assembled at the door of the tabernacle: the office is first made public. Aaron and his sons are washed with water: the office is first made clean. The holy garments are put on and the oil poured out: the office has form, and it belongs to God. The three offerings are presented in order — the sin offering says the man who receives an office is first himself atoned for; the burnt offering says the life that receives an office is wholly given over to God; the ram of consecration says their ears, hands, and feet are first marked with blood, and only then are their hands filled with the offering and the bread. Blood and oil are sprinkled on the garments: two generations, men and garments together, are sanctified. And finally a meal at the door and seven days of keeping set the office down in receiving and in waiting. The seven refrains nail the account from beginning to end and tell us: none of this is man's design; it is all "as the LORD commanded Moses." Hands being filled is the name of this rite, and it is also its order: before the hands are filled, the ear first hears the voice of atonement, the hand first receives the mark of blood, the foot first stands within the reach of redemption; and after the filling, the man must still keep a full seven days at the door. Being filled and keeping one's place are the two sides of receiving an office.

One level of New Testament reading, its boundary marked before it is spoken, and not read back into this chapter as its plain meaning: chapter 7 of Hebrews sets the priests of Aaron's order beside the Lord Jesus — the former are many, and are not suffered to continue by reason of death; the latter ever liveth, holds an unchangeable priesthood, and offered Himself once, accomplishing an atonement that stands forever. The New Testament writer looks back, in Christ, on consecration narratives of this kind and sees both their truth and their limit. Chapter 10 of Hebrews says again that, having our hearts sprinkled and our bodies washed with pure water, we may draw near and enter into the holiest — the language of washing and of sprinkling blood answers Leviticus 8 across the centuries, but it rests on the once-for-all work of Christ, and does not bid us re-enact the rite of this chapter. First Peter chapter 2 calls believers a royal priesthood — a New Testament declaration concerning all the saints, grounded in Christ, not in the genealogy of this chapter. In using this level homiletically, this sermon takes only one response: since the greater High Priest now ever liveth and maketh intercession for us, we who today are the Lord's workers and the Lord's people should all the more examine ourselves by the order of this chapter — has my ear heard the word of atonement? Has my hand been cleansed by the blood? Am I willing to keep at the door where I am stationed, neither rushing ahead nor leaving without permission?

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You for setting the order of receiving an office before our eyes through Leviticus chapter 8. Forgive us for coveting only the filled hands while skipping the water of washing, the blood of the sin offering, and the seven days of keeping; forgive us for treating office as status and refusing to treat it as entrusting. Cleanse our ears, that we may first hear Your word; cleanse our hands, that we may do the work that accords with Your heart; cleanse our feet, that we may stand where You have stationed us and not leave without Your word. Remember every one who is keeping watch at the door: let them not lose heart in the waiting, and let them find peace within the bounds, knowing that this keeping is not waste, but part of receiving the office. In the name of the great High Priest who ever liveth and ever maketh intercession for us, the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Notes

1. millu'im: Hebrew מִלֻּאִים, transliteration millu'im, noun, consecration, ordination rite, Strong number H4394. Meaning according to BDB: consecration, ordination — that is, the rite of consecration and its offerings; HALOT likewise glosses it as consecration (offering). In this chapter the term recurs at verses 22, 28, 29, 31, and 33, denoting the second ram, its flesh, the bread in its basket, and the seven-day period. Literally it is connected with the verb to fill, to make full (male'), and may be rendered directly as "fillings," "hands being filled" — the hands of the consecrated man are filled with the offering and the bread, which supplies the textual ground for this sermon's title. Scholarship discusses the etymology of the term: some trace it to ancient Near Eastern installation imagery in which the hand is filled with offerings as a sign of authorisation; others compare Ugaritic and Akkadian parallels concerning gifts placed in the hand at installation. Milgrom and Hartley both treat the question in dedicated sections, and they do not assign the same weight to the details of derivation and to the ritual analogies. This sermon sets that discussion side by side and takes only the level the narrative itself can state directly: in this chapter the consecrated men's hands are indeed given the offering and the bread (8:26-27); it presents no single etymology as a final conclusion.

2. mashach and shemen hamishchah: the verb מָשַׁח, transliteration mashach, to anoint, to smear, Strong number H4886; BDB: to smear, anoint; HALOT: anoint. The full name of the oil is שֶׁמֶן הַמִּשְׁחָה, transliteration shemen hamishchah, the anointing oil, the oil of anointing, in which mishchah (מִשְׁחָה) bears Strong number H4888; BDB: anointing, anointed portion. In this chapter the oil is listed at verse 2; at verses 10 to 12 Moses anoints the tabernacle, its vessels, and Aaron with it; at verse 30 he sprinkles it, together with the blood from upon the altar, on the garments. The recipe for the oil is given at Exodus 30:22-33; this chapter records only its use and does not repeat the recipe, and the body of this sermon accordingly speaks only of the act of anointing and its direction, without developing the details of the recipe.

3. qahal and the assembling of the congregation at the door: קָהָל, transliteration qahal, noun, congregation, assembly, Strong number H6951; BDB: assembly, congregation, company; HALOT: assembly. At verse 3 of this chapter, the command to assemble all the congregation uses the other term 'edah (עֵדָה, H5712, congregation, community); at verse 4, the record of the congregation's assembling is phrased with the qahal word family. The two terms are often used interchangeably in the Pentateuch, and scholarship has reached no consensus on whether a stable semantic division exists between them; this sermon makes only a narrative observation: the command uses one word and the fulfilment uses the other, and both denote the whole of Israel publicly assembled at the door of the tabernacle. The place of assembling is the door of the tabernacle, a phrase recurring in this chapter (8:3, 4, 31, 33, 35); see note 11.

4. tenukh and the bodily marking with blood: תְּנוּךְ, transliteration tenukh, noun, tip of the ear, lobe, Strong number H8571; BDB: tip of ear, lobe; HALOT: lobe (of ear). This term occurs in Scripture only in the parallel accounts of the consecration rite (Exod 29:20; Lev 8:23, 24; and Lev 14:14, 17, 25, 28, where the rite for the cleansing of the leper takes up the same marking). Used with it is bohen: בֹּהֶן, transliteration bohen, noun, thumb, great toe, Strong number H931; BDB: thumb, great toe. At verses 23 to 24 of this chapter, blood is put on the tenukh of the right ear of Aaron and his sons, and on the bohen of the right hand and the right foot. The three marked places — ear, hand, foot — are given in the original with a single connected set of bodily terms, and the command of Exodus 29 and the fulfilment of this chapter correspond word for word. The summary in the body of this sermon (hearing, doing, walking) belongs to homiletical exposition, was marked as such on the spot, and does not pass itself off as the lexical meaning itself.

5. tenufah: תְּנוּפָה, transliteration tenufah, noun, wave offering, the offering that has been waved, Strong number H8573; BDB: wave-offering; HALOT: wave offering. It is connected with the verb to wave, to swing (nuf, H5130). At verse 27 of this chapter Moses puts the offering and the bread into the hands of Aaron and his sons and waves them before the LORD; at verse 29 he likewise takes the breast of the ram and waves it himself, as Moses' portion. The waving motion is the ritual act of presenting the offering before God; the text does not describe its exact trajectory in detail, and this sermon makes no reconstructive assertion about it.

6. The two verbs for sprinkling blood and sprinkling oil: זָרַק, transliteration zaraq, verb, to sprinkle, to dash (blood), Strong number H2236; BDB: to scatter, sprinkle (blood). At verses 19 and 24 of this chapter the blood is sprinkled round about upon the altar, and at verse 15 blood is put on the horns of the altar and poured at its bottom — all solemn handlings of the blood. The other verb is נָזָה, transliteration nazah (in its causative stem, hizzah), verb, to sprinkle by flicking, Strong number H5137; BDB: to sprinkle. At verse 11 the oil is sprinkled on the altar seven times, and at verse 30 the blood and the oil are sprinkled on the garments, using this verb. The division of labour between the two — zaraq used mostly for dashing blood toward the altar, hizzah mostly for sprinkling with finger or implement — is the usual observation of the lexica and commentaries; this sermon accordingly distinguishes the dashing of sacrificial blood on the altar from the sprinkling of blood and oil on the garments, and does not confuse the two.

7. chatta't: חַטָּאת, transliteration chatta't, noun, sin offering; sin, Strong number H2403; BDB: sin, sin-offering; HALOT: sin, sin offering. The term carries both senses, sin and sin offering. Milgrom argues that in sacrificial contexts its function is best understood from the side of purification, stressing that its blood purifies the sanctuary and the altar; Wenham and Hartley retain the customary rendering "sin offering" and discuss its direction toward atonement. In this chapter it is listed at verse 2 and performed at verses 14 to 17: the first offering of the consecration rite is this offering, and its offerers are Aaron and his sons themselves — the narrative fact on which the exposition in the body of this sermon rests. The differing scholarly interpretations of the offering's meaning are set side by side in this sermon, and no one view is allowed to suppress the others.

8. qadesh and the recurring "sanctify" conclusions: the verb קִדֵּשׁ, transliteration qadesh (in its derived stems, qiddush forms), verb, to sanctify, to set apart as holy, Strong number H6942; BDB: to be holy, consecrate; HALOT: be holy, consecrate. The conclusions "to sanctify it" and "to sanctify him" recur throughout this chapter: at verse 10 the tabernacle is anointed to sanctify it; at verse 11 the altar and its vessels are anointed to sanctify them; at verse 12 Aaron is anointed to sanctify him; at verse 15 the altar is purified and sanctified; at verse 30 the sprinkling of the garments sanctifies the men and their garments together. These five points of sanctification move from vessels to man, and from man to garments, and form the narrative rhythm of the anointing sections of the chapter; the argument in the body of this sermon rests on them as structural observation.

9. The anomaly of Moses performing priestly actions, and the scholarly explanations: the verb of slaughter, shachat (שָׁחַט, H7819, to slaughter), takes Moses as its subject for all three offerings in this chapter (8:15, 19, 23), and the sprinkling of the blood, the putting of the blood, and the wave offering are likewise his. Yet in the narrative of the Pentateuch Moses' office is prophet and leader, not priest; the high-priestly line belongs, from this chapter onward, to Aaron. On this anomaly the scholarly explanations are set side by side as follows, all as references and none as a final conclusion: Milgrom notes that Moses acts here in the extraordinary capacity of founder of the rite, a ministry that ends when the rite is complete, and from which it cannot be inferred that Moses held the priesthood as a standing office; Wenham holds that the officiant of an institution rite need not himself be a priest, and that Moses, in his unique station as mediator between God and the people, completes the institution — which itself shows that the priestly system is instituted, not self-established; Hartley further reminds the reader that Psalm 99:6 lists Moses together with Aaron and Samuel among those who call upon the name of the LORD, leaving room for discussion in later tradition about the relation of Moses to the priestly office. The body of this sermon makes only a narrative observation: from this chapter onward the standing order at the altar belongs to the line of Aaron, and Moses withdraws from the officiant's place.

10. mishmeret and the seven days' keeping: מִשְׁמֶרֶת, transliteration mishmeret, noun, charge, that which is to be kept, Strong number H4931; BDB: charge, watch, observance; HALOT: duty, charge. At verse 35, "ye shall keep the charge of the LORD (mishmeret), that ye die not" — command and consequence are directly joined within a single sentence, and the factual level of the body of this sermon is written accordingly. The seven-day period appears at verses 33 to 35 and corresponds to the command of Exodus 29:35 that the days of consecration shall be seven days; Leviticus chapter 9 follows immediately, opening with the formal ministry of the eighth day. The seven days during which they shall not go out of the door of the tabernacle are the boundary sentence with which the chapter closes, and this sermon does not soften them into a general exhortation.

11. petach 'ohel mo'ed: פֶּתַח אֹהֶל־מוֹעֵד, transliteration petach 'ohel mo'ed, the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, in which petach (פֶּתַח) means door, entrance, Strong number H6607. The phrase occurs five times in this chapter: at verse 3 the congregation is summoned here; at verse 4 the congregation assembles here; at verse 31 the flesh is boiled and eaten here; at verse 33 they shall not go out of this door for seven days; at verse 35 they abide here day and night. In this chapter the doorway carries a threefold narrative function — the place of public witness, the place of receiving the office, and the boundary that may not be left at will — and the summary in the fourth section of this sermon is built upon it, as narrative observation and homiletical exposition, separately marked.

12. Sources at the level of New Testament reading: Hebrews 7:23-28 on the priests of Aaron's order, prevented by death from continuing, and on the Lord Jesus, who ever liveth and offered Himself once; Hebrews 10:19-22 on entering into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, having our hearts sprinkled and our bodies washed with pure water; 1 Peter 2:9, calling believers a royal priesthood. All three are New Testament writers' retrospect and exposition, in Christ, upon the Old Testament priestly narratives. When preaching cites them, the level must be marked, and they must not be read back as the plain original meaning of Leviticus chapter 8; the fifth section of this sermon has marked them accordingly.

## Devotional Excerpt

On the first day of Aaron's consecration in Leviticus 8, he is washed with water and offers a sin offering for himself before he is clothed and anointed. The first lesson of standing before God in service is not ability, but cleansing; not glory received, but sin confessed and atoned for. Today I need not pretend to be flawless, nor shrink back because of my weakness. The blood on the ear, the hand, and the foot reminds me: my ear must first hear the Lord's word, my hand must do the work that pleases Him, and my foot must walk the road He appoints — the whole person redeemed, the whole person given to the Lord. And the seven days at the doorway remind me: where the Lord stations me, there I will first keep my place — not rushing ahead, not leaving without His word — learning faithfulness in waiting and within His bounds. May the Lord teach me today, from the very place where I stand, to live an ordinary day as worship done according to His command, and to offer every small obedience to Him in earnest, as part of the consecration itself.

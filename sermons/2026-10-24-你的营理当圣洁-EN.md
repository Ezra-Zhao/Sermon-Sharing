# Thy Camp Shall Be Holy — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 23

> Status: First translation · Draft translation pending proofreading (首译·初译待校)

> On the title: Four candidates were considered. (1) "Shall Not Enter into the Congregation of the LORD," taken from verse 1, governs only the gate of the first section and cannot cover the camp, the money, and the vineyard. (2) "That Which Is Gone Out of Thy Lips," taken from verse 23, governs only the section on vows. (3) "Thou Shalt Not Put Any in Thy Vessel," taken from verse 24, governs only the boundary of the final section. (4) "Thy Camp Shall Be Holy," taken from the wording of verse 14 itself — the chapter has only one space: the congregation is the camp in its gathered form, the war camp is the camp on the march, and the city is the camp in its settled form; the gate, the camp, the house, and the vineyard all stand under this one sentence. Candidate (4) is adopted. The English title follows the wording of KJV 23:14: "Thy Camp Shall Be Holy" (KJV 23:14: "For the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp, to deliver thee, and to give up thine enemies before thee; therefore shall thy camp be holy: that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee.").

Deuteronomy 23 is the chapter of legal detail with the most miscellaneous jurisdiction in the Torah: the gate of the congregation, the latrine of the war camp, the dwelling of the escaped servant, the purse of the sanctuary, the brother's bond, one's own mouth, and the neighbour's vineyard — twenty-five verses change scene seven times. Miscellaneous, not scattered: every case circles the same question — who may be, and bringing what, in the place where God is present. Verse 1 begins with "enter into the congregation of the LORD"; verse 14 sets the theme with "therefore shall thy camp be holy"; verse 25 closes with the sickle that must not be moved. The gate must be kept, the camp must be clean, the money must be clean, the word must count, and the hand must know where to stop.

## Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Factual Layer] The chapter opens at a gate: "He that is wounded in the stones, or hath his privy member cut off, shall not enter into the congregation of the LORD." (23:1) Inside and outside the gate, people are first sorted: the wounded, the bastard, the Ammonite, the Moabite, the Edomite, the Egyptian, named clan by clan. Then the camera moves to the camp, the city, one's own mouth, and the neighbour's vineyard: the night emission and the paddle, the escaped servant and the bond, the vow once spoken, a cluster of grapes and a handful of ears of corn. Not one verse in the chapter is free of the word "boundary" — the boundary of the body, of the clan, of cleanness, of money, of speech, of taking.

[Theological Layer] One general inference is set down first (this is an inference): the twenty-five verses look like a miscellany on the surface; underneath, they are seven ways of asking one question — what does the presence of God require of the place where He is present? When the congregation gathers, the question is who may enter; when the camp is pitched for war, how the camp must conduct itself; when the sanctuary receives, what money may not come in; when brothers deal with each other, what usury may not be taken; when a person opens his mouth, whether spoken words count; when a neighbour's property is at stake, where permission stops. The five sections of this sermon read along this line.

[Summary Layer] Deuteronomy 23: one gate, one camp, one escaped servant, one bag of money, one spoken vow, one vineyard — twenty-five miscellaneous verses asking one question: in the place where God is present, how should a person conduct himself?

## I. The Gate of the Congregation: Who May Enter (Deut 23:1–8; 05:00–17:00)

[Factual Layer] These eight verses are arranged in the Masoretic text as five small units (1; 2; 3–6; 7a; 7b–8) — admission rules naming class after class, under the general name "enter into the congregation of the LORD" (qāhāl, H6951; six occurrences in this section: v. 1, twice in v. 2, twice in v. 3, v. 8). Class one (v. 1): "He that is wounded in the stones, or hath his privy member cut off, shall not enter into the congregation of the LORD." — two bodily conditions: wounded in the stones (pəṣûaʿ-dakkāʾ, root H6481 and H1795) and the privy member cut off (kərûṯ šāp̄kâ, passive participle of root H3772 + šāp̄kâ, H8212). Class two (v. 2): "A bastard shall not enter into the congregation of the LORD; even to his tenth generation shall he not enter into the congregation of the LORD." — mamzēr (H4464); the ban reaches his descendants, "even to his tenth generation" (dôr, H1755). Class three (vv. 3–6): "An Ammonite or Moabite shall not enter into the congregation of the LORD; even to their tenth generation shall they not enter into the congregation of the LORD for ever:" — the wording of the term differs from verse 2: here it is "even to their tenth generation… for ever." Two reasons are given. First, a historical one: "Because they met you not with bread and with water in the way, when ye came forth out of Egypt; and because they hired against thee Balaam the son of Beor of Pethor of Mesopotamia, to curse thee." (23:4) — they hired (sākar, H7936) Balaam (Bilʿām, H1109). Second, God's reversal: "Nevertheless the LORD thy God would not hearken unto Balaam; but the LORD thy God turned the curse into a blessing unto thee, because the LORD thy God loved thee." (23:5) The rule closes with a lifelong ban: "Thou shalt not seek their peace nor their prosperity all thy days for ever." (23:6) — seek (dāraš, H1875), peace (šālôm, H7965). Class four (vv. 7–8) turns sharply: "Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite; for he is thy brother: thou shalt not abhor an Egyptian; because thou wast a stranger in his land." (23:7) — the reason for the Edomite is kinship (ʾāḥ, H251, brother); the reason for the Egyptian is the grace of sojourning (gēr, H1616); the conclusion grants conditional admission: "The children that are begotten of them shall enter into the congregation of the LORD in their third generation." (23:8) The four classes line up as: never, for ever (v. 3, for Ammon and Moab); to the tenth generation (v. 2, for the bastard); in the third generation (v. 8, for Edom and Egypt); shall not enter (v. 1, the two bodily conditions, with no term given) — the terms themselves are this section's scale.

[Theological Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the "congregation" of this section is not an ethnic club but the assembly of worship (this is an inference): the object of the ban is "the congregation of the LORD" from first to last; it measures qualification to meet the Holy One, not permission for social contact — the text nowhere forbids trading or dwelling with these people; it says only "shall not enter into the congregation of the LORD." To read this section as an exclusion order is to mistake the gate of worship for a city wall. Second, the reasons for the ban on Moab and Ammon are two-layered (this is an inference): the first reason the text gives is historical fact — no welcome on the road, a man hired to curse; but verse 5 immediately roots it deeper: "but the LORD thy God turned the curse into a blessing unto thee, because the LORD thy God loved thee" — the curse became a blessing not because Israel deserved it, but because God loved. The rule remembers the offence, and also remembers the source of the grace: remember, on the wilderness road, who handed over water and who handed over a curse. Third, verses 7–8 and verses 3–6 must be read together to see the section's full scale (this is an inference): in one and the same section, "Thou shalt not seek their peace… for ever" and "Thou shalt not abhor" stand side by side — is the Edomite an enemy? He is a brother. Did Egypt enslave Israel for four hundred years? The text mentions only "thou wast a stranger in his land." This section does not teach people to nurse hatred; it teaches them to distinguish: grace and offence are both entered in the ledger according to fact, and the accounts are kept distinct. The account of Egypt's slavery is not raised in this chapter; only the fact of sojourning is raised — the rule speaks only according to the facts this chapter records.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Four items are set side by side and registered, none adjudicating another. First, the meaning of mamzēr (v. 2) is disputed among scholars: one reading takes it as a child born out of wedlock in general; another takes it as one born of a specific union (for example, a union with a cult prostitute or an incestuous union); the etymology is likewise unsettled (a loanword theory, and a theory connecting it with a root meaning "strange, alien"). Both readings and the etymologies are registered only; this sermon states the base text's "bastard" at face value. Second, the relation between the wordings "tenth generation" and "for ever": verse 2 says of the bastard "even to his tenth generation," verse 3 says of Ammon and Moab "even to their tenth generation… for ever" — some scholars take the tenth generation as a literal term of years, others take it as a round number for "generation after generation"; both readings are registered, neither adjudicated. Third, Ammon and Moab's "met you not" set beside the narrative of the same book: Deuteronomy 2 records that Moab and Edom once allowed Israel to pass through, buying food and water (verse references registered only, no quotation); Judges 11 records the Ammonite dispute separately (registered only); whether the occasion charged in verse 4 is the same event as those is read by scholars in two ways — a division-of-occasions reading and a tension reading; registered only, not adjudicated. Fourth, the Balaam narrative set side by side: Numbers 22–24 is the source narrative of this section (Balaam hired, the curse turned into a blessing); Num 31:16 and Josh 13:22 give later retrospects on Balaam (verse references registered only); the three retrospects assess Balaam from different angles; set side by side only, not harmonized. Also registered is one line concerning Edom the "brother": the Esau–Jacob narrative of Genesis 25–36 and the judgment oracles against Edom in Obadiah stand in a reading-level tension with this section's "Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite"; registered only, not adjudicated.

First, Neh 13:1–3: the returned congregation read in the book of Moses, read that the Ammonite and the Moabite should not come into the congregation of God for ever, and separated from Israel all the mixed multitude (verse reference and theme registered only; the base text at that place was not directly verified word for word, so no quotation) — this is one execution-echo of this section's rule within the Old Testament itself; registered only. [Summary Layer] Section One: one gate, four classes of people — the bodily wounded, the bastard, Ammon and Moab, Edom and Egypt; the terms differ: never for ever, the tenth generation, the third generation. The gate measures qualification for worship; the ledger records the grace and the offence of the wilderness road, each entered according to fact.


## II. Holiness in the Camp: God Walks in the Camp (Deut 23:9–14; 17:00–28:00)

[Factual Layer] These six verses form a single Masoretic unit (no paragraph marker between verses 9 and 14) — the most intact block in the chapter — treating the purity of the camp in time of war. The governing line stands at head and tail: the first sentence, "When the host goeth forth against thine enemies, then keep thee from every wicked thing." (23:9) — keep from every wicked thing (raʿ, H7451); the last sentence gives the reason: "For the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp, to deliver thee, and to give up thine enemies before thee; therefore shall thy camp be holy: that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee." (23:14) The four verses in between give three concrete arrangements. First, what chanceth by night (vv. 10–11): "If there be among you any man, that is not clean by reason of uncleanness that chanceth him by night, then shall he go abroad out of the camp, he shall not come within the camp: But it shall be, when evening cometh on, he shall wash himself with water: and when the sun is down, he shall come into the camp again." — that which chanceth by night (qerî, H7137); the arrangement has three steps: go out of the camp, wash (rāḥaṣ, H7364), and re-enter only when the sun is down; "not clean" (ṭāmēʾ, H2930) carries no language of blame here. Second, the place without the camp (v. 12): "Thou shalt have a place also without the camp, whither thou shalt go forth abroad:" — a place must be designated outside the camp (the Hebrew yāḏ literally means "hand"; see registered detail (14)). Third, the paddle (v. 13): "And thou shalt have a paddle upon thy weapon; and it shall be, when thou wilt ease thyself abroad, thou shalt dig therewith, and shalt turn back and cover that which cometh from thee:" — among the weapons (ʾāzēn, H240) one implement is kept ready; the Hebrew word is for a peg-like tool (yāṯēḏ, H3489); the actions are to dig (ḥāp̄ar, H2658) and to cover (kāsâ, H3680). Verse 14 is the chapter's sentence of reasons: God "walketh" in the camp (hālak, H1980, hitpael form, walks to and fro) with two purposes — "to deliver thee" (nāṣal, H5337) and "to give up thine enemies before thee"; the camp's corresponding state is "shall be holy" (qāḏôš, H6918); the sentence of risk is "that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee" — unclean thing (ʿerwâ, H6172) literally means "nakedness, a shameful thing," and turn away (šûḇ, H7725) literally means "to turn back." Count for this section: "camp" occurs seven times; see Note 2.

[Theological Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the focus of this section's purity is the presence of God, not hygiene as such (this is an inference): the place without the camp and the paddle certainly have a hygienic effect, but the reason the text gives for the paddle is not disease prevention; it is "For the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp" — even the most unseemly corner of the camp lies on the road where God walks. The law hangs its most practical facility rule on the chapter's most exalted sentence of reasons: where God walks, nothing may be done sloppily. Second, the arrangement in verse 10 shows the temperature of this section's view of purity (this is an inference): the night emission is something that "chanceth" — no will, no partner, no charge; the arrangement is one day outside the camp, washing, and return at sunset — no sacrifice, no blame, no record. Ritual uncleanness and moral sin are kept clearly apart in this section: uncleanness requires only waiting and washing; sin is what is required of a person (compare v. 21 in Section Four). To read all uncleanness as sin is the first step in misreading this section. Third, the walking and the turning away in verse 14 form a pair (this is an inference): God's "walking" in the camp is active presence; "turn away" in the original is "to turn back" — presence is not automatic: the camp shall be holy not because God is fastidious, but because presence itself has a shape. How the camp treats its most unclean thing shows whether this camp recognizes who walks in its midst.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items set side by side and registered, none adjudicated. First, the relation of this section to Deuteronomy 20 is, by discipline, set side by side only: Deuteronomy 20 treats exemptions from battle (a new house, a new vineyard, a new marriage, the fearful) and rules for cities and fruit trees (this series' Deuteronomy 20 sermon, "Fear Not," is complete, and its discussion layer set this section side by side); the two chapters share the setting of going out to war — one chapter treats who goes to fight and how cities are treated, this section treats purity within the camp during the campaign; the arrangement is one before the other, the jurisdiction one outward and one inward; the two places are set side by side only, with no inference drawn either way. Second, ancient Near Eastern parallels for war-camp purity: abstinence and pure self-restraint in wartime were common requirements in ancient armies; within this book's own narrative world, two places are registered — 1 Sam 21:4–5 (the holy bread and "the vessels of the young men" / the young men having kept themselves from women) and 2 Sam 11:11 (Uriah: "The ark, and Israel, and Judah, abide in tents," and he would not go home) — verse references and themes registered only, no quotation; scholars who cross-attest them with this section exist; registered only. Third, the framework of ritual uncleanness set side by side: Lev 15:16–18 treats the purity procedure for emission and intercourse (going out of the camp is not there; the skeleton of washing and waiting is similar; verse reference registered only, no quotation); whether verses 10–11 of this section are the same purity system landed in the war camp or a separate system is registered in both readings, not adjudicated. Also registered is one lexical item: "God walks in the camp" and the walking vocabulary of Gen 3:8 and Lev 26:12 (verse references registered only) — the three places are respectively the garden of creation, the covenant people, and the marching camp; set side by side only, not adjudicated.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Two items registered. First, no direct New Testament quotation of this section is registered; the walking-and-dwelling vocabulary of 2 Cor 6:16, "the temple of God" and "I will dwell in them, and walk in them" (verse reference registered only), is discussed at the reading level as an echo with verse 14; registered only, not used to rewrite this section. Second, the washing vocabulary of Eph 5:26–27, "with the washing of water by the word" (verse reference registered only), and this section's washing; registered at the lexical level as an echo only, not adjudicated.

[Summary Layer] Section Two: going out to battle, first keep from every wicked thing; the man with a night emission goes out of the camp for a day, washes, and returns at sunset; a place is marked outside the camp, and a paddle is kept among the weapons — because: the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp; therefore shall thy camp be holy.

## III. The Escaped Servant, the Money of the Sanctuary, and the Brother's Usury (Deut 23:15–20; 28:00–41:00)

[Factual Layer] These six verses form two Masoretic units (15–16; 17–20) and three small groups of rules. Group one treats the escaped servant (vv. 15–16): "Thou shalt not deliver unto his master the servant which is escaped from his master unto thee:" (23:15) — deliver up (sāgar, H5462, hiphil form); the servant (ʿeḇeḏ, H5650) has escaped from his master (the word for "escaped" is nāṣal, H5337, niphal form — the same word as "to deliver thee" in v. 14; Note 5). The settlement clause follows: "He shall dwell with thee, even among you, in that place which he shall choose in one of thy gates, where it liketh him best: thou shalt not oppress him." (23:16) — the dwelling place is chosen by the escaped servant himself; the protective sentence is "thou shalt not oppress him" (yānâ, H3238, hiphil form). Group two treats cult prostitution and vow money (vv. 17–18): "There shall be no whore of the daughters of Israel, nor a sodomite of the sons of Israel." (23:17) — the Hebrew is qəḏēšâ (H6948) and qāḏēš (H6945), whose root is precisely "holy" (qāḏaš), literally "a woman/man set apart," denoting cult prostitutes (Note 6). The money is banned with the persons: "Thou shalt not bring the hire of a whore, or the price of a dog, into the house of the LORD thy God for any vow: for even both these are abomination unto the LORD thy God." (23:18) — the hire (ʾeṯnan, H868) of a whore (zônâ, H2181) and the price (məḥîr, H4242) of a dog (keleḇ, H3611); the banned occasion is the payment of a vow (neder, H5088); the sentence of reasons is "abomination" (tôʿēḇâ, H8441). Group three treats usury (vv. 19–20): "Thou shalt not lend upon usury to thy brother; usury of money, usury of victuals, usury of any thing that is lent upon usury:" (23:19) — usury (nešek, H5392) shares a root with the verb nāšak (H5391), whose basic sense is "to bite"; three objects are named: money (kesep̄, H3701), victuals (ʾōkel, H400), and any thing that is lent upon usury. "Unto a stranger thou mayest lend upon usury; but unto thy brother thou shalt not lend upon usury:" (23:20a) — the contrast of stranger (nokrî, H5237) and brother (ʾāḥ, H251); the reason is a promise: "that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all that thou settest thine hand to in the land whither thou goest to possess it." (23:20b) — bless (bārak, H1288); all that thou settest thine hand to (mišlaḥ yāḏ, H4916).

[Theological Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the protection of the escaped-servant rule is two-layered (this is an inference): verse 15 bans "handing him back"; verse 16 bans "oppress him" — merely not handing him back while leaving him to fend for himself would not discharge the duty; the rule further gives the choice to the escaped servant himself: "in that place which he shall choose… where it liketh him best." The first thing a man who has just escaped his master's hand recovers, before the law, is the right to choose where he lives. Second, what verse 18 bans is not the amount of the money but its source (this is an inference): the same silver may be used for other purposes, but it may not be brought into the house to pay a vow — because paying a vow designates this money to God: money of unrighteous origin cannot be cleansed by being "offered to God." The gate of the house and the gate of the congregation (Section One) measure the same thing: of persons and of things brought before God, the origin must be asked after. Third, the scale of the usury ban lies in the word "brother" (this is an inference): the rule does not ban lending, and does not ban taking usury from a stranger; it bans only taking usury from a brother — a bond between brothers is not business but support (compare Lev 25:35–37, verse reference registered only); verse 20 joins not taking usury with blessing: the portion of usury not collected is entered in the ledger under the promise "may bless thee." The common point of this section's three groups (this is an inference): all three draw the line of "profit" — no profiting from a person (the escaped servant), no profiting from sin and then offering it to God (the whore's hire), no profiting from a brother's distress (usury).

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Four items set side by side and registered, none adjudicated. First, the ancient Near Eastern parallel for the escaped-servant rule is registered as the usual scholarly side-by-side item: ancient Near Eastern law codes and treaties commonly contain clauses on the extradition of escaped servants and heavy penalties for harbouring them (for example, the harbouring clauses of the Code of Hammurabi and the extradition agreements between the Hittites and Egypt — the general picture registered only, no clause text quoted); this section's "Thou shalt not deliver" runs in the opposite direction to that general picture, and scholars have written much on it; this sermon sets it side by side only, draws no adjudication, and makes no modern application. Second, the institution of the qāḏēš is registered in two readings side by side: (1) the cult-prostitution reading — the two words denote temple prostitutes, female and male, in Canaanite religion; verse 17 bans Israelites from serving in that role, and verse 18 bans their income from entering the house; (2) a questioning reading — some recent scholarship questions the scale and reality of the "sacred prostitution" institution itself, holding that the two words may denote temple attendants rather than prostitutes. Both readings are registered only; the main text of this sermon states the mainstream reading of the lexicons and the base text, and marks here that another reading exists. Third, the lexical registration of "dog" as a designation for the male prostitute: keleḇ here is an abusive designation; the "dogs" of Rev 22:15 (verse reference registered only) and the "dogs" of Phil 3:2 (verse reference registered only) are discussed at the reading level as lexical echoes; registered only. Fourth, the etymology of nešek is registered: the lexicons mostly explain it by "bite" — usury bites off a piece; this explanation is registered at the lexicon level, and scholars have other explanations; registered only, not adjudicated. Also set side by side within the same book: Deut 15:3 on the year of release — "Of a foreigner thou mayest exact it again: but that which is thine with thy brother thine hand shall release;" (Deut 15:3, KJV; directly verified word for word in the Chinese sermon against its base text) — the brother/stranger double track appears first in this book in the release rule and here in the usury rule; the two places are set side by side only.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Three items registered. First, the narrative echo of the escaped servant: the apostle Paul sends the escaped servant Onesimus back to his master Philemon, with a letter (the Epistle to Philemon; verse reference and theme registered only, no quotation) — the direction of the narrative is superficially opposite to this section's rule (sending back versus not delivering up); registered side by side only, not harmonized, not adjudicated. Second, echoes of usury: Luke 6:34–35 on lending "hoping for nothing again" (verse reference and theme registered only), the righteous man's portrait in Ezek 18:8, 13, 17, who "hath not given forth upon usury" to a brother (verse references registered only), and Neh 5:7, where Nehemiah rebukes the nobles, "Ye exact usury, every one of his brother" (verse reference registered only) — the latter two are executions and echoes within the Old Testament itself; registered only. Third, one nearby registration within Old Testament narrative: 1 Sam 30:15, where the Egyptian youth asks David to swear "that thou wilt neither kill me, nor deliver me into the hands of my master" (1 Sam 30:15, KJV — quoted only in the excerpt segment the Chinese sermon quotes, the quotation form following the source; in the Chinese sermon this verse was directly verified word for word against the base text on its verse page — the form originally marked, "verified in the cross-reference column," did not hold up and was corrected at sampling round fourteen of the supplementary-leg process; see Note 6) — the request in the narrative and the command in this section mirror each other at the reading level; registered only.

[Summary Layer] Section Three: the servant who has escaped to you is not to be handed back and not to be oppressed; he chooses his own place to live. The money of the whore and the sodomite may not enter the house to pay a vow. Money and food lent to a brother may bear no usury — all three lines are drawn at "profit."

## IV. Vows: What the Mouth Has Spoken Must Count (Deut 23:21–23; 41:00–50:00)

[Factual Layer] These three verses form one Masoretic unit, treating vows made to God (neder, H5088; the verb nāḏar, H5087). The structure is one "When… then…" main clause, plus one exemption, plus one general rule. Verse 21 is the main clause: "When thou shalt vow a vow unto the LORD thy God, thou shalt not slack to pay it: for the LORD thy God will surely require it of thee; and it would be sin in thee." — slack/defer (ʾāḥar, H309, piel form), pay (šālam, H7999, piel form), require (dāraš, H1875 — the same word as "seek their peace" in v. 6, and the same word as "seek" for lost property in Deuteronomy 22; Note 5), sin (ḥēṭʾ, H2399). Verse 22 is the exemption: "But if thou shalt forbear to vow, it shall be no sin in thee." — to forbear to vow (ḥāḏal, H2308, literally "to cease from doing") does not constitute sin. Verse 23 is the general rule: "That which is gone out of thy lips thou shalt keep and perform; even a freewill offering, according as thou hast vowed unto the LORD thy God, which thou hast promised with thy mouth." — that which is gone out of thy lips (môṣāʾ, H4161); the original reads "that which is gone out of thy lips" (śāp̄â, H8193, literally "lip"; the Chinese base text renders it "what has come out of your mouth"); freewill offering (nəḏāḇâ, H5071) is a voluntary offering; the command is the doubled verb "keep and perform." Read together, the three verses say: vowing is wholly voluntary (v. 22); once vowed, it becomes a debt (v. 21); and the evidence of the debt is one's own mouth (v. 23).

[Theological Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, what this section establishes first is "you may refrain from vowing" (this is an inference): before treating payment, the text first gives the exemption — if thou shalt forbear to vow, it shall be no sin in thee. A vow is never a duty in the law; it is a burden one adds to oneself voluntarily ("freewill offering," v. 23); what the law protects is not enthusiasm for vowing but the weight of speech: better not to speak than to speak and not count it. Second, the subject of "require" is God (this is an inference): a vow owed to God has no human collector — the text says the LORD "will surely require it of thee." The seriousness of a vow lies not in the ceremony but in the addressee: it is to God that you opened your mouth. Third, "it would be sin in thee" in verse 21 and "it shall be no sin in thee" in verse 22 are two faces of one scale (this is an inference): sin comes not from "not doing enough" but from "saying and not doing" — what this section fixes is not the quantity of the offering but the distance between word and deed. The mouth is the last vessel in this chapter that must be kept holy: after the congregation, the camp, the house, and the money, it is the lips' turn.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Three items set side by side and registered, none adjudicated. First, parallel law within the same book and the same Torah: Numbers 30 treats in detail the confirmation and annulment of vows (the vows of a woman in her father's house and of a wife in her husband's house, on which the father or husband speaks the same day; verse reference and framework registered only), and Leviticus 27 treats the valuation and redemption of special vows (verse reference registered only) — this section treats only the general rule "voluntary to vow, obligatory to pay," while those two chapters treat procedure and valuation; whether the three places stand as general rule and particular rules, or each as its own case, is registered only, not adjudicated. Second, the semantic registration of neder and nəḏāḇâ: the former is a vow carrying an obligation to pay; the latter is a voluntary offering with no prior pledge; verse 23 describes the content of what was vowed with nəḏāḇâ, and scholars have a reading here of "the content of the vow is in itself a freewill offering"; registered only. Third, the wisdom-tradition echo of Eccl 5:4–5: "When thou vowest a vow unto God, defer not to pay it; for he hath no pleasure in fools: pay that which thou hast vowed. Better is it that thou shouldest not vow, than that thou shouldest vow and not pay." (Eccl 5:4–5, KJV; directly verified word for word in the Chinese sermon against its base text) — the wording is almost isomorphic with verses 21–22 of this section (defer/slack, pay, better not to vow), a clear attested echo between Torah and wisdom literature; registered only, their order not adjudicated.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Two items registered. First, Matt 5:33–37 on swearing — "Again, ye have heard that it hath been said by them of old time, Thou shalt not forswear thyself, but shalt perform unto the Lord thine oaths:" (Matt 5:33, KJV; in the Chinese sermon the first sentence of this verse was directly verified word for word against the base text on its verse page — the form originally marked, "verified in the cross-reference column," did not hold up and was corrected at sampling round fourteen; see Note 6) — it concerns, as this section does, oaths to the Lord; registered side by side only; the New Testament is not used to rewrite this section. Second, the narrative fragments of Paul's vow and payment of a vow in Acts 18 and 21 (verse references and theme registered only, no quotation) form a practice-echo with this section at the reading level; registered only.

[Summary Layer] Section Four: a vow may be left unvowed; once vowed, it must not be slackly paid — the LORD will surely require it of thee. Sin comes not from not doing enough, but from saying and not doing; keeping holiness has come round to the lips.

## V. The Neighbour's Vineyard: Eating One's Fill Is Allowed, Carrying Away Is Not (Deut 23:24–25; 50:00–57:00)

[Factual Layer] These two verses each form their own Masoretic unit (samekh at the end of both v. 24 and v. 25) — the close of the whole chapter — treating the limits of taking when entering a neighbour's property. Verse one treats the vineyard: "When thou comest into thy neighbour's vineyard, then thou mayest eat grapes thy fill at thine own pleasure; but thou shalt not put any in thy vessel." (23:24) — vineyard (kerem, H3754), neighbour (rēaʿ, H7453); "eat grapes thy fill at thine own pleasure" in the original is "according to thy soul, to thy fill" (nepeš, H5315; śāḇaʿ, H7646); the line falls on the vessel (kəlî, H3627): none may be put in. Verse two treats the standing corn: "When thou comest into the standing corn of thy neighbour, then thou mayest pluck the ears with thine hand; but thou shalt not move a sickle unto thy neighbour's standing corn." (23:25; the closing quotation mark falls here, and the chapter's discourse closes) — standing corn (qāmâ, H7054), pluck with the hand (qāṭap̄, H6998) the ears (məlîlôṯ, H4425); the line falls on the sickle (ḥermēš, H2770, occurring only here in the whole Bible): it must not be moved (nûp̄, H5130, hiphil form) to reap. The two verses are exactly parallel in structure: permission first (thou mayest eat thy fill; thou mayest pluck with thine hand), restriction after (but thou shalt not put any in thy vessel; but thou shalt not move a sickle); the measure of the permission is "fill," and the boundary of the restriction is "the tool."

[Theological Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, this section's generosity is quantified (this is an inference): the owner's duty is not written as a command addressed to him, but is written inside the permission addressed to the passer-by — you may eat to your fill. The law lays down an unwritten rule for the owner: your vineyard owes the passer-by one full meal. Second, the line is drawn at the tool, not at the quantity (this is an inference): the text does not limit how much may be eaten ("at thine own pleasure… thy fill"); it bans only two tools — the vessel that carries away, and the sickle that reaps. Eating is present need; putting in a vessel and reaping are carrying away and possessing; plucking ears is permitted, moving a sickle is crossing the line: the line lies not in the size of the action but in its nature. Third, the way this section closes the whole chapter is "smallness" (this is an inference): from the gate of the congregation (v. 1) to the ears of corn in the vineyard (v. 25), the law lands at last on the smallest thing — passing through another's field, what should the hand do? Holiness is not only in the congregation, the camp, and the house; it is also in a cluster of grapes that may not be put in one's pocket.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two items set side by side and registered, none adjudicated. First, set beside the gleaning rules: Lev 19:9–10 and Deut 24:19–21 treat leaving the remainder of the harvest for the poor and the stranger (verse references registered only); together with this section's passer-by taking, they are two faces of one ethic — in one place the owner is commanded to leave, in the other the passer-by is permitted to eat; whether the two places are two clauses of one institution or two independent customs is registered only, not adjudicated. Second, Ruth chapter 2, where Ruth gleans ears of corn in the field of Boaz (verse reference and theme registered only, no quotation) — in the narrative, "gleaning" takes place after the harvest and with the owner's permission, a different occasion from this section's "plucking"; scholars set them side by side in discussion; the likenesses and differences of the two occasions are registered only, not adjudicated.

[New Testament Reading Layer] One item registered. The three parallel cornfield narratives in the Gospels: Matt 12:1–2, Mark 2:23, Luke 6:1–2 — "And it came to pass on the second sabbath after the first, that he went through the corn fields; and his disciples plucked the ears of corn, and did eat, rubbing them in their hands." (Luke 6:1, KJV; directly verified word for word in the Chinese sermon against its base text) What the disciples did was exactly the hand-plucking permitted by verse 25 of this section; the Pharisees' charge falls on the Sabbath (Luke 6:2, verse reference registered only), not on the plucking itself. This item is registered at the reading level only: New Testament narrative never takes this section's permission as the point of dispute; the Gospels are not used to rewrite this section's original meaning.

[Summary Layer] Section Five: enter a neighbour's vineyard and eat to your fill; enter a neighbour's standing corn and pluck ears with your hand — only, put none in your vessel, and move no sickle to reap. The measure of the permission is fill; the boundary is the tool: the hand may; the sickle may not.

## Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Factual Layer] The whole chapter recounted: the gate of the congregation (vv. 1–8); the purity of the war camp (vv. 9–14); the escaped servant, the whore's money, and the brother's usury (vv. 15–20); vows (vv. 21–23); the neighbour's vineyard (vv. 24–25). The closing quotation mark and the chapter-end samekh both close at the end of verse 25 — the text itself shuts its door here.

[Theological Layer] One general inference in closing (this is an inference): the holiness of this chapter is "the holiness of boundaries" — the gate has a boundary, the camp has a boundary, money has a boundary, words have a boundary, the hand has a boundary. The five boundaries are not five walls: at the gate stand Edom and Egypt, who enter in the third generation; in the camp stands the soldier who washes and returns; in the vineyard stands the passer-by who eats his fill. The purpose of a boundary is not to keep people out; it is so that, in the place where God is present, everyone knows how to come in and how to conduct himself. "Therefore shall thy camp be holy" is the summary of all twenty-five verses: because the LORD thy God walks in thy midst.

[Summary Layer] Conclusion: Deuteronomy 23 — the gate must be kept, the ledger cleared, the camp cleansed, the money kept clean, the word made to count, the hand made to stop. Thy camp shall be holy, because God walks in the midst of the camp.

## Devotional Extract

In these twenty-five verses, God's people learn how to live with God's presence. At the gate, the rules sort people into classes — not to teach anyone to despise another, but to teach them to remember: in the congregation of the LORD, entering is never a matter of course. In the camp, God Himself walks, so even the place of relieving oneself is marked outside the camp and the digging paddle is kept among the weapons — the most inconspicuous corner also lies on the road where God walks. The escaped servant knocks at the door: he must not be handed back, and he must be allowed to choose his own dwelling. The whore's money, however much, must not be brought into the house to pay a vow. Money lent to a brother must bear not a fraction of usury. A vow opened with the mouth must not be delayed. In a neighbour's vineyard, eating to one's fill is allowed; putting grapes in one's pocket is not. Every boundary says the same sentence: where God is present, people must learn reverence, and must learn restraint. Holiness, it turns out, is not far away: it is in a vow once spoken, in a portion of usury one refuses to collect, in a cluster of grapes eaten to the full but not carried away, in a dwelling place chosen by an escaped servant who has been taken in. May the "camp" where we dwell today — home, work, and church — likewise be holy: "For the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp, to deliver thee, and to give up thine enemies before thee; therefore shall thy camp be holy: that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee." (Deut 23:14, KJV)

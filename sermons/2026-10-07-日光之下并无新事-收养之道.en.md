# Nothing New Under the Sun: The Way of Adoption

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese sermon manuscript «日光之下并无新事：收养之道».
> **Preacher:** Guangyi Zhao

- **Series:** Nothing New Under the Sun (Eccl. 1:9)
- **Preacher:** Guangyi Zhao (2019, compiled and edited)
- **Theme:** The patterns, pitfalls, and way out in the area of the way of adoption

## Summary

Adoption, in many people's hearts, is a knot: the adopted person fears he is "a second-best choice." This chapter seeks to untie this knot. The Bible says that true Christians are all people adopted into God's household — adoption is not a second-class arrangement, but God's way of unconditionally accepting people. God has His special plan in each person's life.

## I. We Are All Adopted

> "Having predestined us to adoption as sons by Jesus Christ to Himself, according to the good pleasure of His will, to the praise of the glory of His grace, by which He made us accepted in the Beloved." (Eph. 1:5-6)

Here "adoption as sons" in Greek is **huiothesia (υἱοθεσία)** (original text υἱοθεσία, transliterated huiothesia; Strong G5206; according to *Thayer's Lexicon*, "adoption as a son"), referring to formally establishing a person who was originally outside as a son, giving him the status and inheritance of a son. Paul uses this word to speak of our relationship with God, not casually: our status in God's household comes precisely through adoption.

First make the foundation clear: True Christians are all adopted into God's household.

This is not a word of comfort in metaphor. By nature, we are separated from God; it is God, because He loves us, according to the good pleasure of His will, who predestined us to adoption as sons by Jesus Christ. Behind sonship is grace — "to the praise of the glory of His grace, by which He made us accepted in the Beloved" (Eph. 1:6).

Therefore, when a Christian family adopts a child, or when an adopted person thinks of his own background, the first thing to remember is: This matter of adoption, God Himself did first. He accepted us without attached conditions, not waiting until we were good enough or enough like natural-born children before bringing us into the household. Adoption in God's hands has never been making do.

## II. "God Loves Me — Will He Also Leave Me?"

Someone asked like this: It is said my birth mother, because she loved me very much, gave me to others to raise. The Bible says God loves me; does this mean God will also leave me?

Behind this way of asking is a real wound: The person who loved me most still sent me away; will love always end in leaving?

The Bible's answer is very crisp: No.

> "And the LORD, He is the One who goes before you. He will be with you, He will not leave you nor forsake you; do not fear nor be dismayed." (Deut. 31:8)

A birth mother entrusting her child to others to raise, and God forsaking His children, are two different things. People make difficult decisions because of weakness, circumstances, and having no alternative; but God has personally promised that He will not leave nor forsake. An adopted person need not use his most painful experience to guess God's heart — guessing only makes one more and more afraid; return to the promise itself: He is with you; this is what He said.

## III. The Root of Bitterness: "Why Am I Different from Others"

Another adopted Christian said this: I was adopted from childhood, and my adoptive parents are very kind and respectable, but in my heart there is still bitterness, because I always feel I am different from others.

This "different" is the common struggle of many adopted persons. Others do not know your origin, but you remember it every day; as soon as you think of your background, there is a place in your heart you cannot get past. Over time, not getting past it becomes bitterness. The Bible reminds us to guard against this hand:

> "Looking carefully lest anyone fall short of the grace of God; lest any root of bitterness springing up cause trouble, and by this many become defiled." (Heb. 12:15)

The characteristic of a root of bitterness is that it first takes root in the heart, and only when it grows out does it trouble people. Dealing with bitterness cannot wait until it grows large; it must be dealt with at the root. And the method of dealing with it is first to see clearly a fact: Being different is itself not a shame.

Jesus was also a person who was different — He was adopted by His earthly father Joseph. The angel said to Mary:

> "And the angel answered and said to her, 'The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Highest will overshadow you; therefore, also, that Holy One who is to be born will be called the Son of God.'" (Luke 1:35)

Jesus was not Joseph's natural-born son, yet He grew up in Joseph's household and called Joseph father. Though Jesus was different, He always obeyed the heavenly Father's will. His background did not hinder Him; on the contrary, it became part of God's redemptive plan.

Therefore, if an adopted person is to become like Christ, he should accept the fact of being adopted, and believe: God completely knows how to use the special aspects of your life. The "different" in your eyes, in God's hands, can be His special arrangement, not a deduction from your life.

## IV. Root Cause: Regarding Adoption as "A Second-Best Choice"

Digging down from bitterness to the root, it is often one sentence:

**Wrong understanding:** In the depths of my heart, I truly regard adoption as a second-best solution.

As long as this view does not change, however many comforting words others say will not enter. For the problem is not how others treat you, but that in your heart you have first judged yourself as "second-class."

**The right understanding** is this: Adoption may not have been my initial choice, but it is not a second-best choice. God's adoption of me shows me His unconditional acceptance of me; and I know God has a special plan in my life (see Eph. 1:5-6).

Replacing "second-best" is the most important step in this chapter. When the understanding changes, the way one views one's background changes with it: the same experience, formerly seen as a gap, afterwards seen as the concrete path by which God placed a person into a family.

## V. Steps to Resolution: Do Not Remember Former Things, Read Romans Chapter 8

After the principle is understood, there must also be a road that can be walked daily. The steps given in this chapter are very concrete:

**First, memorize Isaiah 43:18-19, let God's word speak for you first:**

> "Do not remember the former things, nor consider the things of old. Behold, I will do a new thing, now it shall spring forth; shall you not know it? I will even make a road in the wilderness and rivers in the desert." (Isa. 43:18-19)

Not remembering former things does not deny that the past happened, but refuses to let the past set the tone for the future. What God will do is a new thing — making a road in the wilderness, and rivers in the desert — both are giving a way out in places that seem to have no way out.

**Second, read repeatedly Romans 8:15-18, reading it into the heart:**

> "For you did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry out, 'Abba, Father.' The Spirit Himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God, and if children, then heirs — heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ, if indeed we suffer with Him, that we may also be glorified together. For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us." (Rom. 8:15-18)

This passage speaks thoroughly of adoption in three layers: What was received is the Spirit of adoption, not the spirit of bondage, so there is no more fear; the Holy Spirit Himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God — identity is not a feeling one holds up by oneself; there is the Holy Spirit's witness; if children, then heirs, joint heirs with Christ — what adoption brings is not living under another's roof, but the right of inheritance. As for the sufferings along this road, Paul does not deny them, but only places them together with the future glory and weighs them: after weighing, they are not worthy to be compared.

## Conclusion

There is nothing new under the sun. Stories of adoption were written long ago in the Bible; our relationship with God is itself an adoption. An adopted person need not continue in bitterness over being "different": The One who accepts you is God, who will not forsake you; He has a special plan in your life. Do not remember former things — He will make a road in your wilderness, and rivers in your desert.

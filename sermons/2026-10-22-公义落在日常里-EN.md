## Summary (about 100 words)

After the Ten Commandments were given, Moses set the first case laws of the Book of the Covenant before the people. The ordinances do not begin with livestock or fields; they begin with the Hebrew servant, the maidservant, the injured person, and the person gored to death by an ox. Justice is not only the thunder on the mountain; justice must land on daily small things — who may go home, who must pay with his life, who is owed compensation — because the covenant-making God looks first at persons, then at property; first He protects the weak, then He deals with the strong.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter God spoke on Mount Sinai amid thunder, lightning, a thick cloud, and the sound of the trumpet. The people stood below the mountain and all trembled, and even Moses said, I exceedingly fear and quake. Ten commandments, each weightier than the last: Thou shalt have no other gods; thou shalt not kill; thou shalt not steal; thou shalt not bear false witness. That is the charter of the covenant, the great principles carved on tablets of stone. But after the charter was given, the people went back to the camp, and life still had to be lived day by day: when a servant bought into the household reached the end of his term, was he to be let go? When two households fought and a man was injured, who was to pay the medical costs? When a neighbour's ox gored a man to death, how was that account to be settled? God did not leave the people in the awe on the mountain; He immediately brought His words into the tents, into the courtyards, and into the disputes between two households. Exodus chapter 21, which we read today, is exactly that bringing-down. Its first sentence is utterly plain, yet it sets the tone for the whole Book of the Covenant: Now these are the judgments which thou shalt set before them.

Notice this word "judgments": in the original, mishpatim (מִשְׁפָּטִים, H4941), case laws, rulings, ordinances [1]. Later generations have called the whole section of Exodus chapters 21 to 23 the Book of the Covenant, and chapter 21 is its opening. The Ten Commandments are the charter; the Book of the Covenant is the first batch of case law in which the charter lands in daily life. Case law differs from commandments: a commandment says, Thou shalt not kill; case law must answer — if a man in a quarrel struck another dead, if a man unintentionally caused a death, if a man let his own ox gore a man to death, how exactly are these matters to be judged, compensated, and brought to a close among God's people? In preaching this chapter, this manuscript keeps three rules, which I state plainly to you first. First, we will not spread out every article one by one: thirty-six verses read article by article would not fit into sixty minutes; we must grasp the structure, the order, and the theological anchors, and see what this batch of ordinances says as a whole. Second, we will not avoid the difficulties: this chapter has servants, maidservants, and clauses about a knocked-out eye or tooth — it is the chapter modern readers find most uncomfortable — and we will neither whitewash it nor force today's words onto the ancients, but honestly explain the ancient Near Eastern world in which it stood. Third, we will not preach the case law directly as New Testament doctrine: where the New Testament has an echo, we will identify it as a later way of reading, an apostolic looking-back, rather than passing it off as the original meaning on the spot in this chapter. Holding these three rules, we can both read the ancients honestly and bring the words steadily into today.

Let us read several key passages of today's text together:

"Now these are the judgments which thou shalt set before them. If thou buy an Hebrew servant, six years he shall serve: and in the seventh he shall go out free for nothing." (Exodus 21:1-2, excerpt, KJV)

"He that smiteth a man, so that he die, shall be surely put to death. And if a man lie not in wait, but God deliver him into his hand; then I will appoint thee a place whither he shall flee. But if a man come presumptuously upon his neighbour, to slay him with guile; thou shalt take him from mine altar, that he may die." (Exodus 21:12-14, excerpt, KJV)

"And if a man smite the eye of his servant, or the eye of his maid, that it perish; he shall let him go free for his eye's sake. And if he smite out his manservant's tooth, or his maidservant's tooth; he shall let him go free for his tooth's sake." (Exodus 21:26-27, excerpt, KJV)

"And if a man shall open a pit, or if a man shall dig a pit, and not cover it, and an ox or an ass fall therein; the owner of the pit shall make it good, and give money unto the owner of them; and the dead beast shall be his." (Exodus 21:33-34, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You do not speak only on the mountain; You also come into our tents and courtyards to speak. Through the opening of this Book of the Covenant today, teach us that Your justice is not a slogan, but something that lands, matter by matter, on persons. Open our eyes to see how You see people, and how You protect the small and weak; and remind us that we ourselves were once servants in Egypt, and must never, after gaining freedom, bring Egypt back into our own homes. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Ordinances Begin with Persons — The Hebrew Servant and the Maidservant (21:1-11)

First look at the order of the whole chapter; this is the first weighty matter in it. At the opening of the Book of the Covenant, it would not have been strange for God to speak first of oxen and sheep, first of fields, first of loans — most ancient law codes did so. But our text, strikingly, speaks first of persons, and first of the persons with the least standing among them: servants and maidservants. The first case law is not about kings, not about priests, not about heads of households; it is about a bought Hebrew servant, and whether, in the seventh year, he may go home. This order itself is the message: the justice of the covenant community is measured first by how it treats the lowliest person in the home. How a society treats its weakest people exposes, earlier than how it worships or how it celebrates, whether it actually resembles Egypt, or the people God intends to make.

First, the Hebrew servant. "Hebrew servant," in the original 'eved 'ivri (עֶבֶד עִבְרִי, H5650 plus H5680), literally, a Hebrew servant [2]. Here we must honestly explain the circumstances of that time: in the ancient Near East, a man sold himself as a servant mostly not because he had been carried off, but because of debt, famine, or having no way to live, he sold his labour to a fellow countryman in exchange for food and a place sheltered from wind and rain. This was not the transoceanic slave trade of modern imagination, yet it was truly a condition of lost freedom. The text does not pretend this system did not exist; what it does is something else: it sets a ceiling for this system and opens a way out. First, the ceiling is the number of years: six years he shall serve, and in the seventh he shall go out free for nothing. "For nothing" means paying no redemption price and owing no remainder; the master may not, when the seventh year comes, find an excuse to keep the man. Six years is the cap; when the term arrives, the door opens. Second, the way out carries dignity: if he came in by himself, he shall go out by himself; if he were married, then his wife shall go out with him. A wife may not be held back as a hostage. Third, there is an arrangement so fine that it stirs the heart: if the master gave him a wife during his service, the wife and her children shall be her master's, and he shall go out by himself. This must be said honestly: by today's eyes this arrangement is broken and incomplete; it is a case ruling within the patriarchal and property order of that time, not a picture of the final state of the kingdom of heaven. The text records it because it gave a workable ruling for that time, not because God delights in the separation of flesh and blood. A preacher should neither whitewash the ancients here, nor stop here, because immediately afterward there is a way out that lays bare the weight of the human heart.

That way out is the scene of the ear bored at the door. The text says: if the servant shall plainly say, I love my master, my wife, and my children; I will not go out free, then his master shall bring him unto the judges; he shall also bring him to the door, or unto the door post; and his master shall bore his ear through with an aul; and he shall serve him for ever. Hear that plain saying clearly: I love my master. In the whole Book of the Covenant, the word "love" appears for the first time not between husband and wife, not between parent and child, but here, in a servant's voluntary word to his master. The law leaves a door open here: when the term expires, the man could leave, yet he may, out of love, choose for himself to stay. This choice must be public, must be voluntary, and must be established before the judges; the ear bored at the doorpost nails a private intention into a public witness, lest anyone later say the master forced it. This manuscript must state the theological proportion plainly: this scene has often later been read as a picture of willing service — Paul called himself a servant of Jesus Christ, and believers have used this to exhort themselves to lifelong service of the Lord; that is a later way of reading, a usable homiletical development, not the teaching on the spot in chapter 21. On the spot it says only one thing: in God's ordinances, even a servant's intention must be respected; if he stays, it must be because he loves, not because he has nowhere to go. If the freedom is not real, the voluntariness does not stand; God first gave him real freedom, and only then could he give real voluntariness.

Next are the clauses on the maidservant — the verses in this whole chapter that most require honesty. "Maidservant," in the original 'amah (אָמָה, H519), a female slave, a maidservant [3]. The text is direct: if a man sell his daughter to be a maidservant, she shall not go out as the menservants do. This sentence first acknowledges a different circumstance: a manservant sold his labour, and when the years expired he could dust off his clothes and leave; a woman sold as a maidservant, in the arrangements of that time, mostly carried an expectation of marriage — she was bought in preparation to become a daughter-in-law or a concubine of this household — and so she could not, like a manservant, simply be sent away when the term expired; for her that would not be freedom, but abandonment. So the clauses follow one by one, all protecting her: if the master betrothed her to his son, he shall deal with her after the manner of daughters; if she please not her master, who had betrothed her to himself, then shall he let her be redeemed, but he shall have no power to sell her unto a strange nation, seeing he hath dealt deceitfully with her; and if the master takes another wife, her food, her raiment, and her duty of marriage shall he not diminish. Food, clothing, and the duty of marriage — these three things, listed one by one in the original, are her most basic rights in this household; to diminish even one is to wrong her. If the master does not do these three unto her, then shall she go out free without money, without any price.

At this point the matter must be spoken to the bottom, without vagueness. First, these clauses are not modern abolitionist language; they did not announce the abolition of the buying-and-selling system of that time; within ancient society they set protection, a ceiling, and a way out for the weakest woman; if we pass them off as a modern declaration of human rights, that is dishonest. Second, they are by no means acquiescence in oppression: in a world where a daughter could be sold by her father, the ordinances deliberately restrain a master from tiring of the old for the new, restrain him from cutting her food and clothing, and restrain him from reselling a disfavoured woman to a foreign nation — at every point putting a bridle on the strong and leaving a back door for the weak. This is exactly how the Book of the Covenant deals with an ancient system: not by tearing down the whole world overnight, but by first, within that world, treating a person as a person. Third, a variant parallel must be noted: when Deuteronomy chapter 15 restates the servant ordinances, male and female servants are treated alike — after six years both are to be released, and at release the master must not let him go empty, but shall furnish him liberally out of the flock, the threshing floor, and the winepress, sending him out with a generous provision (Deuteronomy 15:12-18, cited in sense). Deuteronomy's standard is broader and warmer than Exodus's; the Exodus maidservant clauses stress protection within a marriage-related circumstance, while Deuteronomy makes release with generous provision the general rule. Reading the two together, what we see is not a contradiction, but the same God in different proclamations moving from protection to generous treatment, the direction always toward the side of a person's release. This comparison is an explanation gained from reading the two texts together, and this manuscript identifies it as such.

Let this part close. A servant gains freedom in six years; a maidservant's three things may not be diminished — together these two groups of clauses answer one question: in the home of the covenant people, does the lowliest person count as a person at all? The ordinances answer: yes, and he is counted first. The master has money, power, and a contract in his hand, yet God's words land first on the master: there is a limit of years to your keeping a person, a bottom line to your treatment of a person, and consequences to your wronging a person. Justice landing in daily life lands first on this: the hand of the strong must be restrained.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Life Must Not Be Taken Lightly — Killing, Grave Crimes, and Compensation for Injury (21:12-27)

After the ordinances on servants, the text turns sharply to grave crimes and injuries against the person. One word must first be recognized here, and recognized honestly, so that we do not misstate the original: the verb in "He that smiteth a man, so that he die" is the Hiphil participle of nakah (נָכָה, H5221), to strike, to strike dead — not the ratsach (רָצַח, H7523) of the Ten Commandments' "Thou shalt not kill" [6]. The two words are not one word-line: ratsach denotes unlawful killing, murder, while nakah is the broader verb "to strike." The relation of the Book of the Covenant to the Ten Commandments lies not in a shared root but in case law unfolding a principle: on the tablets the commandment set the boundary of life in a single sentence, Thou shalt not kill; here the Book of the Covenant unfolds the answer: which cases of taking life must pay with a life, and which are left a way to live — and where is the line drawn?

The first line is drawn between intent and unintent. The text says: He that smiteth a man, so that he die, shall be surely put to death — of this sentence there is no negotiation. But then it says: if a man lie not in wait, but God deliver him into his hand, then I will appoint thee a place whither he shall flee. "To lie in wait" is deliberation, premeditation, waiting by the road to strike; "God deliver him into his hand" is an honest Old Testament way of speaking of what happens by accident and not by human intent, like an axe head flying off the handle while cutting wood and injuring a man. These two, in God's ordinances, are judged completely differently: the deliberate man pays with his life; the unintentional man flees. The later system of cities of refuge grew out of this one sentence, "I will appoint thee a place"; only in Numbers and Deuteronomy did it become a complete arrangement of cities — here in this chapter is its seed, and this manuscript notes it so, lest the later completed institution be read back into this chapter. The meaning of the city of refuge is to give a man who unintentionally injured another a place to live and await judgment before the avenger of blood arrives: justice not only punishes evil, it also protects the misunderstood, and does not let revenge act before judgment. But immediately after, the third sentence blocks half of that road of escape: But if a man come presumptuously upon his neighbour, to slay him with guile; thou shalt take him from mine altar, that he may die. The altar is the holiest place in the sanctuary, the place where a man flees before God to plead for mercy; yet the ordinances say plainly that for a deliberate murderer, the altar is not his shield. Religious devotion cannot serve as cover for deliberate evil, and hiding in the sanctuary cannot wash away a crime that must pay with a life. This article later had an instance in the First Book of Kings: Joab had killed Abner and Amasa, and though he caught hold of the horns of the altar, he was still taken out and put to death (1 Kings 2:28-34, cited in sense). This manuscript cites this as an intertextual explanation gained by reading backward from the later text. Holiness and justice are not two separate things here: precisely because the altar is holy, it does not shelter a man who treats human life as a trifle.

After the killing clauses, the text lists in a row three grave crimes placed in the same class as killing, and each deserves a pause. First: he that smiteth his father, or his mother, shall be surely put to death. Second: he that stealeth a man, and selleth him, or if he be found in his hand, he shall surely be put to death. Third: he that curseth his father, or his mother, shall surely be put to death. These three placed side by side tell us which three things God values: life, freedom, and parents. To strike one's parents and to curse one's parents is to break and revile the very root that bore and raised you; the fifth commandment in the Ten Commandments says, Honour thy father and thy mother, that thou mayest live long and that it may go well with thee; here the Book of the Covenant gives it its penal side: the authority and dignity of parents are protected together with human life. The stealing of a man deserves an especially heavy reading: to steal an ox or a sheep, as the text below shows, is only several-fold compensation; to steal a person is a capital crime. In God's account, a person is forever worth more than livestock, and the trafficking of persons stands in the same row as deliberate murder — a line that runs consistently through the Bible. Many years later, the apostle Paul, in his list of the lawless in First Timothy, placed those who smite fathers and mothers together with menstealers (1 Timothy 1:9-10, cited in sense); this is a New Testament echo of this old line, and this manuscript identifies it here as a New Testament echo, not as an expansion of this chapter's original meaning. At this point a plain word must also be said to today's hearers: human trafficking is not a crime only of ancient times; today it has changed its name and its channels, and still harms people in hidden places. When the church reads this chapter, it should not read out only ancient penalties; it should read out God's hatred of this crime, and, within its own reach, protect those most easily kidnapped, sold, and treated as goods.

Next comes a group of case laws on injury and compensation, from brawls to the bodies of servants — seemingly trivial, yet showing most clearly the warmth of the ordinances. If men strive together, and one smite another with a stone, or with his fist, and he die not, but keepeth his bed; if he rise again, and walk abroad upon his staff, then shall he that smote him be quit: only he shall pay for the loss of his time, and shall cause him to be thoroughly healed. Notice these two accounts: the cost of healing and the loss of time. In case law more than three thousand years old, this is written with complete clarity: to injure a man is not settled with an apology; you must accompany him until he is healed, and make up the days he could not work. Sometimes justice is not spectacular; it is simply healing a man thoroughly and making up his days. Next, on servants: if a man smite his servant, or his maid, with a rod, and he die under his hand, he shall be surely punished; notwithstanding, if he continue a day or two, he shall not be punished: for he is his money. This must be read honestly, and felt honestly: by today's eyes this article falls far short; it acknowledges a master's almost proprietary power over servants at that time, dividing the weight of punishment only by whether death was immediate. A preacher who speaks well of the ancients here is dishonest; yet one who only curses the ancients here has not finished reading either, because a few verses further down the ordinances immediately set another line for the bodies of servants: smite out an eye, and let him go free; knock out a tooth, and let him go free. An eye and a tooth — one a grave injury, the other only a small one — and the ordinances count both alike: the bodies of servants are also within the ordinances; wherever a master's hand strikes, the law follows, and if he has injured, he pays with freedom. Reading the two together, we see exactly this chapter's consistent way of dealing with an ancient system: within the world in which it stood, it first blocks the cruelest openings and opens a way out for the victim; it is not the endpoint, yet it is a real step toward dignity. This manuscript's explanation of this tension is an exegetical discrimination, hiding the text of neither side.

The hardest passage in this group is the clause on a pregnant woman who is harmed, and it must be read slowly and honestly. The text says: If men strive, and hurt a woman with child, so that her fruit depart from her, and yet no mischief follow: he shall be surely punished, according as the woman's husband will lay upon him; and he shall pay as the judges determine. And if any mischief follow, then thou shalt give life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot, burning for burning, wound for wound, stripe for stripe. The difficulty lies in those words: "no mischief follow." Does this "mischief" mean a graver injury to the mother, or does it mean harm to the child? Through the ages interpretation has divided into two readings here. One reading holds that "mischief" refers chiefly to the mother: the miscarriage itself is dealt with by a fine, and only if the mother is then gravely injured or dies do the equal terms beginning with "life for life" come into force; those who support this reading point out that the series "eye for eye" that follows is all equal description of the body of an adult victim. The other reading holds that "mischief" covers both mother and child: if the child is merely born early and survives, the matter is dealt with by a fine; if either mother or child suffers graver harm or death, the case is to be judged heavily by the principle of equivalence; those who support this reading point out that the text first says the men hurt a woman with child so that her fruit departs, and in the word order both mother and child are in view. Both readings have support from the text and from ancient versions, and scholarship has reached no consensus to this day. This manuscript's position is written here: both readings are presented together, and no conclusion is drawn. For pastors and preachers, before a disputed text like this, the most important thing is not to rush to take a side, but to let hearers see the dispute itself, and honestly acknowledge that we have limits in reading an ancient text. Whoever takes these few verses as a weapon to press the conscience of either side has used the ordinances in the wrong place; what is truly not in dispute in this passage is that the woman and the life in her womb are both seriously counted by the ordinances, and that to harm them in a brawl is by no means a small matter.

Following this passage, let us thoroughly explain its most famous sentence: eye for eye. In the original, 'ayin tachat 'ayin (עַיִן תַּחַת עַיִן, H5869), literally, an eye in place of an eye [5]. In later ages this sentence has often been taken as a byword for cruelty and revenge, but in the context of this chapter its function is exactly the opposite. In a world accustomed to blood revenge — a life for a wound, a whole family for one man — the ordinances set a ceiling: for injuring one eye, at most one eye may be exacted in return; for injuring one tooth, the account may not be raised to a life. In Latin this principle is called lex talionis, the law of like-for-like retaliation; the commonly held scholarly reading is that in the ancient Near East its significance was to limit revenge and to make punishment equal to the harm, not to encourage private vengeance. This manuscript adopts this common reading and identifies it here. It is a measuring rule for judges, not a knife for victims: the judges decide according to it — what is to be compensated is compensated, what is to be paid is paid — and to strike privately is still against the law. Many years later, the Lord Jesus in the Sermon on the Mount said, Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: but I say unto you, That ye resist not evil (Matthew 5:38-39, excerpt). That is the Lord in the New Testament leading His disciples into a higher kingdom ethic — a later teaching, which this manuscript identifies as the New Testament's deepened reading; it does not overturn this chapter's limiting significance in its own time. The two layers must be read separately: this chapter first blocks unlimited revenge; the Lord Jesus then teaches His disciples to lay down even a fitting revenge.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Oxen, Pits, and Compensation — Property to Property, Life to Life (21:28-36)

In the last section of the text the camera moves from persons to oxen and pits. At first glance this seems to fall from great matters into small ones, yet the proportion is all here: cases of life belong to cases of life, property belongs to property, and the two accounts must not be mixed. First, the ox that gores. If an ox shall gore a man or a woman, that they die: then the ox shall be surely stoned, and his flesh shall not be eaten; but the owner of the ox shall be quit. The ox here is executed as if it were a perpetrator, and its flesh may not be eaten, because a human life was ended on its horns — this beast is unclean — while the owner, because he could not foresee it, is counted quit. But the text immediately adds a layer: but if the ox were wont to push with his horn in time past, and it hath been testified to his owner, and he hath not kept him in, but that he hath killed a man or a woman; the ox shall be stoned, and his owner also shall be put to death. "Wont to push in time past," "it hath been testified to his owner," "he hath not kept him in" — these words nail the responsibility onto the owner: you knew the danger long ago, the warning had reached you, yet for convenience you neither penned nor tied the animal, and a man died — this is no longer an accident; it is negligent killing. Here justice has grown eyes: it can distinguish an unforeseeable accident from knowing negligence, and the root of all of today's responsibility for dangerous objects and dangerous places is in this verse. Of course, the ordinances also leave a way to live: if there be laid on him a sum of money, then he shall give for the ransom of his life whatsoever is laid upon him. This sum for ransoming a life, in the original kofer (כֹּפֶר, H3724), a ransom price, a redemption payment [4]. The negligent owner should have paid with his life, yet the victim's family may set a sum, and he ransoms his life with money; this is not that money can buy a life, because the sum is proposed by the other side and bound by the ordinances — it gives a grieving family a road that need not see blood. If the ox gores a son or a daughter, it is dealt with by this same rule; if it gores a manservant or a maidservant, there is a fixed amount instead: the owner shall give unto their master thirty shekels of silver, and the ox shall be stoned. Remember this number, thirty shekels; it will appear again later in the Bible; in this chapter it expresses only one thing: the life of a servant is also counted at a stated price, and is not a death for nothing. This manuscript argues only from this chapter; the later echoes of this number are not developed here, lest we stray from the subject.

Next, pits and ox against ox. If a man shall open a pit, or dig a pit and not cover it, and an ox or an ass falls in, the owner of the pit shall make it good, giving money to the owner; and the dead beast shall be his. To dig a pit and not cover it is negligence in a public space; whose livestock falls in, whoever dug the pit pays — as a matter of course; and the dead beast belongs to the digger of the pit, so that the compensation is clean and leaves no tail. If one man's ox hurts another's, so that it dies, then they shall sell the live ox and divide its price, and the dead ox also they shall divide; or if it be known that the ox was wont to push in time past, and his owner has not kept him in, he shall surely pay ox for ox, and the dead shall be his own. Dividing equally, paying ox for ox — these rulings contain not one harsh word, yet they settle fairly, one by one, the property disputes between neighbours that most easily turn into enmity. Finally, one look forward must be taken, and identified as belonging to the next chapter: chapter 22, verse 1, on stealing an ox — a stolen ox is repaid with five oxen, and a stolen sheep with four sheep (Exodus 22:1, cited in sense). The multiples of compensation form a line of their own in the Book of the Covenant: for unintentional negligence, restitution at value; for two oxen fighting where neither owner is at fault, an equal division of the loss; but for theft, several-fold repayment, because in theft there is malice. The difference of multiples is a difference of the human heart: a fault is reckoned as a fault, and malice is reckoned as malice, and the ordinances will not blur even this point. Only when read this far is this chapter's line of compensation complete; this forward look is a cross-chapter correspondence this manuscript deliberately identifies, not the counting of the next chapter's content into this chapter.

Gathering the whole chapter, the theological anchor is only one sentence, yet it is worth the whole chapter to uphold it: the order of concern in these ordinances is persons before property, and the weak before the strong. Servants come before oxen, maidservants before livestock, the pregnant woman before pits and wells; and when oxen and pits are reached, cases of life belong to cases of life and property to property, every penny reckoned with complete clarity — yet money is never permitted to crush a human life, and a human life is never permitted to be offset against property. Why is the first batch of case law arranged this way? Because the people hearing these words had, three months earlier, still been slaves in Egypt. They knew Egypt's accounts all too well: in Egypt, Hebrew lives were worthless; male infants could be thrown into the Nile; too few bricks meant the whip; one command of Pharaoh could reckon a whole people's children as zero. God brought such a people out, and His first lesson was not how to win battles or how to grow rich; He first taught them how to judge cases: the servant among you is to be released in the seventh year; if your maidservant has one mouthful less to eat or one garment less to wear, it counts as your wronging her — because if you forget where you came from, you will replicate Egypt in your own homes. This reminder Moses later stated as an express text in Deuteronomy: And remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15, excerpt), and the Sabbath commandment rests precisely on this ground. The preaching here cites Deuteronomy as its basis — an exposition with a textual root, as this manuscript identifies it: the first students of the Book of the Covenant were a people who had just taken off slave clothing; the justice they learned carried the taste of memory — a person who remembers that he was once a slave will never build a new Egypt. This is also the weightiest lesson this chapter leaves to the church today: how we treat those under our hand, in our homes, and in our cities who have the least voice is the very evidence of whether we have actually left Egypt. In the home of a person who has left Egypt, there is no Egypt; in the hand of a person who has been released, no chains are made.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter today there was no thunder and no lightning. God lowered His voice, walked into the tents and courtyards, and spoke with the people about servants, about maidservants, about fights, about oxen and pits. We have learned three things. First, justice begins with persons, and begins with the persons of least standing: the servant gains freedom in the seventh year, and the maidservant's food, clothing, and duty of marriage may not be diminished in any one respect. Second, life has a boundary in the ordinances that may not be crossed: for deliberate murder, even the altar gives no shelter; stealing a man is the same crime as killing; yet revenge has a ceiling — eye for eye is a measuring rule that blocks unlimited revenge, not a knife for striking. Third, faults and property must be reckoned fairly: an owner whose ox was wont to push must bear responsibility, one who digs a pit and does not cover it must compensate, theft must be repaid several-fold — fault as fault, and malice as malice. The root of all this is that old sentence: you were bondmen in Egypt. A person who has left Egypt must not build Egypt again. May our generation, at home, in the workplace, and in the church, be people who treat persons as persons.

Let us pray together: Lord, God of justice, we thank You. Your ordinances reach down to a single eye, a single tooth, a single pit, a single ox, because in Your eyes no matter is too small to deserve fairness, and no person too lowly to deserve protection. Forgive our sin of casually wronging people: we have wronged those who work under our hand, wronged the quietest person in the home, and in our hearts have weighed human life against profit by the pound. Root Egypt out of our hearts entirely; teach us to remember where our release came from, to protect the weak first in treating people, to distinguish fault from malice first in judging matters, and to live out a justice that lands in daily life. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "If thou buy an Hebrew servant, six years he shall serve: and in the seventh he shall go out free for nothing." (Exodus 21:2, excerpt, KJV) "And if he smite out his manservant's tooth, or his maidservant's tooth; he shall let him go free for his tooth's sake." (Exodus 21:27, excerpt, KJV)

Do not be put off by how ordinary the Book of the Covenant sounds. Right after the Ten Commandments, the first case laws are not about sacrifices; they are about whether a servant may go home in the seventh year, and whether a maidservant's food and clothing have been quietly cut. In God's eyes justice is never a lofty word floating above life; it lands on small things: whose wages were withheld, whose dignity was knocked out like a single tooth — all of it counts. And the hearers themselves had been slaves only three months before. God was reminding every newly freed person: if you turn around and wrong someone weaker than you, you have carried Egypt back into your own house. Ask quietly today: is there someone under your hand, in your home, beside you — the one with the least voice — waiting for you to treat him as a person? Do one small thing first: pay what is owed, take back the cutting word, give the respect that is due. And think of yourself as well: how many times have you fallen into God's forbearance, been freely forgiven, freely healed, and lifted up by Him again and again? A person who has received such grace should be the last to haggle over another's small fault, or to show no mercy toward another's weakness. Justice landing in daily life also means handing on, unchanged, to the next person the mercy you yourself have received. A house that has left Egypt should no longer echo with the sound of a whip.

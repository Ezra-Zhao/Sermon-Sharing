# The Blood Is Its Life — Brought to the Door of the Tabernacle

## Abstract

Leviticus 17 opens the Holiness Code. It takes an everyday act — slaughtering an ox or a sheep — and solemnly relocates it to the door of the tabernacle: blood must not be shed at will, and it must never be eaten, for the blood is the life of the creature; God has given the blood to be placed on the altar to make atonement for human life. The chapter teaches reverence for life, singleness in worship, and a sanctified common life worked out in the most ordinary matters of food and labour.

## I. 00:00-08:00 The Opening of the Holiness Code: Holiness Begins at the Edge of the Camp

(Fact) Leviticus falls broadly into two parts: chapters 1-16 treat priests, sacrifices, and cleanness, while chapter 17 opens what scholars commonly call the Holiness Code (siglum H, roughly Leviticus 17-26), whose signature call is that Israel shall be holy, for the LORD their God is holy (Lev 19:2). Chapter 17 stands exactly at the seam: it looks back to sacrifice and blood, and forward to a whole people's holy life. Wenham notes that the audience here is no longer the priests alone, but Aaron, his sons, and all the children of Israel (see the summary of 17:2 below); from this chapter onward, holiness is not a professional concern inside the sanctuary only, but the whole congregation's daily lesson.

(Fact) The chapter opens with the book's recurring revelation formula, summarized as follows: the LORD speaks to Moses and charges him to address Aaron, Aaron's sons, and all the Israelites, declaring this to be the thing which the LORD has commanded (17:1-2, Summary; not quoted verbatim). Note the threefold audience: Aaron the high priest, his sons who succeed to the priesthood, and the entire people. The rules of slaughter must be heard both by those who officiate and by those who bring the animals, because what this chapter corrects is precisely the people's everyday habit of slaughtering.

(Application) This chapter sets the starting point of holiness very low and very near: not behind the veil of the Holy of Holies, but on the open ground of the camp, before a meal. How a person treats an animal's blood, and where he chooses to slaughter, are in God's eyes questions of worship. We habitually split life in two: worship and prayer count as spiritual; eating, working, and trading count as ordinary. Leviticus 17 recognizes no such dividing line. It says that even slaughter, bloody work as it is, must be brought before God. Holiness is not withdrawal from life; it is bringing every part of life before God.

(Fact) The chapter's structure is clear and falls into four sections: first, cattle and flock must be brought to the door of the tabernacle (17:3-7); second, the rule is extended to every burnt offering and sacrifice, sojourners included (17:8-9, Summary); third, the prohibition of eating blood together with the blood's atoning significance (17:10-12, of which vv. 10 and 12 are given in Summary and v. 11 in verbatim KJV quotation); fourth, the blood of game must be poured out and covered, and the cleanness rule for eating what died of itself (17:13-16, of which vv. 13 and 16 are given in Summary and vv. 14-15 in verbatim quotation). The chapter's verbs keep returning to the same small set: bring, sprinkle, burn, cut off, be clean. The sermon below follows this structure.

(Application) A word of reading honesty first: some of these rules bear the stamp of the wilderness camp — all Israel lived in one encampment, the tabernacle stood at its centre, and leading an ox to its door was half a day's walk at most. Later, settled in Canaan's scattered towns, Deuteronomy 12 makes a measured relaxation for those living far away (an intertextual observation; the comparison is treated below). We do not read this chapter today in order to rebuild a wilderness slaughter system literally, but to hear the heart inside the system: life belongs to God, blood must not be trifled with, and worship may not choose its own place and manner. That heart crosses from camp to city and still speaks to us.

## II. 08:00-24:00 Brought to the Door of the Tabernacle: Slaughter, Peace Offerings, and No More Sacrifices to Devils

(Fact) The ordinance begins with a concrete list: "What man soever there be of the house of Israel, that killeth an ox, or lamb, or goat, in the camp, or that killeth it out of the camp," (17:3, KJV) "And bringeth it not unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, to offer an offering unto the Lord before the tabernacle of the Lord; blood shall be imputed unto that man; he hath shed blood; and that man shall be cut off from among his people:" (17:4, KJV). The three animals named — ox, lamb, goat — are exactly the herd and flock animals used in sacrifices such as the peace offerings (cf. Lev 3). The verb rendered "killeth" is shachat (Strong's H7819), to slaughter or slay, used throughout Leviticus chiefly for the ritual slaughter of sacrificial animals (BDB, s.v.). The text does not say that this man worshipped an idol or did any notorious evil; he merely slaughtered an ox of his own. The whole problem is one of place: slaughter not performed at the door of the tabernacle is reckoned as bloodshed, and the man is to be cut off. "Cut off" renders the karet penalty (root karat, H3772, in its passive form); scholars discuss its precise mode of execution, but agree that it means thorough separation from the covenant people.

(Fact) Why does private slaughter count as bloodshed? Verse 4 first pronounces the verdict and then explains it: blood shall be imputed to that man; he hath shed blood. In this chapter's legal logic, the blood of herd animals is not something the slaughterer may dispose of at his own discretion; blood shed without the altar's procedure is reckoned as blood wrongfully shed. (Application) This is a foreign legal instinct to modern readers: we assume our livestock is private property, to dispose of as we please. The biblical worldview differs: sovereignty over life rests with God, and human slaughter is never an absolute right but a delegated, bounded permission. The boundary stands at the door of the tabernacle.

(Fact) The ordinance then states its positive purpose: "To the end that the children of Israel may bring their sacrifices, which they offer in the open field, even that they may bring them unto the Lord, unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, unto the priest, and offer them for peace offerings unto the Lord." (17:5, KJV). Centralization, in other words, gathers the people's scattered field-sacrifices into peace offerings at the tabernacle. The peace offering (shelamim, H8002) is the sacrifice whose meat the offerer eats together with family and priests (Lev 7:11-18, Summary); slaughter is thus transformed from a private transaction into a shared feast before God. Then comes the priest's office: "And the priest shall sprinkle the blood upon the altar of the Lord at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, and burn the fat for a sweet savour unto the Lord." (17:6, KJV). The blood goes to the altar, the fat is burned for the LORD, the meat is eaten by the people — one animal, three portions, and God first.

(Fact) The section climaxes in verse 7: "And they shall no more offer their sacrifices unto devils, after whom they have gone a whoring. This shall be a statute for ever unto them throughout their generations." (17:7, KJV). The KJV's "devils" renders se'irim (H8163), the plural of sa'ir, a he-goat or hairy one — the Chinese Union Version translates the word as "demons" and adds a parenthesis noting that the Hebrew is literally "he-goats." What se'irim denotes is debated; three main views are set side by side here without adjudication: first, goat-shaped deities worshipped in Egyptian or Canaanite folk religion, to whom people offered sacrifices in the fields; second, demonic spirits of the wilderness (in the prophets se'irim appear alongside the wild creatures of ruined places, e.g. Isa 13:21; 34:14 — an intertextual observation); third, a generic term for idols of every kind, the goat being simply the most familiar idol-image of the day. On any of the three readings, the verdict is the same: sacrifice offered to them is called going a whoring, the covenant-marriage metaphor for Israel's unfaithfulness to the LORD. The verse also invites comparison with the goat for Azazel in Leviticus 16, sent into the wilderness bearing the people's sins (Lev 16:8-10, Summary): the same goat imagery — one goat dispatched to the wilderness, one class of goat-beings strictly forbidden as objects of sacrifice. Whether the two chapters refer to each other directly the text does not say; this sermon records the structural intertext only, and draws no conclusion.

(Fact — Dispute Presented, Not Settled) The scope of centralization must likewise be stated honestly. One reading holds that in the wilderness period every slaughter of herd or flock animals had to pass through the tabernacle — slaughter was sacrifice, and eating meat meant eating a peace offering. Another reading holds that the ordinance governs only slaughter intended as sacrifice, not ordinary slaughter for food, pointing to the overlap between the list in 17:3 and the sacrificial lists, and to the purpose clause of 17:5, which speaks only of "their sacrifices." Both readings have scholarly defenders (Milgrom inclines to the former and argues the wilderness setting in detail; Hartley discusses the scope question with careful balance). The comparison with Deuteronomy 12 (an intertextual observation) supports the situational reading: Deuteronomy 12 expressly permits those far from the central sanctuary to slaughter and eat meat in their own towns, forbidding only the blood (Deut 12:15-16, 20-25, Summary). Centralization thus appears as an arrangement for the wilderness camp; its abiding core is the blood prohibition and the singleness of worship, not the slaughter site itself.

(Application) This section holds up two mirrors. The first is singleness in worship. A field altar is convenient, near, and to one's own taste; the door of the tabernacle means a walk, a queue, and surrendering the animal into a priest's hands. The temptation of faith rarely arrives as open apostasy; it arrives as a convenient altar of one's own — worship remodelled to suit my schedule, my preferences, my control. Leviticus 17 says the first lesson of sacrifice is surrendering the choice of place: go where God has appointed, in the manner God has appointed, to meet the God who wills to be met. The second mirror is the shared table. The meat of the peace offering was eaten in company; centralization's unlooked-for blessing is that slaughter becomes communion instead of solitary consumption. The church's love feasts and fellowship tables today are echoes of that picture: food brought before God is no longer mere fuel, but fellowship.

## III. 24:00-42:00 The Blood Is Its Life: The Prohibition of Blood and the Gift upon the Altar

(Fact) The chapter's second major section first extends the rule to the sojourner, summarized as follows: any Israelite, or any stranger sojourning among them, who offers a burnt offering or sacrifice and does not bring it to the door of the tabernacle to offer it to the LORD shall be cut off from among his people (17:8-9, Summary). There follows the chapter's weightiest prohibition, also summarized here: whatever man of the house of Israel, or of the strangers sojourning among them, eats any manner of blood, the LORD will set His face against that soul that eats blood and will cut him off from among his people (17:10, Summary). The expression "set my face" shows that the executor of this prohibition is not a human court but God Himself. Eating blood, in this chapter, is no matter of dietary taste; it is a direct offence against the Lord of life.

(Fact) The ground of the prohibition is the chapter's theme sentence: "For the life of the flesh is in the blood: and I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls: for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul." (17:11, KJV). Note carefully how the two key verses differ. Verse 11 says that the life of the flesh is in the blood, and that the blood makes atonement because the life is in it; verse 14, quoted verbatim below, states the sharper equation — the blood is the life. The sermon's title, "The Blood Is Its Life," rests textually on verse 14; verse 11 is the ground where that theology is unfolded. The two verses must not be conflated, and no wording may be spliced from one into the other.

(Fact — Hebrew) The Hebrew of verse 11 opens ki nefesh habbasar baddam hi', literally: "For the life (nefesh) of the flesh is in the blood." Nefesh (H5315) in the Old Testament denotes life, the living person, the self — it can also denote the throat and appetite — and its centre of meaning is not a soul opposed to the body but the whole of a life being lived (BDB and HALOT, s.v. nefesh, both list life and living being among the senses). Basar is flesh; dam (H1818) is blood. The subject of the clause is nefesh, and it is predicated to be in the blood (baddam, the preposition be with the article and dam). This is therefore not a physiological claim — the ancients knew nothing of circulation — but a claim of belonging: an animal's life is carried and represented by its blood, and where the blood is drained, life ends, as anyone can verify from daily experience. The pivot of the verse follows at once: wa'ani netattiv lakhem 'al-hammizbeach, "And I — I have given it to you upon the altar." The giver and subject is "I," the LORD; the object given is the blood; the recipients are "you"; the place is the altar; and the purpose infinitive is lekhapper, "to make atonement," from kipper (H3722, Piel stem), whose object-phrase here is 'al-nafshotekhem, "for your souls/lives" (nefesh in the plural). The verse closes, ki-haddam hu' bannefesh yekhapper: "for it is the blood that maketh an atonement" — bannefesh, the preposition be with hannefesh, may be read instrumentally or causally, "by means of the life" or "because of the life"; the KJV renders the sense "by reason of the life" in its parallel phrasing of the verse's logic (cf. the ASV), and the KJV text itself reads "for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul."

(Fact — Hebrew) The agency and objects of this verse must be stated exactly; this is where sermons most often go astray. First, the giver of atonement is God: the blood is not a gift humans invented to placate God, but a gift God first gave and assigned a use; the human part is only to use it as given. Second, the medium of atonement is the life carried in the blood: it is not the chemistry of blood that works, but the presentation of a whole living creature's life, represented by its blood. Third, the beneficiaries are human nefesh-lives: the animal's life covers the human life. The related noun kofer (H3724), a ransom or price of a life (e.g. Exod 30:12, Summary), shares the same root and supplies lexical background; but the precise mechanism of kipper in Leviticus is exactly the disputed question set out next. One further caution: a phrase sometimes heard in sermons, on the pattern of zavach tamim, does not occur in this chapter. The chapter's sacrificial vocabulary is zebach (H2077, a sacrifice, the victim of a peace offering) with the verbs shachat and zavach (H2076, to slaughter/sacrifice); this sermon uses only words the text actually supplies.

(Fact — Dispute Presented, Not Settled) How does blood make atonement? Two representative explanations are set side by side, and this sermon reaches no verdict. The traditional substitutionary account holds that the wages of sin is death; the victim dies in the offerer's place, its life answering for his life, and the blood is the ransom price. This reading stresses the nefesh-for-nefesh correspondence, and verse 11 — the blood atones because the life is in it — reads naturally in its favour; the church's preaching has largely followed this line. Milgrom's sanctuary-purgation account holds instead that in Leviticus the direct object of kipper is often the sanctuary and its furniture rather than the person (see the wording of the Day of Atonement in Leviticus 16, Summary): sin and impurity pollute the sanctuary, and blood, applied to the altar and the sanctuary as a ritual purgative, erases the pollution so that God may continue to dwell among His people. On this account, "to make an atonement for your souls" names the effect, while the blood's immediate point of application is the sanctuary. Wenham and Hartley each weigh the two accounts and propose their own balances; readers may consult the bibliography. This sermon's position goes no further than this: both accounts agree that the blood's efficacy rests on God's giving and appointment, and both agree its effect concerns the covering of human life. The question of mechanism belongs to the commentaries and is not preached from the pulpit as settled doctrine.

(Fact) Verse 12 gathers the prohibition into one summary sentence: therefore the LORD said to the children of Israel that no soul among them, and no stranger sojourning among them, shall eat blood (17:12, Summary). Note the prohibition's full coverage: it admits no sacred-secular exception. Blood on the altar belongs to God; the blood of game belongs to the dust (next section); at the table, not one drop may enter the mouth. Genesis 9 had already laid down a like prohibition to Noah and his descendants at the covenant (Gen 9:4, Summary); Leviticus here writes the universal principle into Israel's statute law and supplies the reason given nowhere else: the blood's destination is the altar, not the mouth.

(Application) The most counter-intuitive note in this section is the source of atonement. Our religious instinct assumes that humans offer up their best and thereby purchase God's favour. Leviticus 17 reverses the order: the atoning blood on the altar is first God's "I have given it to you," and only then the people's offering. Every material of redemption, in other words, is prepared by God Himself; what humans offer is only ever the return, in God's appointed manner, of what God has given. This is the grammar of grace running through the whole Bible: in Genesis 22 God provides the ram in Isaac's place (Summary); in the New Testament the grammar reaches its summit — God gives the life of His Son to make atonement for the world (an applicational New Testament echo, not a claim about this chapter's own wording). The human duty before salvation is not invention but reception; not bargaining, but gratitude and obedience.

(Application) The blood prohibition also teaches an ethic of reverence for life. Most of us eat meat without ever seeing blood: supermarket portions are clean and trimmed, and slaughter is hidden away in distant plants. The Israelite of this chapter had to face blood with his own hands — sprinkled on the altar, or poured out and covered with dust, but in any case handled solemnly, never swallowed carelessly. The prohibition trains a way of seeing: meat is never a self-evident entitlement; behind every portion stands the ending of a life, and life belongs to God. Carried into today, this means grace at table is no polite formality but an acknowledgment that nothing on my plate is my due; it means restraint and gratitude toward food and toward the created order. We need not — and should not — rebuild the ancient dietary rules literally (the apostolic council treats the blood question on its own terms in Acts 15, Summary); but the reverence for life which the rule was given to teach remains the lesson it leaves to readers of every age.

## IV. 42:00-56:00 The Blood of Game Returned to the Dust, and the Meat That Died of Itself

(Fact) The fourth section treats blood outside the sanctuary. Verse 13 is summarized as follows: any Israelite, or any stranger sojourning among them, who hunts and catches a beast or bird that may be eaten must pour out its blood and cover it with dust (17:13, Summary). Read this beside 17:3-6: domestic animals are slaughtered at the door of the tabernacle and their blood is sprinkled on the altar; wild animals are taken in open country and cannot be brought to the tabernacle, so their blood is poured on the ground and covered with earth. The handling differs; the principle is one: blood is not to be eaten, nor left lying in disregard; it must be returned with reverence — on the altar, to God; on the ground, to the dust. The picture answers to Genesis, where after Cain killed Abel the blood cries to God from the ground (Gen 4:10, Summary): in the Bible's imagination, the earth is the witness and bearer of blood.

(Fact) The reason is stated verbatim in verse 14: "For it is the life of all flesh; the blood of it is for the life thereof: therefore I said unto the children of Israel, Ye shall eat the blood of no manner of flesh: for the life of all flesh is the blood thereof: whosoever eateth it shall be cut off." (17:14, KJV). The Chinese title of this sermon is drawn from the corresponding sentence of the Chinese Union Version at this verse, which equates the blood and the life directly. The Hebrew, nefesh kol-basar damo hu', reads literally: "The life of all flesh — its blood is it." Here dam and nefesh stand as subject and predicate to one another, complementing verse 11: verse 11 speaks of location (the life is in the blood), verse 14 of identity (the blood is its life). A preacher must keep the verses' wordings distinct — never crediting verse 14's equation to verse 11, and never splicing the two into a homemade quotation; this sermon quotes them separately for exactly that reason.

(Fact) The last two verses treat a grey area: animals that died of themselves or were torn by beasts. "And every soul that eateth that which died of itself, or that which was torn with beasts, whether it be one of your own country, or a stranger, he shall both wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the even: then shall he be clean." (17:15, KJV). Verse 16 is summarized as follows: if he does not wash his clothes nor bathe his flesh, he shall bear his iniquity (17:16, Summary). Note the graded scale of sanctions: eating blood is a cutting-off offence; eating what died of itself brings only uncleanness until evening, with washing of clothes and body, and only refusal to wash leaves the man bearing his iniquity. Why the difference? (Fact) An animal that died of itself was never slaughtered and drained, so its blood remains in the flesh, and eating or handling it conveys the uncleanness associated with death; yet the eater has not deliberately set himself against the blood prohibition's intent — poverty and reluctance to waste food are the usual motives — and so the law answers with a rite of cleansing rather than a penalty. (Application) The law's proportion is precisely here: it leaves room for inadvertent fault and hard circumstance, without cancelling the requirement of cleanness. Grace and rule, in this small ordinance, walk side by side.

(Fact) One more repeated subject deserves attention: the sojourner. Verses 8-16 write the stranger sojourning among them into the same ordinances at every step. The blood prohibition and the cleanness rules are not Israel's national private property; they are the shared lesson of everyone who lives in the land under this covenant's order. (Application) For the church today this is a mirror: a community's ethical witness is never conducted behind closed doors for its own members only. How a community treats life, handles death, and disciplines itself in eating and working is watched by those who sojourn among us. An ordinance's witness often speaks louder than words.

(Fact) The cleansing procedure of verse 15 has three elements, and they form the standard combination of Levitical cleanness law (cf. the similar formulas in Leviticus 11 and 15, Summary): washing the clothes, bathing the body in water, and uncleanness until the evening. Clothes and body are washed together, showing that uncleanness is understood as a contagious, transferable state; the evening sets the uncleanness a term that can be awaited and completed — after sunset, cleanness arrives, and the person may return to the congregation's table and worship. (Application) This small ordinance therefore hides a tender theology: uncleanness is not a life sentence but a timed separation; washing is not theatre but the road back. The law writes the return into daily actions — a basin of water, a washed garment, an evening of waiting — so that the one who has erred knows the road is always there, and when the term is complete he may come back. Set against the cutting off decreed for eating blood, the contrast is deliberate: God is severe toward deliberate contempt for the prohibition of life, but toward the one unintentionally defiled by death He has prepared the steps of washing and waiting.

(Application) Taken together, the four sections draw a complete map of blood: the blood of herd and flock goes up to the altar; the blood of game returns to the dust; the blood of what died of itself renders a person temporarily unclean, to be washed and to wait; and human blood — the life of another person — must never be shed at all, for the human being is made in God's image (Gen 9:6, Summary). No blood of any kind may be handled carelessly. At the centre of the map stands the gift of verse 11: "I have given it to you upon the altar." In Scripture blood is never mere biological material; it is the sign of life, the medium of God's meeting with humanity, and the training ground of reverence. Whoever understands this map understands why the New Testament writers, speaking of the blood of the cross, write with such solemnity and gratitude — not rhetorical exaggeration, but the ancient grammar of blood and life, learned from Leviticus onward, sounding in their hearts (Application).

## V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: Bring Everything to His Door

(Application) Brothers and sisters, Leviticus 17 leaves us three sentences to carry home. The first: bring it to the door of the tabernacle. Your work, your meals, your dealings, the great and small matters of your household — which of them has not yet been brought before God? Privately managed slaughter is reckoned in this chapter as bloodshed; likewise, a life decided privately, unexamined by prayer and by God's word, often sheds blood that should never be shed — the blood of relationships, of conscience, of time. Choose one thing today that you have always decided alone, and bring it to the door of the tabernacle: pray aloud, carry it into the priestly fellowship of the congregation, and offer it to God together with others.

The second sentence: the blood is its life. Reverence for life begins with revering the life of every person beside you — your spouse's, your children's, your colleagues', the stranger's on the road. The ancient prohibition of eating blood becomes, today, the ethic of never despising any life: do not devour people with words, do not drain another's hope with indifference. For we know that in God's eyes no life is material to be handled carelessly.

The third sentence: I have given it to you upon the altar. Salvation is gift from first to last. The blood on the altar was God's gift; the precious blood of the cross is God's gift above all — He gave the life of His Son to make atonement for our lives. We who receive have nothing to boast of, except to bring ourselves, too, before Him and offer ourselves as living sacrifices. May we, from today, bring everything to the door of the tabernacle, and live daily under the covering of the blood that was given. Amen.

## Endnotes

1. The Holiness Code (H): scholars commonly designate Leviticus 17-26 (or through 27) as the Holiness Code, the name deriving from the holiness call of 19:2; chapter 17 stands first and is transitional in character, joining sacrificial ordinance to the ethics of the whole people. See Wenham, The Book of Leviticus, NICOT, 1979, introduction to this chapter.
2. Shachat (H7819): BDB lists the senses slaughter, slay; in Levitical sacrificial contexts it denotes the ritual slaughter of a victim specifically, as distinct from ordinary words for killing (e.g. harag).
3. Cutting off (karet): formally the Niphal of karat (wenikhretah and cognate forms); its agent and mode (divine punishment, loss of posterity, expulsion from the congregation, etc.) are variously discussed, the common denominator being thorough separation from the covenant community. See Milgrom, Leviticus 17-22, Anchor Bible 3A, 2000, note on 17:4.
4. Peace offering, shelamim (H8002): the sacrifice of Leviticus 3 and 7:11-18 whose meat the offerer shares in eating; 17:5 recasts field slaughter as a peace offering, on which scholars base their account of centralization as a feast-centred institution.
5. Se'irim (H8163): plural of sa'ir; in Isa 13:21 and 34:14 the word appears alongside wilderness creatures. For the three views presented here (goat-shaped deities; wilderness demons; a generic term for idols), see the survey in Hartley, Leviticus, WBC 4, 1992, note on 17:7. The Chinese Union Version's rendering "demons," with its parenthesis "literally, he-goats," reflects the same tension.
6. The Azazel connection: Leviticus 16 and Leviticus 17 share the goat (sa'ir, and the 'aza'zel setting): one goat is sent to the wilderness, one class of goat-beings is forbidden as a sacrificial recipient. This sermon claims a structural-intertextual observation only; whether the chapters refer to each other is not a scholarly consensus.
7. Centralization and Deuteronomy 12: Deut 12:15-16, 20-25 permits those far from the central sanctuary to slaughter and eat meat in their towns, forbidding only the blood — an intertextual observation supporting the reading that centralization is a wilderness-situational arrangement whose abiding core is the blood prohibition. Milgrom defends the wilderness "slaughter is sacrifice" reading; Hartley takes a more cautious, balanced position on the question of scope.
8. Nefesh (H5315): both BDB and HALOT list a cluster of senses — life, living being, person, appetite; in Leviticus 17 the word should be read as the whole of a life being lived, not through a Greek soul-body dualism.
9. The syntax of 17:11: ki nefesh habbasar baddam hi' is a nominal clause (subject nefesh, the be-prepositional phrase as predicate); in lekhapper 'al-nafshotekhem, kipper (H3722) is a Piel infinitive and 'al means "on behalf of"; the be of bannefesh at the verse's end may be instrumental or causal (KJV's logic: it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul).
10. The mechanism of kipper, disputed: the substitutionary account stresses life answering for life; Milgrom's sanctuary-purgation account stresses blood's erasure of sanctuary pollution (his analysis of Leviticus 16 is the key argument). For both positions side by side, see Milgrom, op. cit., the long note on 17:11, and Wenham's mediating discussion of the verse in op. cit.
11. Kofer (H3724, ransom): the per-person "ransom for his life" silver of Exod 30:12 uses this word, from the same root as kipper; offered here as lexical background only, not as proof that the mechanism of 17:11 must be a ransom model.
12. What died of itself (nevelah) and what was torn (terefah): see the related prohibitions in Exod 22:31, Lev 22:8, and Ezek 44:31 (Summary). That 17:15-16 answers with temporary uncleanness plus washing, in plain contrast to the cutting off decreed for eating blood, displays the graduated scale of sanctions within the law itself.

## Devotional Excerpt

"For the life of the flesh is in the blood: and I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls: for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul." (Lev 17:11, KJV)

Reading this verse early in the morning, my heart is caught by four words: I have given it. The atoning blood on the altar, it turns out, is not a present human beings scraped together to offer to God; it is a grace God first gave, and then commanded us to use. We so often fret over what we can offer: our time is not whole enough, our gifts are not large enough, our hearts are not warm enough. Leviticus 17 quietly reverses the order: every material of salvation comes from His providing; our part is to take what we have received, bring it to His door, and return it in the manner He has appointed.

Today, practise once what it means to bring something to the door of the tabernacle. Choose one small matter you have always decided on your own — where a sum of money goes, the tone of a conversation, an arrangement at the dinner table — and stop first to pray, placing it plainly before Him before you act. Then give thanks properly at the table: behind this food stands the ending of a life, and my own life lives under the covering of another life that was given. Reverence begins with small, concrete things like these.

(End of the devotional excerpt)

## Summary (about 100 words)

Jacob favoured Joseph and gave him a coat of many colours, and he listened to his dreams of sheaves and of the sun, moon, and stars bowing down to him, so his brothers' hatred grew deeper and deeper. Joseph was sent by his father to Shechem to visit them, but his brothers threw him into a dry pit and sold him to a caravan for twenty pieces of silver, then dipped the coat in goat's blood to deceive their father. Human malice sent the young man into a deep pit, but God's hand caught the dream at the bottom of the pit.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter the Bible recorded Esau's descendants, chiefs, and kings all in one breath, a genealogy written quickly and neatly. In this chapter the camera suddenly slows down and returns to Jacob's house, to the youngest generation. There is no list of kings and no great scene; the story begins with a garment — a coat of many colours that a father made with his own hands for his young son.

How much can one garment matter? In our homes a garment is only for warmth and neatness. But in Jacob's house this coat set the hearts of ten brothers on fire. It first drew out two dreams, then an empty pit, and finally a caravan that carried a seventeen-year-old young man away to Egypt. Only when we look back many years later do we understand: the source of all the glory of Joseph's story is not on the throne of Egypt; its source is in this chapter today — in a father's favouritism, in his brothers' hatred, and in that pit with no water in it.

On the surface this chapter tells of strife between brothers; at its core it tells two things. First, how people turn jealousy step by step into a murderous intent: from favouritism, to telling tales, to dreams that draw hatred, to seeing him afar off and plotting to kill — the chain is linked ring by ring. Second, how God takes over in the place people cannot see: people threw Joseph into a pit, but God used that pit to send him to Egypt. Human malice is real, and God's hand is also real, and God's hand is longer.

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several key passages:

"Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age: and he made him a coat of many colours. And when his brethren saw that their father loved him more than all his brethren, they hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him." (Genesis 37:3-4, excerpt, KJV)

"And Joseph dreamed a dream, and he told it his brethren: and they hated him yet the more." (Genesis 37:5, excerpt, KJV)

"And his brethren said to him, Shalt thou indeed reign over us? or shalt thou indeed have dominion over us? And they hated him yet the more for his dreams, and for his words." (Genesis 37:8, excerpt, KJV)

"And when they saw him afar off, even before he came near unto them, they conspired against him to slay him. And they said one to another, Behold, this dreamer cometh. Come now therefore, and let us slay him, and cast him into some pit, and we will say, Some evil beast hath devoured him: and we shall see what will become of his dreams." (Genesis 37:18-20, excerpt, KJV)

"And they took him, and cast him into a pit: and the pit was empty, there was no water in it." (Genesis 37:24, excerpt, KJV)

"And Judah said unto his brethren, What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood? Come, and let us sell him to the Ishmeelites, and let not our hand be upon him; for he is our brother and our flesh." (Genesis 37:26-27, excerpt, KJV)

"And the Midianites sold him into Egypt unto Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh's, and captain of the guard." (Genesis 37:36, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, today we are going to look at a coat of many colours, and also at a deep pit. First enlighten our own homes and our own hearts: where there is favouritism, there is a wound; where there is jealousy, the pit is not far away. And comfort those who are in a pit right now, and let them know that a pit is not the end of the story, and that Your hand is at the bottom of the pit as well. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: A House Set Alight by a Coat — Favouritism, Tale-Bearing, and Two Dreams (37:1-11)

The chapter opens very calmly: "And Jacob dwelt in the land wherein his father was a stranger, in the land of Canaan." (Genesis 37:1, excerpt, KJV) Then the Bible says these are the generations of Jacob, and says: "Joseph, being seventeen years old, was feeding the flock with his brethren; and the lad was with the sons of Bilhah, and with the sons of Zilpah, his father's wives." Seventeen years old is exactly the age of being half grown, ranked near the youngest among his brothers, with ten grown shepherds above him. Reading further, an important sentence comes: "and Joseph brought unto his father their evil report." (Genesis 37:2, excerpt, KJV)

We must not read this sentence too quickly, nor too heavily. Joseph brought his brothers' evil report to his father — whether it was a dutiful report or a young man's tale-bearing, the Bible does not pass judgment. We only know the result: their father trusted this young son even more, and his brothers isolated him even more. In a home, if only one child can speak before the father, the other children will slowly feel that whatever they say does not count. Joseph may not have intended to harm anyone; he was only seventeen, what did he know? But in a home marked by favouritism, the favoured child often unknowingly becomes another measuring rod: he stands beside the father, his brothers stand opposite him, and the home is divided into camps first in the heart.

The evidence of the camps is that coat. "Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age: and he made him a coat of many colours." (Genesis 37:3, excerpt, KJV) The coat of many colours, in the original ketonet passim (כְּתֹנֶת פַּסִּים, H3801 plus H6446) [1]; the exact sense of passim has been disputed since ancient times, and tradition mostly explains it as a long robe reaching to the palms and the soles — a robe whose sleeves reach the hands and whose hem reaches the feet. Such a garment is not a working garment. Shepherds wear short clothes for convenience; a person in a long robe is one who does not have to go down into the field or drive the sheep, one who is displayed at home to be cherished and honoured. In other words, Jacob did not casually add a winter garment for his son; he made "this child is not like you" into a garment and put it on Joseph, to sway before his brothers' eyes every day.

His brothers' reaction is given by the Bible in one word: they hated him. "And when his brethren saw that their father loved him more than all his brethren, they hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him." (Genesis 37:4, excerpt, KJV) "Hated," in the original sane' (שָׂנֵא, H8130) [5], simply to hate, to detest. In the previous chapter, when we spoke of Leah, we also met this word: the LORD saw that Leah was hated, and it used this very word. In Leah's generation, favouritism wounded a wife; in this generation, favouritism wounds sons. Jacob himself was a man who grew up inside favouritism — his father Isaac loved Esau, his mother Rebekah loved Jacob, and the two brothers contended all their lives; he himself loved Rachel more than Leah and made Leah weep for half her life. This man had eaten the full bitterness of favouritism, yet he put favouritism with his own hands onto his young son. Brothers and sisters, here is the most frightening place about wounds: it is often not that a person does not know how much favouritism hurts; it is that a person has hurt, wept, and fled, and then turns around and makes the same coat for the next generation. If a wound is not dealt with before God, it will change its garment and be passed down.

Do not miss another detail: "and could not speak peaceably unto him." "Peaceably" is in the original peace, shalom. His brothers did not refuse to speak to Joseph at all — driving sheep, eating, dividing work, words still had to be spoken; they would not speak one word of peace to him, not one kindly word. When a home reaches the point where it will not give even one good word, it is not far from laying on hands. Hatred spoils first in the mouth, and afterwards in the hands. The division of many families does not begin on the day a door is slammed; it begins on the day people stop speaking properly to one another.

At just this time, Joseph dreamed. "And Joseph dreamed a dream, and he told it his brethren: and they hated him yet the more." (Genesis 37:5, excerpt, KJV) Dream, in the original chalom (חֲלוֹם, H2472) [2]. The first dream was of binding sheaves: "For, behold, we were binding sheaves in the field, and, lo, my sheaf arose, and also stood upright; and, behold, your sheaves stood round about, and made obeisance to my sheaf." Everyone who works in a field understands this picture: sheaves bowing to a sheaf is people bowing to a person. His brothers understood at once and answered him at once: "Shalt thou indeed reign over us? or shalt thou indeed have dominion over us?"

After some days Joseph dreamed again, and this time he told his father as well: "the sun and the moon and the eleven stars made obeisance to me." (Genesis 37:9, excerpt, KJV) The sun, the moon, and eleven stars — anyone with eyes can count whom they mean: his father, his mother, and his eleven brethren. This time even Jacob rebuked him: "What is this dream that thou hast dreamed? Shall I and thy mother and thy brethren indeed come to bow down ourselves to thee to the earth?" (Genesis 37:10, excerpt, KJV) His father rebuked him and his brothers envied him. But at the end of this passage the Bible leaves one very quiet sentence: "but his father observed the saying." (Genesis 37:11, excerpt, KJV) Rebuke was rebuke, but Jacob did not erase the dream with one stroke. A man who has dreamed himself knows in his heart: the dream at Bethel and the dreams in Laban's house were all real. This old father restrained him with his mouth, but remembered in his heart — this young son's dream was perhaps no empty dream.

Here we must say a fair word for Joseph, and also leave a reminder for young people. The dreams were given by God, not invented by Joseph himself; their later fulfilment also proves the dreams were real. The fault was not in dreaming; it was in the timing and measure of the telling: the hatred at home was already full, and he still carried the dreams out whole before his brothers, and told them twice in succession. A young person with a vision most easily makes this mistake — thinking that because the truth is in his hand, he need not look at people's faces or wait for the right hour. But the vision is given by God, and the wisdom to tell the vision must be learned by the person himself before God. Rashness adds trouble to a vision, and silence sometimes protects it. Of course, we must not turn around and blame the victim: that his brothers hated him and later harmed him is the brothers' sin, not the fault of those two dreams. The dreams only shone more clearly on the hatred that was already in their hearts.

We stop this part here. First remember the first three rings of this chain: a father favoured, and made it into a coat; the coat bred distinction and divided the camps; once the dreams came out of his mouth, hatred was added to hatred. The chain has not yet reached murder, but every ring is sinking downward.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: From Shechem to Dothan — Seeing Him Afar Off, They Conceived a Murderous Heart (37:12-30)

The camera turns: his brothers went to feed their father's flock in Shechem, and Joseph remained in Hebron. One day Jacob sent Joseph out and said a sentence to which we must pay special attention: "go, I pray thee, see whether it be well with thy brethren, and well with the flocks; and bring me word again." (Genesis 37:14, excerpt, KJV) "Well" is still shalom (שָׁלוֹם, H7965) [4]. What his father wanted to inquire after was the peace — the welfare — of his sons; and the one he sent to ask after peace was exactly the one to whom his brothers would not speak a word of peace. The Bible's writing is like this: without a sound, it places the irony in plain view: the one who went to ask after peace lost his own peace first; the one who went to visit could not return home in the end.

Joseph heard his father's words and went. He first reached Shechem and did not find them; he wandered in the field, and a man met him and told him: they have departed from here, and are gone to Dothan. So Joseph went after his brothers and found them in Dothan. This stretch of road is worth pausing over. Joseph could perfectly well have turned back after missing them at Shechem and reported: I searched, I did not find them. But he did not; he pursued them all the way to Dothan. Wearing that coat, he walked mile after mile to find a group of people who did not like him. Young people have a young person's earnestness: what his father entrusted, he would carry through to the end. Only many years later do we know that this earnestness of "going one stretch of road further" sent him into the pit, and also sent him onto the road to Egypt. Many turning points in life, at the time, look like nothing more than going one stretch of road further.

What follows is the coldest scene in the whole chapter. "And when they saw him afar off, even before he came near unto them, they conspired against him to slay him." Note three measures: afar off, before he came near, and conspired. From afar off they recognized him — recognized what? They recognized that coat. The coat was originally the mark of a father's love; now it had become a mark that could be locked onto as a target from a distance. If love is partial, even its mark becomes a target. Before he came near, the murderous heart was already settled: Joseph had not yet spoken a single word and had no chance even to explain, and the judgment was already completed at a distance. Many interpersonal wrongs are tied in exactly this way: the person has not arrived, but the conclusion arrives first; the face has not been seen, but the charge is fixed first. Then look at "conspired": it was not one person's momentary impulse; it was everyone consulting together. "And they said one to another, Behold, this dreamer cometh." They no longer even called Joseph by his name; they only called him "this dreamer." When a person is hated to a certain point, his name is taken away first, and only a label remains. Once the name is taken away, the hand feels lighter — what they are about to harm seems not to be a brother, only an offensive dream.

The plot they fixed was poisonous: "Come now therefore, and let us slay him, and cast him into some pit, and we will say, Some evil beast hath devoured him: and we shall see what will become of his dreams." (Genesis 37:20, excerpt, KJV) Pit, in the original bor (בּוֹר, H953) [3], a pit, a dry well, a dungeon, originally a place in the wilderness for storing water; in the dry season it has no water, and whoever falls in cannot climb out — exactly right for hiding a corpse and erasing the traces. The last sentence in particular must be heard clearly: "and we shall see what will become of his dreams." On the surface the one they wanted to kill was their brother; at the bone it was that dream. They did not believe a seventeen-year-old brother deserved such dreams, and still less could they accept that if God had truly chosen the brother, they themselves — elder brothers, strong men, the ones who did the work — would instead have to bow down to him. The substance of jealousy is refusal to submit to God's sovereignty: the gift God gives to whom, the vision God gives to whom — why should it be him? Brothers and sisters, the most wounding contentions in the church and at home are often not over things; they are over this — why did he receive it, and I did not?

At the very pass where the murderous intent was about to land, the eldest brother Reuben spoke. "And Reuben heard it, and he delivered him out of their hands; and said, Let us not kill him. And Reuben said unto them, Shed no blood, but cast him into this pit that is in the wilderness, and lay no hand upon him." (Genesis 37:21-22, excerpt, KJV) The Bible writes Reuben's intention out plainly for him: "that he might rid him out of their hands, to deliver him to his father again." An eldest son is after all an eldest son: their father had placed the young son in everyone's hands, and if a life were lost, this elder brother would have no way to give an account. He did not dare to block everyone head-on; he only dared to change "kill" into "throw into a pit," thinking to wait until everyone had scattered, then quietly pull his brother up and send him back. This one act of obstruction must be affirmed: among the ten brothers, at least one man said one sentence — we must not take his life. A conscience that has even a timid sentence left is stronger than a whole field of silence. But Reuben's obstruction was also weak: he went around in a curve and did not dare to admit that he simply wanted to protect his brother. Weak goodwill often falls one breath short in saving a person — later the situation changed, he was not present, and Joseph was sold anyway. When he returned to the edge of the pit, tore his clothes, and cried, "The child is not; and I, whither shall I go?" it was already too late. Brothers and sisters, goodwill must be spoken early, spoken clearly, and spoken to the end; a vague kindness often cannot protect a person.

Joseph finally came near to them. The Scripture writes it crisply, and writes it cruelly: "And it came to pass, when Joseph was come unto his brethren, that they stript Joseph out of his coat, his coat of many colours that was on him; and they took him, and cast him into a pit." First strip the coat, then throw him into the pit. The order of their hands is very deliberate: first tear off from his body the mark of his father's favour, then deal with the man himself. That coat shaken once in their hands, Joseph was no longer the son cherished by his father, only a crying young man. The Bible specially adds one sentence: "and the pit was empty, there was no water in it." With no water, a man would not drown at once; but also with no water, a man could cry for thirst and cry for rescue and no one would hear. The light at the mouth of the pit slowly shrank into a circle, and his brothers above — guess what they were doing? "They sat down to eat bread." The people who threw their brother into a pit sat down to eat, and ate with quiet consciences. That a human conscience can grow dull to this point is the result of hatred fed for many years. The chain of jealousy has come to this point, and every ring has closed: favouritism began it, tale-bearing added fuel, the two dreams lit the fire, seeing him afar off fixed the verdict, stripping the coat and throwing him into the pit. Only the last step remains: this young man in the pit — dead, alive, or sold?

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Twenty Pieces of Silver — They Sold Their Brother, but God Took Over at the Bottom of the Pit (37:25-36)

As they were eating, a company of camels came from Gilead, bearing spicery, balm, and myrrh, going down to Egypt — a caravan of the Ishmeelites. At this moment the fourth brother, Judah, spoke. Judah's words must be heard sentence by sentence: "What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood? Come, and let us sell him to the Ishmeelites, and let not our hand be upon him; for he is our brother and our flesh." (Genesis 37:26-27, excerpt, KJV)

These words are half conscience and half business. The half that is conscience is this: in the end he admitted that Joseph was "our brother and our flesh"; a brother's blood, if shed, could not be concealed, and there was no profit in it. The half that is business is this: do not kill, and he can still be sold; once sold, the man disappears from before their eyes, the dreams have no sequel, and their hands carry no blood. Everyone listened to Judah. So as the caravan passed, they drew Joseph up out of the pit and sold him to the Ishmeelites for twenty pieces of silver, and the caravan brought Joseph into Egypt. Twenty pieces of silver, at the price of that time, was exactly the price of a young servant — no more, no less. Divided among ten brothers, each man got only a few pieces. Brothers and sisters, here is the most absurd place about jealousy: hatred accumulated for all these years, and when the account was finally settled it was worth only twenty pieces of silver. They thought they were selling a trouble; in fact they were selling this family's peace for more than twenty years to come: their father's weeping, their own guilty hearts, and their terror in the years of famine are all on the account of this transaction. Many years later, standing before the governor of Egypt, they would say, "we are verily guilty concerning our brother," and that was them repaying this account.

And Reuben returned, saw the pit was empty, and tore his clothes. He had not taken part in selling the man, but he also could not protect him. His brothers then did the last thing, and also the cruelest: they killed a kid of the goats, dipped the coat in the blood, and sent it to their father, saying: "This have we found: know now whether it be thy son's coat or no." Please note this form of sentence. More than thirty years earlier, Jacob had worn his brother's garments, wrapped himself in goatskins, and carried savoury food made from goats to deceive his blind father; now his sons took a coat dipped in goat's blood to deceive him. "And he knew it, and said, It is my son's coat; an evil beast hath devoured him; Joseph is without doubt rent in pieces." (Genesis 37:33, excerpt, KJV) Jacob recognized the coat, and did not recognize the deception. The man who was best at deceiving all his life was deceived completely and thoroughly in the matter of the son he cherished most. He tore his clothes, put sackcloth on his loins, and mourned for his son many days; all his sons and daughters rose up to comfort him, but he refused to be comforted, and said: "For I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning." Thus his father wept for him.

These tears must be read honestly. Jacob's mourning was real, and his love for Joseph was also real. But the root of this mourning lay exactly in his own favouritism: without that coat, without that public distinction, his brothers' hatred would not have burned like this. The price of favouritism is never paid by the one who favours alone; the whole family pays together, and the favoured one pays most bitterly. Parents who hear this chapter must place a hand on their own breast and ask: what I give this child — is it love, or is it a coat of many colours? Love is something every child has a share in; a coat is given to only one, and all the others must be made to see it. Love nourishes people; a coat provokes people. Jacob never drew this line clearly in his whole life, and paid for the lesson with more than twenty years of tears.

The story's last stroke falls in Egypt. "And the Midianites sold him into Egypt unto Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh's, and captain of the guard." (Genesis 37:36, excerpt, KJV) Potiphar the captain of the guard, an officer beside Pharaoh. In Canaan Joseph was a cherished son; now in Egypt he was a slave awaiting sale. One day he was still before his father; the next day he was already standing in a slave market in a foreign land. Within one chapter of Scripture, heaven and earth are turned over.

But brothers and sisters, please look at the whole chapter together: did God speak one sentence in this chapter? No. God did not appear, did not speak, did not stop his brothers' hands, and did not block the mouth of the pit. The God of this chapter seems to be absent. But who gave the dreams? God did. Why did the caravan, which might have come at any time, come from Gilead exactly while they were eating, changing "kill" into "sell" and preserving Joseph's life? Behind Reuben's and Judah's two speeches of obstruction, who was obstructing? Joseph was sold, and was not sold into a small household in the wilderness, but exactly into the house of Pharaoh's captain of the guard — only a few steps from the centre of Egypt's power. This chapter has not one word from God, yet everywhere is the hand of God. Many years later Joseph himself would explain this chapter clearly. He said to his brothers: "be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves... for God did send me before you to preserve life." (Genesis 45:5, excerpt, KJV) And again: "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." (Genesis 50:20, excerpt, KJV) The apostle Stephen, telling the history of Israel, spoke of Joseph in its very first section: "And the patriarchs, moved with envy, sold Joseph into Egypt: but God was with him, and delivered him out of all his afflictions." (Acts 7:9-10, excerpt, KJV) The Psalms, looking back on this road, say it even more thoroughly: "He sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant: whose feet they hurt with fetters: he was laid in iron: until the time that his word came: the word of the LORD tried him." (Psalm 105:17-19, excerpt, KJV)

It turns out the deep pit is not the opposite of the dream; the deep pit is the road of the dream. If Joseph had not been sold, he could not have reached Egypt; if he could not reach Egypt, he could not have entered the prison; if he could not enter the prison, he could not have met the butler; if he could not meet the butler, he could not have stood before Pharaoh. The bowing that God promised had to pass through a pit, slavery, and a prison, station by station, before it reached the throne. We often think that if God gives a vision, He ought to give a straight road; but God's road is this: the greater the vision, the deeper the pit and the more winding the road. It is not that God broke His word; it is that God does two things in the pit: one is to send a person to the place he ought to go, and the other is to grind a person into someone who can bear the vision. Joseph at seventeen, wearing the coat and telling dreams, was frank to the point of rashness; the Joseph who stood up more than twenty years later could interpret dreams humbly before Pharaoh, could say with a face full of tears before his brothers, "I am Joseph," and could feed his enemies' whole family with grain. What lies between them is exactly pits and chains. The pit did not destroy the dream; the pit made the dreamer into a man worthy of that dream.

So for those who are in a pit right now, this chapter has one sentence: the pit is empty and there is no water in it, but the pit is not your grave; it is your way station. That you cannot see God does not mean God is not present; that people threw you down does not mean the story ends here. First stand firm in the pit; do not curse, do not surrender to fate, and keep the dream safe in your heart. God's hand often takes many years before its whole shape can be seen, but the first step is often hidden in the most unbearable day.

For those who are elder brothers and elder sisters, this chapter also leaves a mirror. Jealousy at first is only a thought: why should it be him? If the thought is not dealt with, it will become a mouth that will not speak peaceably, eyes that fix the verdict when they see him afar off, hands that strip a coat and throw him into a pit, and finally an account of twenty pieces of silver. Stop jealousy while it is still a thought: admit that you have soured, say it out before God, and speak one word of blessing for the person you envy. A chain is easiest to break at its first ring; break one ring, and you save the whole family.

For young people, the lesson this chapter leaves is holding fast. If the vision is real, there is no need to proclaim it everywhere to prove it, and no need to doubt it because you have entered a pit. In Joseph's darkest years his dreams had not one echo, yet he did not throw the dreams away: he was faithful in Potiphar's house and faithful in the prison, and the dream walked toward him step by step. To hold fast to a vision without rashness is neither to tell everyone you meet nor to throw it away at the first hardship; work quietly, live cleanly, and wait for God's hour.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have seen a coat of many colours, two dreams, an empty pit, and twenty pieces of silver. A coat shows how expensive the price of favouritism is: the loved one wore it and drew hatred, the father wept for it for more than twenty years, and the brothers carried guilt for it for more than twenty years. Two dreams show that the road of a vision is not straight: the dreams came from heaven, but the road passed through a pit. An empty pit shows how long the chain of jealousy is: from souring in the heart, to coldness in the mouth, to a verdict in the eyes, to stripping and selling with the hands — ring locked to ring. And behind the twenty pieces of silver, something greater still appears: at the end of human malice, God's hand is only just beginning. His brothers said, we shall see what will become of his dreams; many years later they would bow down before Joseph and see with their own eyes what became of that dream. The dream begins not on a throne, but in a pit; and its fulfilment, too, is not in human hands, but in God's hand.

Let us pray together: Lord, through this chapter You have shone a light through every one of our hearts. For parents, remove the favouritism in us; let us not give our children a coat of distinction to wear, but only give them equal love and fair regard. For those with jealousy in their hearts, at the hour when it is still only a thought, break the chain for us at its first ring. For young people with a vision, teach them to hold fast quietly, neither rash nor disheartened. Finally, we pray for the brothers and sisters who are in a pit: there is no water in the pit and no light can be seen, but You are there. Let them know that the place where people threw them down is exactly the place where You take over; the dream will not die in the pit, and You will with Your own hand bring it to the day of its fulfilment. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] ketonet passim: coat of many colours, Hebrew כְּתֹנֶת (ketonet, H3801, a long garment, a robe) plus פַּסִּים (passim, H6446); the garment Jacob made for Joseph in Genesis 37:3, 23, 32 is in every case expressed with this term; see also 2 Samuel 13:18-19, where the coat worn by Tamar the princess is the same. The exact sense of passim is disputed: one view relates it to "palm" and "sole," and tradition mostly explains it as a long garment whose sleeves reach to the hands and whose hem reaches to the feet; another view takes it to mean many-coloured, patterned, or trimmed. The text of this manuscript adopts the traditional explanation of "a long garment reaching to the palms and the soles" and notes the dispute here, and does not determine the garment's style and colours from this one word; the passage on "a mark of one who does not have to work in the field" is a homiletical exposition based on a long garment being inconvenient for labour. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[2] chalom: dream, Hebrew חֲלוֹם (H2472), a dream, a vision in a dream; Joseph's two dreams and the responses of his brothers and his father in Genesis 37:5, 6, 8, 9, 10 all use this word and its cognate verbal forms; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Genesis 20:3, 28:12, 31:10-11, where God repeatedly spoke through dreams in the Old Testament, as the scriptural basis for this manuscript's "the dreams were given by God and were not invented by Joseph himself"; for the later fulfilment of the dreams see Genesis chapters 41-42 and 50:18.

[3] bor: pit, Hebrew בּוֹר (H953), a pit, a dry well, a cistern, a dungeon; the pit of the brothers' plotting and of their casting Joseph away in Genesis 37:20, 22, 24, 28-29 is in every case expressed with this word; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. A bor in the wilderness was originally a hollow for storing rainwater; in the dry season it has no water and its walls are steep and hard to climb, which is why 37:24 specially records "the pit was empty, there was no water in it"; see Jeremiah 38:6, where Jeremiah was cast into a miry pit, and the "dungeon" context of Exodus 12:29, as the basis for this manuscript's "the pit is a dry well, and one who falls in cannot get out by himself."

[4] shalom: peace, Hebrew שָׁלוֹם (H7965), peace, completeness, health, harmony; Genesis 37:4, his brothers "could not speak peaceably unto him," and Genesis 37:14, Jacob sent Joseph to see whether it was "well" with his brothers and with the flocks, both derive in the original from this same root. The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's second part, "the one who went to ask after peace first lost his own peace," is a homiletical exposition based on this correspondence of the same word; at the factual level the word used in both places is the same, as noted here.

[5] sane': to hate, Hebrew שָׂנֵא (H8130), to hate, detest, not love, be unfavoured; Genesis 37:4, 5, 8 record that his brothers hated Joseph, in all three places using verbal forms of this word, and 37:5 and 8 both record that because of his dreams and his words they "hated him yet the more." The meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. See Genesis 29:31, 33, where Leah's lack of favour uses this same word, as the textual basis for this manuscript's "the wound of favouritism continued through two generations"; "hatred spoils first in the mouth and afterwards in the hands" is a homiletical exposition.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And they took him, and cast him into a pit: and the pit was empty, there was no water in it." (Genesis 37:24, KJV) "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." (Genesis 50:20, KJV)

In his seventeenth year Joseph received first a coat of many colours, then two dreams, and then an empty pit. The pit was empty: no water, no ladder, only a small circle of sky overhead and the sound of his brothers eating outside. In this life there are seasons like that: you did not lose your way; you were thrown down. The dream is still in your heart, but the circumstances are all reversed. The Bible does not fill in Joseph's pit for him; it records that in the pit God said nothing, yet never stepped away. A caravan passed the mouth of the pit, the road to Egypt began at its bottom, and many years later the one who was thrown down stood up and fed the whole land, including those who threw him. If you are in a pit today, do not be quick to curse those who threw you, and do not throw the dream away with yourself. A pit is not a grave; it is a way station. Keep the dream safe, keep your hands clean, and wait for God's caravan to pass the pit's mouth. People may have meant evil against you, but God meant it unto good — believe that sentence while you are still in the pit, and when you come out, you will say it with your own mouth.

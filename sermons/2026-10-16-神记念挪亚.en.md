## Summary (approx. 100 words)

Genesis 8 opens with a single sentence: God remembered Noah. The floodwaters did not recede immediately; Noah waited in the ark for many days, sending out a raven and a dove, inquiring time and again whether the ground was dry. This sermon examines three things: God's remembering is not a sudden recollection, but that His covenant has never ceased; waiting is not passively enduring the days, but remaining willing to inquire and prepare while still inside the ark; and the very first thing upon leaving the ark is not taking inventory of losses, but building an altar—those who are saved render worship back to God first.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace be with you all!

In our last message, we finished Genesis 7: the rain fell forty days and forty nights, the waters lifted the ark up from the earth, all flesh that moved on the earth died, and only Noah and those with him in the ark remained.

If the story had stopped there, a question would linger in our hearts: What happened to the people in the ark? When will the waters recede? Does God still remember them?

Today we turn to chapter 8. The very first sentence of this chapter answers that question.

Let us first read the key passages:

"Then God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the animals that were with him in the ark. And God made a wind to pass over the earth, and the waters subsided." (Gen. 8:1)

"...so he put out his hand and took her, and drew her into the ark to himself." (Gen. 8:9, excerpt)

"And he waited yet another seven days, and again he sent the dove out from the ark. Then the dove came to him in the evening, and behold, a freshly plucked olive leaf was in her mouth; and Noah knew that the waters had receded from the earth." (Gen. 8:10–11)

"Then Noah built an altar to the LORD, and took of every clean animal and of every clean bird, and offered burnt offerings on the altar." (Gen. 8:20)

"And the LORD smelled a soothing aroma. Then the LORD said in His heart, 'I will never again curse the ground for man’s sake...'" (Gen. 8:21, excerpt)

Today we will look at this in three parts:

First, 8:1–5, God remembered Noah—the wind blew, the waters gradually receded, and the tops of the mountains slowly appeared;  
Second, 8:6–14, The raven and the dove—Noah inquired during the waiting, one period of seven days, then another seven days, until the ground was dry;  
Third, 8:15–22, Leaving the ark and building the altar—God commanded them to come out, Noah first built an altar to offer sacrifices, and God established the promise never to curse again.

Let us bow our heads in prayer:

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. Before the waters had completely receded, before the door was opened, You already remembered us. We pray that You would comfort those who are waiting today, and teach us that after we are delivered, our very first act should be to worship You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: God Remembered Noah, and the Wind Blew (8:1–5)

### 1.1 "God Remembered": Not that God Forgot and Then Remembered, but that the Covenant Had Reached Its Appointed Time

The first sentence of chapter 8: "Then God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the animals that were with him in the ark" (Gen. 8:1).

"Remembered," in Hebrew *zakar* (扎卡尔), means to remember, recall, or act according to a covenant[1]. When the Bible says God remembers, it does not mean God forgot in the first place. In Scripture, this word is often linked to action: God remembered Rachel, and listened to her and opened her womb (Gen. 30:22); God remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob, and looked upon the plight of the children of Israel in Egypt (cf. Ex. 2:24); Psalm 105:8 says, "He remembers His covenant forever." God's remembering means that in accordance with the covenant He made, He stretches forth His hand at His appointed time.

Therefore, the first sentence of chapter 8 is not news; it is a proclamation: during all those days the ark was floating upon the waters, God did not remove this family from His heart for a single day. That human beings cannot hear God's voice does not mean God is not remembering; that the waters have not yet receded does not mean God is not moving.

Brothers and sisters, this sentence is for those who are waiting. Your ark may have been drifting for a long time: the illness is not yet healed, the child has not yet returned, the job has not yet materialized, and your prayers seem to sink like a stone into the sea. Please read Genesis 8:1 a little slower: *God remembered*—the subject is God, not your feelings. Feelings fluctuate with the water level, but God's covenant does not.

### 1.2 "God Made a Wind to Pass over the Earth, and the Waters Subsided": Deliverance Is Often Gradual

After God remembered, the very first action was the wind: "And God made a wind to pass over the earth, and the waters subsided" (Gen. 8:1).

"Wind," in Hebrew *ruach* (鲁阿赫), means wind, breath, or Spirit[2]. Genesis 1:2, "the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters," uses this exact same word. At creation, the Spirit hovered over the waters; when the flood receded, the wind blew over the earth. Just as God worked in the beginning upon the chaotic waters, so now He works upon the waters of judgment to bring forth dry land once again.

Notice one word: "subsided"—gradually. The waters subsided gradually. It was not dried up in an instant; it receded gradually. Verse 3: "And the waters receded continually from the earth." Verse 5: "And the waters decreased continually until the tenth month. In the tenth month, on the first day of the month, the tops of the mountains were seen."

God works at two different speeds: one is instantaneous, like light coming into being as soon as He speaks; the other is gradual, like water receding inch by inch. What people can least bear is the second kind, because during the gradual days, one has to ask every single day: Has it receded? How much has it receded? When will it reach bottom?

In Exodus 23:30, God told the Israelites that He would drive out the Canaanites "little by little," lest the beasts of the field become too numerous for them (cf. the sense of that verse). The gradual pace is sometimes not God being slow; it is God protecting and preparing people through the process. If Noah had been placed onto muddy ground overnight, with no dry ground, no food, and no shelter, he would not have been able to stand upon leaving the ark. God caused the waters to recede gradually, and He also caused Noah's heart to be gradually prepared for the day he would stand upon the earth once again.

In his Genesis commentary, Warren W. Wiersbe, when explaining the receding of the flood, reminds readers to note that God's salvation involves both deeds and process; waiting is part of salvation, not the absence of salvation. (cf. Warren W. Wiersbe, *The Bible Exposition Commentary: Genesis*)

### 1.3 The Ark Rested on the Mountains of Ararat: The Resting Place Is Chosen by God

"Then the ark rested in the seventh month, the seventeenth day of the month, on the mountains of Ararat" (Gen. 8:4).

The ark had no rudder; Noah could not choose where to rest. It rested on the mountains of Ararat because the water had receded to that height, and God caused it to rest there. The arks of our lives are often like this: you did not choose the place to come to rest, but looking back afterward, you realize that the elevation where it came to rest was just right—any lower and it would not have held fast, any higher and you could not have come down.

Psalm 121 is a psalm set between deep waters and high mountains: "I will lift up my eyes to the hills—from whence comes my help? My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth" (Ps. 121:1–2). When Noah lifted his eyes and saw the mountain peaks appear, what helped him was not the mountains, but the One who made the mountains.

To summarize this first part in one sentence: **God's remembering is not merely a word of comfort; it is the primary cause of the wind beginning to blow and the waters beginning to recede; while you are still waiting in the gradual process, God is already moving.**

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Raven Flew Forth, the Dove Returned with a Leaf (8:6–14)

### 2.1 First Sending Out the Raven: Inquiring Does Not Always Bring an Immediate Answer

After forty days, Noah opened the window and first sent out a raven: "which kept going to and fro until the waters had dried up from the earth" (Gen. 8:7).

The raven brought back no news. It flew to and fro, never stopping by the window of the ark to report anything. The first lesson in waiting is often like this: you inquire once, and there is no answer; you pray once, and heaven remains silent.

Noah did not give up inquiring just because the raven brought no word, nor was he in a hurry to open the door and walk out. He sent out a dove.

Brothers and sisters, no one can escape the raven stage: the message you sent has received no reply, the medical exam has yielded no results yet, the person you are waiting for has not yet returned. That the raven did not bring back a leaf does not mean the ground is not drying. Do not let one unanswered inquiry negate all the work God is doing.

### 2.2 Three Times with the Dove: Once Returning, Once with a Leaf, Once Not Returning

The three times with the dove reveal Noah's discipline in waiting most clearly.

The first time: "the dove found no resting place for the sole of her foot, and she returned into the ark to him" (Gen. 8:9, excerpt). The water was still abundant, and the dove found no place to rest. Noah reached out his hand and brought her in—he did not look down on this inquiry as a failure; he took the dove back and continued to wait.

"And he waited yet another seven days" (Gen. 8:10). This is the biblical rhythm of waiting: seven days at a time, and yet another seven days. The second time, the dove returned in the evening, and behold, a freshly plucked olive leaf was in her mouth. "Olive," Hebrew *zayith* (זַיִת), an olive tree, an olive[3]. What does a single leaf show? It shows that under the water, trees have already come back to life; it shows that upon the earth there is not only death, but newly sprouting life. The leaf was not large, yet it was the very first assurance Noah and his whole family had waited so long to see.

The third time, he waited yet another seven days, sent out the dove, and she did not return to him anymore (cf. Gen. 8:12). Not returning was actually good news: the dove had found a place to rest her feet outside.

Waiting is not doing nothing. Before God opens the door, it is preparing our steps to go out, one step at a time, according to the small assurance He gives.

Proverbs 13:12 says: "Hope deferred makes the heart sick, / But when the desire comes, it is a tree of life." Noah's heart-sickness through those seven days after seven days was real; and on the night the olive leaf arrived, the sense of a tree of life was also real. Scripture does not tell us to pretend that waiting does not hurt. What it records is this: the one who was hurting did not let go—he waited yet another seven days.

Let us ignite our hearts here for a moment.

Brothers and sisters, do not kick open the door of the ark yourself before the dove flies back the second time. When God's time has not yet arrived, the muddy ground will trap your feet; but when God's time arrives, a single leaf is enough for you to know: the ground is drying, the way is opening, and a new day is dawning. It is today, not tomorrow—send your dove out once again today: pray one more time, read the Scriptures once more, make that phone call of reconciliation to that person, pick back up the preparation you had already abandoned. The leaf may return this very evening.

### 2.3 "The Ground Was Dry" Still Waits for God to Speak: Noah Did Not Jump the Gun

By verses 13 and 14, the waters were dried up from the earth, and the face of the ground was dry. Yet notice, Noah still did not run out on his own. He waited until verse 15, when God spoke: "Go out of the ark, you and your wife and your sons and your sons' wives with you."

This is the hardest, and also the most beautiful part of Noah's waiting: going into the ark was listening to God, and coming out of the ark was also listening to God. The ground looking dry and God saying it is time to go out are two different things. How many people jump the gun on the final stretch of the road: stopping follow-up exams as soon as an illness slightly improves, stopping investment in a relationship as soon as the tension slightly eases, announcing a calling on their own as soon as it becomes a little clearer. Noah teaches us to surrender even the final mile to God's voice.

Noah's waiting shows that faith encompasses patience and order; entering and exiting the ark were both governed by God's word, not decided independently based on sight.

To sum up the second part in one sentence: **Waiting is not merely enduring time; it is still being willing to inquire through seven days after seven days, still being willing to take back the dove that brought no answer, and still waiting for God to speak before taking that final step.**

## (42:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The First Thing After Exiting the Ark—First Build an Altar (8:15-22)

### 3.1 "Go Out of the Ark": The Door to the New World Is Opened by God

"Then God spoke to Noah, saying, 'Go out of the ark, you and your wife and your sons and your sons' wives with you'" (Gen. 8:15-16).

In chapter 7, it was God who shut the door; in chapter 8, it is also God who opened the door. When the door was shut, man was preserved; when the door was opened, man was sent forth. The text goes on to say that every living thing of all flesh was to be brought out of the ark, to abound on the earth and be fruitful (cf. Gen. 8:17). Exiting the ark was not the end of a vacation; it was the beginning of a new world: the blessing of "be fruitful and multiply" from chapter 1 was spoken once again after the flood.

After Noah came out, what was the state of the earth? There were no houses, no fields, no altars; everything had to start from scratch. In times like these, what a person does first reveals their heart most clearly.

### 3.2 Build the Altar First, Do Not Calculate the Losses First

"Then Noah built an altar to the Lord, and took of every clean animal and of every clean bird, and offered burnt offerings on the altar" (Gen. 8:20).

Please note the order. Noah did not build a house first, did not take inventory of how much livestock was left first, did not complain first about how bitterly they had suffered this past year in the ark. He built an altar first.

Furthermore, what he offered were clean animals and birds—the very ones specifically brought in by sevens, male and female, in chapter 7. The stock inside the ark was not plentiful to begin with; offering up a portion of them was a genuine cost. "Burnt offering," Hebrew *olah* (עֹלָה), an ascending, wholly burnt offering[4]. The burnt offering was entirely consumed on the altar; man kept no portion for himself to eat. The meaning of this offering is very straightforward: all of this is Yours, and I return it to You first.

The priority of worship reveals the priority of the human heart: God first, self second is not a slogan; it is offering the very best first when resources are at their tightest.

To the question of Psalm 116:12, Noah replied with action: "What shall I render to the Lord / For all His benefits toward me?" The answer is not balancing the ledger; it is building an altar first. Brothers and sisters, what is the first thing you do after being delivered? When you recover from illness, do you catch up on sleep first or worship first? When hardship has passed, do you celebrate first or come before God to give thanks first? If the order is wrong, the heart will slowly begin to take grace for granted.

### 3.3 "I Will Never Again Curse the Ground": After the Soothing Aroma, the Promise Arrives

"And the Lord smelled a soothing aroma. Then the Lord said in His heart, 'I will never again curse the ground for man's sake, although the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth; nor will I again destroy every living thing as I have done. While the earth remains, seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, winter and summer, and day and night shall not cease'" (Gen. 8:21-22).

God did not pretend that humanity had become good. He stated plainly that the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth—this is the exact same diagnosis as chapter 6, "every intent of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually." The promise came not because man had changed, but because God, before the sweet-smelling sacrifice, resolved to uphold this world with patience and order: seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, winter and summer, day and night, running their course season after season.

The New Testament applies the soothing aroma of the burnt offering to Christ: Ephesians 5:2 says that Christ "has given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma." Noah's altar was the shadow; Christ's cross is the substance. Noah offered livestock, and God made a covenant never again to curse; Christ offered Himself, and God established an eternal, unbreakable New Covenant.

To sum up the third part in one sentence: **Those who build an altar first upon exiting the ark know clearly in their hearts who gave them deliverance; as the sweet aroma ascends, the promise descends, and the new world begins with worship.**

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Groups of People

Today's chapter speaks to six groups of people:

**To seekers:** You may currently be in the days when the waters have not yet receded, wondering if God has forgotten you. Genesis 8:1 says God remembered. This God who remembered Noah is also reaching out His hand to you through Jesus Christ. Today, do not merely wait for all your circumstances to improve; first respond to His remembrance: pray to Him, confess that you need His salvation, and enter the ark He has prepared for you—which is in Christ.

**To new believers:** After coming to faith, the waters receding very slowly is the norm, not the exception. Do not measure yourself by someone else's speed. Learn Noah's rhythm: seven days of Scripture reading, seven days of prayer, seven days of waiting, releasing the dove time after time. The leaf will come; do not kick open the door just yet.

**To longtime brothers and sisters in the Lord:** You have already exited the ark—the hardest season has passed. Now examine yourself: did you build an altar first, or did you first busy yourself with your own inventories and arrangements? What is easiest to lose after years of believing in the Lord is the order of worshiping first after receiving grace. This week, intentionally offer up an act of thanksgiving first; do not wait until all conditions are met before giving thanks.

**To parents:** When Noah's family exited the ark, the children saw that the first thing their father did was build an altar. What your children see you do first after a hardship has passed, they will remember for the rest of their lives. Establish an "exiting-the-ark" rule for your family: whenever deliverance is received and a crisis is overcome, the whole family prays and gives thanks together first before discussing anything else.

**To those striving in the workplace:** After a project wraps up, a crisis blows over, and performance targets are met, who receives your first phone call, your first expenditure, your first weekend? The altar of a working professional is setting apart the Lord's Day, giving, and thanksgiving for God even in the busiest times when celebration seems most warranted. Order is a testimony; your colleagues can see it.

**To those currently waiting in suffering:** You may not have even passed the stage of the raven yet: you inquire, and there is no echo. Do not let go. God remembers you; the wind is already blowing, the waters are gradually subsiding, you just cannot see the mountain peaks yet. Hold fast to Genesis 8:1 today and read it every day; wait yet another seven days, and release the dove once more. Persevere for one more seven days, and the leaf may already be on its way.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Responsive Prayer

Let us review today's three statements:

First, God remembered Noah—the people of the covenant, God has not forgotten for a single day;
Second, the waters receded gradually, and the dove brought back assurance only on the third try—waiting must be walked out according to the rhythm of seven days after seven days;
Third, build an altar first upon exiting the ark—those who are delivered return worship to God first, and the promise follows right behind.

Now let us bow our heads and respond to today's word in prayer.

"Heavenly Father, we thank You. However great the flood may be, You remember us; however long the wait, Your wind is already blowing. Forgive us for the days when we were hasty to kick down the door and forgot to build an altar. Teach us not to lose heart in the gradual unfolding, and through seven days and yet another seven days, still to be willing to pray, still to be willing to inquire, and still to wait for You to speak. The moment we come out of the ark, may we first come before You to offer thanksgiving, for deliverance is given by You, and the new world is opened by You. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, Amen."

Amen.

Title verification: The title is taken from the central image of the fourth oracle in verse 17, read in the connected wording of the base text: "there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel" (24:17). The chapter's five movements — no more seeking enchantments on the top of Peor (24:1-2), the third oracle over the goodly tents (24:3-9), the self-defence under the dismissal (24:10-14), the fourth oracle of the Star and the Sceptre (24:15-19), and the three short oracles over the nations and the parting (24:20-25) — are held together by one line: the enterprise of cursing is dismantled, the hand of enchantment is let down, and yet the words that come out go farther and farther, from the camp before his eyes to a Star that is not now and not nigh. Candidates examined verse by verse: "How goodly are thy tents" (24:5) covers only the praise of the third oracle; "Blessed is he that blesseth thee" (24:9) is the close of the third oracle and the echo of the covenant in Genesis, governing one oracle only; "Alas, who shall live when God doeth this!" (24:23) covers only the lament of the last three oracles. Verse 17 alone names both Jacob and Israel and gives at once the Star in heaven and the Sceptre in the hand; it is the chapter's far point and summit, and is therefore the title. The Chinese title is taken independently from the same verse in its own base text; the English title follows the KJV wording of the same verse.
Base-text notes (where the two base texts or common paraphrase differ, each base text is recorded as it stands): KJV 24:1 reads "to seek for enchantments," and "but he set his face toward the wilderness"; the older Chinese rendering once paraphrased in circulation ("to meet demons") does not stand in either base text here. At 24:3 and 15 the KJV reads "the man whose eyes are open," where the Chinese base text reads "the man whose eyes are closed," carrying its own parenthetical note "closed, or: open" — the two base texts differ at this word, the difference is registered in the sermon text and in the discussion layer, and neither is rewritten to match the other. KJV 24:6 reads "as the trees of lign aloes which the LORD hath planted, and as cedar trees beside the waters"; the Chinese base text names the trees as aloes trees and cedar wood, not "cedar trees" in the paraphrased form. ## Summary

At the end of chapter 23 a third set of new altars stands on the top of Peor (23:29-30). Chapter 24 opens — and the enterprise stops: when Balaam saw that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel, he went not, as at other times, to seek for enchantments, but set his face toward the wilderness (24:1). No altar, no going apart to meet, no "perhaps" — he lifted up his eyes and saw Israel abiding in his tents according to their tribes, and the spirit of God came upon him (24:2), and the third oracle comes straight out: "How goodly are thy tents, O Jacob, and thy tabernacles, O Israel!" (24:3-9). Balak smites his hands together and issues the dismissal, counting the account aloud himself: "thou hast altogether blessed them these three times" (24:10-11). Balaam answers with the old boundary he cannot cross though Balak give him his house full of silver and gold (24:12-13), yet before he goes he leaves one word behind — what this people shall do to thy people (24:14). The fourth oracle rises from it: "I shall see him, but not now: I shall behold him, but not nigh: there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel" (24:15-19). Then three short oracles sweep past Amalek, the Kenites, and the ships from the coast of Chittim (24:20-24), and the whole chapter closes in a single parting sentence: Balaam rose up and returned to his place, and Balak also went his way (24:25). The great cursing enterprise of three chapters ends with nothing left but a Star that is not nigh, and two men each gone home.

## Main Text

### 1. No More Seeking Enchantments: Facing the Wilderness (Numbers 24:1-2) (00:00-08:00)

(Fact) Verse 1 is the dismantling of a procedure, quoted in full as it stands in the base text: "And when Balaam saw that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel, he went not, as at other times, to seek for enchantments, but he set his face toward the wilderness." (24:1) Registered item by item: first, the motive is "when Balaam saw" — he first saw a fact: it pleased the LORD to bless Israel. The two oracles of chapter 23 (23:8, 20) had already said this out in full; in this chapter he at last adjusts his action to the fact. Second, what is dismantled is "to seek for enchantments": at the other times (23:3, 15) he had to send Balak away and go forward alone to meet; in this chapter that whole step is deleted — no going apart, no high place, no "peradventure the LORD will come to meet me" (23:3, in the KJV wording of that place). Third, the new action is only this: he set his face toward the wilderness. The wilderness is where Israel's camp is (the camp of this book has been journeying in the wilderness ever since the exodus, cross-reference layer); in the two chapters before, he turned his back on the camp to go and meet God; in this chapter he faces the camp, and the word of God comes. Verse 2, quoted in full: "And Balaam lifted up his eyes, and he saw Israel abiding in his tents according to their tribes; and the spirit of God came upon him." (24:2) "Lifted up his eyes" and "he saw" are registered: what he sees is no longer a corner or an edge of the camp (22:41; 23:13), but the whole ordered camp, "abiding in his tents according to their tribes." "The spirit of God came upon him" is the only express statement of its kind in the three chapters of the Balaam narrative (checked within chapters 22-24, see Endnote 4): in chapter 22 the ass speaks and the angel appears; in chapter 23 God meets him and puts a word in his mouth; in this chapter the spirit comes upon him — three chapters, three ways of drawing near, and this time it rests on no altar and no seeking, but comes precisely as he faces the wilderness and lifts up his eyes to look.

(Application) This section must preach the word "no more" through and through. The turning point of Balaam's faith is not in what more he did, but in what he stopped doing: he stopped seeking enchantments. Seeking enchantments was the root of his profession — a set of procedures for probing, guiding, and securing a desired answer. Twice he brought that procedure before the true God; the procedure ran as usual, and the answers came not at all from the procedure. In this chapter he learns the first lesson: some words come unsought, and what is sought out is often only one's own echo. A man's greatest progress before God is often not that he has learned a new action, but that he has laid down an old means. Our generation's "enchantments" wear no long robe: they are the repeated divinatory prayer that begs for an agreeable answer, the offering and the service treated as a token of exchange, the running about asking after "more effective" spiritual techniques — at bottom all one thing: the wish to use a procedure to guide God onto one's own order form. The lesson of this chapter's first step is very plain: face the wilderness, and seek no more. That the LORD is pleased to bless Israel is his character; it does not wait for a man to seek it. That his spirit came upon Balaam is his sovereignty; it is not invited by a procedure. Lay your means down, lift your eyes up, see the God who is already blessing — the oracle begins here.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Enchantments" is nəḥāšîm, from nāḥaš (H5173), divination, the seeking of omens, the same word as "enchantments" at 23:23 (its number was verified in the sermon on Numbers 23). "Spirit" is rûaḥ (H7307). "Wilderness" is midbār (H4057). The construction behind "came upon him" is "came upon him / over him"; this sermon registers the base text's rendering. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing (for the method of verification, see Endnote 5).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) How the change of intent in verse 1 — "when Balaam saw... he went not, as at other times" — is to be characterised is set out in two lines of the commentary tradition, without adjudication: one takes it as a measure of awakening in Balaam — after two verdicts he owns that enchantment is of no effect against this God, and abandons the art to speak; the other takes it as an adjustment of professional judgement — he sees the order cannot possibly be filled as placed, and substitutes direct looking for seeking, and whether his motive turned, the text does not say. The facts reach only as far as this chapter: everything he says in this chapter is blessing and prophecy, and this chapter records no act of cursing or of plotting by him. Later records concerning Balaam exist (the historical sequel in Numbers 25 and the identification at 31:16, belonging to the fact layer of this book's later text), and what relation they bear to the characterisation of this chapter has been much discussed through the ages; this sermon registers only that the later text exists, does not read it back into this chapter's fact layer, and sets the views side by side without deciding between them.

(Fact) One further contrast between this chapter's opening and chapter 23's opening must be registered (a contrast within the two chapters themselves): chapter 23 opens with Balaam requisitioning an enterprise — build me here seven altars, and prepare me here seven oxen and seven rams (23:1); chapter 24 opens with Balaam cancelling the enterprise — he went not to seek for enchantments, but set his face toward the wilderness (24:1). In chapter 23 the word of God came after the rite and in the meeting (23:4-5); in chapter 24 the spirit came at the moment of looking (24:2). In chapter 23 three rounds of altar-building make twenty-one altars in all; in chapter 24 the number of altar-buildings is zero — of this chapter's five oracles, not one passed through an altar. The narrative sets this contrast out with complete clarity: the altars are dismantled, and the words go farther and fuller instead. Procedure and utterance are formally uncoupled in this chapter: in the two chapters before, men still thought the word was the product of the rite; this chapter proves the word was always the product of the spirit; the rite is at most a background, and looking is the position.

### 2. How Goodly Are Thy Tents: The Third Oracle (Numbers 24:3-9) (08:00-24:00)

(Fact) At verse 3 the third oracle begins, and the specification changes first, as it stands in the base text: "And he took up his parable, and said, Balaam the son of Beor hath said, and the man whose eyes are open hath said:" (24:3) "He took up his parable" occurs five times in this chapter (24:3, 15, 20, 21, 23), in the KJV's wording as quoted. (The Chinese base text reads at this word, in its own wording, "he lifted up his oracle-song," and at "eyes are open" the Chinese base text reads "whose eyes are closed," with its own parenthetical note "closed, or: open" — registered as it stands, the reading left to the discussion layer; the KJV here reads "open," and this manuscript follows its own base text.) The self-introduction is registered: the first two oracles (23:7, 18) both open directly; in this chapter Balaam first issues himself a name-card — the son of Beor, the man whose eyes are open. Verse 4 is the lower half of the name-card, quoted in full: "He hath said, which heard the words of God, which saw the vision of the Almighty, falling into a trance, but having his eyes open:" (24:4) His credentials are four: he has heard words, he has seen a vision, he has fallen down, and his eyes are open. Not one of the four is his own achievement: the words were heard, the vision was granted to sight, the falling was a being pressed down, the opening was a being opened. Verse 5 is the oracle's first sound, and it is admiration, quoted in full: "How goodly are thy tents, O Jacob, and thy tabernacles, O Israel!" (24:5) One transposition is registered: the theme of the first two oracles was how God sees this people (23:9, 21); the third oracle opens with Balaam, for the first time, addressing this people directly in the second person and by name, with his own eyes — not a report, but praise spoken face to face, and the object of the praise is still the very camp he was commissioned to come and curse. Verse 6 sends out four similes in succession, quoted in full: "As the valleys are they spread forth, as gardens by the river's side, as the trees of lign aloes which the LORD hath planted, and as cedar trees beside the waters." (24:6) The four similes fall into two groups: a group of terrain (valleys, gardens) and a group of trees (lign aloes, cedar trees); both groups carry water — by the river's side, beside the waters. Verse 7, as it stands in the base text: "He shall pour the water out of his buckets, and his seed shall be in many waters, and his king shall be higher than Agag, and his kingdom shall be exalted." (24:7) "His king" is entered on the record: this is the first time in the three chapters of oracles that a "king" of Israel himself appears as the subject of the future (the king at 23:21 is the LORD acclaimed as king in the midst of the camp; here it is this people's own king), and "his kingdom" makes its first appearance with it. Verse 8 names the exodus again (taken up in this chapter after the second oracle at 23:22), quoted in full: "God brought him forth out of Egypt; he hath as it were the strength of an unicorn: he shall eat up the nations his enemies, and shall break their bones, and pierce them through with his arrows." (24:8) Verse 9 closes the third oracle with the lion image and a couplet, quoted in full: "He couched, he lay down as a lion, and as a great lion: who shall stir him up? Blessed is he that blesseth thee, and cursed is he that curseth thee." (24:9) The lion of 23:24 rises up; the lion of this verse couched and lay down — the rising is the posture of attack, the lying down the posture of settled dwelling; a lion that can lie down at rest is more unprovokable than one standing, because its territory is already fixed.

(Application) The admiration of verse 5 must be stopped at and read slowly. Balaam is the first man in Scripture to praise the beauty of Israel's tents face to face, and he is a foreign diviner, standing on an enemy kingdom's hilltop, holding an order to curse. What did he see? Tents abiding according to their tribes. What is goodly about tents? That was nothing but a migrant camp: in it were men who had just murmured, men who had just passed through the fiery serpents (chapter 21), and leaders who had contended at Meribah (chapter 20). Seen from inside the camp, the camp is full of problems; seen through the eyes God opened in Balaam, its goodness is not in the tents but in the order — abiding according to their tribes is the arrangement God himself laid down (chapters 1-2, cross-reference layer); the goodness is not in the men, but in the One who dwells in the midst of this camp. This section is a mirror for the church: when we look at our own church we often see only the list of problems — men's weakness, the difficulty of the work, the gaps in resources; when God looks at his church, what he sees is the order and beauty of his own presence. It is not that the problems do not exist; it is that the place of looking differs: at the foot of the hill a man counts problems; on the hill he sees goodness. Balaam's eyes were opened not only to see the future; first he learned to see the present with God's eyes: that camp which you have judged hopeless — God says, how goodly.

(Application) The couplet in the second half of verse 9 is the heaviest echo in the whole chapter. "Blessed is he that blesseth thee, and cursed is he that curseth thee" — this is not Balaam's invention (cross-reference layer: the promise of God to Abram in Genesis 12:3 runs, in the KJV wording of that place, "I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee" — the correspondence of wording is the point registered). After three chapters and so great a detour, the expert Moab hired with the resources of a whole kingdom concludes by reading out, as it stands, the first sentence of the covenant. The couplet's place in this chapter is like a boundary stone set up on the ruins of the cursing enterprise: the circuit of blessing and cursing was connected long ago, and its direction cannot be changed — bless this people, and the blessing returns upon you; curse this people, and the curse returns upon your own head. Balak is the living example: he paid money to buy a curse, and after the third oracle his own kingdom is pronounced upon in the fourth oracle (24:17). A man cannot stand outside the circuit and manipulate the circuit; every one of us only chooses a side within this couplet: either bless what God blesses and have a share in the blessing, or set oneself against God's blessing and take the ruin oneself. This is also why the third oracle does not curse Balak — there is no need: the couplet does its own work.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Parable" (oracle) is māšāl (H4912, its number verified in the sermon on Numbers 23). "Tents" is ʾōhel (H168); "tabernacles" is miškān (H4908). "Gardens" is gan (H1588); "river" is nāhār (H5104); "cedar" is ʾerez (H730). "Unicorn" is rəʾēm (H7214); "lion" is ʾărî (H738); "great lion" is lāḇîʾ (H3833) — the numbers of these three words were verified in the sermon on Numbers 23. "Blesseth / blessed" is bārak (H1288); "curseth / cursed" is ʾārar (H779, verified in the sermon on Numbers 23). "Vision" is maḥăzeh (H4236); "falling" is nāpal (H5307); "open" belongs to the gālâ (H1540) group, to uncover, to lay open. The word-form behind "lign aloes" and the number of "Agag" are marked in this sermon as not yet verified; the form and the base text's rendering alone are given, no number is supplied from memory — see Endnote 5.

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two points are set out without adjudication. First, the juxtaposition of "whose eyes are open" at verses 3 and 15 (so the KJV; the Chinese base text reads "whose eyes are closed," with its own parenthetical note "or: open") with "having his eyes open" at verses 4 and 16: the Chinese base text's own note at the "closed" reading shows that the reading of this word has carried two understandings in the tradition of copying and translation — one understanding takes it as eyes closed in entrancement (the outer eye shut, the inner eye open, standing in exact contrast with the "eyes open" of the next clause), the other as the man whose eyes have been opened. The precise semantics of the original word-form have no settled conclusion in scholarship; this sermon registers the base texts as they stand and keeps the parenthetical note, builds no spiritual doctrine on this word in the sermon text, and sets the tension between "closed" and "open" side by side only. Second, "Agag" at verse 7: one view takes it as a common title of the kings of Amalek (as Pharaoh for Egypt), another as the proper name of a particular Amalekite king of the time; this chapter adds no explanation, and its intertextual relation with the Agag of 1 Samuel 15 is registered as a shared name only, the views being kept at this layer.

(Fact) The manner in which the third oracle comes out, set against the first two, is closed with one last account (a contrast within this book itself): the first and second oracles both passed through four steps — "build altars — go apart — meet — a word put in the mouth" (23:1-6, 13-17); the third oracle's steps are two only — "lifted up his eyes and saw" and "the spirit came upon him" (24:2). Yet the third oracle's content carries the highest specification: the first oracle treats this people's standing (dwelling alone, 23:9), the second its protection (enchantments of no effect, 23:23), the third its present and its future — the present goodness of its tents (24:5) and the king and kingdom to come (24:7). The occasion with the simplest procedure saw the farthest. It must also be registered that the third oracle contains Balaam's first direct praise of Israel (24:5, in the second person) and the first appearance of this people's own king (24:7) — the words come nearer and nearer, and farther and farther: near, to praise face to face; far, to see a king. These two things stand in one and the same oracle, not as a contradiction, but as the two focal lengths of one pair of opened eyes.

### 3. Three Blessings and a House Full of Silver and Gold: Under the Dismissal (Numbers 24:10-14) (24:00-44:00)

(Fact) At verse 10 Balak's composure breaks, quoted in full: "And Balak's anger was kindled against Balaam, and he smote his hands together: and Balak said unto Balaam, I called thee to curse mine enemies, and, behold, thou hast altogether blessed them these three times." (24:10) "He smote his hands together" is entered on the record: this is Balak's only loss of bodily control in the three chapters — in the two chapters before he only questioned (23:11) and changed the order (23:25); in this chapter he smites his hands. More important still is the count he gives with his own mouth: "these three times" — the first oracle (23:7-10), the second (23:18-24), the third (24:3-9); the employer has inspected the delivery himself: the order was cursing, the goods delivered were all blessing, three times. Verse 11 is the dismissal and the argument about honour, quoted in full: "Therefore now flee thou to thy place: I thought to promote thee unto great honour; but, lo, the LORD hath kept thee back from honour." (24:11) Balak here speaks a sentence that is half right: "the LORD hath kept thee back" — he owns that the hindrance of these three chapters came from the LORD; what he miscalculates is the second half: in his eyes, to be kept back from honour is Balaam's loss, the bad debt of this transaction. Verses 12-13 are Balaam's defence, a quotation of his own earlier words, quoted in full: "And Balaam said unto Balak, Spake I not also to thy messengers which thou sentest unto me, saying," (24:12) "If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the commandment of the LORD, to do either good or bad of mine own mind; but what the LORD saith, that will I speak?" (24:13) Registered item by item: this boundary is not newly set up; it was declared when the messengers first arrived (22:18) and is now restated for the third time; the clause "to do either good or bad of mine own mind" draws "good" itself into the forbidden zone — he may not curse of his own mind (do bad), and he may not bless of his own mind either (do good); the good and bad of this mouth are not his to manage himself. Verse 14 is the announcement of the farewell gift, quoted in full: "And now, behold, I go unto my people: come therefore, and I will advertise thee what this people shall do to thy people in the latter days." (24:14) (The Chinese base text reads at the close of this verse, in its own wording, "what this people shall hereafter do to thy people," without the KJV's phrase "in the latter days" in that form — registered as it stands.) Two items are registered: first, Balaam's intention to go is settled ("I go unto my people" runs in the same direction as the dismissal of verse 11; which came first, the text does not distinguish, and the two are set side by side only); second, on the point of leaving he does not close his mouth, but of his own accord leaves one more passage behind — it is not bought by a further offer from Balak; it is what he himself wills to say: "come therefore, and I will advertise thee." The door of the fourth oracle is pushed open by the prophet himself.

(Application) The "three times" of verse 10 is for everyone who makes inspections to hear. Balak is the most earnest inspector in the three chapters: he was present at every altar-building, he heard every oracle to the end, and in this chapter he gives the general verdict: three times, blessing throughout. This inspection report comes from the opposing side's mouth, and carries more weight than any testimony of Israel's own — because the inspector's interest runs exactly against his conclusion; he has no motive for a single word of exaggeration. The church's history is often the same: the hardest evidence that God's people are blessed is sometimes not in their own testimony but in the opponent's ledger. Persecutors have counted the church's growth, mockers have recorded the answers to prayer, and Balak-style inspections repeat across the ages. Conversely, this gives us a mirror too: when we take our own year-end stock of our ministry, what do we count? Balak counted only whether the goods he wanted to buy had arrived. If we are honest, we should ask God to do the counting: this year, in the words that came out of my mouth, how many times blessing, how many times cursing? How many times obedience, how many times self-direction? If God himself takes up this counting, it will be truer than our own summaries.

(Application) The argument about honour in verse 11 needs one layer more. Balak says: the LORD hath kept thee back from honour. He speaks of God's hindrance as Balaam's loss. But seen in the light of the whole chapter, this very hindrance is the moment of Balaam's life nearest to blessing: without it, he would long ago have gone home to his place with the wages of cursing and the guilt of cursing on him; it is precisely the being "kept back from honour" that preserved his record of "three times blessing." When God blocks our road, what he often blocks is the honour we imagine for ourselves: the partnership that would have been concluded, the high salary that was within reach, the cutting remark that was on the tip of the tongue — only afterwards, looking back, do we know the hindrance was not a deduction but a preservation. Men always reckon "God did not give me" as loss; few reckon "God did not let me carry it through" as grace. Balaam received a blessing he himself may not have appreciated at the time: his failure is the reputation God kept for him. When we examine our own record, we should add this column: of the things in those years that were never concluded, never obtained, never spoken — how many of them, counted today, are God saying, "I kept thee back"? That is not his treating you ill; it is his keeping your mouth shut for you, across the enterprise of three chapters.

(Application) Verse 13's "to do either good or bad of mine own mind" is the strictest guard Balaam ever kept over his eloquence, and the most piercing sentence for today's servants. We usually guard only the "bad" side: not to speak words of cursing of our own mind, not to do harm of our own mind. But Balaam surrendered the "good" as well: even blessing he does not issue of his own mind. This is not to say that good deeds need no man to do them; it is to say that in the office of bearing a message, the source of the message is one only: what the LORD saith, that will I speak. The most dangerous thing on a pulpit is not evil speech; it is self-produced "good words": promises enlarged to please men, boundaries widened to make the occasion look well, stirrings manufactured to appear spiritual — all of them doing good of one's own mind, and the same offence as doing bad of one's own mind, because both exchange God's word for one's own. Balaam, this outsider, sets the rule for everyone who bears a message: neither good nor bad is mine to decide; the switch is not in my hand. The man who can keep this one rule is fit to stand in a pulpit; the man who cannot, however beautifully he speaks, is only doing business on the top of Peor.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Honour" is kāḇôd (H3519), the glory and weight that comes from God or from men; the "great honour" of this section is its causative use, registered in the base text's rendering. "Gold" is zāhāḇ (H2091); "silver" is kesef (H3701). The "commandment" in "go beyond the commandment of the LORD" belongs in the original to the construction of "mouth" (a command as a word that has come out of the mouth); this sermon registers the base text's rendering only and does not expand it. The action behind "smote his hands together" is in the original a striking of the palms; this sermon registers the base text's wording and makes no philological expansion of it. All numbers above were verified one by one before writing (for the method, see Endnote 5).

(Fact) The account of persons and speeches in this paragraph is closed as follows: two speakers in this paragraph (Balak speaks twice, 24:10-11; Balaam speaks twice, 24:12-13 and 24:14); Moab's messengers and the Israelites still have not a word. Balak's two speeches are a summing-up and an expulsion; Balaam's two are a self-defence and a foretelling — one man looking back (three times, all wrong), one man looking forward (what shall be hereafter). The conversation of the three chapters changes direction here: every negotiation before this was about "how to curse now"; at verse 14, for the first time, the word "hereafter" — in the KJV's wording, "in the latter days" — appears on the negotiating table. After verse 14 Balaam has spoken, and Balak has not another sentence (checked within this chapter: at 24:15-25 Balak has no lines) — not because he has nothing to say, but because the words are no longer on his table. Through the fourth oracle and the last three oracles he is present and hears them all, and cannot get a single word in. The man who made the offers ends with even his seat as a hearer assigned to him: what God willed him to hear, he heard, not a sentence less.

### 4. A Star Out of Jacob: The Fourth Oracle (Numbers 24:15-19) (44:00-52:00)

(Fact) At verse 15 the fourth oracle begins; the self-introduction is re-issued as it stands, while its specification quietly rises, as it stands in the base text: "And he took up his parable, and said, Balaam the son of Beor hath said, and the man whose eyes are open hath said:" (24:15) Verse 16 is the highest specification of self-introduction in the whole chapter, quoted in full: "He hath said, which heard the words of God, and knew the knowledge of the most High, which saw the vision of the Almighty, falling into a trance, but having his eyes open:" (24:16) Read against verse 4, the additions and changes are clear: alike in hearing words, alike in falling with eyes open, one item is newly added — "knew the knowledge of the most High" (in the KJV, "saw the vision of the Almighty" stands in both verses, and the advance is this added knowing): by the fourth oracle of the three chapters, the prophet's self-introduction has advanced from hearing and seeing to knowing. Verse 17 is the summit of the whole chapter, quoted in full: "I shall see him, but not now: I shall behold him, but not nigh: there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth." (24:17) (The Chinese base text reads at the close of this verse, in its own wording, "destroy the children of tumult," where the KJV reads "destroy all the children of Sheth" — registered as it stands; see the discussion layer below.) Registered clause by clause: first the distance in time is set up — "I shall see him" and "I shall behold him" are present acts, but their object is "not now" and "not nigh": he can be seen, but not touched; beheld, but not waited for to arrive. Next the double image is set up — a Star comes out of Jacob, a Sceptre rises out of Israel: the Star is light in heaven, the Sceptre authority in the hand; one who comes out of this people is to appear with both at once. This verse gives him no name and no date; it gives only his source (Jacob / Israel) and his acts (smite, destroy). First in the list of objects struck in the second half stands Moab — the kingdom that paid to buy a curse has its name written first in the sentence. Verse 18, quoted in full: "And Edom shall be a possession, Seir also shall be a possession for his enemies; and Israel shall do valiantly." (24:18) Verse 19 closes the fourth oracle, quoted in full: "Out of Jacob shall come he that shall have dominion, and shall destroy him that remaineth of the city." (24:19) Whether "he that shall have dominion," who comes out of Jacob, is the same referent as the Star and the Sceptre of verse 17, this chapter adds no footnote; only the juxtaposition of the three within one passage is registered: the Star (v. 17), the possessor (the "he" of v. 18), and he that shall have dominion (v. 19); the narrative reads the three verses consecutively, and the identity of sameness is left to the discussion layer.

(Application) "But not now... but not nigh" — in the KJV's fuller wording, "I shall see him, but not now: I shall behold him, but not nigh" — are the words this section leaves to everyone who waits. Balaam saw with complete clarity, and had to own on the spot: this is not a matter of now, nor a matter of nigh. The hardest part of prophecy is not seeing inaccurately; it is seeing accurately and still having to wait. Balak could not wait — what he wanted was a curse that took effect today; Balaam finished the answer for him, and the answer hangs in the distance. We are often just as anxious over God's promises: we have plainly heard, plainly seen — why not now, why not nigh? This chapter's answer is very quiet: because that One's appearing stands on no man's timetable — not on Balak's military timetable, not within Balaam's professional career, not even within the years Balaam would live to see. God gave the farthest words to the man least able to wait to speak them first, to teach us this: the truth of a word is not proved by the speed of its fulfilment; the Star has not yet risen, and the Star is already real. The waiting people in the church today — waiting for sickness to be healed, for a child to turn back, for a situation to be reversed — should paste this verse on their hearts: the One you behold is not now, not nigh, and yet on the road of certain fulfilment. To see, and to be able to wait, is the lower half of faith's work; the upper half is seeing, the lower half is standing firm.

(New Testament Reading) Two New Testament places stand at a distance opposite this verse, set side by side at the reading layer only: Matthew records the wise men from the east arriving in Jerusalem and saying, "we have seen his star" (Matt 2:2, the point of the original wording) — a sign in the stars leading foreigners to come and worship the King of the Jews who had been born; and at the end of Revelation the Lord names himself "the bright and morning star" (Rev 22:16, the point of the original wording) — the Star has passed from an image in prophecy to a name he reports for himself. Both are registered at the reading layer only: the New Testament's reading is not poured back into this chapter — the Star of this chapter, within this chapter, points only to one who comes out of Jacob and smites Moab and has dominion; his name and his date this chapter nowhere states; the New Testament's unfolding is a later matter, and the reader registers here only the echo between the two texts, without reading the later revelation back as this chapter's fact.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "See" is rāʾâ (H7200); "behold" is šûr (H7789); "Star" is kôkāḇ (H3556); the original behind the Star's "come" is dārak (H1869), at root to step forth, to march; "Sceptre" is šēḇeṭ (H7626), carrying the senses of rod, sceptre, and tribe alike; "rise" is qûm (H6965); "smite" is māḥaṣ (H4272); "corners" is peʾâ (H6285), an edge, a border; "destroy" is the reduplicated form of qarqar (H6979), to tear down, to overturn. "Jacob" is Yaʿăqōḇ (H3290); "Israel" is Yiśrāʾēl (H3478); "Moab" is Môʾāḇ (H4124); the original behind "Sheth" (the Chinese base text renders the phrase "children of tumult") is Šēṯ (H8351, so registered on the interlinear page), and the dispute over its reading is kept in the discussion layer. "Knowledge" is dāʿaṯ (H1847), knowledge, understanding; "the most High" is ʿElyôn (H5945); "the Almighty" is Šadday (H7706). The numbers of verse 17 were checked word by word against the interlinear (strongs) page of BibleHub; the rest were cross-checked against lexicon pages (for the method, see Endnote 5).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two tensions in this section are set side by side, not harmonized. First, the reference of the "Star": the commentary tradition has at least three lines — the David-historical reading (taking David as the primary fulfilment: in 2 Samuel 8 David smites Moab and sets garrisons in Edom, matching the list of place-names in verse 18 of this chapter with startling closeness; this is registered as historical cross-reference only, see Endnote 6); the messianic-final reading (taking Star and Sceptre as referring exclusively to the King of the last days; the Jewish targum tradition and later Jewish movements both read it so, and the Christian tradition follows this line as well); and the double-reference reading (the near David and the far One both standing within this oracle's field of vision, near first and far after). This chapter's text gives source and acts only, no name and no date; at this chapter's level none of the three lines can be falsified, and none can be established from this chapter; the views are set out without adjudication. Second, "the children of Sheth" and "the children of tumult": the KJV reads "all the children of Sheth," the Chinese base text reads "the children of tumult," and the 2010 Chinese revision and several ancient versions read "the Sethites / the children of Seth" — the one word Šēṯ is understood in one line as the descendants of the proper name Seth, and in another as a noun of the same root as noise and tumult. The two readings coexist in the tradition of the versions; this sermon registers its own base text, and its argument in the sermon text uses only the undisputed first half, "shall smite the corners of Moab," building no doctrine on the reading of the second half.

(Fact) The fourth oracle's inward march is registered verse by verse in its order, as the base text stands: first the self-introduction is re-issued (24:15-16, its specification risen to "knew the knowledge"); next the distance in time is set up (24:17a, seeing and beholding, not now and not nigh); next the double image and the first enemy (24:17b, Star, Sceptre, Moab); then the borders are spread out (24:18, Edom and Seir as a possession); and it closes at last with him that shall have dominion (24:19, one out of Jacob, destroying him that remaineth). This order stands in exact contrast with the third oracle: the third begins from the present camp (24:5), the fourth from the Star to come (24:17); the third's enemy is the general "the nations his enemies" (24:8), the fourth's enemies are named — Moab, Edom, Seir. By the fourth oracle the map is already spread open and the list already written — and all of this is spoken on the road, after the employer has already been dismissed and as the prophet is about to go home. The farthest words come out at the most scattered moment; if Balak had sent him away at verse 14, he would not have heard this passage. That he stayed and heard it through is the one right thing he did in these three chapters — though having heard it, he was powerless all the same.

### 5. The First of the Nations Shall Perish For Ever: The Last Three Oracles and the Parting (Numbers 24:20-25, with the Whole Chapter) (52:00-60:00)

(Fact) Verse 20 is the first short oracle, quoted in full: "And when he looked on Amalek, he took up his parable, and said, Amalek was the first of the nations; but his latter end shall be that he perish for ever." (24:20) "He looked on" is registered: the third and fourth oracles looked at Israel and at the Star; the last three oracles look at the nations — the object of looking has changed, and the character of the sentence changes with it: for Israel, blessing and a future; for the nations, an end. "the first of the nations" and "he perish for ever" stand side by side in one sentence: the highest rank and a perishing end are not contradictory; they are the two halves of one sentence. Verses 21-22 are the second short oracle, quoted in full: "And he looked on the Kenites, and took up his parable, and said, Strong is thy dwellingplace, and thou puttest thy nest in a rock." (24:21) "Nevertheless the Kenite shall be wasted, until Asshur shall carry thee away captive." (24:22) First its strength is allowed (dwellingplace, nest in a rock), then the sentence of wasting is pronounced — the strength is real, and the wasting is real too; a rock can keep out soldiers and horses, but it cannot keep out the years; "until Asshur shall carry thee away captive" gives the end-form of the wasting: not a swift death, but a carrying away after gradual decline. Verses 23-24 are the third short oracle, opening with an exclamation, quoted in full: "And he took up his parable, and said, Alas, who shall live when God doeth this!" (24:23) "And ships shall come from the coast of Chittim, and shall afflict Asshur, and shall afflict Eber, and he also shall perish for ever." (24:24) Registered: this is Balaam's only sigh in the three chapters ("Alas"); the chain of affliction runs — ships come from Chittim and afflict Asshur and Eber, and he who does the afflicting "also shall perish for ever": the judge is himself under judgement, and no party can stand outside the chain of perishing. Verse 25 is the parting, quoted in full: "And Balaam rose up, and went and returned to his place: and Balak also went his way." (24:25) The whole chapter, the whole act, closes here: no bargain struck, no reconciliation, no final payment — two men who came carrying one and the same order each go home; on the hill there remain only twenty-one empty altars (chapter 23) and seven oracles nobody ordered (chapters 23-24 together).

(Application) The last three oracles give everyone who "looks at rankings" a sentence of cold water. Amalek was the first of the nations, and it was the first to march out against Israel at the exodus (Exodus 17, cross-reference layer) — its high rank and its early hand are both written in the record; yet its sentence is only this: he perish for ever. The Kenites have a nest in a rock and old dealings with Israel (the Judges cross-reference layer is registered), and their sentence is that they shall be wasted. Men look at the world by its leader-board: who stands first, who is strong, whose nest is set in a rock. God, through Balaam's mouth, reads the leader-board backwards: the first perishes, the strong is wasted, and he who executes judgement also perishes for ever. This is not to say rank means nothing; it is to say rank does not constitute the end — the end is written only in God's word. If we stake our security on rank (of an industry, of wealth, of reputation), we are the men who set their nest in a rock: the rock is real, and Asshur's fleet is also on the road. The believer's security was never in rank; it is in that one sentence already spoken: a Star shall come out of Jacob — our future does not hang on where we rank now, but on whose side the Ruler who is to come stands.

(Summary) The words of this section are stated as philology only. "Looked on" continues the use of the rāʾâ (H7200) group, the same word as "see" at verse 17. "Perish" belongs to the ʾāḇaḏ (H6) group, to perish, to vanish, occurring twice in this section (24:20, 24); the "wasted" of verse 22 is a different verb, registered separately in the base text's rendering. "Asshur" is ʾAššûr (H804); "Eber" is ʿĒḇer (H5677); "Chittim" is Kittîm (H3794); "Amalek" is ʿĂmālēq (H6002); the "Kenites / Kenite" is Qênî (H7017). The word-form behind "nest" and the number of "Cain" (the form at 24:22) are marked in this sermon as not yet verified; see Endnote 5. All numbers given above were verified one by one before writing (for the method, see Endnote 5).

(Scholarly and Historical Discussion) Two points in the last three oracles are set out without adjudication. First, the reference of "Chittim": in the original, this name points first to a maritime land in the region of Cyprus, and later readings extrapolate it layer by layer — some take it of the Greek world, some of Rome, and the "ships of Chittim" of Daniel 11:30 are read as Rome in later versions (cross-reference discussion layer). This chapter registers only the water-route direction, "ships shall come from the coast of Chittim," and the chain of affliction, adopting no school's historical equation; the views are kept at this layer. Second, the relation of the last three oracles to the whole chapter: scholarship discusses at length the date of composition and the editorial place of these three short oracles — some regard them as a later-added appendix, others as the act's original close. This sermon stands only on the narrative fact of the canonical text: their place in this chapter is after the fourth oracle and before the parting, and their function is to push the field of vision from Israel the one people out to the end of the nations — no chronological conclusion is adopted as a premise.

(Fact) The whole chapter's data are closed, every item counted (for the details, see Endnote 4): the chapter has 25 verses in all; the sections run 2 (24:1-2), 7 (24:3-9), 5 (24:10-14), 5 (24:15-19), and 6 (24:20-25) verses respectively, totalling 25. The oracles are five in all: the third oracle (24:3-9), the fourth oracle (24:15-19), and the last three short oracles (24:20, 21-22, 23-24); "he took up his parable" occurs five times (24:3, 15, 20, 21, 23). Balaam's self-introduction occurs twice (24:3-4, 15-16); the KJV reads "whose eyes are open" twice (24:3, 15; the Chinese base text reads "whose eyes are closed" at both places) and "having his eyes open," of falling, twice (24:4, 16). "He looked on" a nation occurs twice (24:20, 21). "Perish for ever" occurs twice (24:20, 24), with verse 22 reading "shall be wasted" besides. In this chapter altars are built zero times, meetings sought zero times, and a word "put in his mouth" zero times — all three procedures of chapter 23 are absent, and in their place stands "the spirit of God came upon him," once (24:2). The speakers are still two men only: Balak speaks twice (24:10-11, and after verse 14 has no words), Balaam throughout the chapter; the Israelites, across the three chapters together, have not a single line. God has no direct speech quoted in this chapter — his ways of drawing near across the three chapters complete their three forms here: through the ass and the angel (chapter 22), meeting and putting a word in the mouth (chapter 23), the spirit coming upon him (chapter 24). The words grow freer and freer; the sovereignty yields not a fraction.

(Application) The chapter closes by leaving one sentence each for three kinds of men. First, for the Balak kind of man: settle your account clearly — three chapters, twenty-one altars, the promise of a house full of silver and gold, and the final harvest is one sentence: "Balak also went his way." The man who places orders with God ends by going home empty-handed; tear up the order early, and change to the position of verse 9: come and stand on the side of blessing, and the blessing will come in at the door by itself. Second, for the Balaam kind of man: across these three chapters your mouth was kept beyond reproach — you refused the house full of silver and gold, you did not speak the curse, you spoke the blessing in full, and even the farthest words you spoke for God to the end. The remaining lesson is in the feet: the road back to your place is long, and what you will meet on the road and what you will decide, this chapter does not write; the later text has its own record (registered at the fact layer). To keep the mouth is only to pass; to walk the feet aright is to graduate — may every step of your return match every sentence you spoke here. Third, for the men in the camp: across three chapters you spoke not a sentence, dismantled not an altar, struck not an enemy — the battle was all fought on the hill, the blessing all spoken on the hill, and your duty from beginning to end was one thing only: abide in your tents according to your tribes, and encamp. The Star is already written into the words; it is not now, it is not nigh, but it shall come out of your midst. Keep your tents well, live your days well, and when the Star rises, let the camp still be in its place — that is enough.

## Devotional Excerpt

This time Balaam built no altar and sought no enchantments. He only set his face toward the wilderness, lifted up his eyes for one look at Israel's camp, and the spirit of God came upon him; and the first sentence out of his mouth was: "How goodly are thy tents, O Jacob, and thy tabernacles, O Israel!" (24:5) When we look at our own lives we often see only the disorder: problems unsolved, faults uncorrected, a wilderness unfinished. But when God looks at his own people he sees goodliness — not because we are good, but because he dwells in our midst, and so the camp has order and the tents have light. If today you think yourself nothing but a torn tent, remember: the one who assesses you is not the man at the foot of the hill counting problems, but the God who dwells in the camp. And he has left a farther word besides: "there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel" (24:17). The Star is not now, it is not nigh, and yet it is already written and fixed. Your present condition is not your end, and your wilderness is not your destination; he that shall have dominion shall come out from among the people of this God whom you believe, and when his time comes nothing can hinder him. Today's lesson is very simple: seek enchantments no more, and use your own means no more to press God; face him, lift up your eyes to what he has already accomplished, encamp, and wait for the Star to rise. When the camp is at rest, the Star draws near.

English (KJV-based, as it stands in the companion Chinese manuscript):

"I shall see him, but not now: I shall behold him, but not nigh: there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth." (Num 24:17, KJV) Balaam saw him from a distance — not now, not nigh — and still the word stood. What God has promised over your life may not be for today or tomorrow, but a star written into His word does not fall. Stop striving to force the timing; encamp in His order, and wait for the Star to rise.

Geography and line of sight, checked station by station against the base text: This chapter opens continuing 23:28, the top of Peor (24:1 does not name the place again, recording only that Balaam "set his face toward the wilderness"); the line of sight runs in order: he lifted up his eyes and saw the whole camp abiding according to their tribes (24:2); he beheld him that is not now and not nigh, the Star (24:17); he looked on Amalek and the Kenites (24:20-21); he saw ships coming from the coast of Chittim (24:24); and it closes with the two men each returning to his place (24:25). The total arc of sight across the three chapters: in chapter 22 he sees the edge of the camp, in chapter 23 a corner of it, in chapter 24 the whole camp, the Star, and the end of the nations — what is seen travels farther and farther, and the order is emptied further and further.
8. Blanks of this chapter, registered as blanks: The manner and the process in detail of the spirit of God's coming upon him are not stated in this chapter; only "came upon him" is recorded. The name and the date of him to whom the Star and the Sceptre point are not stated in this chapter. The exact date of the "hereafter" is not stated in this chapter. Balaam's course after he went to his people, and Balak's after he went his way to Moab, are not carried in this chapter (the later text records them separately; this sermon does not extrapolate). Whether Balak's bounty was paid at all between the five oracles is not recorded in this chapter. The above are registered as blanks only and are not filled in by extrapolation.

# The Secret Things Belong unto the LORD — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 29

> Status: First translation · Initial translation pending proofreading (首译·初译待校). This is the first English translation of the finalized Chinese sermon on Deuteronomy 29, "The Secret Things," which passed the Chinese strong re-review (ZH 强复核) on 2026-10-25. All Scripture quotations in this English text are taken verbatim, including punctuation, from the King James Version (KJV), which serves as the base text for quotations in this translation; quotations are never re-translated from the Chinese. The KJV chapter text follows the chapter anchor (Bolls.Life KJV, Deuteronomy 29, stored as 申29-EN-KJV.txt; see the production record at the end); the marginal glosses carried in that anchor file (e.g. at vv. 12, 17, 18, 19, 21, 22, 26) are not part of the quoted text and are not reproduced inside quotations. Hebrew transliterations and Strong's numbers are carried over from the Chinese source as registered there, including its correction of nātaš to H5428. Counts are re-counted from the KJV text itself and registered on the KJV's own account; where the English form differs from the Chinese fixed text's form, both are stated side by side and neither is smoothed over (see note 2 and the production record).

> Verification of the title: Three candidates were considered. (A) "The Secret Things Belong unto the LORD," from the closing sentence of verse 29, "The secret things belong unto the LORD our God" — the seal of the whole chapter: the first twenty-eight verses set out the words of the covenant, the witnesses to the covenant, and the warnings of the covenant one by one, and the final verse then draws a line — some things belong to God, and some things belong to man; the title takes the chapter's landing point. (B) "Ye Stand This Day All of You before the LORD Your God," from the assembly sentence of verse 10 — the chapter's scene. (C) "Lest There Should Be among You a Root That Beareth Gall and Wormwood," from the warning sentence of verse 18 — the chapter's cutting edge. Candidate (A) is adopted: the assembly of verse 10 is the scene, the root of verse 18 is the warning, and verse 29 is where the chapter arrives — after seeing so much and standing so long, it lands on "those things which are revealed belong unto us... that we may do." The English title follows the wording of KJV 29:29.

> Note on the format: This sermon consists of an introduction, four sections of body text, and a conclusion; the body covers 29:1–29, and the time codes run continuously over sixty minutes (Introduction 00:00–06:00; Section One 06:00–19:00, the retrospect and the evidences, vv. 1–9; Section Two 19:00–32:00, the assembly and the making of the covenant, vv. 10–15; Section Three 32:00–45:00, the warning and the evil root, vv. 16–21; Section Four 45:00–53:00, the questions of later generations and the secret things belong to the LORD, vv. 22–29; Conclusion 53:00–60:00). Within each section the writing is marked in five layers: the factual layer, the theological layer, the summary layer, the New Testament reading layer, and the scholarly and historical discussion layer. Four adjudication disciplines: (1) the readings of 29:4, "hath not given you an heart to perceive," are stated side by side only, not adjudicated; (2) the three renderings of the last clause of 29:19 are stated side by side, not adjudicated; (3) the reference of 29:29 and the intent of the scribal pointing are stated side by side in their several readings, not adjudicated, and its relation to the doctrine of predestination is registered only; (4) the prohibition on present-day transplantation (note 9): no curse of this chapter may be transplanted into a present-day assertion about any individual's or group's sickness, calamity, poverty, or wealth; the application of the "evil root" section is limited to the pastoral form of congregational self-examination and may not be used as a warrant for identifying others. ## Introduction (00:00–06:00)

[Summary layer] Chapter 28 writes out the blessings and the curses item by item to the end; chapter 29, however, does not go on writing rewards and punishments — it calls a meeting: Moses gathers all the people to stand, and makes the covenant publicly. The difference of tone between the two chapters is registered first: chapter 28 is throughout in future conditional sentences, "if thou... then thou shalt...," words spoken to a generation that has not yet entered the land; chapter 29 draws the future back into the present day — the evidences in the past tense (ye have seen), the assembly in the present tense (ye stand this day), the warning in the "lest" form (lest there should be among you), and only the question-and-answer is released to the future (the generation to come shall say). Four tenses stand complete within one chapter; this is the chapter's literary skeleton. This chapter is the ritual text of the covenant in Moab — before it, forty years of history as evidences (vv. 1–9); in the middle, a roll of all the members as witnesses (vv. 10–15); after it, a warning directed inward (vv. 16–21) and a question-and-answer for later generations (vv. 22–28); and it closes with one sentence that draws a boundary (v. 29). [Factual layer] The chapter's place in the whole book is registered in two items: (1) this chapter is the concluding part of Moses' second discourse since 1:5, joined head-to-tail with the list of rewards and punishments in chapter 28 — chapter 28 states the consequences of the covenant, chapter 29 performs the rite of the covenant; (2) the place of the covenant-making and its relation, as the chapter itself reports them, are in verse 1: the land of Moab, beside the covenant in Horeb — this is the self-report, within Deuteronomy, of a second covenant-making moment (note 6). [Theological layer] The course of this sermon's four sections: Section One reads the evidences — the covenant stands upon "ye have seen all," yet at verse 4 it runs head-on into the hardest sentence in the chapter; Section Two reads the roll — who stands within the covenant, counting in even those who are not present; Section Three reads the warning — the danger is not outside the camp but in a root in the midst of the assembly; Section Four reads the question-and-answer and the boundary — later generations will surely ask, and the last sentence of the answer is not an explanation but a boundary: the secret things belong to God, the revealed things belong to us. This sermon's argument rests only on the text itself: the recurrence of its verbs (see, seven times; this day, seven times), the order of its lists, the nesting of its quotations — wherever a reading is disputed, the readings are stated side by side and not adjudicated (disciplines one to three).

## Section One (06:00–19:00) Ye Have Seen All (29:1–9)

[Factual layer] Verse 1 is the chapter's narrative heading; it stands outside the quotation marks of the discourse, and is given in full from the base text: "These are the words of the covenant, which the LORD commanded Moses to make with the children of Israel in the land of Moab, beside the covenant which he made with them in Horeb." Registered item by item: (1) the verbal subject of the covenant-making is the LORD commanding and Moses executing — the initiating party of the covenant is marked first in this verse; (2) "beside the covenant which he made with them in Horeb" — the covenant in Moab reports itself as a further covenant-making, registered alongside the covenant in Horeb; the text locates the relation between the two covenants with the single word "beside," and this sermon does not develop it further (note 6); (3) this verse is Masoretic 28:69 (note 1): the Masora attaches this verse to the end of chapter 28, the Chinese and English to the head of chapter 29 — this verse is the hinge between the two chapters, and its numbering form is registered as such. [Factual layer] Verses 2–3 open the list of evidences; the discourse's quotation marks open at verse 2, given in full from the base text: "And Moses called unto all Israel, and said unto them, Ye have seen all that the LORD did before your eyes in the land of Egypt unto Pharaoh, and unto all his servants, and unto all his land; The great temptations which thine eyes have seen, the signs, and those great miracles:" Registered item by item: (1) "see" is the chapter's backbone verb, occurring seven times in the chapter in the Chinese fixed text (vv. 2, 3, 4, 17, 22, 23, 24; note 2), and its first two occurrences are here — the first foundation stone of the covenant is the eye: Pharaoh, all his servants, and all his land, three levels of objects, all of them things that happened "before your eyes"; on the KJV's own account the see-forms stand at five verse-positions (vv. 2, 3, 4, 17, 22), the two accounts stated side by side in note 2; (2) the three designations of verse 3 are counted by hand: the great temptations (massâ, H4531), the signs (ʾôt, H226), and those great miracles (môpêt, H4159) — temptation, sign, and miracle are the three characterisations of the exodus events; (3) a detail of sentence form: verse 2 closes with a comma in the Chinese fixed text and the sentence runs on into verse 3, and no closing quotation mark is seen in the CUNPSS simplified line at verses 2–9 (note 4); (4) the position of the witness is registered: in the syntax of verse 2, Israel is throughout the watcher — the one who acts is the LORD, those acted upon are Pharaoh, his servants, and all the land of Egypt, and those who watch and bear the effect are "ye": the "all" of "Ye have seen all" gathers in the whole people, and not one of them has it by hearsay — the evidence of the first covenant generation is first-hand experience, not tradition; and this is precisely why, when verse 15 extends the covenant to "him that is not here," it must say so expressly: the way the present generation holds its evidence cannot be reproduced by later generations, yet the covenant is to bind later generations all the same. [Factual layer] Verse 4 is the chapter's pivotal difficulty, given in full from the base text: "Yet the LORD hath not given you an heart to perceive, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, unto this day." Registered item by item: (1) the triad — an heart to perceive (lēbāb, H3824), eyes to see, ears to hear: verses 2–3 have just said "Ye have seen all," and verse 4 says the eyes have not been given to see — the same pair of eyes, the seeing of outward appearance and the perceiving of the heart, are set squarely apart within this chapter; (2) "hath not given you... unto this day" — the subject of the verb is the LORD, in the negative form: "hath not given" is the original form of this verse; its readings are stated side by side in this section's discussion layer, and this sermon does not adjudicate (discipline one); (3) "this day" occurs seven times in the chapter in the Chinese fixed text (vv. 4, 10, 12, 13, 15, 18, 28; note 2), its first occurrence here: the day of the covenant-making is first marked by an unresolved knot. On the KJV's own account "this day / to day" occurs eight times, the eighth being the second occurrence in verse 15 (note 2). [Factual layer] Verses 5–6 are the evidences of the wilderness, given in full from the base text: "And I have led you forty years in the wilderness: your clothes are not waxen old upon you, and thy shoe is not waxen old upon thy foot. Ye have not eaten bread, neither have ye drunk wine or strong drink: that ye might know that I am the LORD your God." Registered item by item: (1) the two items of clothing and shoe — the clothes (śalmâ, H8008) are not waxen old (bālâ, H1086), the shoe (naʿal, H5275) is not waxen old: the things of daily use over forty years have not worn out; this is an evidence that could be inspected every day; (2) the two negative items of verse 6: ye have not eaten bread (leḥem, H3899), neither have ye drunk wine (yayin, H3196) or strong drink (šēkār, H7941) — the supply of the wilderness was not the produce of ordinary agriculture; (3) the purpose clause is written plainly: "that ye might know that I am the LORD your God" — "know" occurs twice in the chapter in the Chinese fixed text (vv. 6, 16; note 2): the supply itself is characterised as teaching material for knowing God; read alongside verse 4: heart, eyes, and ears not given, and a supply meant "that ye might know" — the two sentences stand in the same chapter, and the tension is registered only; (4) the person-form is registered separately: in this purpose clause the CUNPSS reads in third-person report ("the LORD is your God"), while the KJV reads in the first-person self-report form, "that ye might know that I am the LORD your God" — in one purpose clause the Chinese and English base texts differ in person, registered in note 3. [Factual layer] Verses 7–9 are the evidences east of the Jordan and the close of the retrospect section, given in full from the base text: "And when ye came unto this place, Sihon the king of Heshbon, and Og the king of Bashan, came out against us unto battle, and we smote them: And we took their land, and gave it for an inheritance unto the Reubenites, and to the Gadites, and to the half tribe of Manasseh. Keep therefore the words of this covenant, and do them, that ye may prosper in all that ye do." Registered item by item: (1) the two kings — Sihon the king of Heshbon and Og the king of Bashan: the evidence of the battles is the most recent stretch, on this very ground before their eyes; (2) "we took their land, and gave it for an inheritance" — the land has already been divided first to the two and a half tribes: the oath of the covenant has not yet been taken, and part of the covenant's inheritance has already been delivered in advance; the fact runs ahead of the rite; (3) verse 9 closes the retrospect section with "therefore": there is only one command — "Keep therefore the words of this covenant, and do them"; the result is "that ye may prosper in all that ye do" (śākal, H7919, in its causative form) — the retrospect section begins with evidences and ends with a command, and everything between is evidence; (4) at the end of Masoretic 29:8 (i.e. this verse) a petuhah is seen (note 1): the retrospect section closes as a unit of its own within the Masoretic paragraph markers. [Theological layer] In Section One, hold on to the order of the evidences: first the distant matter of the exodus (vv. 2–3), then the daily matter of the forty years in the wilderness (vv. 5–6), and last the near matter of the two kings east of the river (vv. 7–8) — three layers, distant and near, all of them "ye have seen" and "ye know." But verse 4 is stuck in the middle: they have seen, yet He has not given them to perceive. This chapter does not evade that knot: the party to the covenant is not a company of people who already perceive in full; it is a company that has seen the evidences and must still be called to stand, and warned against the evil root. The solemnity of the covenant lies not in the completeness of the hearers but in the certainty of the evidences and the plainness of the words spoken. [Summary layer] This section falls in four points: (1) the narrative heading (v. 1, the covenant in Moab's self-report); (2) the evidences of the eyes (vv. 2–3, seeing and the three designations); (3) the heart not given (v. 4, the pivotal difficulty of heart, eyes, and ears); (4) the evidences of daily life (vv. 5–9, clothes and shoe, food and drink, the two kings and the division of the land, closing with "Keep therefore... and do them"). [New Testament reading layer] This section registers one index only and does not read backwards: the triad vocabulary of verse 4, "an heart to perceive, and eyes to see, and ears to hear," belongs to one reading tradition with the kindred prophetic vocabulary the New Testament cites repeatedly (the Isaiah 6:9–10 system); the New Testament authors read Israel's spiritual dullness in their own generation by that tradition (the index of the relevant passages is registered only; this sermon has not verified them word by word first-hand and does not quote them) — the reading relation is registered as such, and on the level of this verse's original meaning its subject and negative form stand as registered in the factual layer. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] This section states the three readings of verse 4 side by side: (1) the sovereignty reading — "hath not given" is taken literally as God's withholding, and perceiving or not perceiving lies within God's sovereign apportioning; (2) the rhetorical reading — the sentence is a word of rebuke: the evidence sufficed and the receiving never came to birth, and "hath not given" is a verdict-form statement upon forty years of stubbornness; (3) the generational reading — the sentence closes the account of the wilderness generation: that generation died without receiving, a new generation stands in Moab, and the words are a warning to the new generation. The three readings are stated side by side, not adjudicated (note 8).

## Section Two (19:00–32:00) Ye Stand This Day All of You before the LORD Your God (29:10–15)

[Factual layer] Verses 10–11 are the chapter's roll; the quotation of the assembly's words opens at verse 10 (note 4), given in full from the base text: "Ye stand this day all of you before the LORD your God; your captains of your tribes, your elders, and your officers, with all the men of Israel, Your little ones, your wives, and thy stranger that is in thy camp, from the hewer of thy wood unto the drawer of thy water:" Registered item by item: (1) the list's eight items, counted by hand in the Chinese fixed text — captains, tribal heads, elders, officers, all the men of Israel (the five items of government and of all the males), your little ones and wives (ṭap, H2945), thy stranger that is in thy camp (gēr, H1616), and the hewer of thy wood unto the drawer of thy water (ḥāṭab, H2404; šāʾab, H7579): from the captains who give the orders down to the lowliest hired hands in the camp, the assembly of the covenant has no spectators' gallery; in the KJV the list runs "your captains of your tribes, your elders, and your officers, with all the men of Israel, Your little ones, your wives, and thy stranger that is in thy camp, from the hewer of thy wood unto the drawer of thy water," and the item-division follows the Chinese registration, stated side by side in note 3; (2) the parenthesis "(in the original, 'tribes')" is the CUNPSS's own note, reproduced as written — the original word at that place is precisely "tribes," and the 2010 edition's text reads "your several leaders, tribal heads, elders, and officers" (note 3); (3) "stand" (niṣṣāb, H5324) occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 10, 15; note 2): standing is the posture-verb of covenant-making; the KJV reads "Ye stand" at verse 10 and "standeth" at verse 15, two occurrences on its own account as well; (4) the verse-boundary form: the CUNPSS contains the second half of verse 11's list within its line for verse 10 (note 1), while the 2010 edition divides the verses as verse 10 the government roll and verse 11 the little ones, wives, and stranger — the two division forms are registered side by side. [Factual layer] Verses 12–13 are the act of covenant-making and its effect, given in full from the base text: "That thou shouldest enter into covenant with the LORD thy God, and into his oath, which the LORD thy God maketh with thee this day: That he may establish thee to day for a people unto himself, and that he may be unto thee a God, as he hath said unto thee, and as he hath sworn unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob." Registered item by item: (1) the factual form of the singular-plural alternation: at verses 12–13 the party to the covenant is addressed in the singular "thou" (the KJV reads thou/thee), and from verse 14 the plural "you" resumes — the form of a collectivity addressed in the singular is registered as such, and its readings are stated side by side in this section's discussion layer; (2) "That thou shouldest enter into... covenant" — the original verb ʿābar (H5674) means at root "to pass over, to pass by," and is here understood as "to enter into covenant," the same word as "we came through the nations" in verse 16 (note 5): one and the same "passing" — in one place passing through the nations, in another entering into the covenant; (3) covenant and oath are named together: the covenant (bərît, H1285) and his oath (ʾālâ, H423) — the latter turns, at verses 19–21, to its "curse" face (note 5); one and the same word has two faces in this chapter: the oath that is sworn, and the curse of breaking the covenant. Further, "this day" occurs in two successive verses in this section (v. 12, "maketh with thee this day"; v. 13, "establish thee to day" — the third and fourth of the chapter's occurrences on the Chinese account, note 2): the making of the covenant and the establishing as a people are nailed to one and the same "this day" — the covenant of this chapter is not a recounting of past events but an act of the present day; (4) verse 13 is the chapter's covenant-formula sentence: "That he may establish thee to day for a people unto himself, and that he may be unto thee a God" — establish (qûm, H6965, in its causative form); the three names of the fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, are registered name by name: today's covenant-making is expressly tied to the old oath to the fathers. [Factual layer] Verses 14–15 are the roll's extension in time, given in full from the base text: "Neither with you only do I make this covenant and this oath; But with him that standeth here with us this day before the LORD our God, and also with him that is not here with us this day:" Registered item by item: (1) "Neither with you only do I make" — at verse 14 the speaker of the discourse refers to himself in the first person, "I"; the first person within the discourse is also seen at verse 5 ("I have led you forty years in the wilderness") and at verse 15 ("do I make this covenant and this oath" in the KJV's carrying of the sentence), three places in all, each the form of Moses speaking in his capacity as the covenant's mediator, registered for the record; (2) the two classes of persons set in parallel at verse 15: those present (him that standeth here with us this day before the LORD our God) and those not present (him that is not here with us this day) — the fifth of the chapter's "this day" occurrences on the Chinese account and the second of its two "stand" occurrences are both in this verse: those who are not present are counted within the standing by the same verb; (3) the KJV has "this day" twice in this verse, and the difference from the CUNPSS's one occurrence is registered in note 2. [Theological layer] In this section, hold on to the roll's two directions: horizontally, to the bottom — captains and drawers of water in one list; the covenant leaves no exemption by rank; vertically, to the far distance — persons not yet born in one list; the covenant sets no exemption by time. Deuteronomy 29:15 is the chapter's most singular inclusion: a person's boundness can be established before his existence does, and the place in the text of every later reader of this chapter has already been written by this verse — "him that is not here with us this day." This also explains the severity of Section Three's warning: the harm of an evil root never injures the present generation only. One further axis may be added: inequality of inheritance does not divide the inside of the covenant from its outside — in the narrative order, the land-inheritance of the two and a half tribes east of the river is delivered first (vv. 7–8), and the assembly of all the members opens only afterwards (v. 10): those who have already received land and those who have not yet received land stand in one and the same roll and hear one and the same set of warnings. The inside and outside of the covenant are not drawn by inheritance; they are drawn only by standing. [Summary layer] This section falls in four points: (1) the roll of all members (vv. 10–11, the eight items registered); (2) entering into the covenant (v. 12, the singular "thou," with covenant and oath named together); (3) established for a people unto himself (v. 13, the formula sentence and the oath to the fathers); (4) reaching those who are not here (vv. 14–15, the covenant's extension in time). [New Testament reading layer] This section registers one index only and does not read backwards: the sentence-structure of "those not present are also within the covenant" is used repeatedly in later covenant discourse as a textual basis for the covenant's continuance across generations (the relevant index is registered only; this sermon has not verified the relevant New Testament passages word by word and does not quote them) — this sermon points only to the textual form: the inclusion sentence of verse 15 is Deuteronomy's own statement. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] This section states two readings side by side: (1) two readings of the singular-plural alternation — the collective-personality reading (Israel is regarded as a single covenant party, and the singular is the ancient manner of collective address) and the composite-redaction reading (the singular and plural strata display the text's composite origin), stated side by side, not adjudicated; (2) two readings of the reference of "him that is not here with us this day" in verse 15 — it refers either to descendants not yet born, or to contemporary members not present on that occasion (such as those of the two and a half tribes already settled east of the river who could not attend); the two readings are stated side by side, and this sermon's theological layer uses only its textual form and does not depend on a settled verdict.

## Section Three (32:00–45:00) Lest There Should Be among You a Root That Beareth Gall and Wormwood (29:16–21)

[Factual layer] Verses 16–17 are the origin of the warning, given in full from the base text (the parenthesis is the KJV's own parenthesis at this place, and the quotation follows the KJV's text): "(For ye know how we have dwelt in the land of Egypt; and how we came through the nations which ye passed by; And ye have seen their abominations, and their idols, wood and stone, silver and gold, which were among them:)" Registered item by item: (1) at verse 16 the CUNPSS simplified line opens a new set of quotation marks for a short retrospect (in the modern-punctuation edition it is set as a parenthesis; note 3) — "we have dwelt in Egypt, and came through the nations" stands at a distance opposite the list of evidences in Section One: the same stretch of road has on one side God's acts (vv. 2–9) and on the other the idols men have seen (v. 17); (2) "ye know" is here the second of the chapter's two occurrences of "know" on the Chinese account: what is known is not only God's supply but also the nations' abominations; on the KJV's own account the know-forms stand at three verse-positions (vv. 6, 16, and "knew not" at v. 26), registered in note 2; (3) verse 17's "ye have seen" is the fourth of the chapter's seven "see" occurrences on the Chinese account, and its object is reversed — their abominations (šiqqûṣ, H8251) and their idols (gillul, H1544), with the four materials counted by hand: wood and stone, silver and gold; the eyes saw in Egypt and among the nations another class of "great" things, the display of idols; (4) at verse 17 no closing mark answering the opening mark of verse 16 is seen in the CUNPSS simplified line; the form is registered in note 3. [Factual layer] Verse 18 is the chapter's cutting edge, given in full from the base text: "Lest there should be among you man, or woman, or family, or tribe, whose heart turneth away this day from the LORD our God, to go and serve the gods of these nations; lest there should be among you a root that beareth gall and wormwood;" Registered item by item: (1) the doubled "lest... lest" — the warning is issued in the "lest" sentence-form, and its object is the interior of the assembly: man, or woman, or family, or tribe, four classes counted by hand, answering exactly the eight-item roll of verse 10: not one person on the roll is absent from the warning list; (2) "whose heart turneth away this day" — the sixth of the chapter's "this day" occurrences on the Chinese account: the tense of turning away is not the future but this day; (3) the evil-root imagery, registered word by word: root (šoreš, H8328), gall (roʾš, H7219 — a homograph of rōʾš, H7218, "head," a different word with the same form; this sermon, following the lexicons, establishes the poisonous-herb sense as H7219 and registers it in a separate entry), wormwood (laʿănâ, H3939): the root is underground and the herb above ground; the warning's botany is this — calamity first has a root that cannot be seen, and only then leaves that can; the KJV renders roʾš as "gall"; (4) renderings registered separately: the CUNPSS simplified line reads "serve" and "wormwood" in one set of character forms and the traditional line in another; the 2010 edition reads "or clan or tribe" (without the word "head/chief") and "a root that groweth gall and wormwood" (without the word "evil") — registered separately in note 3. [Factual layer] Verse 19 is the individual's self-congratulation, given in full from the base text: "And it come to pass, when he heareth the words of this curse, that he bless himself in his heart, saying, I shall have peace, though I walk in the imagination of mine heart, to add drunkenness to thirst:" Registered item by item: (1) this verse is the chapter's only first-person inner monologue in one place (embedded in single quotation marks in the Chinese fixed text; note 4): the words of this curse (ʾālâ, H423, the same word as "oath" in v. 12) have just been read out, and there is one way of hearing them that still blesses itself; (2) "the imagination of mine heart" — śǝrirût (H8307), the stubbornness of the heart; the heart is the same organ, in two states, as the "heart to perceive" of verse 4; (3) the direct-rendering forms of the last clause are registered separately: the CUNPSS reads "involving the many in my ruin, yet I shall still have peace"; the KJV reads "to add drunkenness to thirst"; the 2010 edition reads "so that the watered and the dry are alike swept away" — the original verb sāpâ (H5595) carries both a "sweep away" and an "add" reading; the three readings are stated side by side in note 7, not adjudicated. [Factual layer] Verses 20–21 are the warning's landing, given in full from the base text: "The LORD will not spare him, but then the anger of the LORD and his jealousy shall smoke against that man, and all the curses that are written in this book shall lie upon him, and the LORD shall blot out his name from under heaven. And the LORD shall separate him unto evil out of all the tribes of Israel, according to all the curses of the covenant that are written in this book of the law:" Registered item by item: (1) "will not spare" — the chapter's heaviest negative sentence, with its subject the LORD appearing three times (the divine name occurs three times in v. 20; note 2); (2) "the anger of the LORD and his jealousy" — jealousy in the original is qinʾâ (H7068), the covenant God's jealous zeal; "shall smoke" is the KJV's form, and the imagery of the anger is smoke; (3) "all the curses" — two of the chapter's four occurrences of the curse-word are in these two verses (vv. 19, 20, 21, 27; note 2): the curses are no longer items on a list; they "lie upon him"; (4) "blot out his name" (māḥâ, H4229) and "separate him" (bādal, H914) are registered side by side: elsewhere in this book the word "separate" is mostly used of separating unto God (registered as an index, not quoted), and here it is used of separating unto evil — the same word in reverse use, its form as such; (5) the designation "book" occurs twice here — "in this book" (v. 20) and "in this book of the law" (v. 21); "book" occurs three times in the chapter (vv. 20, 21, 27; note 2): the basis of the warning is throughout the book that is written down; (6) the landing-forms of the curses are registered: against the individual, verse 20 reads "shall lie upon him"; against the whole land, verse 27 reads "to bring upon it" — one and the same body of "all the curses," landing on a person as lying upon him and on the land as brought upon it, two landing-points used at two places in this chapter and not written confusedly. [Theological layer] In Section Three, hold on to the warning's address: it is not written to people outside the camp but to people who have just stood through the roll. The danger of the evil root lies not in the leaf but in the root — at the root stage it can neither be heard nor seen, and by the time the gall grows out, it is already a patch. Verse 19's self-blesser gives the root's confession: it is not that he does not know the curses; he has heard them and still issues himself a guarantee of peace. This chapter's warning is therefore not a problem of information but a problem of hearing: at the same passage of curse-words, one man hears and stands straight, another hears and blesses himself. [Summary layer] This section falls in four points: (1) the other side of what was seen (vv. 16–17, the list of idols); (2) the root of the "lest" (v. 18, turning away and the evil root); (3) the self-blesser (v. 19, the inner monologue and the three readings); (4) no sparing, and separation unto evil (vv. 20–21, the blotting out of his name). [New Testament reading layer] This section, under the assigned discipline, registers only and does not read backwards: within the reading tradition of the Epistle to the Hebrews, Hebrews 12:15 has the phrase "any root of bitterness" — in the KJV, fetched first-hand in this translation round from Bolls.Life (KJV): "Looking diligently lest any man fail of the grace of God; lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled;" (Heb 12:15, KJV, verbatim as fetched; the Chinese source registers that its fixed text at this verse reads "a poisonous root" in its own wording, "bitter root" being the New Translation's wording there, registered separately) — which answers in vocabulary to this verse's "a root that beareth gall and wormwood," a mutually recognised intertextual index: the New Testament author warns the church community by it against inward turning away and mutual defilement. This reading is the New Testament's own use; whether it is to be read back as this verse's original meaning, this sermon registers only and does not adjudicate. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] This section states two items side by side: (1) the three readings of the last clause of verse 19 (note 7): the sweep-together reading (the stubborn man's guarantee of peace falls void, and his calamity sweeps away the watered and the dry together), the involving reading (followed by the CUNPSS: one man's stubbornness involves the many), and the proverb reading (followed by the KJV: "to add drunkenness to thirst," a metaphor of a craving that cannot be filled) — the three readings stated side by side, not adjudicated; (2) the dispute over the botanical reference of "gall": what poisonous herb roʾš denotes — the commentators point some to the hemlock class and some take it as a general term for "bitter juice," and laʿănâ denotes the wormwood class generally — the reference is not settled by verdict, and this sermon registers only the word's range of sense (a poisonous / bitter plant imagery) and does not fix a species by force.

## Section Four (45:00–53:00) Later Generations Will Ask, and the Secret Things Belong to the LORD (29:22–29)

[Factual layer] Verses 22–23 are the future seeing, given in full from the base text: "So that the generation to come of your children that shall rise up after you, and the stranger that shall come from a far land, shall say, when they see the plagues of that land, and the sicknesses which the LORD hath laid upon it; And that the whole land thereof is brimstone, and salt, and burning, that it is not sown, nor beareth, nor any grass groweth therein, like the overthrow of Sodom, and Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboim, which the LORD overthrew in his anger, and in his wrath:" Registered item by item: (1) the ones who see have changed — the generation to come of your children and the stranger from a far land: the fifth and sixth of the chapter's seven "see" occurrences on the Chinese account are in these two verses (the arc of seeing through the chapter: at vv. 2–3 they see the miracles, at v. 17 the idols, at vv. 22–24 the plagues — one and the same verb walks through three acts, and the seventh occurrence at v. 24 closes the arc; on the KJV's own account the see-form stands at v. 22 within this pair, registered in note 2); (2) the two names of calamity: the plagues (makkâ, H4347) and the sicknesses (taḥăluʾîm, H8463); (3) the three "no's" of desolation, counted by hand: it is not sown, nor beareth, nor any grass groweth therein; (4) the three things of desolation: brimstone (goprît, H1614), salt (melaḥ, H4417), and burning — the comparison with Genesis 19 is, under the discipline, stated side by side only (note 8); one difference of composition is added for comparison: "salt" is not seen in the list of destroying things in Genesis 19's overthrow sentence — in Genesis 19:24 the destroying things in the base text are "brimstone and fire" named together, with no salt (in this translation round the verse was fetched first-hand from Bolls.Life (KJV): "Then the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire from the LORD out of heaven;" Gen 19:24, KJV, verbatim as fetched; the Chinese source registers its own first-hand verification of that verse on the wd.bible verse page) — only the difference in the lists' composition is registered, and no argument is built on it; (5) the four city names, registered name by name: Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboim — "the overthrow" (mahpēkâ, H4114); for the name "Zeboim" the original form has one ketiv/qere entry: in this verse the Masoretic written form (ṣĕbîyîm) differs from the read form (ṣĕbôyim), the two forms seen together in parenthesis in this chapter's BHS line (the Masoretic 29:22 line), registered in note 1; (6) the sentence-ending form of verse 23 is registered separately: the CUNPSS simplified line closes with a full stop and the traditional line with a dash (note 3). [Factual layer] Verse 24 is the question of all nations, given in full from the base text: "Even all nations shall say, Wherefore hath the LORD done thus unto this land? what meaneth the heat of this great anger?" Registered item by item: (1) those who ask are "all nations," in the Chinese fixed text "those who have seen, even all nations" — verse 24's "see" is the seventh and closing occurrence of the chapter on the Chinese account (note 2); the KJV at this verse carries no see-verb, and the difference of form is registered in note 2; (2) the structure of the two questions: wherefore (the cause) and what meaneth (the meaning) — the nations' manner of asking asks first about the act and then about the anger; (3) the original word-form of "heat" in "the heat of this great anger," ḥōrî, is seen directly in this chapter's BHS line; its number is listed in this sermon as unverified and is not supplied by force (see the format note). [Factual layer] Verses 25–28 are the answer prepared beforehand, given in full from the base text: "Then men shall say, Because they have forsaken the covenant of the LORD God of their fathers, which he made with them when he brought them forth out of the land of Egypt: For they went and served other gods, and worshipped them, gods whom they knew not, and whom he had not given unto them: And the anger of the LORD was kindled against this land, to bring upon it all the curses that are written in this book: And the LORD rooted them out of their land in anger, and in wrath, and in great indignation, and cast them into another land, as it is this day." Registered item by item: (1) the answer begins within single quotation marks in the Chinese fixed text (v. 25), embedded at the same level as the question of verse 24, and closes at the end of verse 28, closing at the same time the assembly quotation opened at verse 10 (note 4): the chapter's quotation marks come to their close here; (2) the answer's first cause is forsaking (ʿāzab, H5800) — the object forsaken is "the covenant": the covenant-word of verse 25 answers to verse 1 across the chapter ("covenant" occurs eight times in the chapter on the Chinese account, note 2; on the KJV's own account "covenant" occurs seven times, at vv. 1 ×2, 9, 12, 14, 21, 25, registered in note 2); (3) "gods whom they knew not, and whom he had not given unto them" stands as a vocabulary index against this book's 4:19: at 4:19 the base text reads, fetched first-hand in this translation round from Bolls.Life (KJV): "And lest thou lift up thine eyes unto heaven, and when thou seest the sun, and the moon, and the stars, even all the host of heaven, shouldest be driven to worship them, and serve them, which the LORD thy God hath divided unto all nations under the whole heaven." (Deut 4:19, KJV, verbatim as fetched) — the original there likewise uses ḥālaq (H2505) in its apportioning sense, and the Chinese source registers its own first-hand verification of that verse's fixed-text wording on the wd.bible verse page, noting that an earlier draft's summary of that verse's vocabulary differed from the fixed text's literal wording and was corrected to the fixed text's actual words in that round (note 6); (4) verse 27's "all the curses" is the last of the chapter's four curse-occurrences, and its "in this book" the last of the chapter's three "book" occurrences: the basis of the answer is still the book; (5) verse 28's triple anger, counted by hand: in anger, and in wrath, and in great indignation; its double action: rooted them out (nātaš, H5428) and cast them into another land (šālak, H7993); and its close, "as it is this day" — the last of the chapter's "this day" occurrences on the Chinese account: the "this day" of the discourse and the "this day" of the fulfilment are the same word in this chapter, and the prophetic sentence carries the tense of its own fulfilment within it; the form is registered as such. [Factual layer] Verse 29 is the chapter's seal, given in full from the base text: "The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law." Registered item by item: (1) the sentence falls in two halves — the secret things (nistārôt, the participle form of sātar, H5641) belong unto the LORD our God; those things which are revealed (niglôt, the participle form of gālâ, H1540) belong unto us and to our children for ever: the line between hidden and revealed is drawn by belonging, not by man's seeking after knowledge; (2) the purpose clause closes the whole chapter: "that we may do all the words of this law" — answering across the chapter to verse 9's "Keep therefore the words of this covenant, and do them": the retrospect section sets out with doing, and after the warning and the question-and-answer have run their course, the chapter closes with doing; (3) the scribal pointing: in the original of this verse, over the phrase "unto us and to our children" (lānû ûləḇānênû) several letters carry dots above them, a form of the scribal tradition; in this round it is registered as seen directly in the character form of the BHS line, and the readings of its intent are stated side by side in the discussion layer, not adjudicated (notes 1 and 8); (4) at the end of this verse in the Masora a samekh (ס) is seen, and the whole chapter closes with a closed paragraph (note 1). [Theological layer] In Section Four, hold on to the chapter's arc: seeing (v. 2) → standing (v. 10) → lest (v. 18) → shall say (v. 24) → the secret things (v. 29). By verse 28 the words have been spoken to the end — how the calamity comes, where its cause lies, in which book it is written, all of it is among "those things which are revealed"; verse 29 adds not one item of new information, it only draws a line: the revealed things are already enough for us to do, and the secret things are not on our account. Look again at the list of instances of "those things which are revealed" within this chapter: the words of the covenant (spoken aloud at v. 9), the words of the curse (read out to all at v. 19), all the curses written in the book (named three times at vv. 20, 21, 27) — the body of this chapter throughout is itself a specimen of "those things which are revealed": the boundary of verse 29 is not a footnote added from outside; it is this chapter's declaration of its own nature — the words in this chapter belong to the class of things "unto us." The chapter's last wisdom is not knowing more; it is knowing which side belongs to us. The generations' question-and-answer is thereby closed into a loop as well: what later generations ask, the answer to it has long been written down; what later generations need not ask, its answer belongs to God. [Summary layer] This section falls in four points: (1) the future seeing (vv. 22–23, the plagues and the desolation); (2) the question of all nations (v. 24, the two questions); (3) the prepared answer (vv. 25–28, forsaking the covenant, and being rooted out and cast); (4) the seal (v. 29, the secret things belong to the LORD, the revealed things belong to us, that we may do). [New Testament reading layer] This section registers an index only and does not read backwards: the boundary-drawing sentence-form of verse 29, "secret / revealed," is cited repeatedly in the later reading tradition as a marker of the limits of revelation — the revealed things are the range man is to do, the secret things the range God keeps back (the existence of that reading tradition is registered as such; this sermon has not verified the relevant later literature word by word first-hand and does not quote it); this sermon points only, as regards the text, to this: the original form of this boundary is a belonging sentence (belong unto... belong unto...), not a knowledge sentence. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] This section states three items side by side: (1) the three readings of the reference of "the secret things" in verse 29 — the unrevealed details of judgment reading, the future manner of the covenant's execution reading, and the consequences of secret sin reading — stated side by side, not adjudicated; and, arising from it, this verse's relation to the tradition of the doctrine of predestination: historically there has indeed been a use of this verse as a warrant for the boundaries of that doctrine (in both directions); that relation is registered only, not adjudicated (note 8); (2) the readings of the pointing's intent, stated side by side — the scribal tradition may mark the phrase as doubtful by the pointing, or mark it for re-reading with emphasis, or keep a marker for later readings; its intent has no settled verdict, not adjudicated; (3) the comparison of the brimstone-and-fire imagery with Genesis 19, stated side by side — the same: the overthrow imagery of brimstone and fire (in Genesis 19:24 the base text reads "Then the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire from the LORD out of heaven;" KJV, verbatim as fetched first-hand in this translation round; the Chinese source registers its own first-hand verification of that verse's Chinese fixed text); the different: Genesis 19 is a narrative (a thing accomplished), while this chapter's verse 23 is a simile within a prophetic question-and-answer (a warning of what has not yet come to pass), and literary form and tense alike differ; and the composition of the city names differs — Genesis 19:24's overthrow sentence names Sodom and Gomorrah, two cities, while this chapter sets four cities side by side (note 8) — the comparison is stated side by side only, and the relation between the two chapters is not adjudicated.

## Conclusion (53:00–60:00)

[Theological layer] Return, as the assembly in Moab breaks up, to think on one thing: 29:15 says the covenant reaches "him that is not here with us this day" — that sentence writes every later reader of this chapter into the roll. We did not stand in Moab, but the warnings, evidences, and boundary that we read are the body text written for "those not here." This chapter gives us three positions: first, the eyes — the evidences have already been listed; seeing or not seeing is one thing, and perceiving in the heart or not is another, and the knot of verse 4 is left for every generation to face for itself; second, the feet — the roll has already been called to its end; standing is the covenant's posture, turning away is a possibility of "this day," and the warning's address includes us; third, the boundary — the secret things belong to God, and the revealed things belong to us and to our children. The curiosity of theology may be unlimited, but the list of things to be done has already been written plainly. [Factual layer] At the close, three textual facts are registered in supplement: (1) the divine name, "the LORD," occurs nineteen times in this chapter in the CUNPSS (note 2) and twenty times in the KJV on its own account (note 2), and its densest place, the three occurrences of verse 20, is entirely within the warning section — where the name stands densest is not the list of blessings but the verse of "will not spare"; (2) "this day" occurs seven times in the chapter on the Chinese account, from verse 4 to verse 28's "as it is this day" — the chapter's sense of time is nailed throughout to "this day": the covenant is made this day, the turning away is this day, and the fulfilment is read as this day as well; on the KJV's own account "this day / to day" occurs eight times (note 2); (3) the chapter's verbal arc is registered in closing: it opens with "see" (v. 2; seven occurrences on the Chinese account), makes the covenant with "stand" (vv. 10, 15; two occurrences), and ends the chapter with "do" (v. 29, answering across the chapter to verse 9's "Keep therefore... and do them") — see, stand, do: the three verbs are the chapter's skeleton. The whole sermon closes with 29:29, given again in full from the base text: "The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law." The words have been spoken to the end: what was to be seen has been given to be seen, who was to stand has been called to stand, what was to be feared has been spoken as fear — what remains is the doing.

## Devotional Extract

Before the assembly at Moab breaks up, Moses leaves us not a new command but a boundary line. Before this point, everything that could be shown has been shown: "Ye have seen all that the Lord did before your eyes in the land of Egypt," and through forty years "your clothes are not waxen old upon you, and thy shoe is not waxen old upon thy foot." Yet the same chapter honestly records: "Yet the Lord hath not given you an heart to perceive, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, unto this day." Between seeing and perceiving there lies the distance of a heart. Those who stand in the covenant first confess that distance, instead of boasting of their eyesight. Then comes the standing: the roll is called from the captains down to "the hewer of thy wood unto the drawer of thy water," and beyond those present, to "him that is not here with us this day" — you and I are on that roll. Last comes the boundary: "The secret things belong unto the Lord our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law." We always want to know the half God keeps back before we will obey the half He has given; this chapter reverses the order. What is revealed is enough for a lifetime of walking. Leave your heart in His hands, your feet on His words, and your question marks on His side of the line — that is the homework the Moab assembly sends home with us.

The fuller Chinese devotional of the source is represented by this extract.)

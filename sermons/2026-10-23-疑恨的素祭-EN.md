# The Jealousy Offering — A Sermon on Numbers 5

- Text: Numbers 5:1-31
- Author: By Guangyi Zhao, composed from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending cross-review (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English base text: King James Version (KJV); all Scripture quotations are embedded from the KJV text itself, checked verse by verse, never back-translated from the Chinese
- Chinese base text of the original sermon: Union Version with New Punctuation (hereafter NP); quotations in the Chinese original follow that text letter for letter, without honorific substitutions
- Title determination: The working title direction was "jealousy," and the KJV indeed reads "jealousy" throughout this chapter ("the spirit of jealousy," 5:14; "an offering of jealousy," 5:15; "the jealousy offering," 5:18, 25; "the law of jealousies," 5:29). The Chinese base text, however, does not use the word "疑忌" (the term found in the Chinese Union Version 2010 and the Chinese New Living Translation at these verses); the NP reads "疑恨" throughout — "生了疑恨的心" (5:14), "疑恨的素祭" (5:15, 18, 25), "这疑恨的条例" (5:30). The sermon's title is therefore fixed by the wording of its Chinese base text read consecutively, and the English title follows the KJV's own consecutive phrase, "the jealousy offering" (5:18, 25), in the Chinese order "The Jealousy Offering" (see Endnote 2)
- Conventions: In the sermon body, (Fact) marks Scripture and verifiable fact, (Application) marks the preacher's exposition, (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted verbatim, (New Testament reading) marks a New Testament reading of the passage, and (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks academic and historical discussion that remains unsettled; fact and application are labelled separately

## Abstract

Chapter 4 counted the men who came to do the service; chapter 5 turns to cleansing the camp. Because this camp is the camp in the midst whereof the LORD dwells, the unclean must be put out of the camp, what has been wrongfully taken must be confessed and recompensed with a fifth part added, and a case with no witness must be brought before the LORD and decided by the bitter water and the oath. The chapter has thirty-one verses in three passages. The first (vv. 1-4) commands that every leper, every one that hath an issue, and whosoever is defiled by the dead be put out of the camp, both male and female alike, and the children of Israel did so. The second (vv. 5-10) treats restitution for wrongs: when a man or woman commits any sin that men commit, to do a trespass against the LORD, he shall confess the sin, recompense the trespass in full, and add unto it the fifth part thereof, giving it to the one against whom he has trespassed; if that man has no kinsman to receive it, it is recompensed unto the LORD, even to the priest, besides the ram of the atonement; and the holy things brought to the priest are his. The third and longest passage (vv. 11-31) is the law of jealousies: if a man's wife goes aside and there is no witness against her, and the spirit of jealousy comes upon him, he shall bring his wife to the priest with the tenth part of an ephah of barley meal — the jealousy offering, an offering of memorial — pouring no oil upon it and putting no frankincense thereon. The priest sets the woman before the LORD, prepares the bitter water that causeth the curse with holy water in an earthen vessel and dust from the floor of the tabernacle, charges her with an oath of cursing, writes the curses in a book and blots them out into the water, and causes her to drink. If she is defiled, her belly shall swell and her thigh shall rot, and she becomes a curse among her people; if she is not defiled but clean, she shall be free and shall conceive seed. This sermon reads the three passages in the order of the text: first why the camp must be clean, then why a wrong must be confessed and repaid — and which Hebrew word the "fifth part" actually is — and finally, verse by verse, the offering, the bitter water, the oath, and the two outcomes of the jealousy case. The thread to follow is this: the God who dwells in the midst of the camp will not leave uncleanness, unpaid debts, or unresolved suspicion standing in His camp — bodily uncleanness goes out of the camp, debts between man and man are reckoned to the last fifth, and a hidden matter between husband and wife is brought before Him, for Him to decide Himself.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 "In the Midst Whereof I Dwell": The Unclean Put Out of the Camp

(Fact) The first passage has only four verses, yet it lays the foundation of the whole chapter. The LORD speaks to Moses, and the command is quoted word for word from the base text: "Command the children of Israel, that they put out of the camp every leper, and every one that hath an issue, and whosoever is defiled by the dead:" (Num 5:2). Three classes stand side by side: the leper, a grave affliction of the skin; the one that hath an issue, uncleanness from a bodily discharge; and the one defiled by the dead, uncleanness contracted by contact with death. Disease, discharge, and death — three lines of ritual uncleanness. This chapter does not ask whether these persons have sinned morally; it asks only whether they are clean or unclean at this time. Verse 3 then gives both the scope and the reason, quoted word for word: "Both male and female shall ye put out, without the camp shall ye put them; that they defile not their camps, in the midst whereof I dwell." (Num 5:3). Note the wording precisely: the KJV reads "without the camp," and the reason is given as "that they defile not their camps, in the midst whereof I dwell." The disposal falls on male and female alike — "both male and female" in this verse, and "when a man or woman" in verse 6: the Chinese original counted the corresponding phrase "无论男女" in its base text and found it exactly twice in the chapter, at verses 3 and 6, verified verse by verse; the KJV renders the same impartiality with different wording in each place, and this translation reports the count as a fact about the Chinese base text, not as a claim about the English wording. The reason is one sentence, and it outweighs everything: "in the midst whereof I dwell." The camp must be clean not because those outside it are worth less than those inside it, but because at the center of the camp dwells a holy God — the tabernacle stood in the middle of the camp (the arrangement of chapter 2, Summary), and where His dwelling is, uncleanness cannot be left standing around it.

(Fact) Verse 4 records the execution of the command, quoted word for word: "And the children of Israel did so, and put them out without the camp: as the LORD spake unto Moses, so did the children of Israel." (Num 5:4). The phrase "without the camp" is read consecutively in verses 3 and 4 of the KJV — verse 3 the command, verse 4 the execution — and both have been checked verse by verse; verse 2 carries the command in its own form, "put out of the camp," and the three verses are set side by side, not merged into one wording. Between command and execution there is no room for bargaining: as the LORD spoke, so they did — a formula that recurs throughout Numbers as the book's standard sentence of obedience. And it must be seen clearly: going out of the camp is a temporary separation, not a final rejection. How the leper, once cleansed, returns to the camp is governed by the inspection and cleansing rites of Leviticus 13-14 (Summary); the cleansing of discharges is treated in Leviticus 15 (Summary); uncleanness from the dead requires seven days and the water of separation, treated in Numbers 19 (Summary). This chapter issues only the one command, "put out"; the road back is left to the other ordinances. A man goes out in order to be cleansed, and once cleansed there is a road back — separation was never meant to cut him off, but to wait for cleansing.

(Fact) The key word of this passage is "unclean / defiled." In the original, this line runs through the ṭ-m-ʾ word family: its verb ṭāmēʾ (Strong's H2930), to become unclean, to be defiled. The Chinese original checked this form verse by verse at its occurrences later in the chapter — verse 13, "she be defiled," and the two forms of verse 14, "she be defiled" and "she be not defiled," are forms of this word (the checked verses are given as a verified selection, not as an exhaustive claim about the whole chapter). This word family runs through the purity laws of Leviticus: it marks not moral good and evil but ritual fitness and unfitness — a person may be entirely free of moral fault and yet be in a state of uncleanness through childbirth, disease, or contact with the dead (Leviticus 12 and 15, and this chapter, Summary). Keep this distinction clear and the passage will not be misread: not one of the three classes put out of the camp is put out for a crime. They go out because their state is, for the time, incompatible with the holiness of the One at the camp's center. State and guilt are two separate accounts, and the second passage of this chapter is about guilt — the man who has sinned is not put out of the camp; he confesses and makes restitution. The person unclean in state does not confess sins and pay compensation; he goes out and waits for cleansing. The two passages stand side by side precisely to teach us to keep these two accounts apart and never settle them as one.

(Application) "In the midst whereof I dwell" — this sentence is the source of every ordinance in the chapter, and the first thing a reader of this chapter today must hear. The camp must be clean, not because Israel is nobler than the peoples outside, but because there is One who dwells in their midst. God's presence is never merely a word of comfort; presence makes demands. Where He dwells, that place must be ordered according to His character. Many people love the first half of "God is with us" — with Him here, I am at rest — and dislike the second half: with Him here, I cannot leave my uncleanness, my unpaid debts, and my hidden darkness lying around exactly as they were. Presence is grace, and presence is also light; when light comes in, what it reveals can no longer be pretended away. The three passages of this chapter move steadily inward: first uncleanness of bodily state — out of the camp; then wrongs committed in conduct — confess and repay; finally a hidden matter between husband and wife for which there is not even a witness — bring it before Him for Him to decide. From the boundary of the camp all the way into the human heart: this is what a God who dwells in the midst of the camp does to His camp.

(Application) This passage about going out of the camp also calls for a word of fairness on behalf of the three classes. The text never says that their going out is a disgrace, and it never authorizes those inside the camp to despise them. The leper is not a sinner; the one with a discharge is not a sinner; and the one defiled by handling the dead — very often a man who has just buried his own kin — is least of all a sinner: he contracted uncleanness precisely by doing his duty. If ritual uncleanness and moral shame are not kept apart, the church will repeat the ancient mistake of treating the sick as people God has abandoned, and the afflicted as people with hidden sins. This passage teaches the opposite lesson: in God's camp a state may need to be dealt with while the person is not condemned; separation is for cleansing and restoration, not for humiliation and abandonment. The gate of the camp stands open for the one who is cleansed — the day of his cleansing is the day of his return. Deal with problems strictly, and treat persons generously: the two already stand side by side in the first passage of this chapter.

### II. 08:00-24:00 A Trespass Against the LORD: Confession, Full Restitution, and the Fifth Part Added

(Fact) The second passage (vv. 5-10) turns from state to guilt. The ordinance opens, quoted word for word: "Speak unto the children of Israel, When a man or woman shall commit any sin that men commit, to do a trespass against the LORD, and that person be guilty;" (Num 5:6). This verse has three layers, set out one by one. First, the subject is again "a man or woman" — answering to "both male and female" in verse 3: the first two passages of this chapter fall on men and women alike. Second, the definition of the sin: "any sin that men commit" — theft, fraud, withholding what was entrusted, false oath, the class of wrongs between man and man (of the same class as those listed in Leviticus 6:2-3, Summary); yet the text says that such a wrong against a man is at the same time "a trespass against the LORD" — to wrong a man is to trespass against God; the two accounts are kept in one ledger. Third, the verdict: "and that person be guilty." In the original, "be guilty" is a verbal form of the word family ʾāšām (אָשָׁם, Strong's H817; the verb of the family is Strong's H816). The Chinese original checked verse 7 of the original: there the wrongs done and recompensed stand in this same word family (the verb H816, the recompensed debt itself the noun ʾāšām, H817) — the "trespass" is both the act committed and the debt that must be repaid (for the method of checking, see Endnote 7). What a man owes a man, God also books; that is the theological foundation of every restitution ordinance in this passage.

(Fact) The disposal has three steps, and verse 7 is quoted word for word: "Then they shall confess their sin which they have done: and he shall recompense his trespass with the principal thereof, and add unto it the fifth part thereof, and give it unto him against whom he hath trespassed." (Num 5:7). The three steps are: first, confess — "they shall confess their sin which they have done"; in the original, "confess" is the reflexive (Hithpael) form of yādāh (Strong's H3034), and "their sin" is ḥaṭṭāʾṯ (Strong's H2403) — the Chinese original checked this pairing at this verse. Second, recompense in full — the principal is repaid without a penny's discount; "with the principal thereof" tolerates no shortfall. Third, add unto it the fifth part — beyond the principal, a further fifth of the principal, all given to the one who was wronged. Note two points of wording precisely: the KJV reads "add unto it the fifth part thereof," and in the Chinese base text the payment is made "归与" the wronged man — written yú (与), not the "归于" of some modern-punctuated printings, a distinction in the small function words that the Chinese original checked verse by verse and that this translation reports as a fact about its base text (see Endnote 10). This "fifth part" is the crux of the verse, and the place in the whole passage where the original text is most easily gotten wrong, so it must be set firmly in the original: the Hebrew of "the fifth part thereof" here is waḥămîšîṯô (וַחֲמִישִׁתוֹ), which is the feminine form ḥămîšîṯ of the ordinal ḥămîšî (חֲמִישִׁי, Strong's H2549) with a third-person suffix, meaning "its fifth (part)"; the verb "add" is yāsap (Strong's H3254). Before writing, the Chinese original checked this pairing item by item (see Endnote 8): the "fifth part" of Numbers 5:7 belongs to H2549 — it is not ḥōmeš (חֹמֶשׁ, Strong's H2569), which is a different word and which, according to BDB, occurs in the whole Bible only once, at Genesis 47:26, in Pharaoh's fifth of the land, and has nothing to do with the added fifth of the restitution ordinances. The two words share a root and nearly the same meaning, but they differ in form and in number; to cite Numbers 5:7 as ḥōmeš with H2569 would be a mispairing. It is recorded expressly here: Numbers 5:7 uses the feminine ordinal form of the H2549 family, and the same-root mispairing risk flagged in the Leviticus 27 lesson applies at this verse and is avoided in the same way.

(Fact) This pattern — "add a fifth" — is not unique to this chapter. The Chinese original checked the parallel passages listed in the lexical entries item by item and counted nine: Leviticus 5:16 (holy things misused: restitution with a fifth added), Leviticus 6:5 (property withheld under a false oath: restitution with a fifth added; this verse is numbered 5:24 in the Hebrew canon, and this sermon follows the English versification, noting the difference here), Leviticus 22:14 (a holy thing eaten unwittingly: a fifth added), Leviticus 27:13, 27:15, 27:19, 27:27, and 27:31 (redeeming a vowed beast, a house, a field, an unclean beast, and the tithe, each with a fifth added), and this chapter's Numbers 5:7 — nine passages in all, checked item by item (for the counting method, and for the independent recount in this translation, see Endnote 9). The nine fall into two classes: restitution (a man or a holy thing wronged: principal repaid and a fifth added) and redemption (a thing already sanctified is bought back: a fifth added for its release). Both classes use one pattern: wherever an account is to be settled and a relationship repaired, repaying the principal alone is not enough; a fifth must be added. That extra portion is the price of the wrong and the sincerity of the reconciliation, priced in the open, with nothing left vague.

(Fact) If the man who was wronged is no longer living and has no kinsman to receive the restitution, verse 8 is quoted word for word: "But if the man have no kinsman to recompense the trespass unto, let the trespass be recompensed unto the LORD, even to the priest; beside the ram of the atonement, whereby an atonement shall be made for him." (Num 5:8). Take it layer by layer. First, "kinsman" is gōʾēl (גֹּאֵל, Strong's H1350) in the original — the near kinsman-redeemer, the member of the family with the right and the duty to redeem a kinsman's property and to receive what is owed him; here it is the kinsman of the wronged party. Second, where there is no kinsman to receive it, the restitution is not cancelled and not discounted; it is paid over to the priest — because the other end of this account is the LORD (v. 6: a trespass against the LORD): if the human creditor is absent, the account on God's side still stands, and the priest is the man who receives on the LORD's behalf. Here a difference between the base texts must be recorded exactly as it stands: the Chinese base text (NP) at this verse reads that the restitution "shall be given to the priest who serves the LORD" and does not contain the words "unto the LORD," whereas the KJV expressly says "recompensed unto the LORD, even to the priest." The two base texts are each quoted as they stand; neither is conformed to the other (see Endnote 3 — this difference was found by independent checking at the translation stage, not carried over from the Chinese original). Third, "beside the ram of the atonement" — beyond the restitution there is also a ram for atonement: in the original, "ram" is ʾayil (Strong's H352) and "of the atonement" is kippurîm (כִּפֻּרִים, Strong's H3725), whose verb is kāpar (Strong's H3722); the Chinese original checked this set of pairings at this verse. Restitution is restitution, and atonement is atonement: money settles the account with man or with the priest, and the ram is offered to God — the two run side by side, and neither cancels the other. Verses 9-10 are the appendix of the passage: every offering of all the holy things of the children of Israel which they bring to the priest shall be his (v. 9, quoted word for word: "And every offering of all the holy things of the children of Israel, which they bring unto the priest, shall be his." Num 5:9 — "offering" here is tərûmâ, Strong's H8641, checked at this verse), and every man's hallowed things shall be his, that is, the priest's (v. 10, Summary: "And every man's hallowed things shall be his: whatsoever any man giveth the priest, it shall be his" — the Chinese base text at this verse uses the verb "归给" where verses 7-9 used "归与"; the Chinese original notes this small difference in its paraphrase rather than merging the two into one wording).

(Application) What cuts deepest in this passage is that "confess" stands before "recompense." The order God fixes is not first to scrape the money together and make good the loss, but first to open the mouth and confess: I have committed this sin; I have wronged that man. Many people are willing to repay money and unwilling to confess sin — money can settle an account, but confession costs a man his face; and the text deliberately puts the loss of face first. Restitution without confession is hush money; confession without restitution is empty talk. The text binds the two together: confess with the mouth, repay with the hand, and add a fifth besides — only when all three are present is the account clear before God and man. And that fifth part deserves a pause for arithmetic: wrong a man, and beyond the principal you must add a further twenty percent. That extra fifth declares that in God's ledger a wrong is never a neutral event closed by "I paid it back": the days of the withholding, the other man's lost time, the trust destroyed — all of it is priced. Repairing a relationship always costs more than breaking it: breaking takes one reach of the hand; repair demands the principal, the fifth, the confession, and the ram. The text uses an openly stated price to teach us reverence for other people's property and rights: before you reach out your hand to wrong a man, reckon the price of repair, and you will know that hand must not be stretched out.

(Application) The verse about there being no kinsman to receive the restitution closes the escape hatch for every wrong that "nobody knows about and nobody will pursue." The wronged man is gone; he has no kinsman; is the account then simply written off? The text says: no. Pay it to the priest; it is recompensed unto the LORD. It turns out every account of wronging a man has a duplicate copy kept in heaven; if the earthly creditor is absent, the heavenly Creditor never is. Men most easily indulge themselves in "no one saw it," and most easily excuse themselves with "it can't be made good anymore" — this verse takes away both self-absolutions: no one saw it, but God saw it; if it cannot be repaid to man, repay it before God, with the ram of the atonement besides. The account between man and man settled, and the account between man and God settled as well — only then has the man truly walked out of "that person be guilty."

### III. 24:00-42:00 The Jealousy Offering: A Case with No Witness, Brought Before the LORD

(Fact) The third passage (vv. 11-31) is the longest in the chapter: twenty-one verses, the greater part of the chapter's thirty-one (the passages run 4, 6, and 21 verses, totaling 31 — counted passage by passage in the Chinese original, and the arithmetic rechecked at the translation stage). The case is set out in verse 12, quoted word for word: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, If any man's wife go aside, and commit a trespass against him," (Num 5:12 — in both base texts this verse ends with a comma, the sentence continuing into verse 13). The "trespass" is an act of unfaithfulness from the word family māʿal (מַעַל, Strong's H4604) — the same family as the "trespass" of the second passage: to wrong a man is a trespass against the LORD, and to betray a husband is likewise a trespass (the Chinese original checked this word at verses 12 and 27). Verse 13 lays out the difficulty of the case, quoted word for word: "And a man lie with her carnally, and it be hid from the eyes of her husband, and be kept close, and she be defiled, and there be no witness against her, neither she be taken with the manner;" (Num 5:13). Note the wording: "there be no witness against her" — in the Chinese base text the word is "作见证" with the character 作, not the "做" of some other printings; "witness" in the original is ʿēd (Strong's H5707), checked at this verse. This verse erects the premise of the whole ordinance: this is a case with no evidence — no witness, no one taken in the act, only suspicion. Ordinarily adultery is established by the mouth of two or three witnesses (Deut 19:15, Summary); this case has not a single witness. Left there, the case has only two roads: either the husband disposes of his wife by his own suspicion, or a real offense goes forever undecided — and both roads are disasters. This ordinance is the third road cut through that dead end.

(Fact) Verse 14 states what sets the case in motion, quoted word for word: "And the spirit of jealousy come upon him, and he be jealous of his wife, and she be defiled: or if the spirit of jealousy come upon him, and he be jealous of his wife, and she be not defiled:" (Num 5:14 — again ending with a colon in the KJV, the sentence continuing into verse 15). One and the same jealousy, two possible facts: in one case she really is defiled; in the other she is not defiled at all — the text sets the two side by side and prejudges neither. In the original, "the spirit of jealousy" is rûaḥ qinʾâ: rûaḥ (רוּחַ, Strong's H7307), spirit, wind, breath, and qinʾâ (קִנְאָה, Strong's H7068), jealousy; the verb "be jealous" is qānāʾ (Strong's H7065) — the Chinese original checked verse 14 of the original: rûaḥ and qinʾâ each occur twice there and the verb form likewise twice, answering to the twice-repeated structure of the sentence. (The Chinese base text renders rûaḥ here as "心," the heart — "生了疑恨的心," the heart of jealousy is born in him — a difference of rendering between the two base texts, noted as it stands.) The distribution of the key word in the Chinese base text was counted there item by item: verse 14 has four occurrences (the heart of jealousy, and jealous of her, each twice over), verses 15, 18, and 25 have one each (each time "the jealousy offering"), and verse 30 has three (the heart of jealousy, jealous of his wife, and the law of jealousies) — ten occurrences in all, of which the consecutive phrase "疑恨的素祭," "the jealousy offering," occurs three times and supplies the sermon's title (for the counting details, see Endnote 5). For comparison, the corresponding noun in the KJV was counted independently at the translation stage: "jealousy" (including "jealousies") occurs at verse 14 twice, verse 15 once, verse 18 once, verse 25 once, verse 29 once, and verse 30 once — seven occurrences, itemized and checked verse by verse; the Chinese count of ten counts the word 疑恨 in its verbal uses as well ("疑恨她," is jealous of her), and the KJV reaches the same total of ten when its verb forms ("be jealous," 5:14 twice and 5:30 once) are counted with its nouns — the two counts are set side by side in Endnote 5, each as a count of its own base text (see Endnote 5).

(Fact) Once jealousy has arisen, what the husband is to do is stated concretely in verse 15, quoted word for word: "Then shall the man bring his wife unto the priest, and he shall bring her offering for her, the tenth part of an ephah of barley meal; he shall pour no oil upon it, nor put frankincense thereon; for it is an offering of jealousy, an offering of memorial, bringing iniquity to remembrance." (Num 5:15). Take the items one by one. The offering is barley meal, the tenth part of an ephah — barley is the grain of common folk, coarser and cheaper than wheat, and the measure is the customary measure of a meal offering (the daily pattern of Lev 6:20 is the tenth part of an ephah, Summary). Two prohibitions are express: he shall pour no oil upon it, nor put frankincense thereon. A meal offering ordinarily is dressed with oil and frankincense — it is a fragrant, joyful offering (Leviticus 2, Summary); this one alone is permitted neither — because it does not come to celebrate; it comes to open a case. Its name is "an offering of jealousy"; its character is "an offering of memorial, bringing iniquity to remembrance." In the original this offering is a minḥâ (מִנְחָה, Strong's H4503), and the Chinese original checked verse 15: the two phrases stand side by side — minḥat qinʾôṯ, the offering of jealousies, and minḥat zikkārôn, the offering of memorial — one name stating the occasion, the other the function: hidden iniquity is brought before God to be remembered and confronted. Note the restraint of the wording: it "brings iniquity to remembrance"; it does not say it convicts the woman of sin. At this point the case is still undecided; the offering merely presents it at the bar.

(Fact) The procedure after presentation (vv. 16-18) follows the text step by step. The priest brings the woman near, quoted word for word: "And the priest shall bring her near, and set her before the LORD:" (Num 5:16) — the judgment seat of this case is not in the husband's house and not at the city gate; it is before the LORD. The preparation of the water, verse 17 quoted word for word: "And the priest shall take holy water in an earthen vessel; and of the dust that is in the floor of the tabernacle the priest shall take, and put it into the water:" (Num 5:17). The materials are only three: holy water, an earthen vessel, and dust from the floor of the tabernacle — not one of them a drug, not one of them a poison; whatever weight the water carries lies in where it has been taken from and in the oath it is to bear. Verse 18 sets the woman's stance and the two things in hand, quoted word for word: "And the priest shall set the woman before the LORD, and uncover the woman's head, and put the offering of memorial in her hands, which is the jealousy offering: and the priest shall have in his hand the bitter water that causeth the curse:" (Num 5:18). The uncovered head is the posture of mourning and of standing trial; the offering of memorial is placed in the woman's own hands — she herself holds the offering that presents her case; the bitter water is in the priest's hand — the authority of decision is in neither the woman's hand nor the husband's, but in the hand of the One the priest represents. At this point offering, water, person, and oath are all assembled, and only the last and weightiest step remains: the oath and the drinking, to be read closely in the next section.

(Application) This ordinance strikes the modern reader hard, but first see what it did in the world of its own day. In a world where a husband could divorce his wife on a word and dispose of her on a suspicion, this ordinance wrested the suspected case out of the husband's hands: you may be jealous of her — but you may not judge her yourself, and you may not punish her yourself. You shall bring her to the priest, set her before the LORD, and walk the whole procedure through according to the ordinance, for God to decide. Jealousy is no longer a license for the husband's venting; it is a bill of indictment presented at a bar, and once presented it is bound by procedure: the offering according to rule, the standing according to rule, the oath according to rule, the outcome according to rule. For the innocent wife this ordinance is a shield — the husband's suspicion, however great, is not greater than this hearing before the LORD; if she is clean, this water cannot harm her (v. 28, below). For the guilty wife it is a warning bell — however closely the matter is kept, with no witness at all, God still has a way to bring the hidden thing to light. This ordinance does not stand on the husband's side, nor on the wife's side; it stands on the side of truth: the guilty exposed, the innocent cleared, and the jealous heart no longer left to live on conjecture.

(Application) That handful of barley meal with no oil and no frankincense also deserves another layer of thought. Israel offered many fragrant offerings; this one alone is expressly denied fragrance. Why? Because some things, when they are brought before God, must not come in the guise of fragrance: jealousy, hidden sin, a broken marriage — when these are presented at the bar, there is no dressing up, no sweetening, no festive arrangement; a man simply holds the coarse barley meal and stands bare-faced before God. What we bring before God we instinctively dress with oil and frankincense: we make our motives sound a little better, our faults a little more decent, our wounds a little smoother. This offering teaches another way of presenting a case: bring the thing exactly as it is; save the oil and the frankincense, and omit nothing of the truth. God does not despise the coarseness of barley meal; what He despises is adulteration. An offering that brings iniquity to remembrance never needs fragrance; it needs honesty.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) This ordinance is much discussed in scholarship; what follows records the main lines only and decides none of them. First, the function of the ordinance: some scholars stress its protective side for the wife — the power of conviction is taken from the hands of husband and family and lodged in priestly procedure before God, so that a wife suspected of unfaithfulness is not exposed to private violence (Milgrom devotes a special excursus in his Numbers commentary to this passage, treating the ancient Near Eastern ordeal customs and how this passage differs from them); other scholars stress its asymmetry — the ordinance examines the wife and not the husband, a product of its ancient patriarchal setting, to be understood within that historical setting and not carried over directly as a rule for marriages today. Second, the nature of the bitter water: the materials the text records (holy water, dust, ink) are in themselves not poisonous, and scholars therefore generally hold that the water's efficacy lies not in chemistry but in the oath and in God's judgment — the drinking is the ritual shell, the oath the substance of the case; there is also discussion of ancient Near Eastern parallels of deciding cases by drinking or crossing water. Third, in later Jewish tradition the Mishnah tractate Sotah, the tractate on the suspected wife, works out this procedure in great detail, and preserves the tradition that Yohanan ben Zakkai abolished the rite after the destruction of the Temple — this belongs to the layer of historical discussion, recorded here in outline only, and is not used to alter the reading of the text itself. This sermon's exposition does not rest on these discussions: the procedure, the offering, and the outcomes of the text itself are clear verse by verse, and the sermon expounds the text as it stands.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 Charged with an Oath of Cursing, and She Says Amen: The Water Enters, and Two Outcomes Follow

(Fact) The procedure reaches its weightiest step: the oath. The priest first sets out the road of innocence, verse 19 quoted word for word: "And the priest shall charge her by an oath, and say unto the woman, If no man have lain with thee, and if thou hast not gone aside to uncleanness with another instead of thy husband, be thou free from this bitter water that causeth the curse:" (Num 5:19). Note a small divergence of the base texts here, recorded as it stands: where the KJV reads "gone aside to uncleanness," the Chinese base text reads "做污秽的事" — with the character 做, whereas its verses 13, 15, and 26 use the near-synonym 作 ("作见证," "作供物," "作为"); the Chinese original checked each of these verse by verse and quotes each by its own base-text character, without merging them (see Endnote 10). The other face of the oath, verse 20 quoted word for word: "But if thou hast gone aside to another instead of thy husband, and if thou be defiled, and some man have lain with thee beside thine husband:" (Num 5:20 — ending with a colon, the sentence continuing into the curses of verse 21): innocent, she is free; guilty, she falls under the curse — both roads are read aloud to the woman before the oath is sworn. Verse 21 is the body of the curse, quoted word for word: "Then the priest shall charge the woman with an oath of cursing, and the priest shall say unto the woman, The LORD make thee a curse and an oath among thy people, when the LORD doth make thy thigh to rot, and thy belly to swell;" (Num 5:21). Verse 22 completes the oath, quoted word for word: "And this water that causeth the curse shall go into thy bowels, to make thy belly to swell, and thy thigh to rot: And the woman shall say, Amen, amen." (Num 5:22). Here the order of the paired bodily expressions is worth counting item by item: the pair "thigh" and "belly" was checked verse by verse and is found in three verses — verse 21, "thy thigh to rot, and thy belly to swell," the thigh first; verse 22, "thy belly to swell, and thy thigh to rot," the belly first; verse 27, "her belly shall swell, and her thigh shall rot," the belly first again (for the verse 27 quotation, see below). The independent recount at the translation stage found exactly the same distribution of order in the Chinese base text ("大腿消瘦" first at v. 21; "肚腹发胀" first at vv. 22 and 27) — on the structure of these verses the two base texts do not differ (see Endnote 6), though their renderings of the Hebrew differ: the KJV has the thigh "rot," the Chinese base text has it "wither away" (消瘦), and each translation keeps its own base text's word (see Endnote 3). As to why the text uses precisely this pair of expressions, commentators generally take it to concern childbearing — the "thigh" being a euphemism for the generative power (compare the idiom of Gen 46:26, those who "came out of his loins," in the original "from his thigh," Summary) — and the curse falls on childbearing exactly where verse 28's blessing for the innocent is "shall conceive seed," the two set over against each other. This sermon records that this reading exists, while its exposition follows the letter of its base texts: preach what the text writes, adding nothing and substituting nothing.

(Fact) The oath is not empty words; next it is written down and drunk. Verse 23, quoted word for word: "And the priest shall write these curses in a book, and he shall blot them out with the bitter water:" (Num 5:23 — the KJV verse ends with a colon, continuing into verse 24). The words of the curse are first written in a book, and then the writing is blotted out into the bitter water: the words are in the water, the water is in the cup — the oath passes from something heard by the ear to something taken in by the mouth. Here is a further difference between the base texts, recorded without harmonizing: the KJV expressly has the curses written "in a book"; the Chinese base text at this verse reads only "祭司要写这咒诅的话，将所写的字抹在苦水里" — the priest writes these curses and blots the written words into the bitter water — with no word for a book or scroll. Each translation follows its own base text at this verse, and the difference is noted in Endnote 3. Verse 24, quoted word for word: "And he shall cause the woman to drink the bitter water that causeth the curse: and the water that causeth the curse shall enter into her, and become bitter." (Num 5:24). Before the drinking, the offering must still pass over the altar: in verse 25 the priest takes the jealousy offering out of the woman's hand, waves it before the LORD, and offers it upon the altar (v. 25, Summary — its wording has been quoted in the abstract and in section I's discussion of the phrase), and verse 26 is quoted word for word: "And the priest shall take an handful of the offering, even the memorial thereof, and burn it upon the altar, and afterward shall cause the woman to drink the water." (Num 5:26). Note that here the KJV reads "the memorial thereof," matching its verses 15 and 18; in the Chinese base text this verse reads "纪念" where verses 15 and 18 read "思念" — a small distinction of that base text, kept there verse by verse. The order is now complete: first the offering presented, first the oath sworn, first the memorial handful burned — and then the drinking. No verdict is rehearsed before the drinking; the moment of God's judgment is reserved for the last mouthful of water.

(Fact) Once she has drunk, two outcomes follow, and the text writes them in two parallel verses. The guilty side, verse 27 quoted word for word: "And when he hath made her to drink the water, then it shall come to pass, that, if she be defiled, and have done trespass against her husband, that the water that causeth the curse shall enter into her, and become bitter, and her belly shall swell, and her thigh shall rot: and the woman shall be a curse among her people." (Num 5:27). The innocent side, verse 28 quoted word for word: "And if the woman be not defiled, but be clean; then she shall be free, and shall conceive seed." (Num 5:28). One and the same cup, two results: for the guilty, the water enters and becomes bitter, and the curse is fulfilled as sworn; for the innocent, freedom — and more than freedom, "shall conceive seed": her innocence is not merely "nothing happened"; there is a positive blessing — she is publicly cleared, and the road of her marriage and childbearing is opened again. This is the very point of the whole ordinance: the water itself makes no distinction; the distinction lies in the one who drinks. The water tests no poison; God tests the person.

(Fact) Verses 29-31 close the ordinance, and here the two base texts part company in their verse division, which must be stated plainly. In the KJV, verse 29 stands complete as a sentence in its own right, quoted word for word: "This is the law of jealousies, when a wife goeth aside to another instead of her husband, and is defiled;" (Num 5:29). In the Chinese base text, by contrast, verse 29 carries only the first half of a long sentence ("妻子背着丈夫行了污秽的事" — when a wife, instead of her husband, has done uncleanness), and the sentence is not completed until verse 30 reaches "就有这疑恨的条例" (then there is this law of jealousies); the Chinese original therefore quotes verses 29-30 together as one quotation and notes the fact in its endnotes, while this translation quotes the KJV's verses as they stand — each sermon following its own base text, the difference recorded rather than smoothed over (see Endnote 1). Verse 30, quoted word for word: "Or when the spirit of jealousy cometh upon him, and he be jealous over his wife, and shall set the woman before the LORD, and the priest shall execute upon her all this law." (Num 5:30). Verse 31 closes the passage, quoted word for word: "Then shall the man be guiltless from iniquity, and this woman shall bear her iniquity." (Num 5:31). The man is guiltless: having presented the case according to the ordinance instead of disposing of it privately, he bears no guilt for the case. If the woman has iniquity, she bears it herself — the text does not say that the innocent woman bears anything, for her outcome has already been written in verse 28: freedom, seed, innocence restored. The ordinance now closes: the suspicion arose, was presented, was decided; the clean woman goes out cleared, and the guilty bears her guilt — and this unresolved case in the camp is unresolved no longer.

(Application) "And the woman shall say, Amen, amen." In the whole ordinance the woman has only this one sentence — and it is the weightiest sentence of all. Amen is her own confirmation of the oath upon her own lips: she has heard the road of innocence, and she has heard the curse upon guilt; both roads read aloud clearly, and then she says: Amen. She is no prisoner being forced to swallow bitter water; she is a person who, before God, personally accepts this hearing. With that one Amen the case passes from "the husband suspects her" to "she stands face to face with God": the husband's suspicion steps aside, the priest's procedure steps aside, and at the end the one standing before God is she herself — and the truth she knows. Every one of us carries things for which there is no witness — kept close, hid from every eye, hidden even from those nearest us, who can only guess. This chapter tells us: a case with no witness is not a dead case with God; He has His own way of bringing it to the bar. What is hidden does not cease to exist for want of a witness, and it is not left unsettled for want of one either — bring it before Him, swear, drink, and let Him decide. What hidden matters fear most is not exposure; it is remaining forever unresolved — unresolved jealousy slowly hollows out a household. The mercy of this ordinance lies exactly here: it gives an unsettled case a settlement, innocence a vindication, and guilt a bearing — all three accomplished before God.

(Application) Notice also the fairness of this cup of bitter water: the same water, drunk by the guilty, becomes bitter; drunk by the innocent, does no harm. The water has no partiality; the partiality is in the human heart. This answers across the chapter to the fifth part of the second passage: there, the man who wronged another cannot slip away by "nobody knows"; here, the woman under suspicion cannot be wronged by "nobody can prove it" — God takes charge of both ends. He neither allows sin to hide in what is kept close, nor allows suspicion to run wild without evidence. Before Him, truth has only one outcome: it is brought to light. We have no bitter water to drink today, but the principle of this ordinance stands: when groundless suspicion arises between husband and wife, or between brother and brother, do not let it hang and ferment, and do not dispose of it privately — bring it into the light, follow a fair procedure, speak the matter clear, stand the oath firm, and leave the outcome to God. An unresolved case eats a household; a case brought to the bar saves it. This chapter spends twenty-one verses teaching us this one thing, and it is worth it.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: Uncleanness Out of the Camp, Debts Reckoned Clear, Jealousy Brought to the Bar

(Fact) The whole chapter begins with "in the midst whereof I dwell" and ends with "this woman shall bear her iniquity": at its head, the cleanness of the camp; in its middle, the restitution of debts and the presentation of jealousy; at its end, each person bearing his own outcome. Now gather the chapter into three lines. First, the camp has a boundary: the leper, the one with an issue, and the one defiled by the dead, both male and female, are put out without the camp (vv. 2-3), and the children of Israel did so (v. 4) — because this camp is the camp in whose midst the LORD dwells; cleanness is not the rejection of persons, but reverence for the One who dwells in the center. Second, accounts must be reckoned clear: man or woman who does a trespass against the LORD confesses the sin, recompenses in full, and adds the fifth part (vv. 6-7 — the "fifth part" in the original belongs to the H2549 word family, see section II); where there is no kinsman to receive it, it goes to the priest, with the ram of the atonement besides (v. 8); and the holy things and offerings belong to the priest, each under its fixed rule (vv. 9-10). Third, suspicion must be brought to the bar: in a case with no witness, the husband brings his wife to the priest with the jealousy offering (vv. 12-15); the woman stands before the LORD, holds the offering, hears the oath, says Amen, and drinks the bitter water (vv. 16-26); the guilty woman's belly swells and her thigh rots, the innocent is free and shall conceive seed (vv. 27-28); the man is guiltless, and the woman bears her own iniquity (vv. 29-31). Three lines, one source: the God who dwells in the midst of the camp will not leave uncleanness, debt, or unresolved suspicion standing in His camp.

(Application) Close the sermon by gathering the chapter into three sentences. First: what is unclean must not huddle inside the camp pretending to be well — if your state is wrong, go out, wait, be cleansed; admitting your own uncleanness is the first step back into the camp, while the man who insists nothing is wrong with him defiles the whole camp. Second: what is owed must not be left dragging — if you have confessed, repay in full and add the fifth part; the price of reconciliation is stated in the open, and only the relationship whose price is paid can be repaired; a debt left standing does not disappear by itself, it only accumulates longer on God's duplicate ledger. Third: what is suspected must not be left hanging — bring matters for which there is no witness before God and to His bar: let the clean wait for His vindication, and let the guilty bear it before Him, rather than husband and wife sleeping in one bed with divided dreams, or brothers keeping company with divided hearts, hanging in suspense for a lifetime. May there be no hidden uncleanness in our camp, no dragging debt on our account, and no hanging suspicion in our hearts — the One who dwells in our midst is willing to see His camp clean through and clear through.

(New Testament reading) Several New Testament passages have traditionally been read by the church alongside this chapter; as always, the layer is marked, the reading is offered as a reading, and nothing is read back into the text of Numbers itself. The first is in Luke 19: the chief tax collector Zacchaeus stood and said to the Lord that he would give half of his goods to the poor, and if he had taken anything from any man by false accusation, restore him fourfold (Luke 19:8, Summary) — the restitution pattern of this chapter is principal plus a fifth, while Zacchaeus's restitution is fourfold: the repair price the law sets is "add a fifth," but the repair a man makes willingly under grace goes far beyond the law. This sermon simply records the two figures as each text gives them and sets them side by side; it does not read the meaning of the one back into the other. The second is in Galatians 3: there it is said that Christ, being made a curse for us, has redeemed us from the curse of the law (Gal 3:13, Summary) — in this chapter the woman drinks the water that causeth the curse, and the guilty bears the curse in her own body; in the New Testament reading, the One who was without guilt bore the curse of others in His own body: on one side, the sinner bears her own iniquity (v. 31); on the other, the Sinless One bears it for sinners — and between them stands the cross. The third is in 1 John 1: there it is said that if we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins (1 John 1:9, Summary) — the order of this chapter's second passage is exactly first confess, then recompense, then offer the ram of atonement; the New Testament promise gathers that step of the ram into the sacrifice Christ offered once for all. (Application) But the layers must be kept: Numbers 5 is first of all itself — a command to go out of the camp, a restitution pattern of an added fifth part, a jealousy offering and a cup of bitter water. The New Testament reading is the light the church sees looking back in Christ; it must not be used to dissolve this chapter's ordinances into vapor. The solider this chapter's ordinances stand — the camp boundary checkable verse by verse, the restitution pattern quotable word by word, the procedure countable step by step — the weightier the comparison at the reading level becomes: the very place where each one bore his own iniquity is the place where, later, Another bore it for us; the clearer the law reckons the account, the greater grace appears.

(Application) As the sermon ends, put this chapter back into the hands of each hearer. First, ask about the camp: is there something in your heart that you know is unclean, yet you have kept it in the camp all along — a habit, a relationship, a sum of money of doubtful origin? You know it does not agree with the Lord who dwells in your heart, yet you cannot bring yourself to put it out of the camp. Second, ask about the account: is there a debt you have left dragging — you wronged a person once; time has passed; the other party may be gone, or may no longer mention it; yet you have never confessed it and never repaid it. This chapter says that account still stands in God's duplicate ledger; while it can still be repaid, confess, repay, and add the fifth, and clear it. Finally, ask about the suspicion: is there an unresolved case hanging in your heart — you have suspected a person for a long time, without proof or evidence, unable to let it go and unable to settle it? Do not let it hang and eat at your heart any longer: bring it before the Lord and to His bar; confront what must be confronted, entrust what must be entrusted, and let Him decide. May the three works of this chapter — going out of the camp, reckoning clear, and bringing to the bar — each take their place in us; and may the One who dwells in the midst of the camp Himself cleanse our camp, reckon our accounts clear, and decide our suspected cases, so that when we stand before Him, there is nothing left hidden in our hearts.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 5 at first seemed to me only three unrelated rules: the unclean put out of the camp, the wrongdoer paying money, and a jealous husband bringing his wife to drink bitter water. At verse 3 I stopped: "Both male and female shall ye put out, without the camp shall ye put them; that they defile not their camps, in the midst whereof I dwell." The three rules have one source: the camp must be clean, because He who dwells in the midst of the camp is holy. Then I reckoned the account in the second passage: the one who has wronged another must first confess the sin, then recompense in full, and add unto it the fifth part thereof — repair costs more than ruin, and that added fifth is the price of what I have taken from another, not a penny of it to be evaded; and even if the person wronged is gone and there is no kinsman, the account still goes to the priest, for in God's ledger it is never absent. What touches me most is the woman before the bitter water. There is no witness, no proof; her husband is jealous of her, and she can only say "Amen, amen," and leave her innocence for God to decide. Have I not also things in my heart for which there is no witness — kept close, hid from every eye, yet at night not hid from myself? This chapter tells me that a hidden matter is not a dead case with God: bring it before Him, and He has a way to settle it. So I pray today: Lord, cleanse my camp; whatever does not agree with Thee, I am willing to put it out. For those I have wronged, give me courage to confess and to recompense, and gladly to add the fifth part as well. And for the doubtful matters hanging in my heart, I will no longer guess at them and let them eat me away; I lay them all before Thee, and ask Thee Thyself to decide between right and wrong, so that when I stand before Thee there is nothing hidden left in my heart.

(iv) Verse 14, rûaḥ — the KJV renders "the spirit of jealousy come upon him"; the Chinese base text renders "生了疑恨的心," the heart of jealousy is born in him. Each follows its base text. Checking of the "fifth part" word form and number pairing (the most important single original-text check in this sermon): the Hebrew of "add unto it the fifth part thereof" at Numbers 5:7 is waḥămîšîṯô, formed from the feminine ḥămîšîṯ of the ordinal ḥămîšî (חֲמִישִׁי, Strong's H2549) with a third-person masculine singular suffix, "its fifth part." The pairing was cross-checked three ways: in verse-by-verse original-text comparisons (the word at 5:7 is annotated H2549), in verse-by-verse word-study materials (the same word at the same verse annotated H2549), and in the BDB entry (the feminine ḥămîšîṯ "commonly used as an elliptical noun, a fifth part," with citations including Leviticus 5:16 and 27:15 of the same pattern). The other same-root noun ḥōmeš (חֹמֶשׁ, Strong's H2569) likewise means "a fifth part," but according to BDB it occurs in the whole Bible only at Genesis 47:26 (Pharaoh's fifth of the land) and does not occur at Numbers 5:7. Accordingly, wherever this sermon treats the fifth part of Numbers 5:7 it writes the ḥămîšî family (H2549) and never the mispairing ḥōmeš (H2569); the same-root mispairing against which the commission warned has been checked item by item at this verse and avoided. The translation stage independently re-verified this pairing at the translation stage (verse-by-verse annotation of 5:7; the BDB note on ḥōmeš) and concurs with the Chinese original.
9. Count of the "add a fifth" cross-references, checked item by item: the passages of the same pattern (a fifth added in restitution or redemption) as listed in the lexical and verse-comparison materials are — Leviticus 5:16; Leviticus 6:5 (numbered 5:24 in the Hebrew canon; this sermon follows the English/KJV versification and notes the alternative numbering here, so that a reader checking by Hebrew verse numbers does not mistake the two for different verses); Leviticus 22:14; Leviticus 27:13; Leviticus 27:15; Leviticus 27:19; Leviticus 27:27; Leviticus 27:31; Numbers 5:7 — nine passages in all, of which four are restitution (Lev 5:16; 6:5; 22:14; Num 5:7) and five are redemption (the five verses of Leviticus 27), 4 + 5 = 9, agreeing with the total. The translation stage recounts these independently and adds one caution about how such a count is made: a concordance search on the exact KJV phrase "one fifth" returns only seven of these (Lev 6:5; 27:13, 15, 19, 27, 31; Num 5:7), because at Leviticus 5:16 and 22:14 the KJV wording is "the fifth part" ("add the fifth part thereto") rather than "one fifth." The count of nine is therefore a count by content of the rule, verified passage by passage, not a count by an English phrase; the two figures are stated side by side and not harmonized. This count does not include the fifth of Genesis 47 (Pharaoh's tax), where both the word and the pattern differ (see Endnote 8), nor any general use of the ordinal "fifth."
10. Small-word and near-synonym check of the Chinese original, reported for the record (the Chinese quotations were verified against the NP verse by verse): (i) "归与" versus "归于" — the NP at 5:7 reads "也归与所亏负的人," at 5:8 "归与服侍耶和华的祭司," at 5:9 "都要归与祭司," all with 与; some modern-punctuated printings read 于 at the same places. The Chinese original quotes 与 in all three places. (ii) "归给" — at 5:10 the NP reads "归给," differing from verses 7-9; the Chinese original treats verse 10 by summary and marks the difference rather than merging it. (iii) "作" versus "做" — the NP at 5:13 reads "没有作见证的人," at 5:15 "作供物," at 5:26 "作为这事的纪念," all with 作; only at 5:19 does it read "做污秽的事," with 做. The Chinese original quotes each as it stands, without unifying them. (iv) "思念" versus "纪念" — 5:15 and 5:18 read "思念的素祭"; 5:26 reads "作为这事的纪念"; kept distinct verse by verse (compare the KJV, which reads "memorial" at all three verses). (v) Pronouns do not drift — at 5:27 the NP reads "在他民中," with 他, and the Chinese original quotes it so; the second-person forms of 5:19-20 are quoted as the base text gives them, without honorific substitution. (vi) "出到营外" (5:3, 4) and "出营外去" (5:2) are quoted distinctly. (vii) "大腿消瘦" is quoted as the base text gives it, neither shortened to "腿消瘦" nor replaced by another version's rendering. All seven items were rechecked before delivery of the Chinese original, and the translation stage reports them as facts about that base text; the English quotations in this manuscript were independently verified against the KJV and are unaffected by these Chinese-text distinctions.
11. Remaining word anchors, each checked at its verse (a verified selection, not an exhaustive list): rûaḥ (H7307) and qinʾâ (H7068) — at 5:14 the "spirit of jealousy" structure occurs twice in the original (rendered in the Chinese base text as the heart of jealousy), with the verb qānāʾ (H7065) twice in the same verse; the same structure occurs once at 5:30. The verb ṭāmēʾ (H2930) — checked verses include 5:2 (the form in "defiled by the dead"), 5:13, 5:14 (twice), 5:20, and 5:27; the body of the sermon argues only from verses 13-14, the rest being retained as a checked selection. The noun māʿal (H4604) and verb (H4603) — checked at 5:12 (go aside, trespass) and 5:27 (done trespass against her husband). minḥâ (H4503) — the phrases at 5:15, 18, and 24-26 checked (minḥat qinʾôṯ, minḥat zikkārôn, and the like). ʿēd, "witness" (H5707) — checked at 5:13. Every word-and-number pairing above was checked individually; words not verified verse by verse (the discharge, barley, dust) carry no Strong's number anywhere in this sermon and appear only as the base texts' own wording.

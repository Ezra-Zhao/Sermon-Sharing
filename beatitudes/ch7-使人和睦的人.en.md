## I. The Scripture

"Blessed are the peacemakers, For they shall be called sons of God." (Matt. 5:9, NKJV)

This beatitude is easily read as a pleasant saying. Who does not like peace? But what Jesus speaks of here is not that those who like quiet and hate trouble are blessed, but that those who go down into the field to make peace are blessed — walking into relationships that have already frozen, walking into places where neither side is willing to bow its head first, reconnecting what has been broken. This matter takes effort, takes time, and often pleases neither side. Yet Jesus gave the highest title to those who do this matter: sons of God.

Pause here for a moment as you read. The first six beatitudes mostly speak of a person's own inner condition: poor, weeping, gentle, hungry, showing mercy, pure in heart. By the Seventh Beatitude, the person is pushed outward. What is in the heart must be tested in the most difficult interpersonal relationships. Whether you are truly a child of God is seen not only in what you are like when you pray alone, but also in what you bring when you walk into conflict — fire, or peace.

## II. Exegesis of the Original Text

The Greek text: Μακάριοι οἱ εἰρηνοποιοί, ὅτι αὐτοὶ υἱοὶ θεοῦ κληθήσονται.

**1. "The peacemakers" (οἱ εἰρηνοποιοί, hoi eirēnopoioi)**

εἰρηνοποιός (eirēnopoios, Strong G1518) is a compound word, appearing only this once in the New Testament. It is put together from two words: εἰρήνη (eirēnē, Strong G1515, peace) plus a branch of ποιέω (poieō, Strong G4160, to do, to make, to accomplish). Literally it is "people who make peace," "people who make peace come about."

First look at the word "peace." In the Septuagint, eirēnē is often used to translate the Hebrew shalom. Shalom speaks not only of no fighting and no quarreling; it speaks of wholeness, steadiness, everything in its place: man's relationship with God set right, man's relationship with man set right, and man's heart also at rest. So peace in the Bible has never been as thin as "everyone stop quarreling"; it is broken things being restored, things that ought to be together being together again.

Then look at the action "make" — this is the sinew of this beatitude. There is another word in the New Testament describing a person as peaceable in temperament and fond of peace, eirēnikos (see James 3:17; Heb. 12:11, in substance); that speaks of temperament. Jesus did not use that word here; He used people who "make peace." Having a good temperament, not flushing red in the face with people — that is a blessing, but it is not yet the Seventh Beatitude. The Seventh Beatitude speaks of action: peace does not happen by itself; you must go and make it, and pay a cost to make it come about.

The same verbal root was used once by Paul in Colossians 1:20, speaking of God Himself: God, through the blood of Christ's cross, "made peace" (in the NKJV rendering, "having made peace through the blood of His cross"; the original verb is precisely εἰρηνοποιέω, eirēnopoieō, Strong G1517). This parallel is very important. Making peace was originally a matter God Himself did — He did not stand far away shouting "stop fighting"; He Himself paid the heaviest cost to reconnect the relationship broken with man. When people go to make peace among people, they are learning the way the heavenly Father does things; they are doing again, among people, the reconciliation they themselves have received.

For this reason, making peace must be distinguished from two other matters. One is papering over peace: afraid of offending people, afraid of an ugly scene, covering up problems where they exist, covering over cracks where they exist, a harmonious appearance on the surface while things rot underneath. This is not making peace; this is postponing an explosion. True peace can withstand bringing problems out into the open, because the result it wants is restoration, not merely quiet. The other is muddling through without principle: not asking right or wrong, giving each side fifty strokes of the board, forcing the wronged party to "be magnanimous." In shalom there is a place for justice; peace without right or wrong is asking the weaker party to swallow the wrong — that is not called peace, it is called suppression. The person who makes peace stands in the middle, not without a standpoint; his standpoint is: the truth must be spoken clearly, responsibility must be acknowledged by someone, a cost must be willingly paid by someone — often it is he himself who pays first.

In Jesus' age, the peace in people's hearts was Roman peace: quiet exchanged for a knife at the throat. The picture Jesus gives is completely different. His peace is not who has suppressed whom; it is enemies able to sit at the same table, debts able to be forgiven, broken things able to be reconnected. His disciples go into the world to make peace, relying not on suppression, but on first admitting their own wrong, first extending their own hand, first bearing their own loss.

The rendering of this verse in the Lü Zhenzhong Translation is broadly close in meaning to the Chinese Union Version; both rest on "peacemakers" and "they shall be called sons of God."

English versions for comparison:
- KJV: "Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God."
- NIV: "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God."
- NRSV: "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God."

The NRSV and NIV render "sons" as children, taking into account that this title is not limited to males; the Chinese Union Version and KJV retain "sons," retaining the weight of inheritance and standing that "son" carried in the ancient world. Each rendering has its basis; when reading, receive both layers: this title is both the standing of children, and the acknowledgment of inheriting the family estate and being worthy of this surname.

**2. "They shall be called sons of God" (υἱοὶ θεοῦ κληθήσονται, huioi theou klēthēsontai)**

"Shall be called" is κληθήσονται (klēthēsontai), from the verb καλέω (kaleō, Strong G2564, to call, to name), in the future passive: they shall be called, they shall be recognized as. As with the passives in the preceding beatitudes, the one who does this is God. It is God Himself who opens His mouth to acknowledge them: this is My son, this is My daughter.

In Jewish idiom, "whose son" often speaks not only of bloodline, but of whose temperament one resembles. Jesus called James and John "Sons of Thunder," saying their temper was like thunder (Mark 3:17); in Luke's Gospel, "a son of peace" (Luke 10:6, in substance) speaks of a person whose temperament accords with peace. Reading the Seventh Beatitude according to this usage, the meaning becomes transparent: when a person makes peace among people, bystanders — and ultimately God Himself — will recognize: this temperament resembles his Father. God is the God who makes peace (Rom. 15:33 and Heb. 13:20 in the NKJV both call God the "God of peace," the same rendering in both places); when His children do the same thing, it is the family character.

Jesus said this again a little later in the same sermon, only changing the scene: "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who spitefully use you and persecute you, that you may be sons of your Father in heaven" (Matt. 5:44–45, NKJV, in substance). Loving enemies and making peace are, for Jesus, two ways of saying the same matter. The person willing to extend his hand first between adversaries most resembles the heavenly Father, because the heavenly Father extended His hand first in just this way toward those who were His adversaries (cf. Rom. 5:10: when we were enemies, God's Son already died for us).

There is also a future layer. κληθήσονται is future tense. Those who make peace today may not be recognized by people — more often they are misunderstood by both sides together. The promise of the text does not rest on people's reputation, but on God's final naming: on that day, how God calls you will be weightier than how everyone discusses you today. This is not a word of comfort; this is the confidence by which peacemakers can keep on doing it, without needing to prove themselves in haste.

## III. The Place and Logic of This Beatitude in the Sermon on the Mount

By the time the Beatitudes are read to the Seventh, the preceding road draws together.

The Fifth Beatitude, mercy; the Sixth, purity of heart; the Seventh, making peace — these three beatitudes are a string. Mercy is the hand willing to loosen; purity of heart is having only one Master inside; making peace is what this heart goes out and accomplishes. The order cannot be reversed: a person harboring double-mindedness and calculation in his heart, entering a conflict, will only choose sides, settle accounts, and pour oil on the fire; it is not that he does not want peace — he has no capital for it. Only when the heart has first been cleaned can a person, when neither side will yield, speak not standing on his own interests, but standing only on the matter of restoration.

The Seventh Beatitude is also the outward peak of the Beatitudes. Where is the poor in spirit person finally to go? Not to hide in the mountains for solitude alone, but to return among people and do the work of joining in the most broken places. At this step, the disciple's identity also changes from "one who receives" to "one who resembles the Father" — the preceding blessings mostly use the tone of receiving and promise; here, the title is given directly: sons of God. The deepest mark of children is not what they can say, but acting as the Father acts.

And the Eighth Beatitude immediately following is being persecuted for righteousness' sake (5:10). This connection is jarring at first reading, honest on reflection: even peacemakers will be persecuted. The work of peace does not guarantee being welcomed. You go to mediate, and may be taken for being partial; you speak the truth, and may offend both sides together; you admit wrong first, and may be taken as weak and bully-able. Jesus did not deceive disciples by saying, if you go to make peace, everyone will thank you. He placed the two beatitudes side by side, which amounts to giving notice in advance: doing the right thing and paying the cost are the same package. The tears of the Eighth Beatitude, in fact, begin to be counted from the Seventh Beatitude.

In the whole Sermon on the Mount, the Seventh Beatitude also has its echoes. Speaking of offering sacrifices, Jesus said, first go and be reconciled to your brother, then come and offer your gift (Matt. 5:23–24, in substance) — reconciliation is placed before worship. Speaking of prayer, He taught people to pray, forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors (Matt. 6:12). Speaking of treating people, He said, whatever you want men to do to you, do also to them (Matt. 7:12). The Seventh Beatitude is not an isolated maxim; it is the source of the interpersonal ethics of the whole sermon: God's children on earth first become people of reconciliation.

## IV. Cross References

- Ps. 34:14 — "Depart from evil and do good; Seek peace and pursue it." (NKJV) "Seek" is not enough; one must "pursue" with one heart. Peace can run; if people do not pursue it, it will not stay by itself. Peter later quoted this line (1 Pet. 3:10–11), teaching the church how to speak and act in persecution.
- Ps. 133:1 — "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is For brethren to dwell together in unity!" (NKJV) David compares unity to the precious oil poured on the head, and again to the refreshing dew. In the Bible, peace has never been making do; it is a good thing, a thing with fragrance.
- Isa. 9:6; Isaiah 52:7 — The prophet on one side prophesies that the coming child will be called "Prince of Peace," and on the other side praises the feet of those who bring good news: "How beautiful upon the mountains Are the feet of him who brings good news, Who proclaims peace" (Isa. 52:7, NKJV, in substance). Before the Prince of Peace comes, there are first people proclaiming peace running on the road. When disciples make peace, they are preparing the way for that Prince.
- Matt. 5:23–24 — At the altar, remembering that your brother has something against you, first leave your gift there, go and be reconciled. Note Jesus' wording: it is the brother who has something against you; it is not entirely your fault, yet you are to go first. The peacemaker does not calculate who should take the first step; often it is the more mature one who steps first.
- Eph. 2:14–17 — Paul says Christ "Himself is our peace," who broke down the middle wall of separation between Jews and Gentiles, made peace, and preached peace to those far and near. The church itself is the greatest finished product of the Seventh Beatitude: people who formerly would not associate with each other even in old age and death now sit at the same table. If the church first splits into several piles internally, this finished product is smashed in its own hands.
- 2 Cor. 5:18–19 — God, through Christ, reconciled us to Himself, and has given us "the ministry of reconciliation." The word Paul uses is "ministry" — an entrustment, a commission. Making peace is not an elective course for certain good-tempered believers; it is written on the commission of everyone who has received reconciliation.
- Rom. 12:18 — "If it is possible, as much as depends on you, live peaceably with all men." (NKJV) In this sentence is Paul's honesty: the words "if it is possible" acknowledge that peace is not entirely in our hands; the words "as much as depends on you" state our responsibility in full — whether the other party accepts is his matter; whether I have done my utmost on my side is my matter.
- Heb. 12:14 — "Pursue peace with all people, and holiness." (NKJV) Peace and holiness are pursued side by side; neither can be lacking. With holiness only and no peace, a person becomes a cold, hard judge; with peace only and no holiness, a person becomes a people-pleaser without right or wrong.
- James 3:18 — In the NKJV, "Now the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace." This sentence in James is almost an echo of the Seventh Beatitude: the fruit of righteousness is not argued out in quarrels; it is sown, seed by seed, by peacemakers, and grows out.

## V. Life Application

**Scene One: At home and in the church, take that first step.** Most ruptures do not begin with great matters. A sentence not spoken well, a matter left hanging for a few days, a misunderstanding no one is willing to explain — slowly it freezes into ice. By the time people sit at the same table at New Year or festivals, there is a wall between their hearts; it is the same in worship — people singing the same song together may not have spoken properly to each other for three months. The requirement of the Seventh Beatitude here is very concrete: do not wait for the other party to come first. The order in Matt. 5:23–24 is: remembering there is a matter, first lay down the gift in your hand, go first. Going does not mean taking all right and wrong onto oneself; going is first opening the door: I heard there is a matter in your heart; I want to hear you finish speaking. What many deadlocks lack has never been reasoning, but the first person willing to sit down and listen to the end. Leaders in the church and elders at home especially should pay attention to this matter: if you do not take your step, those below often dare not take theirs. An atmosphere of peace often begins when the person of higher standing bows his head first.

**Scene Two: In the workplace and in group chats, do not be a person who passes the fire on.** Many conflicts today do not break out face to face; they grow large in messages. An unverified sentence forwarded out, a screenshot with head and tail cut off thrown into a group — within a few minutes it can light up two departments, several families. In such an environment, a peacemaker has a few very small but very hard lessons: do not pass on unverified words; hearing idle talk, first ask one question, "Did you hear it with your own ears?"; do not reply to that email or that message in the heat of anger — rather let it pass overnight; when the two sides say different things, bring both sides to the same table to speak clearly, and do not be a go-between passing messages — by the third hand, nine out of ten sentences change flavor. These matters are inconspicuous, yet they are precisely the daily version of that word ποιέω, "make": peace is made one message at a time, one meal at a time, and is also destroyed one by one in the same way. Whether you are a person who lowers pressure or raises pressure in a team — colleagues know in their hearts, and that ledger is remembered more clearly than you yourself think.

**Scene Three: Facing an adversary one truly cannot get past, do your utmost, but do not guarantee the result.** In some relationships, you extend your hand and the other party does not receive it; you apologize, and the other party takes it as a handle; you retreat a step, and the other party advances two steps. At such a time, the Seventh Beatitude does not ask you to retreat without limit, still less to call being bullied spiritual. The measure in Rom. 12:18 is important here: doing one's utmost is my duty; whether peace is achieved is not entirely my report card. Boundaries that should be set must be set, procedures that should be followed must be followed, when it is time to say no, say no — Jesus Himself did not retreat unconditionally before everyone. The greatest difference between making peace and being a people-pleaser lies precisely here: the people-pleaser fears conflict, lets others pay every cost, and hides himself in quiet; the peacemaker does not fear speaking problems clearly; he is simply willing to pay his own share of the cost first. Having done one's utmost, if the other party still refuses, that regret can be handed to God. The title promised in the text is "they shall be called," not "they shall succeed" — God commends peacemakers, not only those who have succeeded in making peace.

## VI. Response Prayer

Lord, You are the God who makes peace. When we were at enmity with You, You did not stand far away waiting for us to bow our heads first; You sent Your Son to bring us back at the cost of the cross. That I can sit here praying today is itself something Your peacemaking has accomplished.

Please first do this work in my heart. Those faults in me unwilling to be admitted first, hands unwilling to be extended first, face unwilling to be laid down — You know them more clearly than I. Give me a heart that has been purified, so that when I walk into conflict, I do not first calculate my own gain and loss, but first think how to reconnect what has been broken.

At my home, and in the places where I serve and work, please use me as the person who takes the first step. Where I should apologize, let me be able to say it; where I should listen to the end, let me have patience to listen to the end; where right and wrong should be spoken clearly, let me not hide; where, having done my utmost, it still cannot be reconnected, please let me place the result in Your hands, without bitterness and without self-blame.

You have promised that peacemakers shall be called Your children. I do not ask that everyone understand me; I ask only that on that day, You acknowledge me as resembling Your child. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ I pray, Amen.

Summary: Leviticus chapter 2 treats the only bloodless offering among the five offerings — the meat offering (grain offering). A man brings fine flour, oil and frankincense, cakes baked in the oven, on the griddle and in the pan, and first ripe ears dried by the fire, before the altar; a handful, the memorial of it, is burned upon the altar, and the remnant belongs to the priests as a thing most holy; leaven and honey are forbidden to the fire of the altar, but salt must never be lacking from any offering, for salt is the covenant — the sign that God's covenant shall not fail.

## I. An Offering Without Blood: Bringing the Produce of Labour Before God 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, in the last chapter we saw the burnt offering: bullock, sheep and bird wholly burned upon the altar, the smoke ascending, accepted by God for a sweet savour. In chapter 2 the picture suddenly changes. There is no lowing of cattle, no slaughter of sheep, no blood sprinkled round about the altar. Set before us are a basin of fine flour, a little oil, a pinch of frankincense, cakes baked in an oven, thin cakes baked on a griddle, food fried in a pan, and fresh ears of grain rubbed in the hand and dried by the fire. Many readers, coming to this, ask first: can this also be called a sacrifice? Without blood, without death, how can it stand beside the burnt offering and the sin offering, and be counted among the five offerings? It is precisely this question that brings out the importance of the meat offering. Chapter 2 first teaches us that sacrifice does not speak only one language. There is not only one road, speaking with blood, by which God accepts man's coming. Beside the offering of blood there is another offering, which speaks with the produce of the land, with the labour of man's hands, and with daily bread.

Let us state the factual level clearly. Leviticus chapters 1 to 7 treat in order the burnt offering, the meat offering, the peace offering, the sin offering and the trespass offering, together called the five offerings. The meat offering stands second. In Hebrew it is called minchah, in its basic sense a gift, a tribute — what an inferior presents to a superior, a people to a king, man to God. In Leviticus chapter 2 the word takes a specialised use and denotes specifically an offering consisting chiefly of grain, what our Chinese Bible renders as the grain offering. The rendering grasps its character: it consists of vegetable matter, uses no livestock, and sheds no blood. It is the only bloodless offering among the five. "Only" must be stated accurately: the other offerings either consist chiefly of livestock or necessarily involve the handling of blood; the meat offering alone has for its substance the produce of plants — fine flour, oil and grain — with no slaughter and no shedding of blood. This distinction is a fact of the text, not a metaphor added later by the preacher.

That the meat offering is without blood does not mean that it is slight. The text likewise says that it is offered upon the altar, likewise calls it a sweet savour, and likewise calls it an offering made by fire unto the LORD: "And when any will offer a meat offering unto the LORD, his offering shall be of fine flour; and he shall pour oil upon it, and put frankincense thereon" (Lev 2:1, KJV). The offerer of a meat offering likewise comes to the door of the tabernacle, likewise places the offering in the priest's hand; likewise a portion is burned to God upon the altar, and the remnant that remains likewise has a holy assignment. The form differs; the seriousness of worship does not diminish. To make a food of flour into a sacrifice shows precisely that God values man's daily life. Bullocks and sheep are the wealth of herding; fine flour and fresh ears are the wealth of farming; the former is what is in the fold, the latter is the harvest of the field. Israel, dwelling in tents, has only just come out of Egypt and is still in the wilderness, yet God already takes the life of the future — entering Canaan, tilling the ground and gathering grain — into the ordinances of worship through the meat offering. The wilderness has no fields yet, but the ordinance speaks first of the produce of the fields. This is not an empty fancy detached from reality; God first settles the people's future days within the order of worship: in time to come, when you have land, wheat, mills and ovens, what comes from your fields and what is made by your hands must also be brought before God.

This brings out the second factual character of the meat offering: it offers the produce of labour. The livestock of the burnt offering is also the fruit of man's keeping, and labour is naturally present there too; in the meat offering the traces of labour are more direct and more daily. Wheat must be sown, reaped, threshed and ground into fine flour; cakes must be kneaded, mixed with oil, put into the oven, turned on the griddle and fried in the pan; fresh ears must be plucked, dried and rubbed. In every process there are man's hands, and there is sweat. When a man sets apart grain obtained by his labour and brings it before God, the act itself is a declaration: the earth is the LORD's, the produce comes from God's gift, labour is under God's care, and therefore the first portion and the best portion of the produce must first return to God, rather than waiting until a man has eaten his fill and his barn is full, and then fobbing God off with the scraps that remain.

Here, too, we must distinguish levels, lest we say too much. That the meat offering is the produce of land and labour offered to God is a factual summary drawn from the composition of the offering and the acts of offering: the offering does indeed come from the field and does indeed pass through man's working. That the meat offering reminds man that God is the Lord who gives grain, and that man should answer grace with labour, is a homiletical exposition drawn from this fact — a reasonable extension, but not a slogan already written in chapter 2. Distinguishing these two levels makes preaching stronger, not weaker. We do not pass off our own reflections as the words of the text; we stand before the facts the text sets out, and watch how they naturally lead a man from the altar back to the field, and from the tabernacle back to the kitchen. Worship, it turns out, does not happen only at special moments; it also falls upon the ingredients of every meal. What is offered to God is not only man's great decisions; it includes the ordinary harvest he gains day by day with his own hands.

Note also that in the later narrative of Scripture the meat offering often does not appear alone. It often accompanies the burnt offering and the peace offering, following the animal sacrifice as an accompanying offering. In the ordinances of Numbers, when a bullock, a ram or a lamb is offered, how much fine flour and how much oil are to accompany each is fixed by measure; in the settings of feasts and vows, livestock and meat offering are likewise offered together. Read alone, chapter 2 might make us think the meat offering is an independent offering; set back into the whole sacrificial system, it is more often an offering that walks together with the offering of blood. This observation is important, for it keeps us from two extremes: despising the meat offering, as if without blood it had no weight; and exalting the meat offering, as if without blood it were more spiritual and cleaner, and could replace the offering of blood. The text teaches neither. The meat offering has its place, and the blood offering has its place, each present before the altar in the order God appointed. God's teaching is whole; man must not set aside any part of it at his own preference.

Finally, notice how the text names the offerer. Chapter 2 opens: "And when any will offer a meat offering unto the LORD" (Lev 2:1, KJV) — literally, when any soul, any living person, offers. Corresponding to "if any man of you" in chapter 1, the initiator of the sacrifice is still the lay offerer himself. He brings the flour from his home, brings the cakes he has made, brings the first ripe ears. The priest is responsible for burning the memorial of it upon the altar and for receiving the remnant, but he cannot decide in the offerer's place what to offer, how much to take, or what grade of produce to use. Drawing near to God remains the people's own affair, and offering one's labour remains each man's own response. God did not leave the affairs of the field only to farmers, nor the affairs of the stove only to cooks; He spoke the ordinance of the meat offering to the whole people, teaching everyone who has a harvest to learn to bring his harvest before Him. This chapter begins with just such a basin of fine flour.

## II. Fine Flour and Its Forms: One Offering, Different Ways of Making 08:00-24:00

The ordinance of chapter 2 may be divided by its manner of making into several forms. Preaching takes only the main line and does not spread out every item, because the point of the ordinance is not to teach men how to make flour foods, but to teach them this: one and the same offering may have different manners of making, and though the manners differ, reverence does not diminish. The text first treats the most basic form: raw fine flour with oil poured on it and frankincense put on it. If the offerer offers fine flour as a meat offering, he pours oil upon the fine flour, puts frankincense on it, and brings it to the priest. Three words must be grasped here. Fine flour is wheat ground finest and sifted cleanest, as against coarse flour and whole grain — the best grade of flour. Oil is olive oil, both an element of daily food and, in sacrifice, regularly mingled with flour to make the meat offering a complete offering. Frankincense is a precious resinous spice, strong in fragrance when burned, offered together with the fine flour in the meat offering, so that the bloodless offering likewise rises with a sweet savour. Together the three form the most typical composition of the meat offering: flour is the body, oil mingles and shapes it, frankincense makes it a sweet savour.

Why must it be fine flour? This question must not be answered offhand with spiritualised explanations — that "fine" means careful, or means broken; that would be homiletical use, not a fact of lexical meaning. Returning to the factual level: fine flour is the most fully worked and highest-quality flour. The use of the best grade in sacrifice is a principle common to the five offerings. The burnt offering requires an animal without blemish, the meat offering requires fine flour; the reasoning is the same: what is offered to God must be the good among what a man has, not what he himself does not want. A farmer grinds his wheat: he eats the coarse flour from the first grinding himself and keeps the fine flour for the offering. That choice itself is reverence. Whether a man is willing to set apart the best first for God is shown in the heart before the altar is even lit.

The treatment of this form of raw fine flour is also the plainest. The priest takes out of it his handful — a handful of the flour, a handful of the oil, with all the frankincense — and burns it upon the altar as a memorial: "he shall take thereout his handful of the flour thereof, and of the oil thereof, with all the frankincense thereof; and the priest shall burn the memorial of it upon the altar, to be an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD" (Lev 2:2, KJV). Note: the whole basin is not burned. The priest grasps a handful, together with all the frankincense, and burns it upon the altar; the greater part that remains belongs to the priests. The relation between this handful and the remnant is the theme of the next section; here we record the act: the offerer brings a basin, the priest takes a handful and burns it to God, and what remains becomes the priests' food. The offerer's intention is represented by that handful; God receives the sweet savour of the handful, and the priests receive the supply of the remainder. Offering a basin of flour is at once worship of God and supply for those who serve in the sanctuary. In God's arrangement, worship and supply are never separated.

The second form is what is baked in the oven: "And if thou bring an oblation of a meat offering baken in the oven, it shall be unleavened cakes of fine flour mingled with oil, or unleavened wafers anointed with oil" (Lev 2:4, KJV). The oven was a common baking implement of the time, a ground oven or a clay oven, such as the people had in their homes. The third form is what is made on the griddle, a flat iron or clay plate set over the fire, on which fine flour mingled with oil is baked into a cake; when made, it is parted in pieces, and oil is poured on it: "Thou shalt part it in pieces, and pour oil thereon: it is a meat offering" (Lev 2:6, KJV). The fourth form is what is made in the frying pan. The implement in the text is close to the griddle yet slightly different; scholarship discusses the exact shapes of the two implements — one held to be a shallow plate for baking, the other a deeper pan for frying — and the detail need not be settled dogmatically. The main line is clear: one is baked on a plate, the other fried in a pan, and both are daily ways of cooking at the stove. Set side by side, the three forms show us that God brought worship into the people's kitchens. Oven, griddle and pan are all things that wives and servants used every day. God did not say that worship may only use rare vessels that the people's homes do not have; He said: what is made in the oven in which you ordinarily bake cakes, on the griddle on which you bake, and in the pan in which you fry, may also become a meat offering. Holiness lies not in the rarity of the vessel, but in the offering's being made according to God's ordinance and belonging to God as holy.

The three forms share one limitation, of great importance: all must be made unleavened. The oven-baked cakes are unleavened cakes, the wafers are unleavened wafers, and what is made on the griddle and in the pan stands under the general principle forbidding leaven. The question of leaven is left for concentrated treatment in the next section; here we set down the fact: in the forms in which the meat offering is made into cakes, leaven is wholly unused. Being unleavened is not only a detail of making; it is a mark of the meat offering's identity. A man may not bring the leavened dough ready at home and offer it; the unleavened must be made specially for the offering. In that specialness the distinction between the meat offering and daily food appears: daily food may have leaven; the offering may not. Separation is established precisely in this tension between daily vessels and a special manner of making — the vessels are daily, the manner of making is set apart as holy.

The last form is the form of the firstfruits, and it differs from those before it. The text says: "And if thou offer a meat offering of thy firstfruits unto the LORD, thou shalt offer for the meat offering of thy firstfruits green ears of corn dried by the fire, even corn beaten out of full ears. And thou shalt put oil upon it, and lay frankincense thereon: it is a meat offering" (Lev 2:14-15, KJV). This speaks of the first ripe grain of the field, still in the ear, not yet ground into flour. The manner of offering is to dry the fresh ears by the fire, or to beat out and rub the fresh grain, put oil on it and lay frankincense on it, making it the meat offering of the firstfruits. This form brings out the seasonableness of the meat offering. The fine flour and cakes of the earlier forms may be offered at any time; the firstfruits have a time limit — they must be the first ripe, and must be offered at the very beginning of the harvest. The first ripe belongs to God first; only then does a man eat of the new grain himself. This order must not be reversed. If a man waits until his barn is full before remembering to offer the firstfruits, they are no longer firstfruits. The preciousness of the firstfruits lies not in their quantity but in their priority — before a man has himself tasted the new grain, he brings the first handful before God, acknowledging that this season's harvest comes from God. This form echoes, at a distance, the later ordinances of the feast of firstfruits and the new meat offering at Pentecost, forming the rhythm of thanksgiving in Israel's calendar.

Set the forms together, and the whole picture of the meat offering becomes clear. Raw fine flour is the offering of an ingredient; oven-baked, griddle-baked and pan-fried are offerings of cooked food; fresh ears are the offering of what is fresh in season. The rich have the rich man's manner of offering, with fine flour, oil and frankincense, full measure and full spice; ordinary households have the ordinary household's manner, making a cake with the oven and griddle at home; the farmer, at the start of the harvest, has the farmer's manner, holding a handful of ears dried by the fire. Like the burnt offering, the meat offering builds in room for offering according to one's means: God does not ask a man to offer what he does not have and cannot make; God asks a man, according to what he has and with what he knows how to make, to offer according to the ordinance. The compassion of grading is in chapter 1 and is also in chapter 2; the two chapters are of one line. The difference is that in chapter 1 the grading lies in the size of the livestock, while in this chapter it lies in the simplicity or complexity of the making. Whether complex or simple, the combination of fine flour, oil and frankincense, the limitation of unleavened, the memorial's belonging to the altar and the remnant's belonging to the priests, are all consistent. The form may differ; the intention and the order must not be disordered. This is the main line the forms teach us: worship has never counted only when done in one single manner, but every manner must be done earnestly within the bounds God appointed. Carelessness is not freedom; variety in conformity with the ordinance is the picture the meat offering gives us.

## III. A Handful Burned Upon the Altar, the Remnant Most Holy 24:00-42:00

How the meat offering is divided is the hinge for understanding chapter 2. As said above, the priest takes a handful out of the meat offering and burns it upon the altar. To this handful the text gives a special name: the memorial of it. "Memorial" is not vague remembrance; it is a technical term of the rite: that small portion taken out, burned upon the altar, and presenting the whole offering before God. The priest takes a handful of the flour and a handful of the oil, with all the frankincense, and burns them upon the altar of burnt offering; smoke and fragrance rise together, and the text calls it an offering made by fire, of a sweet savour unto the LORD. Notice the contrast of measure: what is burned is only a handful; what is brought is a basin, an ovenful, a panful. What God receives is, in quantity, the smallest portion, but in meaning it represents the whole. The offerer may not say his offering is discounted because the greater part was not burned; nor may he, because only a handful is burned, withhold the greater part at home and bring only a handful. The order of the ordinance is: the whole offering is brought first, and the handful is then taken out. The whole is represented by a handful — this is precisely the ritual language of the part standing for the whole. The smoke of that handful upon the altar declares that the whole basin of flour and the whole ovenful of cakes have already been given to God.

The assignment of the frankincense also deserves attention. Of the flour only a handful is taken, of the oil only a handful, but the frankincense is burned wholly and left not at all to the priests. Commentaries explain differently, some from the frankincense's being inedible, some from fragrance belonging wholly to God; the explanations may be set side by side, but the main line is clear: the most precious and most fragrant part belongs wholly to the altar and is not made man's food.

What of the remnant? The text is unmistakable: "And the remnant of the meat offering shall be Aaron's and his sons': it is a thing most holy of the offerings of the LORD made by fire" (Lev 2:3, KJV), and again, "And that which is left of the meat offering shall be Aaron's and his sons': it is a thing most holy of the offerings of the LORD made by fire" (Lev 2:10, KJV). "Most holy" is a word of great weight in Leviticus. Among the furnishings of the sanctuary there is a distinction between holy and most holy, and among offerings likewise. The burnt offering is wholly burned and has no remnant; the remnant of the meat offering, together with the remnants of the sin offering and the trespass offering, is called most holy, belongs to the priests, and may only be eaten by the priests in the holy place; it may not be taken home to be shared with the family, still less taken to the market to be sold. The words "most holy" fix the nature of the remaining cakes: they are not simply the priests' wages, not a fee the offerer pays to those who handle his business; they remain within the realm of the holy — food God assigns from His own altar to those who serve. When the priests eat this remnant, they are not receiving an ordinary ration; they are receiving, within the bounds of holiness, the supply God gives.

What does this arrangement mean for the offerer? It means his offering has two destinations: the handful toward God is burned; the portion toward man feeds the priests who serve at the tabernacle. The basin of flour he brings is joined at one end to the altar and at the other end to the priests' table. The priests have no fields, and their source of livelihood is precisely the remnants of the offerings the people present, together with the ordinances of firstfruits and tithes. If the people offer willingly, the priests receive supply and serve with undivided heart. God binds these two things together: every offering is at once worship, and a completing of another's service.

Then the text turns and speaks of two things the fire of the altar must never admit. The text says: "No meat offering, which ye shall bring unto the LORD, shall be made with leaven: for ye shall burn no leaven, nor any honey, in any offering of the LORD made by fire" (Lev 2:11, KJV). This verse is the turning point of chapter 2; two forbidden things appear side by side: leaven and honey. Take leaven first. Leaven was used every day in the food of the time — a piece of old dough kept back, to leaven tomorrow's fresh dough; this was common knowledge. Sacrifice excludes leaven from the fire of the altar. Why? Chapter 2 does not itself give the reason directly, and we must state this honestly: the reasons are inferences drawn by later readers from the context and the usage of the whole book, not reasons written plainly in this verse. The inferences have several lines of evidence, which may be set side by side. First, the memory of the exodus: when Israel left Egypt there was no time for dough to be leavened, and they ate unleavened bread; the unleavened was thereafter joined to the redemption of the exodus, and removing leaven at the feasts became an act commemorating redemption. Second, fermentation has the character of swelling, spreading and making the whole lump rise, and at the symbolic level is often associated with the spread of corruption. Third, fermentation is a process of change over time, whereas the offering seeks a state set apart as holy and presented whole, standing opposite the change fermentation brings. Each line has its evidence, and scholars differ in emphasis; this sermon draws no final conclusion, and points only to the fact itself, which is clear enough: the portion of the meat offering that enters the fire of the altar must have no leaven.

Take honey next. Honey is forbidden side by side with leaven, and likewise may not be burned upon the altar. The word must first be clarified: honey in Scripture may denote bees' honey, and may also denote a syrup boiled from grapes, dates and other fruit. Whether the honey forbidden in chapter 2 denotes bees' honey specifically, includes fruit syrup as well, or chiefly denotes fruit syrup, is discussed in scholarship, and the notes set the two views side by side. Here we grasp the boundary first: what is forbidden is burning honey upon the altar as an offering made by fire; it is not a forbidding of eating honey, nor a statement that honey itself is unclean. The land of Israel is called a land flowing with milk and honey; honey is a good of that land, yet it may not go up onto the altar. Not everything good is fit to be offered upon the altar; the boundary of worship is drawn not by man's liking but by God's word.

Leaven and honey, set side by side, also show a common point: both are connected with fermentation or concentrated sweetness, and both represent man's impulse to work the offering over again and add flavour to it. The flour of the meat offering, in its original form, is fine flour with oil; with leaven added, it swells; with honey added, it turns sweet. God's ordinance says: offer it in the form of the meat offering as it is — do not swell it, do not sweeten it. This level belongs to homiletical extension and must be marked as such: the fact of the text is that leaven and honey are forbidden to the fire; the exposition "do not swell, do not sweeten" is an application the preacher draws from this fact, not a reason stated plainly in the original text. Once marked, the exposition still carries weight: the two things a man most easily does when he comes before God are precisely to puff himself up a little and to sweeten his words a little. By means of a cake, the ordinance first teaches us: before the altar there is no need of swelling, no need of sweet words; what is needed is an offering as it truly is, according to what God appointed.

To close this section, gather the picture of the division: a handful of flour, a handful of oil, and all the frankincense, burned upon the altar as the memorial, rising as a sweet savour; the flour and cakes that remain, belonging to the priests as most holy, eaten in the holy place; and upon the fire of the altar, no admission at all for leaven or honey. The offerer's flour is thus divided completely between altar and priests, with nothing falling outside the boundary. Yet within this orderliness two knots remain untied: if leaven and honey must on no account be offered, why does the next verse say that they may be brought with the firstfruits? And if salt is only a seasoning, why does the text, in its most solemn tone, say that it must never be lacking from any offering? These two questions lead us to the highest point of chapter 2.

## IV. Thou Shalt Not Suffer the Salt of the Covenant to Be Lacking 42:00-56:00

Untie the first knot first. In the very next verse after the prohibition, the text says: "As for the oblation of the firstfruits, ye shall offer them unto the LORD: but they shall not be burnt on the altar for a sweet savour" (Lev 2:12, KJV). "Them" denotes precisely the offering of leavened cakes and of things with honey when they are presented as an oblation of the firstfruits. The tension between the two passages must be set out honestly side by side, and neither passage may be smoothed away for the sake of easier preaching. Verse 11 says that no leaven and no honey shall be burned as an offering made by fire; verse 12 says that leaven and honey may be offered to the LORD with the firstfruits, only they shall not be burned upon the altar. Set together, the meaning of the ordinance is complete: leaven and honey are not things that must on no account be offered; they are things that must on no account go up onto the altar. They may be brought, may be presented, may enter the uses of the sanctuary, may become the priests' food or the feasts' offerings, but they do not enter the fire of the altar and are not turned into a rising sweet savour. "To bring" and "to burn upon" are two different acts; "to offer to the LORD" and "to burn upon the altar as an offering made by fire" are two different levels. Confuse these two levels, and the tension becomes a contradiction; distinguish them, and the tension becomes a teaching about boundaries: God accepts the people's thanksgiving of the firstfruits, yet still guards the holiness of the altar; thanksgiving offerings may be rich, but the fire of the altar may burn only what God has commanded. In the tradition of the feast of firstfruits, at Pentecost a new meat offering is offered — precisely cakes baked with leaven, waved before the LORD but not burned upon the altar (cf. Lev 23:17). That is the working out of the ordinance of verse 12 within the feasts, an intertext at the factual level. Prohibition and permission standing together are therefore not an oversight of the text but a deliberate arrangement of the system: some offerings belong to the altar, some belong to waving, eating and supply, and as their assignments differ, so do the manner and degree of their holiness. In reading Leviticus, the greatest mistake is to cover these fine distinctions with one vague sentence.

Now look at the salt. Verse 13 opens with a different weight altogether: "And every oblation of thy meat offering shalt thou season with salt; neither shalt thou suffer the salt of the covenant of thy God to be lacking from thy meat offering: with all thine offerings thou shalt offer salt" (Lev 2:13, KJV). Notice the scope: not only this form of meat offering, and not only the meat offering as one offering — the text says with all thine offerings salt shall be offered. The livestock of the burnt offering must be accompanied by salt, the cakes of the meat offering must be accompanied by salt, and the peace offering and the rest stand likewise within the scope of this "all." Salt rises from a seasoning ingredient to a necessary condition common to all offerings. This is rare in the ordinances of the five offerings: other ingredients differ from offering to offering; salt alone may be lacking from none. The reason the text gives is only one sentence, yet it is the weightiest sentence of the whole chapter: thou shalt not suffer the salt of the covenant of thy God to be lacking. The Chinese Union Version renders the sentence concisely; the sense of the original is: the salt of thy God's covenant — thou shalt not cause it to be absent from thy meat offering. Salt and covenant are here directly joined together. Salt is the sign of the covenant; an offering lacking salt is an offering lacking the sign of the covenant. The title of this sermon, taken from the anchor of verse 13 — salt is the covenant — comes from this very verse.

Why can salt serve as the sign of the covenant? Returning to the facts of life in the ancient Near East, several levels may be examined. First, salt is a preservative. In an age without refrigeration, salt keeps food from spoiling and preserves meat for long. To use what never corrupts as the sign of the covenant fits the nature of the covenant exactly: a covenant is not a momentary agreement, but a bond meant to endure and not to fail. Second, salt belongs to eating together. In antiquity the making of a covenant was often accompanied by eating and drinking together, and to eat salt together meant to share a table, to share in common, and to be bound to one another; to have eaten a man's salt was to have entered into fellowship with him and to owe him loyalty. Third, salt has its own place in the uses of the sanctuary: salt is added to the incense of the tabernacle, and salt accompanies the offerings beside the altar of burnt offering; salt permeates every part of worship, reminding man that worship itself stands upon the foundation of the covenant. Fourth, salt is indispensable yet used in very small quantity — a little salt seasons the whole offering — just as the covenant, without show, runs through everything. These levels overlap one another, and scholarship discusses which level is the main background of this verse; this sermon sets them side by side without deciding for one. At the factual level this can be fixed: verse 13 joins salt and covenant directly, and in Scripture this joining is no isolated instance.

There are two intertexts, both expansions at the factual level, which must be quoted in full. In Numbers 18:19 God says to Aaron that all the heave offerings of the holy things which the children of Israel offer unto the LORD are given to the priest and his sons as a perpetual portion, and says: "it is a covenant of salt for ever before the LORD unto thee and to thy seed with thee" (Num 18:19, KJV). "Covenant of salt" there denotes God's covenant giving the priests their perpetual portion — never to fail, as salt does not corrupt. In 2 Chronicles 13:5 Abijah, king of Judah, calls out to Jeroboam's camp in battle: "the LORD God of Israel gave the kingdom over Israel to David for ever, even to him and to his sons by a covenant of salt" (2 Chron 13:5, KJV). "Covenant of salt" there denotes God's covenant of the kingship with the house of David, firmly established and unmoved. Read together with Leviticus 2:13, the semantic field of "covenant of salt" is complete: a covenant of salt is an everlasting covenant, a firmly established covenant, a covenant that shall not fail. Leviticus says the salt of the covenant must not be lacking; Numbers says the priests' portion is a covenant of salt; Chronicles says David's kingship is a covenant of salt — three passages using one thing to figure one covenant, showing that when Israel heard salt and covenant named together, they understood a lasting, loyal, unbreakable bond. To sprinkle salt upon an offering is not to complete a step in cooking; it is, at every single sacrifice, to rehearse this once more: our relation with God is not hire, is not buying and selling, is not a temporary arrangement, but covenant — a covenant He first made, a covenant He keeps, a covenant signed with incorruptible salt. A man who brings salt with his offering brings the memory of the covenant with his offering; a man who lacks salt still has the motions of sacrifice, but has lost the sign of the covenant. And God says: this must not be.

Salt stands, moreover, in a contrast easily overlooked — a contrast with the prohibitions of the meat offering itself. Leaven makes flour swell and must in the end corrupt; honey is sweet, yet may not go up onto the altar; salt alone both seasons and preserves, both has flavour and endures. The text places the three ingredients within one chapter: two are forbidden to the fire of the altar, one is necessary for every offering. This contrast is no accidental arrangement; it is the high point of the structure of chapter 2. Within one chapter, by means of the things most familiar in the people's kitchens, God taught them the deepest lesson: in worship there must be no swelling, and no sweet flattery, but there must never be lacking the savour and the endurance of the covenant. A salty flavour is not pleasing, yet it is true; a sweet flavour is pleasing, yet it is stopped outside the altar. This contrast stands at the border between structural observation and homiletical exposition, and this sermon marks it honestly: that the three stand side by side is a fact of the structure; to read from that fact a teaching about the character of worship is exposition. Once marked, we must still honestly admit that this exposition is driven by the structure of the text itself, and is not a label the preacher has forced upon it.

One more word on the measure of salt: the text prescribes no quantity. How much, how many grains, how many pinches — the ordinance records none of it. This silence is itself part of the teaching: the sign of the covenant does not lie in great quantity. Among the proportions at the altar many have fixed measures; for salt alone the text says only that it must not be lacking, and not how much. Just so, the covenant comes to man not reckoned by man's measure, but established by God's faithfulness. The small cake beside the poor man's dove must have salt; the whole offering of the rich man's bullock must have salt; salt once more levels the offerings of every grade: whatever is offered, the covenant is present alike. At this point the main line of chapter 2 is gathered complete: the bloodless meat offering brings labour before God; its forms set the kitchen apart as holy; the division between the handful and the remnant shows worship and supply joined together; the prohibition and permission concerning leaven and honey show the boundary of the altar; and the necessity of salt shows that all worship stands upon the covenant. In the last short section we shall, according to rule, mark the New Testament reading separately, hear how Jesus speaks of salt and how Paul brings the language of sacrifice into the believer's life, and then return to the covenant of salt itself to conclude.

## V. Salt Must Not Lose Its Savour: New Testament Reading and Today's Offering 58:00-60:00

What follows belongs to the level of New Testament reading and homiletical use, and must not be read back into Leviticus chapter 2 as its plain original meaning. Jesus speaks of salt in two places of greatest importance. In Matthew 5:13 He says: "Ye are the salt of the earth: but if the salt have lost his savour, wherewith shall it be salted? it is thenceforth good for nothing, but to be cast out, and to be trodden under foot of men" (KJV). The identity of salt comes before the function of salt: the disciples first are salt, and only then is there talk of preserving and seasoning; salt that has lost its savour still looks like salt in outward form, yet has wholly lost its nature. Mark 9:49-50 speaks still more directly in the language of the altar: "For every one shall be salted with fire, and every sacrifice shall be salted with salt. Salt is good: but if the salt have lost his saltness, wherewith will ye season it? Have salt in yourselves, and have peace one with another" (KJV). Mark's use awakens the Old Testament background of salting sacrifices, and turns it into the life of the community of disciples: to have salt in yourselves is for the community to have within it the loyalty of the covenant, the preservation of holiness, and the truth of peace. The homiletical use of Romans 12:1 belongs to the same level: Paul beseeches the believers to present their bodies a living sacrifice. The meat offering is a bloodless offering, a daily offering; the use that takes believers offering their bodies and their daily labour as a living sacrifice answers in direction to the meat offering's bringing field and kitchen before the altar — yet it must still be marked: this is New Testament interpretation and application, not a direct statement that Leviticus chapter 2 was already speaking of the believer's life as a sacrifice. Only when the level is marked does the use stand firm.

Let us close with this chapter itself. A basin of fine flour is brought; a handful is burned upon the altar; the remnant belongs as most holy; leaven and honey stop outside the altar, but salt must never be absent from any offering. May our offering today not lack the one thing most necessary: the sign of the covenant. Our work may be as plain as flour, our days as daily as cakes; so long as they are brought with salt, they are offered within the covenant. Salt is the covenant, and the covenant is God's faithfulness that shall not fail. Bringing this faithfulness, let us offer; holding this faithfulness, let us go to our work and our living. May our flour, our cakes and our fresh ears never lack the salt of the covenant. Amen.

## Notes

1. minchah: Hebrew מִנְחָה, transliteration minchah, noun, gift, tribute, offering, Strong number H4503. Meaning according to BDB: gift, tribute, present, offering, sacrifice; HALOT: gift, present, tribute, offering. In its broader Old Testament use it may denote an ordinary gift or tribute, as with the gifts Jacob sent to Esau and to the Egyptian ruler in Genesis; in Leviticus 2 it denotes specifically the grain offering consisting chiefly of grain. The distinction in the sermon between the basic sense and the specialised sense rests on this difference between broad and narrow usage.

2. solet: Hebrew סֹלֶת, transliteration solet, noun, fine flour, finest flour, Strong number H5560. Meaning according to BDB: fine flour; HALOT: fine flour, semolina. It denotes the finest flour of wheat, finely ground and sifted, as against coarse flour, and appears repeatedly in the meat offering of Leviticus and in the fixed measures accompanying offerings in Numbers. In a few settings, such as the new meat offering at Pentecost, cakes are baked from dough, but the ingredient still rests on fine flour as its base.

3. levonah: Hebrew לְבֹנָה, transliteration levonah, noun, frankincense, Strong number H3828. Meaning according to BDB: frankincense; HALOT: frankincense. It is the resin of the frankincense tree, strong in fragrance when burned, and a precious spice. In the meat offering all the frankincense is burned upon the altar and none is left to the priests; this corresponds to the frankincense set beside the shewbread and to the ingredient of the holy incense of the tabernacle — in each case fragrance belonging to God.

4. azkarah: Hebrew אַזְכָּרָה, transliteration azkarah, noun, memorial portion, memorial, Strong number H234. Meaning according to BDB: memorial portion of an offering; HALOT: memorial part. It is connected with the root meaning to remember and to cause to be remembered. In the meat offering it denotes the handful the priest takes out and burns upon the altar, representing the whole offering, together with all the frankincense; the word is used at Leviticus 2:2, 2:9, 2:16, 5:12, 6:15 and elsewhere, and in Joshua in the broader sense of memorial stones and the like.

5. se'or: Hebrew שְׂאֹר, transliteration se'or, noun, leaven, leavened starter, Strong number H7603. Meaning according to BDB: leaven; HALOT: leaven. It denotes the leaven kept in dough to ferment the whole lump. Connected with it is chametz: Hebrew חָמֵץ, transliteration chametz, noun, what is leavened, what is soured, with the verb root at Strong number H2556 and the noun usage at H2557; BDB explains it by fermenting and souring, and HALOT likewise lists leavened cakes among its senses. The leaven forbidden in Leviticus 2:11 belongs in the original to the se'or group of concepts, and read together with chametz in the Passover tradition of removing leaven, shows that the prohibition covers both the starter and the leavened product.

6. devash: Hebrew דְּבַשׁ, transliteration devash, noun, honey, Strong number H1706. Meaning according to BDB: honey; HALOT gives honey, with the line of interpretation "also fruit syrup" standing alongside. The scholarly dispute is set out side by side: on one view, the honey of this chapter denotes bees' honey specifically, and the prohibition is directed against the common use of honey in sacrifice in Canaanite religion; on another view, it includes or chiefly denotes a syrup boiled from grapes, dates and figs, since the Hebrew Bible, when it figures the abundance of Canaan by honey, often names it together with the produce of fruit, and fruit syrup was in the ancient Near East a more common sweetening offering than bees' honey. Milgrom inclines to the fruit-syrup view or to an inclusive view; Wenham keeps the usual reading of honey and notes that the dispute is undecided. The sermon sets the two views side by side and draws no final conclusion.

7. melach: Hebrew מֶלַח, transliteration melach, noun, salt, Strong number H4417. Meaning according to BDB: salt; HALOT: salt. melach berit: covenant of salt; in Leviticus 2:13 the original reads מֶלַח בְּרִית, transliteration melach berit, the salt of thy God's covenant; Numbers 18:19 reads בְּרִית מֶלַח עוֹלָם, an everlasting covenant of salt; 2 Chronicles 13:5 likewise uses covenant of salt for the covenant with David. Both BDB and HALOT explain the figure of the covenant of salt by permanence, preservation from corruption, and unfailingness. Intertexts: in Num 18:19 the covenant of salt denotes the priests' perpetual portion; in 2 Chron 13:5 the covenant of salt denotes the covenant of David's kingship — both expansions at the factual level.

8. The implements and forms: at 2:4, baked in the oven, the implement in the original is tannur, an oven; at 2:5 and 2:7, griddle and frying pan, the original words are respectively machavat and marcheshet, transliterations machavat, marcheshet, Strong numbers H4227, H4802. Both BDB and HALOT note that the two implements are close in shape, and that the distinction between the shallower and the deeper has no settled conclusion. Wenham and Hartley both interpret by the main line of the manner of making, and draw no excessive symbolic inferences from the implements.

9. Fresh ears: at 2:14 the original concerning the firstfruits involves the words aviv, fresh ears, and geres, beaten grain; BDB glosses aviv as first ripe green ears and geres as crushed grain. "Dried by the fire" denotes parching by fire so that the grain may be rubbed out for offering. Hartley treats the relation of the meat offering of the firstfruits to the season of harvest, and Milgrom its connection with the ordinance of the new meat offering at Pentecost.

10. Most holy: at 2:3 and 2:10 the remnant of the meat offering is called most holy; the original is qodesh qodashim, holy of holies. Both BDB and HALOT list this doubled expression as the highest grade of holiness. Leviticus 6:14-23, chapter 10 and other places prescribe the manner and place of eating most holy offerings; the remnant of the meat offering stands in this grade together with the remnants of the sin and trespass offerings, and the priests must eat it in the holy place — showing that the remnant still belongs to the holy realm and is not a payment for common use.

11. The tension between the forbidding of leaven and honey and the permission for the firstfruits: 2:11 forbids burning leaven and honey as an offering made by fire, while 2:12 permits leaven and honey to be offered with the oblation of the firstfruits without going up onto the altar. Milgrom resolves the tension by the distinction between the two acts of offering and burning, adducing the leavened cakes of Pentecost at Leviticus 23:17, waved but not burned, as evidence; Wenham reads it the same way; Hartley stresses in addition that the holiness of the altar fire and the graded assignment of offerings are the system's own intention. The three readings agree in direction and differ in emphasis of detail; this sermon rests on setting the tension side by side and distinguishing the acts.

12. Sources at the level of New Testament reading: Matthew 5:13, the salt of the earth; Mark 9:49-50, every sacrifice shall be salted with salt, and have salt in yourselves; Romans 12:1, a living sacrifice. All three are New Testament interpretations and uses of the language of salt and of sacrifice; when preaching cites them, the level must be marked, and they must not be read back as the plain original meaning of Leviticus 2. Compare also Ezekiel 43:24, salting the offering, an intertext within the Old Testament at the factual level.

## Devotional Excerpt

My daily labour, too, can become a grain offering. A basin of fine flour, a cake baked in my own oven, a handful of first ripe grain — God accepts them all, on one condition that cannot be missing: the salt of the covenant. Salt reminds me that my relation with God is no passing bargain, but His covenant and His faithfulness, incorruptible and enduring like salt. Whatever I do today, whatever I set on the table, teach me, Lord, to do it with a covenant heart: not puffed up like leaven, not sweetened like honey, but bringing my plain flour and bread honestly before You, with that pinch of salt. A handful is burned on the altar as a memorial; the rest of my days are lived securely within the covenant. The toil of the morning's work and the weariness of the evening's rest — You see them all; I do not ask that my days suddenly turn sweet, only that each day's plain produce, for the covenant's sake, may become a sweet savour, offered upon Your altar and remembered by You. May my work today not lack the salt, and may my life carry the covenant's true flavour — honest, lasting, and at peace with others. Keep my family and my companions too, Lord, that we may all have salt in ourselves, live at peace with one another, and keep this covenant together, until Your promise is fulfilled. Your covenant shall never fail; I will place all that I have today within this covenant and offer it to You, resting in Your faithfulness, and in Your faithfulness finding strength renewed, to live every day within the covenant.

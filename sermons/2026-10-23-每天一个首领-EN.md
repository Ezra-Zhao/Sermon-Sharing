# One Prince Each Day — A Sermon on Numbers 7

- Text: Numbers 7:1-89
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft translation, pending review (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished). English text is a first translation, pending native-level proofreading.
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Numbers 7; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations; verses not checked word for word are given in summary only, never as quotations.
- Note on the title: The tentative directions for a title — words such as "offering gifts" or "offerings," and a phrase that circulated in the commissioning summary, "the rite of the altar's consecration" — were checked against the Chinese base text of Numbers 7 and set aside: the base text contains no such unbroken phrase for the dedication rite. Its own wording stands in two forms side by side — "the rite of presenting the altar" at 7:11 and 7:88, and "the rite of dedicating the altar" at 7:84, each verse checked. Verse 11 also carries the unbroken wording that gives the chapter its skeleton: "one prince each day shall come and present his offering." The Chinese title takes those characters — "One Prince Each Day" — and the English title renders that corrected title. See Endnote 2.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Chapter 7 is the longest chapter in Numbers — eighty-nine verses — yet it holds only three events. First: on the day Moses finished setting up the tabernacle and anointed the altar and sanctified it, the princes of Israel — the same tribal princes of chapters 1 and 2 who were over those who were numbered — came before the altar. They first presented six covered wagons and twelve oxen for the Levites' use, and then offered for the dedication of the altar; and the LORD commanded Moses that the princes should present their offerings one prince each day. Second: the offerings of the twelve days. On the first day Nahshon, prince of Judah, presented the full list; on each of the eleven days after him one prince presented a list identical item by item — a silver charger, a silver bowl, a golden spoon full of incense, and the beasts for burnt offering, sin offering, and peace offerings, not one item more and not one less — yet the text writes the full list out twelve times over, and closes every day's account with a name: "This was the offering of" that man. Third: when the twelve days were done, the text renders the account in public — totals for the silver vessels, the gold, and every kind of beast. In preparing this sermon every daily item was multiplied by twelve by hand and checked against the chapter's own stated totals, item by item; the result is exact agreement at every line, without a single difference. The chapter's last verse alone speaks of something else: Moses went into the tabernacle to speak with the LORD, and heard the voice of one speaking to him from off the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim. Twelve days of gifts, twelve lists of names, one rendering of account — and it all gathers at last into one sentence: the end of offering is not an altar heaped with gifts, but God opening His mouth to speak. This sermon reads the chapter in the text's own order: first the occasion of the offerings and the command of one prince each day; then the first day's full list, item by item; then the roll of the twelve days set out in full, and the meaning of the twelve identical lists; then the totals verified item by item; and last, a pause before the voice above the mercy seat. The thread it traces is this: in God's ledger, every offering stands under the name of the man who offered it, and every name has a day entirely its own — no joint offering, no offering by proxy, and no man's name merged into another man's day.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 The tabernacle finished, the altar anointed: the princes come before the altar

(Fact) Chapter 7 opens by fixing its day — the day the tabernacle was set up. In the Chinese base text, verse 1 records that Moses finished setting up the tabernacle and anointed it and sanctified it, and anointed all its vessels, and the altar and all the vessels of the altar, and sanctified them (Num 7:1, Summary in this manuscript; the Chinese manuscript quotes the verse word for word from its base text). Mark the order in that verse: first "finished setting up," then "anointed," and the aim throughout is "sanctified" — the tabernacle itself, its vessels, the altar, the altar's vessels, anointed one by one, sanctified one by one. All the offerings of the eighty verses that follow take place after this sanctifying: it is not that the princes offered, and so the altar became holy; the altar was first anointed and sanctified, and then the princes brought their offerings to an altar already holy. That order is this chapter's first foundation stone, and it must be set down before anything is preached about gifts: the gifts of this chapter are a response, not an exchange. The altar is first set apart as God's, and only then do men carry their gifts to it, piece by piece.

(Fact) Who comes before the altar is stated in verse 2 (Summary): the princes of Israel, heads of their fathers' houses, princes of the tribes, who were over those who were numbered. These men are not strangers. In chapter 1, when the people were numbered, one prince of each tribe stood with Moses and Aaron to help them (Num 1:4-16, Summary); in chapter 2, at the encamping, the roll of the princes of the camps and tribes is listed again (Numbers 2, Summary). The "princes" of verses 2-3 of this chapter are the same company on those two rolls; the twelve names of the twelve days match the assistants' roll of chapter 1 name for name (see the roll in section III and Endnote 5), though the order of the offering days follows a different arrangement, not the order of chapter 1's naming. In other words, the men who come to the altar are the men who first numbered others and governed others: in chapter 1 they stood on the side of those who take the number; in chapter 2 on the side of those who lead the hosts; in chapter 7 they come, one by one, to the side of those who offer. First administration, then presentation; first calling other men's names, then writing their own names underneath an offering.

(Fact) What the princes presented first was not an offering for the altar at all, but wagons and oxen (vv. 3-9, Summary): they brought their offering before the LORD — six covered wagons and twelve oxen, a wagon for two of the princes and an ox for each one — and presented them before the tabernacle. The LORD told Moses to accept them, to be used in the service of the tabernacle, and to give them to the Levites, to every man according to his service. Moses gave two wagons and four oxen to the sons of Gershon according to their service, and four wagons and eight oxen to the sons of Merari according to their service, under the hand of Ithamar the son of Aaron the priest; but to the sons of Kohath he gave none, because the service of the sanctuary belonging to them was that they should bear it upon their shoulders. The assignment of the wagons has already been treated, on its side of office and charge, in the companion sermon on chapter 4, "Coming Forward to Serve"; this sermon marks the cross-reference here and does not unfold it again — the figures stand in verses 3-9 of this chapter, the exposition of the charge in chapter 4. One layer only needs noting here: the princes' first gift was not burned on the altar; it was handed to the Levites to haul and carry with. Before the altar's business began, the hauling had been provided for; the offerers thought first of other men's burdens — and that order is itself worth recording.

(Fact) Only then comes the altar's turn. Verse 10 records that when the altar was anointed, the princes offered for the dedication of the altar, and presented their offering before the altar (v. 10, Summary). Verse 11 gives the LORD's word to Moses, in the KJV: "And the LORD said unto Moses, They shall offer their offering, each prince on his day, for the dedicating of the altar." (Num 7:11, KJV) Weigh what this command does. Twelve princes, twelve full lists of gifts: presented all together in one day, they could have been recorded in a single verse. The LORD instead commands them divided — each prince on his day. One day is given to one prince's offering alone; one prince has a whole day entirely his own. This slowness is commanded slowness, stated in so many words; it is not narrative padding. The chapter's title is drawn from this verse's own unbroken wording in the Chinese base text: "one prince each day." And the wording must be corrected here as well: a phrase that circulated in the commissioning summary, "the rite of the altar's consecration," does not stand in the base text; the base text's phrases are "the rite of presenting the altar" (7:11, 7:88) and "the rite of dedicating the altar" (7:84) — two forms standing side by side, each quoted in the Chinese manuscript from its own verse, never blended and never altered (see Endnote 2).

(Application) This command — one prince each day — deserves a pause from everyone who is in a hurry. When we present things, we like to finish in one batch and close the account at once, as if nothing were lost by crowding twelve gifts into a single day. God does not keep accounts that way. He gives each prince a whole day: on that day there is one man alone before the altar, the whole congregation's eyes on one gift, the pen of the record on one name. With God, offering is never batch processing — every gift must have its own day, and every offerer must have his own day standing before the altar. Twelve days are twelve times "now it is your turn." No joining together, no merging, no sending another in one's place, and no slipping a gift out hidden in the crowd. Consider: on the day Nahshon offered, Nethaneel of Issachar could only stand by and watch; and when Ahira's twelfth day came, the man of the first day had waited eleven days. The days of waiting and the day of offering are both part of this command — to stand quietly until one's day comes, and to step forward in person when it comes: that is the first lesson "one prince each day" teaches everyone who offers.

(Application) There is one more layer, in the contrast between the offerers' order and their identity. In chapter 1 these twelve men were the men "over them that were numbered" — they held other men's rolls in their hands; their voices were the voices of name-callers. In chapter 7 they set themselves, one by one, at the last line of the roll: every day's record closes with "this was the offering of" a named man. The name-callers are now the named; the counters of others now report their own gifts item by item for the record. The leader's order before God is never to stand permanently outside the ledger administering it, but in the end to walk to the altar and write his own name into the ledger. A man may call the roll for a whole camp, lead its hosts, arrange its order — and in the end his own page before God holds only the one gift he offered on his own day. This chapter binds the twelve princes' names to their offerings twelve times over, and what it binds is exactly this: who you are is not told by how many men you governed, but by whether the gift recorded before the altar carries your name.

### II. 08:00-24:00 The first day's full list: Nahshon's one gift, set out item by item

(Fact) The first to offer on the first day was Nahshon the son of Amminadab, of the tribe of Judah (v. 12, Summary; the Chinese manuscript records the verse's statement as checked fact from its base text). Judah stands first, answering to the encamping order of chapter 2: the camp of Judah lay on the east side toward the rising of the sun, and was to set forward first (Num 2:3-9, Summary); the first day of offering also opens with Judah's prince — first in the camp, first at the altar; the order holds steady between the two chapters. Now set Nahshon's gift out item by item. This is the chapter's "full list," the pattern of all twelve gifts; the eleven days after it follow the same form, and this sermon quotes the list in full here only, treating the later days by structure and roll. The first item, the silver vessels and the meal offering, stands in the KJV: "And his offering was one silver charger, the weight thereof was an hundred and thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, after the shekel of the sanctuary; both of them were full of fine flour mingled with oil for a meat offering:" (Num 7:13, KJV) The second item, the gold vessel and the incense: "One spoon of ten shekels of gold, full of incense:" (Num 7:14, KJV) The third item, the beasts of the burnt offering (v. 15, Summary, items and numbers as the text gives them): one young bullock, one ram, one lamb of the first year, for a burnt offering. The fourth item, the sin offering (v. 16, Summary): one kid of the goats, for a sin offering. The fifth item, the beasts of the peace offerings, stands in the KJV: "And for a sacrifice of peace offerings, two oxen, five rams, five he goats, five lambs of the first year: this was the offering of Nahshon the son of Amminadab." (Num 7:17, KJV) The full list is now complete: one silver charger, one silver bowl, one golden spoon full of incense; three beasts for the burnt offering, one for the sin offering, and seventeen for the peace offerings — the peace-offering item added up kind by kind: two plus five plus five plus five makes seventeen; and the gift's beasts in all: three plus one plus seventeen, twenty-one head. This arithmetic was counted item by item in preparing this sermon (see Endnote 6).

(Fact) The wording of this full list needs several anchors set steady in the original, every one of them checked in preparing this sermon against the Strong's-tagged text of the chapter, verse by verse. First, the word "offering" runs through the whole chapter — "his offering" at verse 13, "the offering of Nahshon" at verse 17 — and in the Hebrew it is qorbān (Strong's H7133), a gift brought near and presented to God; the word's root sense is bound up with drawing near and presenting: an offering is a thing carried into God's presence. Second, "prince" is nāśîʾ (Strong's H5387), used day by day from verse 18 onward in the naming verses — checked, for example, at verse 18, "prince of the children of Issachar." Third, the two silver vessels: "charger" is qeʿārâ (Strong's H7086), and "bowl" is mizrāq (Strong's H4219) — these two numbers were checked individually at verse 13 in the Hebrew tagging: the charger carries H7086, the bowl H4219, and the two must not be exchanged. Fourth, the gold vessel: the Chinese base text renders it a gold "yu" (a small vessel), where the KJV reads "spoon"; the Hebrew is kap̄ (Strong's H3709), whose base sense is a hollow, palm-shaped vessel. The two translations take the same incense vessel from two sides; they are set out side by side here and are not to be forced into one word (see Endnote 7). Fifth, "incense" is qeṭōret (Strong's H7004). Sixth, the names of the offerings: "meat offering" (the grain offering) is minḥâ (Strong's H4503); "burnt offering" is ʿōlâ (Strong's H5930); "sin offering" is ḥaṭṭāʾt (Strong's H2403); and "peace offerings" in the Hebrew is zeḇaḥ šelāmîm, where zeḇaḥ, the sacrificial offering, is H2077, and šelāmîm, peace, is H8002 — the two words stand together at verse 17 in the original, and each pairing was checked individually (see Endnote 8). All the numbers and word-forms above are listed only from verses actually checked in the base texts for this sermon, as a verified selection; no claim is made about exhaustive counts of occurrences across the chapter.

(Application) Read this full list from end to end, and one thing must be preached: this gift is complete. What one prince presents in one day is not a souvenir; it furnishes, in a single gift, every line the altar requires: fine flour mingled with oil for the meal offering, carried in the silver charger and bowl; incense, filling the golden spoon; the burnt offering wholly burned and ascending to God; a sin offering for sin; peace offerings for communion and for the shared table. Silver and gold, grain and spice and beasts — five kinds of material, four names of offering, all present in one gift. This is no list thrown together at convenience; it is drawn up against the whole need of the worship: what the altar would use, he prepared; what the altar would receive, he brought. The offerer's commonest fault is to offer only what lies ready to hand: flour if he has flour, a lamb if he has a lamb, and then to feel the intention has done its work. Nahshon's list teaches us that "the intention has arrived" does not settle the account; the list must be drawn against the altar's need: whatever the altar lacks belongs in the gift. It is not a matter of offering whatever I happen to have and being done; it is that whatever belongs before the altar, not one item may be missing. A gift's completeness does not lie in its costliness, but in its correspondence.

(Application) Notice also one thing hidden inside this full list: the weights — all weighed, all written down. The charger, an hundred and thirty shekels; the bowl, seventy shekels; the spoon, ten shekels: not "a silver charger, quite heavy," but a figure weighed out, by the shekel of the sanctuary, and written into Scripture. Offering in the Bible is never an estimating trade: however heavy the gift is, that is what is recorded; however much incense, "full" is the measure. God's ledger takes no "roughly." What we love to say, when we offer, is "more or less," "near enough," "just a token": more-or-less a hundred and thirty is, in the ledger, a hundred and thirty — not a fraction more, not a fraction less. This is not God being petty; it is God being exact: He writes every prince's charger's weight into His book, and a thousand years and more later, men can still read it and work the sum again for themselves. A gift recorded like that is a real gift; the offerer weighs it true and hands it up, and the Receiver writes the weight down exactly as it stands — both sides in earnest, and the gift stands firm.

(Fact) The first day's list read, one fact about the record itself must be owned. From the second day onward, the text writes this full list out again eleven times, without sparing a single item. Each day takes six verses: the opening verse gives the day and the prince's name; the four verses between list again the charger, the bowl, the spoon, the burnt offering, the sin offering, the peace offerings; the last verse closes, "this was the offering of" that man (vv. 18-83, Summary). That is to say: of this chapter's eighty-nine verses, more than sixty are the repetition of one list. Judged by "efficiency" of writing, this is the least efficient chapter in the book: one sentence — "the remaining eleven days offered as Nahshon had" — would have done. The text refuses to write it. This is not a text that cannot save words — Numbers saves words in plenty of places — it is a text that will not save them here: every prince's gift is to be written out again in full, and every name is to appear again with a complete list of gifts. The next section follows this unsparing manner of record, and sets the twelve days' names out in full, one by one.

### III. 24:00-42:00 Twelve days, one pattern: every name has a day entirely its own

(Fact) Now the roll of the twelve days, set out in full. Each prince's name, father's name, tribe, day, and verses were checked verse by verse against the Chinese base text, and the proper names cross-checked against the KJV spelling. The first day: Judah — Nahshon the son of Amminadab (vv. 12-17). The second day: Issachar — Nethaneel the son of Zuar (vv. 18-23). The third day: Zebulun — Eliab the son of Helon (vv. 24-29). The fourth day: Reuben — Elizur the son of Shedeur (vv. 30-35). The fifth day: Simeon — Shelumiel the son of Zurishaddai (vv. 36-41). The sixth day: Gad — Eliasaph the son of Deuel (vv. 42-47). The seventh day: Ephraim — Elishama the son of Ammihud (vv. 48-53). The eighth day: Manasseh — Gamaliel the son of Pedahzur (vv. 54-59). The ninth day: Benjamin — Abidan the son of Gideoni (vv. 60-65). The tenth day: Dan — Ahiezer the son of Ammishaddai (vv. 66-71). The eleventh day: Asher — Pagiel the son of Ocran (vv. 72-77). The twelfth day: Naphtali — Ahira the son of Enan (vv. 78-83). Twelve days, twelve men, twelve tribes — none absent, none twice.

(Fact) The order of this roll is not arbitrary. Read it against the encamping order of chapter 2 (Numbers 2, Summary): on the east, the camp of Judah — Judah, Issachar, Zebulun — exactly days one to three; on the south, the camp of Reuben — Reuben, Simeon, Gad — exactly days four to six; on the west, the camp of Ephraim — Ephraim, Manasseh, Benjamin — exactly days seven to nine; on the north, the camp of Dan — Dan, Asher, Naphtali — exactly days ten to twelve. The sequence of the four camps' quarters is the sequence of the twelve offering days; the side on which a tribe encamped is the stretch in which its prince offered. This also explains why Reuben, the firstborn, stands only on the fourth day: the offering is ordered not by birth but by the camps' quarters — Judah holds the first company's place in march and encampment (Num 2:9, Summary), and the offering also opens with Judah. The order has its ground, and the two chapters witness to each other; this sermon states the correspondence as a fact of the two texts read together, and builds no further allegory on it.

(Fact) Two names on this roll are the ones most easily miswritten in retelling, and they were checked verse by verse for this sermon, in both manuscripts. The first belongs to the fifth day: the KJV stands, "On the fifth day Shelumiel the son of Zurishaddai, prince of the children of Simeon, did offer:" (Num 7:36, KJV) — and the Chinese base text at this verse writes the name with the character for "road" in its second syllable, not the similar-looking character for "straw" that copyists and retellers slip into; the Chinese manuscript quotes the verse from its base text and records the point there. One character's difference, and the name is another name: on a roll that is this chapter's skeleton, not one character may be written from memory. The second belongs to the sixth day: the KJV stands, "On the sixth day Eliasaph the son of Deuel, prince of the children of Gad, offered:" (Num 7:42, KJV) — the father's name is Deuel in this chapter (and in chapter 1), and the Chinese base text at this verse agrees; in some places and traditions the name is written otherwise (see the discussion layer below), and this sermon follows this chapter's text for this chapter's roll, without altering one letter on the strength of another chapter's variant.

(Fact) Whether the twelve days' gifts are in fact identical must also have its checking stated plainly. The six verses of each day were read against the first day's verses 13-17 as the standard, day by day: a silver charger of an hundred and thirty shekels, a silver bowl of seventy shekels, a golden spoon of ten shekels full of incense, three beasts for the burnt offering, one for the sin offering, and the peace-offering quota of two, five, five, and five — for the eleven days after the first, the corresponding verses agree in number and kind with the first day throughout, and differ only in the closing name. On the KJV side, this sermon spot-checked the peace-offering closing verses of the later days — verses 47, 53, 59, 65, 71, 77, and 83 — and each gives the same figures, "two oxen, five rams, five he goats, five lambs of the first year," closing with that day's prince's name. The two base texts witness together: the twelve days' gifts are one gift twelve times over, not twelve versions of one gift; every difference lies in the names, and none in the list.

(Application) Now the layer of the twelve identical lists that cuts deepest: twelve gifts, exactly alike, not a fraction's difference even in weight. What does it mean? It means that Ahira, on the twelfth day, offered a gift not one piece different from Nahshon's on the first — no more, though he had waited longest; no less, though he stood last. It means that the prince of a small tribe and the prince of a great tribe stand before the altar with the same list: Benjamin's census count falls far short of Judah's (the two chapters' numberings may be read together, Summary), yet Abidan's charger is the same an hundred and thirty shekels. In these twelve days there is no doctrine of "the first gift especially precious," and no indulgence of "the last gift may be simplified." God measures the twelve tribes with one rule and records them in one ledger. When we offer, we love to compare: whose is costliest, whose most conspicuous, who stands in front. This chapter blocks every road of comparison — the lists are all the same; what is there to compare? The only difference left is the name: whose gift this is. And it is precisely that one difference that the text records twelve times over. If there is any "unfairness" in this chapter, it is that it is so fair as to leave nothing to say: twelve days, twelve men, twelve identical gifts, twelve names written fairly and squarely.

(Application) Preach, too, the sixty-odd verses of "repetition." Reading this chapter, by the fourth or fifth day we begin to skim — "it is all the same anyway." The Author of the text did not skim. He let the pen stay on each prince for six whole verses, writing it all out again, item by item: this is Elizur's charger, an hundred and thirty shekels; this is Shelumiel's bowl, seventy shekels; this is Pagiel's incense, full. Why? Because in God's ledger there is no such entry as "the rest as above." In His sight, no prince's gift is "the nth repetition"; each is "this man's gift." We feel repetition because we look only at the list; God feels none, because He looks at the names. The lesson serves everyone who offers his portion inside a multitude: the portion you present among the congregation may be exactly like your neighbour's — the same hymn, the same offering, the same service — and men will call it repetition; but in God's ledger it is a page of its own, and at the head of the page stands your name. Do not slight your own portion because "others offered too," and do not dash it off carelessly because "it is all the same anyway." The Lord who wrote the full list out twelve times remembers clearly whose each portion was.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) This chapter's long repetition has been discussed by commentators since early times. One kind of reading holds that the twelve day-by-day records were originally the gift-lists presented and archived by each tribe separately, and that at the editing they were taken in entire, not one item struck out — hence the present form. Another holds that the repetition is the text's deliberate form: a literary, measured recurrence that writes out the twelve tribes' complete unanimity before the altar, with none absent. This sermon sets out the drift of these two discussions only, and decides for neither; on either reading, the facts of the text stand: the twelve full lists are all present, and the names are complete day by day. Again: the name of the Gadite prince's father stands as Deuel in this chapter and in chapter 1 (KJV "Deuel"), while at the corresponding place in chapter 2 the KJV reads "Reuel" — a difference belonging to the familiar discussion of initial-letter variation in the copying tradition. This sermon keeps that matter at the discussion level only: the quotations and the roll in this sermon follow this chapter's text — Deuel — and do not alter one letter of this chapter for the sake of another chapter's reading. Such readings belong to the discussion layer alone; the roll and the quotations of the sermon text follow the base text only.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 The account rendered in public: every daily item multiplied by twelve, and checked against the stated totals

(Fact) When the twelve days were done, the text renders its own account (vv. 84-88). This sermon is not content to copy that account out — since this chapter has written the daily items so completely, the total account must be verified by hand: every daily item multiplied by twelve, and checked against the totals the base text itself states, item by item; where they agree, the agreement is recorded, and where they did not — there is no such case here — the two figures would be set out side by side. First, the KJV's own summary at the head of the passage: "This was the dedication of the altar, in the day when it was anointed, by the princes of Israel: twelve chargers of silver, twelve silver bowls, twelve spoons of gold:" (Num 7:84, KJV) Then the silver total: "Each charger of silver weighing an hundred and thirty shekels, each bowl seventy: all the silver vessels weighed two thousand and four hundred shekels, after the shekel of the sanctuary:" (Num 7:85, KJV) And verse 86 (Summary, figures as the text states them): the golden spoons were twelve, full of incense, weighing ten shekels apiece, after the shekel of the sanctuary; all the gold of the spoons was an hundred and twenty shekels. Now the working for the vessels, item by item. The silver chargers: one each day, at an hundred and thirty shekels each, over twelve days make twelve chargers, weighing an hundred and thirty multiplied by twelve — one thousand five hundred and sixty shekels. The silver bowls: one each day, at seventy shekels each, over twelve days make twelve bowls, weighing seventy multiplied by twelve — eight hundred and forty shekels. The whole silver: one thousand five hundred and sixty added to eight hundred and forty makes two thousand and four hundred shekels — exactly the "two thousand and four hundred shekels" the text itself states at verse 85. The golden spoons: one each day, at ten shekels each, over twelve days make twelve spoons, weighing ten multiplied by twelve — an hundred and twenty shekels — exactly the an hundred and twenty the text states at verse 86. The counts of the vessels: chargers, bowls, and spoons, twelve each — exactly the three "twelve"s stated at verse 84.

(Fact) Now the beasts. Verse 87 (Summary, figures as the text states them) gives the totals for the burnt offering, with its meal offering, and the number of the goats for the sin offering: for the burnt offering, twelve bullocks, twelve rams, and twelve lambs of the first year, with their meat offering; and the kids of the goats for sin offering, twelve. Verse 88 stands in the KJV: "And all the oxen for the sacrifice of the peace offerings were twenty and four bullocks, the rams sixty, the he goats sixty, the lambs of the first year sixty. This was the dedication of the altar, after that it was anointed." (Num 7:88, KJV) The working, item by item. The burnt offering: bullocks, one each day, one multiplied by twelve is twelve — agreeing with the stated twelve; rams, one each day, twelve — agreeing; lambs of the first year, one each day, twelve — agreeing. The sin offering: kids of the goats, one each day, twelve — agreeing. The peace offerings: oxen, two each day, two multiplied by twelve is twenty-four — agreeing with the stated twenty and four; rams, five each day, five multiplied by twelve is sixty — agreeing; he goats, five each day, sixty — agreeing; lambs of the first year, five each day, sixty — agreeing. Eight kinds of beasts, eight exact agreements. And a cross-check on the whole: the beasts of one day are twenty-one head (three for the burnt offering, one for the sin offering, seventeen for the peace offerings), and twenty-one multiplied by twelve makes two hundred fifty and two head; added by the text's own stated classes — the burnt offering thirty-six (twelve plus twelve plus twelve), the sin offering twelve, the peace offerings two hundred and four (twenty-four plus sixty plus sixty plus sixty) — thirty-six plus twelve plus two hundred and four is again two hundred fifty and two. Two roads of reckoning meet at the same figure; there is no difference anywhere. The case stands otherwise than in chapter 3: there, the items summed to twenty-two thousand three hundred against a stated total of twenty-two thousand — a difference of three hundred, which the companion sermon sets out side by side exactly as it stands. In this chapter, working and stated total fit without a seam, and this sermon states that agreement as it stands. Every multiplication and every addition above was worked by hand in preparing this sermon — and worked again independently for this English manuscript against the KJV figures rather than carried over from the Chinese manuscript's working (the working and the comparison are set out in Endnote 6); what is recorded here is this sermon's own verification, not a conclusion borrowed from a commentary.

(Application) When the account is worked, one thing deserves slow preaching: why does the text first spend more than sixty verses writing the full list twelve times, and then spend five verses reporting the totals once? If the totals alone were wanted, verses 84-88 would have sufficed, and all the repetition before them would be surplus; if the daily rolls alone were wanted, the twelve sections would have sufficed, and the totals after them would be surplus. The text insists on both: first day by day, man by man, item by item; then gathered, totalled, reported whole. That is the rule of God's counting-house — it keeps both the itemised ledger and the summary ledger: the items must add up to the total, and the total must open out into the items. The twelve full lists are the itemised ledger: every gift under the name of the man who offered it. The five verses of totals are the summary ledger: all the princes' gifts gathered into one dedication of the altar. Without the items, the total would be a great nameless number; without the total, the twelve gifts would scatter into twelve private affairs. The church's giving and service should keep the same two ledgers: every portion recorded under a name — not to exalt men, but so that no portion of devotion sinks nameless into a total; and the whole gathered into one sum — not twelve tribes each offering to its own god, but one Israel offering to one God together. The itemised ledger makes every portion honoured; the summary ledger makes every portion accompanied. A gift kept in such accounts is both a man's own, and the whole people's.

(Application) One word of comfort must also be heard out of this account. The result of the verification is exact agreement at every item, without a single difference. In the Bible's bookkeeping that is no small thing. Twelve princes, twelve days, several hundred vessels and beasts — from the first day's charger to the twelfth day's lambs, not one piece lost, not one beast wrong, not one gift mislaid, merged away, or entered under the wrong name on the way. What we offer to God, we ourselves soon remember imperfectly — what we offered, when, and how much grows hazy with the days. This chapter tells us: the offerer may forget; the One who keeps the ledger does not. His account, from the items to the total, agrees line by line; and when the day of rendering comes and it is laid open, every item will agree exactly. On which day your portion stood, of what weight it was, and under whose name it is recorded — His book holds it all. Before a Keeper of accounts like this, the offerer may rest: no gift is offered in vain, and no name will be left out.

(Fact) After the account, one point of wording in the text must be set straight, lest retelling blur it. The closing phrase of verse 88 in the Chinese base text uses the two-character form "presenting" for the dedication rite, while the corresponding phrase at the head of verse 84 uses the single character "dedicating." The same act, in the same chapter, four verses apart, stands in the base text in two forms side by side — and the Chinese manuscript quotes each verse exactly as its own verse stands, and does not standardise the base text's wording on its behalf. That is the discipline of quotation: it is not enough for the sense to be right; the characters must be right. In quotation, the two forms are two texts, and this sermon never blends them. In its own summaries it likewise notes the verse for each form, so that a reader checking the base text finds every verse as quoted.

### V. 58:00-60:00 The gifts complete, the voice sounds: above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim

(Fact) At the chapter's end one verse remains, in the KJV: "And when Moses was gone into the tabernacle of the congregation to speak with him, then he heard the voice of one speaking unto him from off the mercy seat that was upon the ark of testimony, from between the two cherubims: and he spake unto him." (Num 7:89, KJV) Two of this verse's words must be set steady in the original. "Mercy seat" is kappōret (Strong's H3727), in its base sense the lid of the ark, the cover of atonement — the pairing of this word with H3727 was checked individually against the lexicon entry in preparing this sermon. "Cherubims" is kerûḇ (Strong's H3742); the two cherubim are the pair set facing one another upon the ark's cover, and this pairing likewise was checked (see Endnote 9). The verse's position must be seen first: it stands directly after the rendering of the account — the twelve days' gifts complete and the account closed, Moses goes into the tabernacle, and the voice comes from off the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim. The text does not add a separate paragraph saying "and God was pleased"; it writes one thing only: Moses went in to speak, and heard the voice that answered. When the ark was made, God had promised to meet Moses there and commune with him from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim (Exod 25:22, Summary); this closing verse is that promise recorded as fulfilled, for the first time after the tabernacle was set up: the voice was indeed there, coming from the place of the promise. The gifts were on the altar; the voice is over the ark; the altar stands in the court, the ark in the holy of holies — and the things presented for twelve days at the outer altar gather at last into this one speaking within.

(Fact) The direction of the speaking in this verse must also be owned. The text says Moses went in "to speak with him," and the result is that he heard "the voice of one speaking unto him" — and the verse's own close names the Speaker: "and he spake unto him." Moses went in; the LORD spoke. Through the chapter's first eighty-eight verses, men are carrying things toward God: wagons, oxen, chargers, incense, beasts. In the last verse the direction turns: God speaks toward man. Here is where offering ends — not with men delivering their goods and dispersing, but with the goods delivered, and the man going into the tabernacle to meet the One who received them, and to hear Him speak. If the altar's gifts had not led out into this voice, the twelve days would have been only a magnificent donation; with this verse, the twelve days are a meeting: men present the gift upward, and God gives the word downward.

(Application) Let this sermon gather the whole chapter into three sentences. First: offering keeps its queue — one prince each day, no joint offering and no offering by proxy; when your day comes, you are to stand before the altar in person, and present in person the portion recorded under your name. Second: offering is to be complete, and its account clean — the list drawn up full against the altar's need, the weights weighed true, and after the offering the account must be renderable: items and totals in exact agreement, which is the order of this counting-house, and the earnestness every offerer should learn. Third: offering is to walk on until the voice is heard — the gift is not the end; the voice above the mercy seat is. When the gift is presented, go into the tabernacle, and between the two cherubim hear Him speak to you. May each of us, on his own day, present his own portion — complete, weighed true, with name and record plain; and having presented it, not hurry away, but go in before Him, and quietly hear Him open His mouth. That is where these twelve days were always leading us.

(New Testament reading) Two New Testament passages are traditionally read by the church alongside this closing verse; as throughout, the layer is labelled, stated as a layer of reading, and not read back into the text of Numbers. The first is Romans 3, where Christ Jesus is called the propitiation — the Greek word there, hilastērion, is the very word the Septuagint uses to render this chapter's "mercy seat" (kappōret, H3727) (Rom 3:25, Summary): the One whose voice Moses heard from above the mercy seat answers, in the New Testament reading, to the One who Himself became the mercy seat — where Moses once heard a voice between the cherubim, believers now meet that voice made flesh. The second is Hebrews 4, which exhorts believers to come boldly unto the throne of grace, that they may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need (Heb 4:16, Summary): on one side, Moses alone entering the tabernacle to hear the voice; on the other, all the children exhorted to come boldly together to the mercy seat; and between the two stands the road the great High Priest has already walked through. (Application) But the layers must be kept straight: Numbers 7 is first of all itself — twelve princes, twelve full lists, one rendering of account in exact agreement, and at the end one voice from off the mercy seat. The New Testament reading is the light in which the church, in Christ, looks back on it; it must not be used to dissolve this chapter's lists. The more solid this chapter's lists — every name checkable, every figure workable, every day countable one by one — the more weight the reading layer's correspondence carries: the place where one prince stood one day at a time, and the voice was heard through the veil, has become the place before the mercy seat to which all the children come boldly together.

(Application) As the sermon ends, this chapter must be handed back into every hearer's own hands. You may be standing in the queue, waiting for your own day: other men's offering days flash past, and your own day seems never to come. This chapter says to the waiting: across the twelve days, Pagiel, who offered on the eleventh day, waited ten days, and Ahira, on the twelfth, eleven — and the list the text gives them does not differ from the first day by one item, and the space it gives them is not one verse less than the first day's. Waiting does not shrink your portion. Your day will come, and when it comes, your page will be as complete as any man's. And you may have offered already, and feel your portion too ordinary — exactly like everyone else's, not worth mentioning. This chapter says to you: ordinariness is the order of these twelve days. What God asks was never a portion different from all others, but the portion recorded under your name — complete, weighed true, and presented. May we all present our own portion on our own day, and then learn from Moses to go into the tabernacle — to be quiet before the mercy seat, and to hear the Lord who keeps His accounts without a fraction's error speak to us Himself, in person.

## Devotional Excerpt

English (KJV-based): Numbers 7 first frightened me by its length: eighty-nine verses, and the same list of gifts written out twelve times. I tried to work the sum for myself. One silver charger at an hundred and thirty shekels, twelve of them, makes one thousand five hundred and sixty; one silver bowl at seventy, twelve of them, eight hundred and forty; the two together are two thousand and four hundred — exactly the total the text records, not a shekel different. The golden spoons, ten shekels apiece, twelve of them, are an hundred and twenty, and that agrees too. The beasts are two hundred fifty and two, and added up by their kinds they are still two hundred fifty and two. Slowly the taste of it came to me: twelve princes, gifts exactly alike, the only difference the names. Ahira, who offered on the twelfth day, brought a gift not one piece smaller than Nahshon's on the first day; eleven days of waiting did not buy him a discounted offering. This touches my heart. I often fear I am near the end of the queue, that by my turn only leavings will remain. But the text says there is no "the rest as above" in God's ledger: every gift is written out in full again, and every name has a day of its own. So I pray: Lord, I thank Thee that Thy bookkeeping is so careful — how heavy my charger is, whether my spoon is full of incense, Thou hast recorded it all. Make me, on my own day, neither hide, nor send another in my place, nor offer in haste, but present in full the portion Thou hast measured to me; and when I have offered, not hurry away, but learn from Moses to come before Thee, and be quiet at the mercy seat, and hear Thee speak unto me.

## Summary (about 100 words)

Abraham was old, and he sent the oldest servant who managed all his household back to his own country and kindred to find a wife for Isaac. The servant took ten camels and, at the well outside the city of Nahor, prayed that God would give him good success that day. Before he had finished speaking, Rebekah came out carrying her pitcher, gave the servant a drink, and drew water for all ten camels until they had finished drinking. The servant bowed in worship, recognizing that God had shown steadfast love and faithfulness to his master. This sermon looks at three things: the direction of the entrustment, a specific prayer, and the confirmation hidden in diligence — God is often already on the road before we open our mouths.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

In the previous chapter we said farewell to Sarah. Abraham mourned and wept for her, then rose and negotiated with the Hittites, weighed out the silver in full, and bought the cave of Machpelah as a burial place. A man who has just lost his wife would most easily stop — and live beside the grave. Abraham did not. This chapter opens with the Bible saying: "And Abraham was old, and well stricken in age: and the LORD had blessed Abraham in all things." (Gen. 24:1, excerpt, KJV) Well stricken in age reminds us that time is short; and what this old man did next was not to arrange his own funeral, but to set his son's marriage on the road.

Genesis 24 is the longest chapter in the book, sixty-seven verses, with no great battle and no miracle — only a servant traveling, water being drawn at a well, and a family eating and talking together. Yet this whole chapter is about our everyday life: our children's future, choices about marriage, entrusting matters to others, and opening our mouths in prayer. God left such a long chapter for a household matter to tell us: He prepares not only on Mount Moriah, He also prepares at the well; He cares not only for the great matter of sacrifice, He also cares whom you meet when you go out today.

Let us first read several key passages:

"And Abraham said unto his eldest servant of his house, that ruled over all that he had, Put, I pray thee, thy hand under my thigh: And I will make thee swear by the LORD, the God of heaven, and the God of the earth, that thou shalt not take a wife unto my son of the daughters of the Canaanites, among whom I dwell: But thou shalt go unto my country, and to my kindred, and take a wife unto my son Isaac." (Gen. 24:2-4, excerpt, KJV)

"And the servant took ten camels of the camels of his master... and he arose and went... to the city of Nahor. And he made his camels to kneel down without the city by a well of water at the time of the evening, even the time that women go out to draw water." (Gen. 24:10-11, excerpt, KJV)

"And he said, O LORD God of my master Abraham, I pray thee, send me good speed this day, and shew kindness unto my master Abraham." (Gen. 24:12, excerpt, KJV)

"And it came to pass, before he had done speaking, that, behold, Rebekah came out... with her pitcher upon her shoulder... The damsel was very fair to look upon, a virgin, neither had any man known her: and she went down to the well, and filled her pitcher, and came up." (Gen. 24:15-16, excerpt, KJV)

"And they blessed Rebekah, and said unto her, Thou art our sister, be thou the mother of thousands of millions, and let thy seed possess the gate of those which hate them!" (Gen. 24:60, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, teach us through this chapter to pray, to wait, and to recognize Your hand. Many things — before we have spoken, You were already on the road. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 23:00) Part One: The Direction of the Entrustment — Not to Canaan, but Back to His Kindred (24:1-9)

First, look at the entrustment. To whom did Abraham entrust this matter? "His eldest servant of his house, that ruled over all that he had" — tradition often identifies him as Eliezer of Damascus, mentioned in chapter 15; the Bible does not name him here, and we will follow the text only: this was a man who had followed his master for decades, managed the entire household, and was most trusted. Great matters must be entrusted to trustworthy people. Abraham did not casually send a young man on an errand; he placed the execution of his son's lifelong matter on the oldest, steadiest shoulders in the house. Brothers and sisters, when we entrust matters in the church and at home, seniority is not show — it is a record of faithfulness: a person who has not betrayed a trust for decades has weight in his words.

The servant first had to swear, with his hand under his master's thigh — the most solemn posture for making a covenant at that time, close to swearing before life and descendants. The oath had two parts. The first was a prohibition: "Thou shalt not take a wife unto my son of the daughters of the Canaanites." The second was a direction: "Thou shalt go unto my country, and to my kindred." Note the order: first say what not to do, then say what to do. Abraham was extremely clear about the direction of marriage: the daughters of Canaan worshipped idols, and their customs did not agree with the faith of the promised household; even though they were close at hand, he would not compromise. The women of his kindred were a thousand miles away, yet he would go there. Distance is not a reason; if the direction is wrong, the more convenient it is, the more dangerous it is. When we make choices today for our children and for ourselves, we should also ask about direction first, not convenience first.

The servant asked a very practical question: "Peradventure the woman will not be willing to follow me unto this land: must I needs bring thy son again unto the land from whence thou camest?" Abraham's answer was decisive: "Beware thou that thou bring not my son thither again." He explained the reason fully: "The LORD God of heaven, which took me from my father's house, and from the land of my kindred... he shall send his angel before thee, and thou shalt take a wife unto my son from thence." (Gen. 24:7, excerpt, KJV) Hear this logic well: since God led me out, He must never let my son go back. A wife could be found by another way, but Isaac could not return to Mesopotamia to settle. A person may travel a long road to accomplish something, but may not move back to accomplish it.

Yet he also left the servant a way out: "And if the woman will not be willing to follow thee, then thou shalt be clear from this my oath." — An entrustment must have a bottom line, and also consideration: do your faithfulness; a result you cannot bear will not be pressed on you. The standard is unambiguous, the responsibility is not unlimited — this is what we should learn when entrusting people.

## (23:00 - 40:00) Part Two: The Prayer at the Well — A Specific Request and Immediate Confirmation (24:10-27)

The servant chose ten camels, took all kinds of goods, went to Aram-naharaim, to the well outside the city of Nahor, and made the camels kneel. The time was "at the time of the evening, even the time that women go out to draw water." The old servant understood life: to look for a bride, do not go to a banquet to meet her; go to the well — a woman's character in a day is hardest to hide when she draws water.

Then came that famous prayer. He stood by the well and said: "Behold, I stand here by the well of water... I pray thee, send me good speed this day" [2]. This "send me good speed," in the original qarah (H7136), means to meet, to happen upon [2]. The word the servant used was extremely humble: he was not asking for a miracle, but for a "chance meeting." How many great matters enter in Scripture as "by chance": Ruth happened to come to Boaz's field, Esther happened to be chosen. God's hand often does not beat gongs and drums; He schedules "chance" into the day. What we need to learn is not to mystify coincidence, but to place our schedule in prayer — before going out, kneel first; after meeting, recognize it.

The content of the prayer was very specific: he asked that the young woman standing by the well, when he said, "Let down thy pitcher, I pray thee, that I may drink," if she answered, "Drink, and I will give thy camels drink also," then let her be the one God had appointed as wife for Isaac. Why set this sign? Because drawing water for camels is heavy work: for ten camels to drink their fill means going down to the well and drawing more than a hundred times. The servant was not asking for a sign of beauty, but a sign of character — mercy toward a stranger, compassion for animals, diligent hands that do not calculate. In choosing a spouse, employing a person, making friends, do not look at how someone dresses up in front of others; look at how they treat the weak and things with no return — that is most accurate.

"Before he had done speaking" — the confirmation came faster than the prayer. Rebekah came out, her pitcher on her shoulder, very fair to look upon. The servant ran to meet her and said the practiced words: let down your pitcher a little, please, and give me some water to drink. She said, "Drink, my lord," and hastened to let down the pitcher onto her hand and gave him drink. When he had finished, she added of her own accord: "I will draw water for thy camels also, until they have done drinking." — Note, these were exactly the words in the prayer, not one word different, and she said them herself; no one hinted them to her. Then she "hastened" to empty the pitcher into the trough, "ran again unto the well to draw water," and drew for all ten camels until they had finished drinking. The text uses in succession several "hastened" and "ran again" — this woman's rhythm in doing good was to do it running.

And the servant? He "wondering at her held his peace, to wit whether the LORD had made his journey prosperous or not." This is where he was most seasoned: he did not rush to announce a miracle; he watched to the end first. Only after the camels had finished drinking did he take out the gold earring and bracelets and ask whose daughter she was. When he learned she was the daughter of Bethuel — precisely Abraham's kindred — he "bowed down his head, and worshipped the LORD," and spoke the finest praise in the whole chapter: "Blessed be the LORD... who hath not left destitute my master of his mercy and his truth." [1] Mercy and truth, in the original chesed (H2617), is love that is faithful and unchanging within the covenant [1]. The servant did not say, "My prayer worked"; he said God's covenant with his master had not broken. When you reach old age, if you can still say, "He has not left destitute my master of His mercy and His truth," your life has not been walked in vain.
## (40:00 - 52:00) Part Three: The Retelling at the Table and Rebekah's Answer — I Will Go (24:28-67)

Rebekah ran home to tell the news; her brother Laban came out to receive the servant, brought him into the house, ungirded the camels, gave straw and provender, and water to wash his feet. At the table there was a scene that most shows the old servant's true character: food was set before him, and he said, "I will not eat, until I have told mine errand." First handle the entrustment, then care for himself — at the well he did not announce first, at the table he did not touch the food set before him first; his whole life's order was like this. Then he retold the whole experience from beginning to end, from his master's oath, his own prayer, to Rebekah's answer, adding nothing and omitting nothing. Retelling is the most basic skill of a faithful servant: do not exaggerate the confirmation, do not hide the process, give the decision back to the other party.

When he finished, he put the question on the table: "And now if ye will deal kindly and truly with my master, tell me: and if not, tell me; that I may turn to the right hand, or to the left." No coercion, no going in circles. Laban and Bethuel answered crisply: "The thing proceedeth from the LORD: we cannot speak unto thee bad or good. Behold, Rebekah is before thee, take her, and go, and let her be thy master's son's wife, as the LORD hath spoken." The servant again bowed himself to the earth before the LORD, then brought out jewels of silver, jewels of gold, and raiment for Rebekah, and also gave precious things to her brother and her mother. By this point, it could be said that everyone was satisfied.

Early the next morning, the servant proposed to depart at once back to his master. Her mother and brother were reluctant and wanted to keep their sister ten days, at least to accompany her some days. The servant's answer was gentle and firm: "Hinder me not, seeing the LORD hath prospered my way; send me away that I may go to my master." The family made a decision that was fairly enlightened for that era: "We will call the damsel, and enquire at her mouth." Rebekah was called, and there was only one question: "Wilt thou go with this man?" Her answer was only two words: "I will go." [4]

Please note these two words. Rebekah had never seen Isaac, never seen Canaan; what she had in her hands was only an old servant's retelling, a meeting at a well, and bridal gifts all over her. Her saying "I will go" was faith, was courage, and was also a claiming of her identity as the new bride in this household. Many great followings in the Bible begin with an answer that is not long: Abraham went when he heard the call; Rebekah went when she heard the account. Some wait until everything is certain before they will move; Rebekah mounted the camel when there was only one testimony.

They sent her away, and their blessing corresponded from afar to the promise God gave Abraham: "Be thou the mother of thousands of millions, and let thy seed possess the gate of those which hate them!" [5] This is exactly the echo of that sentence on the mountain in chapter 22, "thy seed shall possess the gate of his enemies." The seed obtained by the oath on the mountain was now riding a camel toward Canaan — a promise does not stay as words in heaven; it becomes a specific person, walks a specific road, and arrives on a specific evening.

On the return road, the Bible records no more of the servant's words, but we can fully imagine his feelings: on the way there the camels carried goods; on the way back they carried the bride and the continuation of the promise. What he accomplished on this trip was not merely a marriage, but moved the covenant God made with Abraham forward one generation. Brothers and sisters, do not belittle those errands in your hands arranging things for others: as long as the direction is right, the prayer is enough, and the retelling is honest, it may be far greater than you imagine. God's great matters are often accomplished through a servant willing to go on the road.

The beauty of the ending is like a painting: the servant brought Rebekah back. Isaac at that time lived in the south country, and in the evening went out to meditate in the field [3]; he lifted up his eyes and saw the camel train coming. Rebekah also lifted up her eyes, saw Isaac, hurried down from the camel, and took a veil and covered herself. The servant told Isaac all the things he had done. Isaac brought Rebekah into his mother Sarah's tent, took her as wife, and loved her. "And Isaac was comforted after his mother's death." The last sentence of the whole chapter gently joins two lines: a son who had just lost his mother was comforted, and a woman who had just left home had a place to belong. God's preparation never only solves a task; He also comforts people.

## (52:00 - 58:00) Application: Six Kinds of People

First, parents who are anxious about their children's future. Learn from Abraham: fix the direction first and firmly, entrust the details, leave the process to God. You cannot walk your children's road with them the whole way, but you can choose a direction for them, pray for them, and guard the first step for them.

Second, people whose prayers are always empty words. Learn from the old servant: make your request specific — time, place, sign, standard. A specific prayer is not commanding God; it lets you yourself recognize it when the confirmation comes. A person whose prayer is vague does not know whom to thank even when it is answered.

Third, people waiting for "chance." The meeting you are waiting for may already be scheduled into tomorrow's itinerary. Pray first, then go out, and keep your eyes wide open on the road. God's "chance" is shown only to prepared people: if the servant had not gone to the well, for whom would Rebekah have drawn water?

Fourth, people entrusted with tasks. Learn the servant's order: do not eat until the matter is done, do not announce until you have watched the confirmation to the end, add nothing and omit nothing when retelling. We all hold other people's entrustments in our hands — the company's, the church's, our family's — faithfulness is not a slogan; it is a series of small orders.

Fifth, people standing in Rebekah's place. Before you is a call you have only heard in testimony and never seen with your eyes, and your family wants to keep you ten more days. Will you say that sentence, "I will go"? Delay is sometimes consideration, sometimes a leak of faith. When you have heard clearly, get on the camel.

Sixth, people who have just lost a loved one. Isaac was comforted while his mother's tent was still empty. God's comfort is often not explaining the emptiness away, but bringing in new people and a new season. On the evening when you meditate in the field in the south country, the camel train may already be on the horizon.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Responsive Prayer

This chapter tells us today: God was already on the road. While the old man was still in Canaan arranging matters to come, the answer was already drawing water at the well in Nahor; before the servant had finished speaking, Rebekah had already come out of the city gate. His mercy and truth do not cease, and His preparation is never late.

Let us pray together: Lord, thank You — before we open our mouths You have already begun to work; in places we cannot see You prepare people, prepare roads, prepare comfort. Give us Abraham's sense of direction, the old servant's faithfulness and specific prayer, and Rebekah's courage to say "I will go." May we, to old age, also bow like the servant and say: You have continually shown mercy and truth to me. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] chesed: Hebrew חֶסֶד (H2617), mercy, love of loyalty, covenant faithfulness; in Gen. 24:12 the servant prayed asking God to "shew kindness unto my master," and in 24:27 the servant praised God who "hath not left destitute my master of his mercy and his truth," rendered together in the KJV at 24:27 as "his mercy and his truth"; the meaning is according to BDB and HALOT.

[2] qarah: Hebrew קָרָה (H7136), to meet, to happen upon, to cause to meet; in Gen. 24:12 the servant prayed, "send me good speed this day," using the causative stem, asking God to cause a chance meeting; the meaning is according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's statement "God schedules chance into the itinerary" is a homiletic exposition based on this word.

[3] Isaac's meditation: Gen. 24:63, "Isaac went out to meditate in the field," where "meditate" in the original is the verb śûaḥ (שׂוּחַ, H7742), to ponder, to meditate; this word appears in the Old Testament only here; this manuscript follows the KJV rendering and notes it here specially.

[4] Rebekah's answer: Gen. 24:58, Rebekah answered "I will go," in the original ēlēk (אֵלֵךְ), the first-person imperfect form of the verb "to go, to walk," brief and decisive; based on this, this manuscript teaches "an answer of faith need not be long."

[5] The correspondence between the blessing in 24:60 and 22:17: Rebekah's family's blessing, "let thy seed possess the gate of those which hate them," corresponds to God's oath language in Gen. 22:17; based on this, this manuscript points out the narrative connection by which the promise moves from the oath on the mountain to its fulfillment in the marriage at the well.

[6] The identity of the old servant: Genesis 24 does not state the servant's name explicitly; tradition often identifies him with Eliezer in Gen. 15:2; this manuscript speaks only according to "the eldest servant... that ruled over all that he had" (24:2) and draws no conclusion.

## Devotional Excerpt

Genesis 24:12, 27 (KJV, excerpt): "O LORD God of my master Abraham, I pray thee, send me good speed this day, and shew kindness unto my master Abraham." "Blessed be the LORD God of my master Abraham, who hath not left destitute my master of his mercy and his truth." Before setting out, the old servant knelt and prayed with striking specificity: standing by the well, asking for a sign, seeking a worthy wife for Isaac. Before he had finished speaking, Rebekah arrived, gave him a drink, and drew water for all ten camels until they had finished drinking. We often imagine God to be far from our daily routines, yet He is already on the road: before you have spoken, the answer may already have come out of the city gate. Whatever hangs unresolved in your hands today — a child, a job, a household matter — name it concretely before God, then walk your road with open eyes. His steadfast love and faithfulness never cease, and His "coincidences" are often already written into today's schedule. Before going out to handle a matter, first learn from the servant to pray standing by the well; after the matter is accomplished, also first learn from him to bow in worship, not rushing to credit your own luck. May you recognize His hand today and bow like the servant, saying: You have not forsaken Your steadfast love toward me.

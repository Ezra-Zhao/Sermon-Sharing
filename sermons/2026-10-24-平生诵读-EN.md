# He Shall Read Therein All the Days of His Life — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 17

> **English first translation · 初译待校 (first translation, pending native proofread)** — translated in full from the Chinese fixed text (CUNPSS-based sermon manuscript, strongly rechecked and corrected, finalised 2026-10-25). All Scripture quotations in this English version are taken verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), grabbed first-hand from BibleGateway's KJV text and cross-checked against a full-chapter KJV text; none is translated from the Chinese. See the Translation Record at the end of this file.

> **Determination of the title**: Five candidates were considered — (1) *In His Life He Shall Read* [平生诵读], from v. 19, "he shall read therein all the days of his life"; (2) *He Shall Write Him a Copy*, from v. 18; (3) *That His Heart Be Not Lifted Up Above His Brethren*, from v. 20; (4) *Thou Shalt Not Decline … to the Right Hand, nor to the Left*, from v. 11; (5) *Ye Shall Henceforth Return No More That Way*, from v. 16. Candidate (1) is adopted: the chapter's three bodies of material — the purity of sacrifice, the levels of judgment, and the limitation of the king — all land, in the end, on the relation between the man on the throne and one book: the book is to "be with him," and the man is to "read therein all the days of his life" (v. 19; "all the days of his life" is in the original kol-yəmê ḥayyāyw, "all the days of his life"); and the purpose of the reading is not learning, but "that his heart be not lifted up above his brethren" (v. 20). The English title uses the chapter's own wording in the KJV at 17:19: **He Shall Read Therein All the Days of His Life** (checked directly, in this round, on the per-verse page at wd.bible, as recorded in the Chinese fixed text).

> **Note on the base text and the collation**: In the Chinese fixed text, Scripture is quoted verbatim from the CUNPSS (Chinese Union Version with New Punctuation, simplified); the original text was collated against the Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia (BHS), and the English against the King James Version (KJV), all checked directly on the per-verse pages of wd.bible (Deuteronomy 17:1–20, the whole chapter, and 1 Samuel 8:5, 8:7; Hosea 13:11; 1 Kings 10:28, 11:1, 11:4; Deuteronomy 19:15). In this English version the base text for all quotation is the KJV, grabbed first-hand (see the Translation Record); the collation items below are carried over from the Chinese fixed text and registered as they stand. **Three matters of numbering are stated first**: (i) Deuteronomy 17 in the CUNPSS has 20 verses, aligned verse by verse with the Masoretic text (MT) and the KJV, **with no empty numbers and no fusions** — the forms found in this book at chapter 12 (a final verse shifted back), chapter 13 (two empty numbers and two fusions) and chapter 15 (one empty number and one fusion) do not occur here; this chapter, like chapters 14 and 16, is of the regular form. The account may not be inferred from chapter to chapter by analogy; it is recalculated chapter by chapter. (ii) **The join at the chapter boundary**: 16:21–22 and 17:1 all belong to the prohibitions concerning the purity of altar and sacrifice, as registered in the sermon on Deuteronomy 16: in the BHS each of the three verses is a closed section by itself, ending with samekh; the word for detestation at 17:1 is tôʿăḇâ ("abomination"), while at 16:22 it is śānēʾ ("hateth") — two different words in the original. This sermon takes chapter 17 as its boundary: 17:1 is both the third item in the chain of prohibitions at the end of the previous chapter and the opening of this chapter's "thou shalt not sacrifice … wherein is blemish"; the two readings stand side by side and are registered only. (iii) The grouping of the quotation marks: in the CUNPSS the opening mark of the outer quotation appears at the head of vv. 1, 2, 8 and 14, and the closing mark appears only at the end of v. 13 and at the end of v. 20 — by the usual convention for quotation running across paragraphs (the quotation mark reopened at the head of each paragraph, and closed only in the last paragraph), the outer quotation of the whole chapter falls into two great groups: vv. 1–13 (the heads of vv. 2 and 8 being reopenings at paragraph heads) and vv. 14–20 (opened at v. 14, closed at the end of v. 20). There are two embedded quotations: at v. 14, the words of the people, "I will set a king over me, like as all the nations that are about me" [in the CUNPSS, "I will set a king over me, like the nations round about"]; and at v. 16, the word of the Lord quoted there, "Ye shall henceforth return no more that way." Two punctuation variants are further registered: at v. 11 the fixed text runs on with semicolons ("…thou shalt do: thou shalt not decline from the sentence…," in the Chinese, a semicolon-linked series), where the modern-punctuation edition divides with full stops; at v. 12 the fixed text likewise runs on ("…even that man shall die: and thou shalt put away…" in sense), where the modern-punctuation edition has a full stop. Quotations in the Chinese fixed text follow the fixed text throughout; quotations in this English version follow the KJV as grabbed.

Deuteronomy 17 treats three matters: the purity of sacrifice and the investigation of the sin of idolatry (vv. 1–7), cases too hard and the central court (vv. 8–13), and the law of the king (vv. 14–20). At the end of chapter 16 judges have just been appointed in every town (16:18); this chapter at once adds two levels to the administration of judgment: at the town gate one class of sin, the gravest, is investigated first — the serving of other gods (vv. 2–7) — and the cases the town cannot decide are sent up (vv. 8–13). Then the pen is pushed further into the future: one day the people will want to set a king; the law does not wait for that day, but writes the shape of the king in advance (the "shalt say" of v. 14 is a conditional clause; kingship in this chapter is a future form legislated beforehand). The three scenes of the chapter — before the altar, in the court, on the throne — have one thing in common: at every place of greatest power, the same book of the law stands round it. The sacrificial animal before the altar must be without blemish; the sentence in the court must not decline to the right hand or to the left; the man on the throne must read therein all the days of his life. This sermon first reads the three sections verse by verse (Sections One to Three), and then sets the law of the king side by side with the echoes in 1 Samuel 8 and Hosea 13:11 and with the record of Solomon (Section Four) — at every point of juxtaposition the sermon registers only, and does not adjudicate.

> **Note on format**: This sermon is divided into four sections (Deut 17:1–7; 8–13; 14–20; the juxtaposition section), with time codes running continuously for 60 minutes. Each section sets side by side five layers: fact, discourse, New Testament reading, scholarly discussion, and outline. Discourse is marked at every place "this is an inference / discourse"; New Testament echoes are marked at every place "registered as paraphrase"; and the relations between the parallel records in Samuel, Kings and Hosea are set side by side only, without adjudication and without arranging a stemma of sources. The discipline of adjudication in this sermon has three hard points: first, the relation between the law of the king (vv. 14–20) and the narrative of the demand for a king in 1 Samuel 8 is registered only; second, the comparison between the three prohibitions on the king and the record of Solomon in 1 Kings is set side by side only; third, the echo-relation of Hosea 13:11 to Deuteronomy 17 and 1 Samuel 8 is registered only. ## Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Factual layer] The place of this chapter in the book is registered first: Deuteronomy 12–26 is the body of the law; chapter 12 establishes the place of worship, chapter 13 the loyalty of worship, chapter 14 the holiness of daily life, chapter 15 the economics of release, chapter 16 the calendar of worship and the judges in every town; chapter 17 then pushes "office" upward: first the threshold of sacrifice (v. 1), then the two levels of court (vv. 2–13), and last the king (vv. 14–20) — together with the judges at the end of chapter 16 and the priests and prophets of chapter 18, these form the group of special sections on office within the body of the law: officers, king, priests, prophets, each with its own section and each with its own boundary. The divine name "the LORD" occurs 11 times in the whole chapter, distributed over 9 verses (vv. 1, 2, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 19); the forms of address are of four kinds — "the LORD thy God" in the Chinese fixed text's wording "the LORD your God" (plural address), 7 occurrences (v. 1 once, v. 2 twice, and vv. 8, 12, 14, 15); "the LORD thy God" in the singular-address wording, 1 occurrence (v. 1); without a possessive suffix, 2 occurrences (v. 10, "the place which the LORD shall choose"; v. 16, "the LORD hath said unto you"); and "the LORD his God," 1 occurrence (v. 19, referring to the king). The last form is worth noting in advance: the one time in the whole chapter that God is called "his God" falls in the verse about the king reading in the law — the relation between the king and God is marked out separately in the very form of address. (On the KJV's own forms of address, which merge the two Chinese forms, see Note 6.)

[Discourse layer] This is a summary inference: chapter 16 gives the calendar; chapter 17 gives power. The question of the calendar is "when"; the question of power is "who decides, and by what right." The answer of this chapter is the same throughout: before the altar, what decides is the condition of the sacrificial animal; in the court, what decides is enquiry and witness; on the throne, what decides is not the king, but the book on the king's desk. This is an inference (drawn by induction from the closing sentence of each of the three sections; it is not the self-evident doctrine of any one verse).

[Outline layer] The whole chapter in one sentence: from altar to court to throne, the greater the power, the nearer the book — the sacrificial animal is nearest the altar, and its condition is examined first; the case is nearest the centre, and enquiry comes first; the king is highest above the people, and he first copies a book to keep by him, and reads therein all the days of his life. The four sections that follow: first, the purity of sacrifice and the investigation of the sin of idolatry (vv. 1–7); then cases too hard and the central court (vv. 8–13); then the law of the king (vv. 14–20); and last, 1 Samuel 8, Hosea 13:11 and the record of Solomon set side by side in comparison.

## One: Thou Shalt Not Sacrifice Whatsoever Hath a Blemish, Neither Shalt Thou Serve Other Gods (Deut 17:1–7; 05:00–20:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 1 is a free-standing prohibition on sacrifice: "Thou shalt not sacrifice unto the LORD thy God any bullock, or sheep, wherein is blemish, or any evilfavouredness: for that is an abomination unto the LORD thy God." In the original, two items stand side by side — mûm (blemish, defect) and kol-dāḇār rāʿ (any evil thing, any bad condition); the fixed text renders them "wherein is blemish, or any evilfavouredness" [in the CUNPSS, "that has a defect, or anything wrong with it"]. This verse and the two prohibitions beside the altar at 16:21–22 each carry samekh in the BHS and form a closed section by themselves (for the join, see item (ii) of the note on the base text above); the theme is the same throughout: what is presented to God must be clean — beside the altar there may be no wooden pole-image, and on the altar no bullock or sheep with a blemish. At v. 2 the text turns to the administration of judgment: "If there be found among you, within any of thy gates which the LORD thy God giveth thee, man or woman, that hath wrought wickedness in the sight of the LORD thy God, in transgressing his covenant," — the original šəʿāreḵā is literally "thy gates"; the fixed text [CUNPSS] renders it "the towns [which the LORD thy God giveth thee]," and the KJV renders it "within any of thy gates." The gate is the seat of judgment in an ancient town (in chapter 16 the judges are set precisely in the several towns/gates), and the scene of judgment in this section is at the gate from beginning to end: the sin is found within the gates (v. 2), and the person is brought forth unto the gates for execution (v. 5). The charge is at v. 3: "And hath gone and served other gods, and worshipped them, either the sun, or moon, or any of the host of heaven, which I have not commanded;" — the two verbs "served" (ʿāḇaḏ) and "worshipped" (hištaḥăwâ, to bow oneself down) stand side by side, and their objects are four: other gods, the sun, the moon, and the host of heaven (kol-ṣəḇāʾ haššāmayim, all the host of heaven, all its shining bodies). The clause "which I have not commanded" is the boundary line of the conviction: the problem lies not only in what is worshipped, but in "I have not commanded" — neither the object nor the manner of the worship stands within the authorisation of the covenant. (On the corrected reading "served" at 17:3 in the Chinese fixed text, see the Strong Recheck Record.)

[Factual layer] Verses 4–7 are the most complete judicial procedure in the whole of Deuteronomy. Verse 4: the action after a report is enquiry: "And it be told thee, and thou hast heard of it, and enquired diligently, and, behold, it be true, and the thing certain, that such abomination is wrought in Israel:" — "enquired diligently" in the original is dāraš hêṭēḇ, enquiry (dāraš) plus "thoroughly" (hêṭēḇ, from yāṭaḇ); the two criteria are "true" (ʾĕmet, the fact established) and "certain" (nāḵôn, the matter confirmed). Note that the enquiry in this verse uses the single word dāraš, whereas the enquiry into a rumour in chapter 13 piles up three near-synonymous verbs (for the enquiry account, see the sermon on Deuteronomy 13; this sermon registers the point only). Verse 5: the execution after conviction: "Then shalt thou bring forth that man or that woman, which have committed that wicked thing, unto thy gates, even that man or that woman, and shalt stone them with stones, till they die." — "bring forth" in the original is hôṣēʾṯā, to bring out; the place of execution is outside [unto] the gates, and the instrument is stones (sāqal). Verse 6: the threshold of evidence: "At the mouth of two witnesses, or three witnesses, shall he that is worthy of death be put to death; but at the mouth of one witness he shall not be put to death." — "mouth" (peh) occurs twice; the establishment of testimony is counted in the plural: one mouth is not enough; two or three mouths are required. This rule is later set up as a general principle at 19:15: "One witness shall not rise up against a man for any iniquity, or for any sin, in any sin that he sinneth: at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established." (Deut 19:15, checked directly in this round in the Chinese fixed text; here quoted in the KJV grabbed first-hand.) This verse is the precedent instance within a capital case; chapter 19 is the general principle for establishing a matter; the two places are registered side by side. Verse 7: the order of the execution: "The hands of the witnesses shall be first upon him to put him to death, and afterward the hands of all the people. So thou shalt put the evil away from among you." The hands of the witnesses (ʿēdîm) come first (bāriʾšōnâ, at the first), and the hands of all the people afterwards (bāʾaḥărōnâ, at the last); the closing formula, "put the evil away from among you," is Deuteronomy's fixed formula (bāʿar), occurring twice in this chapter (vv. 7, 12); at v. 12 it reads "from Israel" — the scope-word differs, and the two are registered separately.

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: what most deserves notice in this section is the proportion between procedure and penalty. As to the gravity of the sin, this is one of the gravest sins in the whole law — transgressing the covenant, serving other gods; as to the strictness of the procedure, it is also one of the most complete procedures in the whole law: first enquire diligently, then verify that it is true and the thing certain, then pass the threshold of two or three witnesses, and last the witnesses put their hands first. The graver the sin, the less the procedure may be spared. The rule "the witnesses shall be first" deserves special notice: to testify is not to hand in a sheet of paper; it is to put one's own hand on first — if the accusation is false, the one whose hand is first is the first to be stained with blood. By this rule the law weighs testimony: if your mouth is to decide a man's life or death, your hand must pay the price first. This is discourse (an ethical inference drawn from the order of vv. 4–7; the text itself does not state this reason expressly).

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three matters are registered. First, the distribution of the worship of the host of heaven (sun, moon and stars) in the ancient Near East: scholarship commonly points out that the deified worship of sun, moon and stars held a prominent place in Assyrian and Babylonian religion, and that Israel, in its contacts with Assyria in the period of the kingdom, was repeatedly affected by it (the records of host-of-heaven worship in 2 Kings 21 and 23 may be compared; this sermon has not checked those chapters verse by verse, and registers the chapter numbers only). This verse sets the host of heaven side by side with "other gods" and draws the boundary with "which I have not commanded"; for the scholarly discussion, see the bibliography cited in the notes; this sermon registers the question only and does not adjudicate its origin. Second, the use of tôʿăḇâ (abomination) in Deuteronomy: it occurs twice in this chapter (v. 1, of the sacrificial animal; v. 4, of the sin of idolatry), while 16:22 uses a different word, śānēʾ (hateth) — a blemish in sacrifice and a breach of covenant in worship fall, in the original, under the one category of "abomination," while the pole-image beside the altar is expressed with "hateth"; the division of labour between the two words is registered, and no theological adjudication is made. Third, the relation between execution "unto thy gates" [outside the gate] and the formula "put the evil away": the formula closes capital sections at many places in Deuteronomy (chapters 13, 17, 19, 21, 22, 24 all have it; this sermon has not counted them one by one, and registers only that it is the book's fixed closing form); the two occurrences in this chapter, at vv. 7 and 12, close the sections on the local court and the central court respectively — a symmetrical placement.

[New Testament reading layer] (Registered as paraphrase.) The principle of two or three witnesses is repeatedly taken up in the New Testament: Matthew 18:16, on the church's recovery of an erring brother, cites precisely "in the mouth of two or three witnesses"; 2 Corinthians 13:1 and 1 Timothy 5:19, on accusations and adjudication within the church, use the same principle; Hebrews 10:28 looks back to the old rule that one who despised Moses' law died under two or three witnesses (all the above are registered as paraphrase, not checked word for word). One point of reading is registered only: the Old Testament's threshold of evidence entered the church life of the New Testament — the threshold for conviction was not lowered on account of grace; the procedure of recovery became, if anything, finer. This sermon does not rewrite the historical setting of this section on the basis of the New Testament.

[Outline layer] Section One in one sentence: the gravest sin, handled by the strictest procedure — the enquiry must be diligent, the witnesses must be two or three, and the hand that moves first belongs to the witnesses. The sacrificial animal on the altar is examined first for blemish; the case in the town is examined first for truth; what is offered to God and what is adjudged to men — neither may be handled carelessly.

## Two: A Matter Too Hard for Thee Must Go Up (Deut 17:8–13; 20:00–35:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 8 opens a new topic (the fixed text reopens the quotation at the head of this verse; the closing mark of this group of quotation stands at the end of v. 13): "If there arise a matter too hard for thee in judgment, between blood and blood, between plea and plea, and between stroke and stroke, being matters of controversy within thy gates: then shalt thou arise, and get thee up into the place which the LORD thy God shall choose;" "Too hard" in the original is yippāleʾ, from pālēʾ — beyond one's capacity, too difficult to do. The difficulty of the case lies not in unclear facts (that was the enquiry of the previous section) but in judgment without ground: between blood (dām) and blood, between plea (dîn) and plea, and between stroke (negaʿ) and stroke, the measure has no precedent to appeal to. In the original all three groups are in the contrast form "between X and X" — the same shedding of blood: is it murder or manslaughter? The same plea: on which side does the right lie? The same stroke: to what degree is the sentence to run? The fixed text [CUNPSS] renders the three as a parallel list ("…or for bloodshed, or for a plea, or for a stroke…" in sense), gathering the contrast form into the sense. The way out is geographical: "arise" (qûm), and "get thee up" — the original ʿālâ is "go up" — go up to the place which the LORD shall choose. The local court is at the town gate; the central court is at the place of worship; when a case goes up, judgment is brought before the place where the Name is put. Verse 9: on arrival, the object of the enquiry is twofold: "And thou shalt come unto the priests the Levites, and unto the judge that shall be in those days, and enquire; and they shall shew thee the sentence of judgment:" — the priests the Levites (plural) and the judge (singular, "that shall be in those days," in the original "the one in those days") stand side by side; the action is still dāraš (enquire — the same word as the enquiry of v. 4): the local officers enquire into the facts, and the central court gives the sentence (dəḇar hammišpāṭ, the word of judgment).

[Factual layer] Verses 10–11 are the chain of obligation in execution. Verse 10: "And thou shalt do according to the sentence, which they of that place which the LORD shall choose shall shew thee; and thou shalt observe to do according to all that they inform thee:" — the "LORD" of this verse carries no possessive suffix (for the register, see the forms of address in the Introduction), standing in contrast with "the place which the LORD thy God shall choose" at v. 8: in the one place it is the place chosen by "thy God," in the other it is only "the place which the LORD shall choose" — the authority of the sentence does not hang on the enquirer's relation, but on that place itself. Verse 11 states the root of the sentence: "According to the sentence of the law which they shall teach thee, and according to the judgment which they shall tell thee, thou shalt do: thou shalt not decline from the sentence which they shall shew thee, to the right hand, nor to the left." — "they shall teach" in the original is yôrûkhā, of the same root as "the law" (tôrâ), namely yārâ: the office of the priests and the judge is to "teach the law"; the sentence is not a new rule made out of nothing, but the law landing on one concrete case. The standard of execution is "thou shalt not decline … to the right hand, nor to the left" (lōʾ tāsûr … yāmîn ûśəmōʾl) — the word for declining, sûr, occurs three times in this chapter: at v. 11 the sentence must not be declined from; at v. 17 the king's heart must not turn away; at v. 20 the king must not turn aside from the commandment; the same word places the people in the court and the king on the throne under one and the same discipline. Verse 12 is the consequence of refusing to execute: "And the man that will do presumptuously, and will not hearken unto the priest that standeth to minister there before the LORD thy God, or unto the judge, even that man shall die: and thou shalt put away the evil from Israel." — "presumptuously" in the original is zādôn, deliberate overreaching; what is punished is not an error of judgment but deliberate refusal of the final instance. The closing formula of this verse reads "put away the evil from Israel," of wider scope than "from among you" at v. 7 — to refuse the central court shakes the order of all Israel, not of one town only. Verse 13 is the echo among all the people: "And all the people shall hear, and fear, and do no more presumptuously." — hear (šāmaʿ), fear (yārēʾ), and do no more presumptuously (zûḏ, of the same root as zādôn at v. 12): a formula of the same shape as the distant parallel at 13:11 (registered in this series' sermon on Deuteronomy 13); this sermon registers only the recurrence of the formula's shape in the two chapters.

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: this section gives the administration of judgment a shape that still holds — gradation. What can be decided at the town gate is decided at the town gate; what cannot be decided goes up. To go up is not to shirk; it is to acknowledge that judgment has a boundary. Nor does the authority of the central court consist in greater numbers or power; it consists in its standing at the meeting point of law and worship: the sentence comes from men who "teach the law," and its place is "the place where the Name is put." Look again at the order of the obligations: first enquire, then do, then do not decline, and only last the penalty — and what the penalty falls on is not the loser of the case, but the man who "will do presumptuously" and will not hearken: to refuse a judgment and to lose a case are two different things. This is discourse.

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters are registered. First, the historical form of the central court: the twofold arrangement of this section, "the priests the Levites and the judge that shall be in those days," finds its nearest record in Israel's later history in the judgment set up by Jehoshaphat at Jerusalem in 2 Chronicles 19 (2 Chron 19:8–11 records Levites, priests, chief of the fathers and rulers appointed side by side, with Amariah and Zebadiah in charge respectively of "the matters of the LORD" and "the king's matters" — this sermon has not checked that passage verse by verse, and registers the chapter-and-verse numbers and the outline only). Scholarly positions differ on the relation between this section of Deuteronomy and the record in Chronicles (blueprint and realisation, or a later institution projected back); this sermon sets them side by side only and does not adjudicate their order. Second, the relation between the singular "the judge that shall be in those days" and the appointment of judges in the period of the judges and of the kingdom, and the role of the priests in the court (joint adjudication, or consultation on the law), are read differently in scholarship; both are registered. The two readings are set side by side; this sermon takes neither and drops neither.

[New Testament reading layer] (Registered as paraphrase.) No direct verbatim quotation of this section in the New Testament is registered. In the later church tradition, the church order in which there are councils above the local church and cases may be appealed level by level is commonly traced back to the double precedent of this geographical-judicial structure and of the Jerusalem council in Acts 15 (registered as paraphrase, not checked word for word). One point of reading is registered only: in this section the authority of the final instance hangs throughout on law and worship, not on rank itself — and this point draws the boundary for every later reading. This sermon does not rewrite the setting of this section on the basis of later institutions.

[Outline layer] Section Two in one sentence: a case that cannot be decided must not be decided by force; it must go up; and once it has gone up, act according to the sentence, declining neither to the left nor to the right; and the one who deliberately refuses is punished not for his case, but for his presumption.

## Three: A King May Be Set, but the King Must First Be Written into the Law (Deut 17:14–20; 35:00–52:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 14: the law of the king opens with a conditional clause: "When thou art come unto the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee, and shalt possess it, and shalt dwell therein, and shalt say, I will set a king over me, like as all the nations that are about me;" Three verbs come first — come unto, possess, dwell: the thought of kingship does not arise in the wilderness, nor in the midst of conquest, but only after settling down. The people's words are recorded verbatim in the body of the law (an embedded quotation; see item (iii) of the note on the base text): "I will set a king over me" — "set" in the original is śûm, the same word as "thou shalt … set" at v. 15; "like as all the nations that are about me" — in the original kəḵol-haggôyim ʾăšer səḇîḇōṯāy, like "all" the nations round about. Note that the fixed text [CUNPSS] here reads "the nations," while the words of the people to Samuel at 1 Samuel 8:5 read, in the CUNPSS, "like all the nations" with a fuller form — the difference of wording between the two places is registered separately (for the juxtaposition, see Section Four). Verse 15: the law's answer is not prohibition but regulation: "Thou shalt in any wise set him king over thee, whom the LORD thy God shall choose: one from among thy brethren shalt thou set king over thee: thou mayest not set a stranger over thee, which is not thy brother." Three regulations: the man must be one whom the LORD thy God shall choose (bāḥar, the same word as in "the place which … shall choose" — the God who chooses the place also chooses the king); his origin must be from among thy brethren (miqqereḇ ʾaḥeḵā, from the midst of thy brethren); and the prohibited item is a stranger (ʾîš nokhrî, one who is not a brother). A king is not refused; he is to be set in conformity with the rule.

[Factual layer] Verses 16–17 are the three prohibitions, all in the form of a negative clause plus "to himself" (lô). The first, at v. 16, concerns horses: "But he shall not multiply horses to himself, nor cause the people to return to Egypt, to the end that he should multiply horses: forasmuch as the LORD hath said unto you, Ye shall henceforth return no more that way." "Multiply" (rāḇâ) occurs twice — he shall not multiply to himself, and he shall not, in order to multiply, cause the people to return to Egypt; the reason is a backward reference in quotation form: "Ye shall henceforth return no more that way." The second and third are at v. 17: "Neither shall he multiply wives to himself, that his heart turn not away: neither shall he greatly multiply to himself silver and gold." To multiply wives (nāšîm) and to multiply silver and gold stand side by side; the item of wives carries a consequence clause — "that his heart turn not away": turning away (sûr) is the same word as at vv. 11 and 20; the direction in which the heart turns belongs to the same category as declining from the sentence and from the commandment. In the item of silver and gold, the original order is kesef wəzāhāḇ (silver and gold); the fixed text [CUNPSS] reads "gold and silver" — the difference of order is registered separately; the KJV follows the original order, "silver and gold." Besides the three prohibitions there is a variant of the prohibition at v. 15 (a stranger may not be set), but the three prohibitions refer specifically to the king's limitation of himself: he shall not multiply horses to himself, he shall not multiply wives to himself, he shall not greatly multiply silver and gold to himself — "to himself / for himself" occurs four times in the whole chapter in the Chinese register (v. 16 once, v. 17 twice, v. 18 once), and the last occurrence is reversed into a positive command: he shall write him a copy of this law for himself. The one thing the king is commanded to accumulate "for himself" is not horses, not wives, not silver, but a book. (On the KJV's rendering of this tally, "to himself" three times with v. 18 reading "write him a copy," see Note 6.)

[Factual layer] Verses 18–20 are the king's daily lesson. Verse 18: "And it shall be, when he sitteth upon the throne of his kingdom, that he shall write him a copy of this law in a book out of that which is before the priests the Levites:" — the point in time is the accession (in the original, "when he sitteth upon the throne of his kingdom"); the exemplar of the copy is the one "before the priests the Levites": the master copy of the law is kept in the custody of the priests (answering to the priests the Levites of the central court at v. 9), and the king's copy comes out of the master copy. In the original, mišneh hattôrâ is "a copy of this law" — mišneh is precisely "a second copy, a duplicate"; the Septuagint renders the word deuteronomion ("second law"), from which the Greek name of the whole book of Deuteronomy, and its English name, derive (registered: for the derivation of the book's name, see the notes). Verse 19: the use of the copy: "And it shall be with him, and he shall read therein all the days of his life: that he may learn to fear the LORD his God, to keep all the words of this law and these statutes, to do them:" — its keeping ("it shall be with him," in the original "it shall be with him"), its frequency (all the days of his life, in the original "all the days of his life"), its action (read therein, qārāʾ, to read aloud), and its two-level purpose: to learn to fear (lāmaḏ + yārēʾ), and to keep and do (šāmar + ʿāśâ) all the words of this law and these statutes. Verse 20: the fruit of the reading is a double "that … not": "That his heart be not lifted up above his brethren, and that he turn not aside from the commandment, to the right hand, or to the left: to the end that he may prolong his days in his kingdom, he, and his children, in the midst of Israel." The heart is not lifted up (rûm ləḇāḇô, his heart is lifted up) above his brethren: at v. 15 the king is set "from among thy brethren," and at v. 20 the people are still called his brethren — accession does not change the form of address; the conduct does not turn aside (sûr) from the commandment; and the promise is prolonged days — he and his children, in his kingdom, in the midst of Israel. In the whole section on the king there is not one sentence about the king's powers (war, taxes and judgment are nowhere mentioned — standing in contrast with "the manner of the king" listed at 1 Samuel 8:11–17; for the juxtaposition, see Section Four); from beginning to end it treats only the king's limits and the king's reading.

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: in the ancient world the king was commonly the source of the law — the king promulgated the code, the king was justice. This section reverses the order entirely: the law exists before the king; when the king comes into the world, the book is already written and is kept in the custody of the priests; the king's first act of state is not to issue an edict but to copy a book; and the king's life lesson is not to rule men but to read. The three prohibitions draw the lines round the three oldest forms of the capital of power: horses are the capital of armed force, wives the capital of alliance and kinship (royal marriages in the ancient world were mostly covenants), and silver and gold the capital of wealth — none of the three may be multiplied "to himself"; only the book is to be copied for himself, kept for himself, and read for himself all his life. This is discourse (the word "capital" is a discourse borrowing from modern vocabulary; the text itself lists only the three things and their consequence).

[Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Three matters are registered. First, the date and character of the law of the king: whether this section is legislation in advance (legislating for a king at a time when there was no king) or a retrospective regulation after the establishment of the kingdom — scholarly positions differ; connected with it is the order-relation between "like as all the nations that are about me" at v. 14 and the narrative of 1 Samuel 8, of which at least three readings coexist — Deuteronomy 17 as the preparation for 1 Samuel 8 (the regulation first, the demand for a king afterwards), 1 Samuel 8 as the echo of Deuteronomy 17 (the narrative written according to the existing law), and the two passages parallel and each independent (set side by side in Section Four). This sermon sets them side by side only and does not arrange a stemma. Second, what mišneh refers to: whether "this law" means the whole book of Deuteronomy, or the law of the king in this section, or some compilation of law — scholarly readings differ; connected with it, that the name "Deuteronomy" derives from the Greek rendering of this word is the common scholarly account, and is registered here as a report of that common account. Third, the quotation at v. 16, "Ye shall henceforth return no more that way," has no verbatim corresponding command sentence in Exodus: scholarship commonly regards it as Deuteronomy's summary backward reference to the Exodus tradition (Deuteronomy 28:68 has a similar warning about returning to Egypt; this sermon has not checked that verse directly, and it is entered among the cross-references pending verification; see Note 5); the exact source of the quotation is still to be examined, and is registered only, not asserted as settled.

[New Testament reading layer] (Registered as paraphrase.) The New Testament does not directly quote the law of the king in this section. The reading that may be registered in comparison is the echo, in the Gospels, of the reversal of the view of kingship: Matthew 20:25–28, on "the princes of the Gentiles exercise dominion over them" — KJV: "But Jesus called them unto him, and said, Ye know that the princes of the Gentiles exercise dominion over them, and they that are great exercise authority upon them. But it shall not be so among you: but whosoever will be great among you, let him be your minister; And whosoever will be chief among you, let him be your servant: Even as the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many." — is commonly read side by side with this section's "the king's heart shall not be lifted up above his brethren" (the comparison is registered as paraphrase in the Chinese fixed text; the verses themselves are here quoted in the KJV grabbed first-hand in this translation round, Matt 20:25–28, 4 verses checked). Further: the king of this section, "set from among thy brethren" and whose "heart shall not be lifted up above his brethren," is read in the New Testament's narratives of the king's lineage with a further layer of echo; this sermon registers only that the reading exists, does not develop a Christological inference, and does not rewrite the historical context of this section on the basis of the New Testament.

[Outline layer] Section Three in one sentence: Israel may set a king, but the king must first become a student — horses must not be many, wives must not be many, silver must not be many, and the book must not leave his hand; the crown is on the king's head, the law is on his desk, and the promise of prolonged days lies not in power, but in reading.

## Four: Juxtaposition — the Echoes of the Demand for a King and the Comparison with Solomon (52:00–57:00)

[Factual layer] This section performs juxtaposition only: first the texts are set out, then their agreements and differences, and no verdict is given. First, the words of the people at Deuteronomy 17:14 stand over against the words of the people at 1 Samuel 8:5. Deuteronomy 17:14: "I will set a king over me, like as all the nations that are about me;" 1 Samuel 8:5 (checked directly in this round in the Chinese fixed text; here in the KJV grabbed first-hand): "And said unto him, Behold, thou art old, and thy sons walk not in thy ways: now make us a king to judge us like all the nations." The two places share the skeleton "set/make a king to judge/rule" and "like … the nations"; the differences are registered: the words of Deuteronomy 17 are hypothetical words in the first person singular ("and shalt say"), while those of 1 Samuel 8 are the elders' face-to-face request to Samuel, carrying one further layer of reason — Samuel is old, and his sons walk not in his ways. Second, 1 Samuel 8:7 (checked directly in this round) records the LORD's words to Samuel: "And the Lord said unto Samuel, Hearken unto the voice of the people in all that they say unto thee: for they have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them." — the word "rejected" (māʾas) and the characterisation "that I should not reign over them" appear only in the narrative of 1 Samuel 8; the text of the law in Deuteronomy 17 carries no such characterising language. Set side by side: in the one place, calm legislation in advance; in the other, a historical moment carrying a characterisation. Third, Hosea 13:11 (checked directly in this round): "I gave thee a king in mine anger, and took him away in my wrath." — this sentence in the prophets looks back on the giving of a king with "anger / wrath," belonging, with the "rejected" language of 1 Samuel 8, to the characterising language of retrospect; Deuteronomy 17 itself still carries no tone of this kind. The relation among the three (preparation, narrative, echo) is registered in arrangement only; their order and their stemma are not adjudicated.

[Factual layer] Fourth, the three prohibitions and the record of Solomon are set side by side item by item. The prohibition concerning horses (Deut 17:16: he shall not multiply horses, nor cause the people to return to Egypt) against 1 Kings 10:28 (checked directly in this round): "And Solomon had horses brought out of Egypt, and linen yarn: the king's merchants received the linen yarn at a price." — the prohibition in Deuteronomy 17 names Egypt specifically as the source of horses, and the record in 1 Kings states specifically that Solomon's horses came out of Egypt; set over against each other, the facts speak for themselves; this sermon sets them side by side only. The prohibition concerning wives (Deut 17:17: he shall not multiply wives, that his heart turn not away) against 1 Kings 11:1 (checked directly in this round): "But king Solomon loved many strange women, together with the daughter of Pharaoh, women of the Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites, Zidonians, and Hittites:" and 11:4 (checked directly in this round): "For it came to pass, when Solomon was old, that his wives turned away his heart after other gods: and his heart was not perfect with the Lord his God, as was the heart of David his father." — what Deuteronomy 17:17 fears is "that his heart turn not away" (sûr), and what 1 Kings 11:4 records is precisely a heart turned away after other gods; note further that the charge at Deuteronomy 17:1–3 is precisely that a man "hath gone and served other gods, and worshipped them" — the prohibition concerning the king's wives and the charge at the opening of this chapter answer each other head to tail; that is a structural fact within this chapter, while the correspondence with the record of Solomon belongs to the items of juxtaposition. The prohibition concerning silver and gold (Deut 17:17: he shall not greatly multiply to himself silver and gold) against 1 Kings 10: that chapter records the gold Solomon received in one year and the wealth of the royal house at its height (the passage 1 Kings 10:14–29; this sermon has not checked that passage verse by verse, registers the chapter-and-verse numbers only, and quotes no figures). Once the three prohibitions and the three records stand side by side, whether the editor took Solomon as the counter-example to the law of the king, and the order-relation between the text of the law of the king and the editing of the Book of Kings, are matters on which scholarly discussion exists; this sermon sets them side by side only and does not adjudicate.

[Outline layer] Section Four in one sentence: before the people opened their mouths to ask for a king, the law had already written the shape of the king; and after the king was firm on his throne, history recorded, item by item, his distance from that shape. The two documents are placed side by side here; the judgment is left to the reader; this sermon's responsibility is only to check the words accurately and to align the pages.

## Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Factual layer] The chapter's chain of closing sentences is reviewed once: v. 1 closes at "for that is an abomination unto the LORD thy God"; v. 7 at "So thou shalt put the evil away from among you"; v. 12 at "thou shalt put away the evil from Israel"; v. 13 at "do no more presumptuously"; v. 20 at "he may prolong his days in his kingdom." The last sentence of the whole chapter is not a penalty but a promise; and the object of the promise is not the people, but the king who is willing to copy the book, willing to read therein, and willing not to lift himself up above his brethren.

[Discourse layer] This is an inference: Deuteronomy 17's final definition of power does not lie in the size of the power, but in the distance between the power and the book. The sacrificial animal is nearest to God, and is examined first for blemish; the case is nearest to men, and passes first through enquiry; the king is highest above all, and first copies the law. The common answer of the three scenes is one sentence: the nearer a place stands to the sanctuary and to authority, the more it must first be examined. This is discourse.

[Outline layer] The whole sermon in one closing sentence: "He shall read therein all the days of his life" — these words are the lesson-table this chapter leaves to everyone in power: the book at one's hand, the heart among one's brethren, and the days on the throne are long.

## Devotional Extract

The king's desk holds only one book, and he copied it with his own hand. The law does not command the king to read all the books under heaven; it commands one thing only: on the day he sits upon the throne, he shall write him a copy of this law, out of that which is before the priests the Levites, and keep it with him, and read therein all the days of his life. Why must the king, of all men, read the most? Because power lifts the heart more easily than anything else — above his brethren, above the commandment, at last above the God who set him there. Reading therein is the remedy: a man who opens the same book every day remembers, every day, who he is — a brother set up from among his brethren; the crown is a temporary trust, the law abides. The reading has no day of completion, because "all the days of his life" allows no early dismissal. We do not sit on thrones, but each of us holds in his hand his own share of "power": a household, a work, a company of people who trust us. The man who keeps God's word at his hand and reads therein all his life does not easily lift his heart up; and the man whose heart is not lifted up above his brethren is the man whose days run long. There is a further layer of meaning hidden in "he shall write him a copy": the king is not the author of this book; he is only its student; every word he writes out, stroke by stroke, must first be written into his own days. Open the book today, read a short passage, read it aloud — reading therein begins with the voice; reverence begins today.

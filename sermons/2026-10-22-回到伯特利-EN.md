## Summary (about 100 words)

After the events at Shechem, Jacob's household carried blood and a bad name, yet God told him to go up to Bethel, settle there, and build an altar. Jacob commanded his whole household to put away the foreign gods, purify themselves, and change their garments, and God set His terror upon the surrounding cities. At Bethel God restated the promise and named him Israel; on the road Rachel bore Benjamin and died. Between tears and promise, the family still walked on.

---

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The end of the previous chapter is neither pleasant to hear nor pleasant to see. Dinah was violated at Shechem, Simeon and Levi took up the sword in revenge, and all the males of the city were killed. Jacob said to his two sons a heavy and very truthful sentence: you have made me to stink among the inhabitants of the land. Hold that picture for a moment: something has happened in the household, its name is broken outside, there is the blood-impulse of sons inside, and fear in the father's heart. When a person reaches this point, where should the next step go? Hide farther away? Move somewhere else and start over? Or settle accounts with people first and win back his face?

At exactly this moment, God spoke. Genesis chapter 35 is a chapter in which God speaks first. God did not first ask Jacob how these years had gone, nor did He first settle the account of Shechem. The first words God spoke were: Arise, go up to Bethel. What kind of place is Bethel? It is the place where Jacob, on his flight, slept in the wilderness, used a stone for a pillow, and dreamed of the ladder; the place where he first heard God say with His own mouth, "I am with thee"; the place where he set up a pillar, poured oil, and made a vow. God's meaning is very clear: you cannot solve an inner problem by changing places; you must return to the place where you first met God. Returning to Bethel is not nostalgia; it is realignment. When a person has drifted for a long time, the danger is not that the road back is far, but that he can no longer recognize the road he started on. In this chapter, God Himself leads Jacob back.

Let us read several key passages together:

"And God said unto Jacob, Arise, go up to Bethel, and dwell there: and make there an altar unto God, that appeared unto thee when thou fleddest from the face of Esau thy brother." (Genesis 35:1, excerpt, KJV)

"Then Jacob said unto his household, and to all that were with him, Put away the strange gods that are among you, and be clean, and change your garments: And let us arise, and go up to Bethel; and I will make there an altar unto God, who answered me in the day of my distress, and was with me in the way which I went." (Genesis 35:2-3, excerpt, KJV)

"And they journeyed: and the terror of God was upon the cities that were round about them, and they did not pursue after the sons of Jacob." (Genesis 35:5, excerpt, KJV)

"And God appeared unto Jacob again ... and blessed him. And God said unto him, Thy name is Jacob: thy name shall not be called any more Jacob, but Israel shall be thy name." (Genesis 35:9-10, excerpt, KJV)

"And Rachel travailed ... and the midwife said unto her, Fear not; thou shalt have this son also. And it came to pass, as her soul was in departing, (for she died) that she called his name Benoni: but his father called him Benjamin." (Genesis 35:16-18, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, among us too there are people standing after a Shechem — wounds at home, trouble outside, and fear in the heart. Speak that sentence to us also: Arise, go up to Bethel. Lead us back to the place where we first met You, remove from among us everything that has taken Your place, rebuild the altar, and let us hear again the name You give us. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: Arise, Go Up to Bethel — Be Cleansed First, Then Set Out (35:1-8)

First look at the four actions God commanded: arise, go up, dwell there, and also build an altar. Not one of these words is vague. Arise means stop lying paralyzed where you are. Jacob had already settled at Shechem, bought land, built an altar, and even named the altar El-elohe-Israel, meaning God, the God of Israel. It sounds very spiritual, but while the man lived at Shechem, his heart was slowly assimilated by Shechem: his daughter went out to see the daughters of the land, his sons learned to take up the sword like the men of the land, and this household, living in Canaan, gradually took on the flavor of Canaan. When God said arise, He refused to let this go on mixed any longer. Go up is direction. Bethel is in the hill country; the road goes upward. Spiritual restoration never slides downhill; it must be climbed with effort. Dwell there means do not treat Bethel again as a one-night stop. In his youth Jacob slept at Bethel only one night and hurried on when he woke; this time God wanted him to settle there and turn the first moving encounter into a settled life. Build an altar means place the settled days within worship. The true mark of a person's return to God is not that he wept through one meeting, but that his home has an altar again.

Jacob understood. He did not treat this command as his personal devotional exercise; he turned and spoke to his whole household. This is one of the rare times Jacob, as father and head of the house, acted with such clarity. He said: Put away the strange gods that are among you, and be clean, and change your garments. Notice the order: first put away, then be clean, then change. If the foreign gods have not been put away, changing into clean garments is only appearance; if the idols are still clutched in the arms, a person may wash all he likes and be dirty again the moment he turns around. How did Jacob's household come to have foreign gods? There were the images brought from Laban's house, things picked up at Shechem, on the road, and in trading, and also invisible gods: relying on scheming to stay alive, relying on sons to vent anger, relying on land for security. An idol is not only a figure set on a shelf; an idol is the first thing a person leans on when trouble comes. This time Jacob would not let his household keep hiding these things. He himself first confessed the most important sentence: that God is the One who answered me in the day of my distress and was with me in the way which I went. The first step back to Bethel is to hand the credit back from one's own hands to God, in front of the whole family.

The household's response is also worth reading slowly: "And they gave unto Jacob all the strange gods which were in their hand, and all their earrings which were in their ears; and Jacob hid them under the oak which was by Shechem." Even the earrings were handed over, because those rings were mostly connected with foreign worship. There is a moving detail here: he buried them. He did not sell them for money, nor put them away in case they might be wanted later; he buried them under the oak, left them at Shechem, and did not take them up to Bethel. Brothers and sisters, some things cannot travel with you on God's road. If you want to return to your first love, there must first be a serious burial: bury that relationship you have been living by, bury that contact you cannot bring yourself to delete, bury that way of doing things you know is unclean. Purifying and keeping oneself is not a mood in a prayer; it requires the action of handing things over and burying them. Jacob's whole household changed their garments. The Bible does not write much about it, but the picture is beautiful: a family standing under the oak at Shechem, taking off the old, putting on the clean, and then setting out. That is what repentance looks like — not declaring that from now on you will be a good person, but first loosening your grip on the idols clutched in your hands.

Next, God Himself took over the thing people fear most: safety. "And the terror of God was upon the cities that were round about them, and they did not pursue after the sons of Jacob." After the Shechem affair, what Jacob feared most was revenge, that the peoples around would join hands and attack. God did not make Jacob's sons fight another battle, nor send Jacob around making apologies and arrangements; God set fear upon the surrounding cities. When people do their part in cleansing, God Himself undertakes the protection. This must be said accurately: it does not mean God approved the cruelty of Simeon and Levi — on his deathbed Jacob would still speak about their anger, and God's righteousness is not blurred at all. What it means is that God blocked the chain of blood revenge, so that a household that had just turned back would not be swallowed by enemies on the road. Grace is often like this: God does not erase what you did wrong, but on the road of your return He personally blocks for you what you cannot block yourself.

There is another layer we must not miss: God's command to go up to Bethel was not only a command to change residence; it was a command to change the order of the whole family's life. At Shechem the order of this household was reversed: the sons ruled by the sword, the father worried afterward, no one mentioned their daughter's wound, and no one asked for God's word. At Bethel the order had to be set right: God speaks first, the father leads in listening, the children follow in handing over idols, and the whole family sets out together. Brothers and sisters, a family's restoration often begins when its order is set right. It is not that the family worships God only after the children have all become sensible; it is that God is welcomed back to first place first, and the children slowly learn sense within worship. This time Jacob did not wait until the whole family was ready before setting out; he set out with a household that had just been through a catastrophe. The altar was rebuilt on the road, and the people were repaired by God as they walked. Do not wait until you are completely well before returning to God; return first, and God has a way to heal you while He leads you on.

When they reached Bethel, Jacob built an altar and called the place El-Bethel, meaning the God of Bethel [1]. In his youth he had named the place Bethel, the house of God; more than twenty years later he deepened the name by one layer: not only the house of God, but the God who appeared to him in the house of God. Young people remember places; seasoned people remember God Himself. In this same period the text slips in a bereavement: Deborah, Rebekah's nurse, died and was buried under an oak below Bethel, and that tree was called Allon-bacuth, meaning the oak of weeping. Deborah was no great figure; she was the one who had accompanied Rebekah from far away when she came to marry, the oldest servant of this household. Her death reminds us: on the road back to Bethel, even bidding farewell to the old is included. Restoration is never wiping out the past at one stroke; it is burying on one side and building the altar on the other; shedding tears for those who have gone on one side, and setting up the altar for those who remain on the other.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: Named Anew at Bethel — Not One Word of the Promise Missing (35:9-15)

Once the people had built the altar, God appeared. We must see this order clearly: it was not that God waited until Jacob was perfect in everything before coming; as soon as Jacob set out back to Bethel, God was waiting for him at Bethel. The text says that when Jacob came from Paddan-aram, God appeared unto him again and blessed him. That word "again" carries great weight. The first appearing at Bethel came when he was most destitute and helpless; this appearing came when he was most disgraced and ill-named. God's appearings both times chose the moment when a man could produce no report card. Brothers and sisters, we often think God speaks to us only when we are in good condition. The record of Genesis is precisely the opposite: the more a person has nowhere to go, the more clearly God speaks. God appeared not because Jacob deserved it, but because God remembered the words He Himself had spoken.

God first changed his name: "Thy name is Jacob: thy name shall not be called any more Jacob, but Israel shall be thy name." This name had in fact already been given once in Genesis chapter 32, after Jacob wrestled with God at Peniel. Why say it again at Bethel? Because receiving a name is not the same as living it out. For more than twenty years, how often did Jacob still live like Jacob: grasping, scheming, hiding, fearing. At Bethel God restated this name to lead him out of the old way of living once more: from now on, live as Israel, not as Jacob. Jacob means heel-grabber, the one who grasped his brother from birth and lived by his wits; Israel is the new identity God set for him, the one who strove with God and with men and prevailed. Receiving the name anew is not denying the past; it is God declaring: the past may no longer define you. For a person who returns to the place where he first met God, the greatest grace is not that his emotions are comforted, but that his identity is confirmed again. Who you are is not determined by that affair at Shechem, nor by the bad name people gave you, but by the name God gives you.

Then God introduced Himself, in what amounts to just a few words: I am God Almighty [4]. In the original it is 'el shaddai (אֵל שַׁדַּי, H7706). God had spoken this sentence to Abraham, and then it was covenant-making; God spoke this sentence to Jacob again, and now it is continuation. Three generations of people lie between, and God is still that same God. A man's household may fall into disorder, a man may grow old, his wife may pass away, his father may pass away, but the One who speaks to you is still God Almighty. His almightiness is not for displaying power; it falls immediately on the most practical things: be fruitful and multiply; a nation and a company of nations shall be of thee, and kings shall come out of thy loins; and the land which I gave Abraham and Isaac, to thee I will give it, and to thy seed after thee. Notice, not one word of the promise was deleted. A nation would come from him; kings would come from him; the land would be given to him and to his seed. The affair at Shechem did not make God withdraw the promise, old age did not make God discount the promise, and Jacob's weakness even less so. In that year at Bethel he had vowed: if God will be with me, then shall the LORD be my God. Now, in the same place, God told him: I heard that vow long ago, and what I will do is far greater than what you vowed. The vows people make often carry conditions; God's promise carries sovereignty. Only when you return to the place of the beginning do you discover that in that year you thought you were making a vow to God, but from beginning to end it was God keeping covenant with you.

Jacob's response was to do again, solemnly, what he had done more than twenty years before: in the place where God had appeared, he set up a pillar [3], poured a drink offering on it, and poured oil on it, and called the place Bethel. This act of setting up a pillar [3] — in Hebrew matzevah (מַצֵּבָה, H4676), a stone set upright — is a mark, a boundary stone, something that makes people and descendants remember when they see it. Setting up the pillar in his youth was astonishment: how dreadful is this place. Setting up the pillar this time was claiming: I receive this promise. Brothers and sisters, rebuilding the family altar requires visible marks like this. Set a time in the home when the whole family prays; in the car and at the dining table, put prayer back in its original place; let the children see with their own eyes that in trouble their parents pray first instead of quarreling first; let the home have a Bible worn from reading and a passage the whole family remembers. An altar is not finished in the day it is built; an altar needs someone adding wood to it every day. Jacob settling at Bethel, building the altar, and setting up the pillar was nailing his family's days back onto the word of God.

Notice also the test that follows immediately upon the giving of the name. The name had been changed, but the land was not yet in his hand; the promise had been spoken, but the kings had not yet been born. What God gave Jacob was never a check cashed on the spot; it was a road to be walked for a lifetime. The name Israel had to be lived out, day by day, in the days of sojourning ahead, the days of losing his wife, the days of strife among his sons. Brothers and sisters, the moving, the name, and the vision God gives you in worship all need to be walked out by your feet in ordinary days. Build the altar on the Lord's Day and settle down from Monday to Saturday — that alone is dwelling at Bethel. If the altar is built with great excitement but the days are still lived in the old way, that is not rebuilding; that is sightseeing. Jacob's difference this time was that he settled down, and after leaving Bethel, however great the tears he met, he never went back to Shechem again.

Add one more sentence here, for those who feel they "cannot go back anymore." Jacob returned to Bethel after more than twenty years, with deception, living under another's roof, fear of his brother, strife at home, and the blood of Shechem in between. He did not come back clean; he came back carrying a whole life of stories. But God's first sentence at Bethel was not settling accounts; it was renaming. It was not condemnation; it was blessing. On the road of return, God has never minded that you come back late; what He minds is that you refuse to arise. At the place where you first met God, God is still standing there. However long you have lost that first heart, God can count it clearly, and He can receive it back. Arise today. Do not wait until everything at home is smooth before coming back; it is precisely because home is not smooth that you must come back.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: On the Road to Ephrath — Tears and Promise Walk Together (35:16-29)

Leaving Bethel, the road ahead suddenly changed key. The text says they journeyed from Bethel, and there was still some distance to Ephrath when Rachel travailed, and she had hard labor. Ephrath is the later Bethlehem. On that road toward Bethlehem, the person Jacob loved most in his life fell. The midwife comforted her: Fear not; thou shalt have this son also. This sentence was true: the son was born safely. But for Rachel, it was also the last comfort she heard in this world. As her soul was departing, for she died, she called his name Ben-Oni [2]. Ben-Oni, ben-'oni (בֶּן־אוֹנִי, H1126), 'oni (H205) carries the sense of sorrow and pain — "son of my sorrow," "the son in my pain." The name a mother gave with her last strength is the summary of her life: she began by being loved at the well, went through rivalry with her sister, the shame of years without bearing, contending with her sister, contending through maidservants for sons, and at last died on the road of childbirth. Her name was Rachel, but the taste of her life was Ben-Oni.

But his father called him Benjamin [2]. Benjamin, binyamin (בִּנְיָמִן, H1144), yamin (יָמִין, H3225) is the right hand, so Benjamin is "son of the right hand." The right hand in the Bible is the place of strength, honor, and nearness. The same child: his mother saw him from within pain and called him son of sorrow; his father saw him from within the promise and called him son of the right hand. Both names are true: his birth truly carried his mother's death, and his existence truly was the warrant for this household to keep walking forward. Jacob did not erase Ben-Oni; he simply did not let Ben-Oni be the final naming. Brothers and sisters, this is exactly what faith does: it does not deny the tears, and it does not let the tears write the ending. When the pain of bereavement comes, a person is most tempted to rename his whole life Ben-Oni — from now on I am the one who lost, and my days are sorrow. Today, through Jacob's hand, God tells us: child, you may weep, you may remember, but God will still write your name forward. This child later became the ancestor of the tribe of Benjamin, from whose descendants came Ehud, came Saul, and came the apostle Paul, who called himself of the tribe of Benjamin. The son of the right hand was born from the arms of a mother who died on the road. The promise often comes into the world exactly like this: not bypassing tears, but passing through tears.

Jacob set up a pillar [3] on Rachel's grave, the pillar of Rachel's grave. Setting up a pillar appears twice in this chapter: the pillar at Bethel commemorates God's appearing; the pillar at the grave commemorates a person's departure. The two pillars, one before and one after, state the whole faith of this chapter clearly: those who believe in God set marks for grace, and also set marks for tears. We do not need to pretend at a grave that we are not sad. Many years later, shortly before his death, Jacob still mentioned this passage to Joseph: "when I came from Padan, Rachel died by me in the land of Canaan in the way, when yet there was but a little way to come unto Ephrath: and I buried her there" (Genesis 48:7, excerpt, KJV). Decades had passed, and he had not forgotten a single word. A person who holds fast to the promise is not a person without wounds; he is a person who walks on with wounds, remembering the grave. Later generations wrote the most moving words about Rachel's weeping: the prophet Jeremiah wrote: "A voice was heard in Ramah, lamentation, and bitter weeping; Rahel weeping for her children refused to be comforted" (Jeremiah 31:15, excerpt, KJV). A mother's tears can be recorded by God into the book of a prophet and kept for more than a thousand years. Your tears, God remembers with just such care.

Further down, with very restrained strokes, the text records a household disgrace: Israel journeyed on, and Reuben went and lay with Bilhah his father's concubine, and Israel heard of it. The Bible uses only this one sentence: no embellishment, no concealment, no expansion. The eldest son's stain is recorded just so, beside the genealogy. We should read this stroke slowly: returning to Bethel does not mean this household had no sin from then on; building the altar does not mean people will never fall again. The restoration is real, and human corruption is also real. Reuben later paid the price for this; on his deathbed Jacob said plainly that he was unstable as water and would not excel. God's record is this honest to give us no illusions about sin, and also to keep us from despairing over those who turn back: the altar is built, and the days must still be lived, day by day, in purity and self-keeping.

Immediately after comes the list of Jacob's twelve sons, set out in good order: the sons of Leah, the sons of Rachel, the sons of the two maidservants, not one missing, and Benjamin among them. This list is like a seal God set at the end of this chapter: however disordered and wounded this household was, of the twelve God had ordained, not one was lost. Finally, Jacob returned to Hebron, to Mamre, and saw his father Isaac. Isaac lived a hundred and eighty years; he was old and full of days, breathed his last, died, and was gathered to his people; and his two sons Esau and Jacob buried him in the cave of Machpelah — the cave where Abraham and his wife Sarah, and Isaac and his wife Rebekah, were buried, and where Leah also was buried (Gen 49:31). Jacob had seen off the nurse, seen off his wife, and now saw off his father. This chapter begins with building an altar and ends with a burial. But let us not misread its key: burial is not the final end. The fathers lay in the cave of Machpelah, and outside the cave stood the twelve sons — a household already renamed Israel. The promise was not buried with any one person; amid day after day of tears, the promise was handed forward by God's own hand.

Hosea chapter 11 is what the later prophet said when looking back over this history: God drew His people with cords of a man, with bands of love — looking back on the love of deliverance from Egypt and from the fathers onward. That cord, in Genesis chapter 35, was already tied to Jacob: drawing him away from Shechem, drawing him back to Bethel, drawing him past Rachel's grave, drawing him to his father's bedside. The hand with which God draws people sometimes feels like the strength that pulls us upward, and sometimes merely like the strength that keeps us from collapsing in tears. There is no miracle of resurrection in this chapter, no great water divided; there is only a household that did not fall apart in God's hand. That is already an immense grace. When we look back over our own lives many years from now, we will find that God's greatest work was often not taking the hardships away, but that, as hardships came one after another, this household was still there, this person was still there, and this promise was still there.

Let the application come down to earth in three most practical matters. First, returning to your first love must be done while it is still today. That first fervor between you and God will not grow back by itself; like Jacob, you must arise, walk back, and bury, one by one, the things that stand in between. Second, rebuilding the family altar must begin with the one who heads the home. Jacob did not tell his household to find its own way; he himself spoke first, confessed first, and told the whole family of God's faithfulness first. Today, the one who speaks first among parents and spouses is the one who builds the altar. Third, holding fast to the promise amid the pain of bereavement means allowing yourself to weep, while refusing to let pain rename you. A pillar may be set at the grave in remembrance, but the days must be lived forward as Benjamin: set food on the table for the living, pray for the children, and keep giving thanks for the portion God has left you. Tears and promise were never an either-or. The people of this chapter lifted up the child weeping, and they also built the altar weeping.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

In this chapter there are two place names, two pillars, and two names. Bethel reminds us where we began, and Ephrath reminds us where we are going; the pillar at Bethel commemorates God's appearing, and the pillar on Rachel's grave commemorates human tears; Ben-Oni speaks our pain, and Benjamin speaks God's promise. After Shechem, God did not give up on this household; He said: Arise, go up to Bethel. Be cleansed, rebuild the altar, receive the name anew, and then, between tears and promise, walk forward step by step — this is the road Genesis chapter 35 leaves to us.

Let us pray together: God Almighty, God of Bethel, thank You that at our most disgraced moment, the first words You spoke were not settling accounts, but leading us home. Give us courage to put away every foreign god among us, bury the old way of living, and rebuild the altar in our homes. Comfort those among us in the pain of bereavement: You remember Rachel's tears, and You remember theirs. Teach us not to deny Ben-Oni, yet to live out Benjamin; not to let pain write the ending, but to let Your promise write it. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] bet-'el and 'el bet-'el: Bethel, Hebrew בֵּית־אֵל (bet-'el, H1008), bet is "house" and 'el is "God," i.e., "the house of God"; in Genesis 35:7 Jacob called the place El-Bethel ('el bet-'el), i.e., "the God of Bethel," and in 35:15 it is again named Bethel; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "in his youth he remembered the place; later he remembered God Himself" is a homiletical exposition based on the comparison of the two namings, not the word meaning itself, and is kept distinct from the facts.

[2] ben-'oni and binyamin: Ben-Oni, Hebrew בֶּן־אוֹנִי (ben-'oni, H1126), ben is "son," and 'oni (אוֹנִי) is related to 'on (אוֹן, H205, sorrow, pain, vanity), i.e., "son of my sorrow"; Benjamin, Hebrew בִּנְיָמִן (binyamin, H1144), yamin (יָמִין, H3225) is "the right hand," i.e., "son of the right hand"; both appear in Genesis 35:18; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the mother saw him from within pain, the father saw him from within the promise" is an exposition based on the scriptural narrative.

[3] matzevah: pillar, Hebrew מַצֵּבָה (matzevah, H4676), a stone set upright, a pillar, a memorial stele; in Genesis 35:14 Jacob set up a pillar at Bethel and poured a drink offering and oil on it, and in 35:20 he set up a pillar on Rachel's grave as its marker; see also Genesis 28:18, where Jacob first set up a pillar at Bethel; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. "Setting marks for grace and setting marks for tears" is this manuscript's exposition.

[4] 'el shaddai: God Almighty, Hebrew אֵל שַׁדַּי ('el shaddai, H7706, shaddai is "the Almighty"); in Genesis 35:11 God revealed Himself to Jacob by this name; see Genesis 17:1, where God revealed Himself to Abraham by the same name in making the covenant; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. The exact etymology of shaddai is debated among scholars; this manuscript follows the customary sense "the Almighty" and does not expand on the etymological debate.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "Then God said to Jacob: Go up to Bethel and settle there, and build an altar there to God, who appeared to you when you were fleeing from your brother Esau." (Genesis 35:1, NIV) "Your name is Jacob, but you will no longer be called Jacob; your name will be Israel." (Genesis 35:10, NIV)

Jacob heard these words at his lowest point. The blood of Shechem had not yet dried and his bad name had already spread; he thought God had come to settle accounts, but God said: Go up to Bethel. Back at the place where he first met God, the first thing was not explanation but cleansing: hand over the foreign gods, bury them under the oak, change your garments, and build the altar again. Once a man returns to Bethel, God gives him back the name he lost: you will no longer be called Jacob; your name will be Israel. However long you have wandered, Bethel has waited for you just as long. Do not prepare your excuses today; set out first. Hand over the thing you have been clutching in place of God, bury it, and go back to the place where you first prayed. God does not mind that you come back late. He is already waiting there, and He will speak your name again with His own mouth. The one who returns to Bethel will still meet tears on the road — Rachel's grave lies just ahead; but a person who walks weeping is different from a person who walks aimlessly: every step he takes, he takes within the promise. Set up a pillar in your heart today, and remember how God led you back; tomorrow, carrying that mark, go on fulfilling your duty toward the living, and live the days out.

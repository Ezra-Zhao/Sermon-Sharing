## Summary (about 100 words)

After wrestling at the ford of the Jabbok, morning came. Jacob, limping, arranged his wives and children in groups front and back, went ahead of them all himself, and bowed seven times as he approached. Esau, however, ran to embrace him, kissed him, and wept with him. A twenty-year feud was not reasoned out first; there was an embrace first. Jacob said: I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God. A person who has been treated by grace can look at the face of an enemy and see the face of God.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

The end of the previous chapter was a man wrestling in the night. Jacob wrestled with God at the ford of the Jabbok until the hollow of his thigh was strained, and as day was breaking he walked out limping, yet bearing a new name on his face: Israel. The sun rose, and the Scripture says that as the sun rose upon him he passed over Penuel, limping on his thigh. The man was wounded, but the day had dawned.

What comes in this chapter, after daybreak, is the truly hard part. Wrestling with God in the night, one can grit one's teeth and hold on; when day comes, one must meet people — must meet, face to face, a brother not seen for twenty years, who twenty years ago declared he would kill him. That is the real test. Many people are like this: in prayer they are willing for anything, tears even flow as they kneel on the ground, but once they stand up and must go see that person, must go knock on that door, their hands go weak. In the end, faith always lands on one concrete human face. The face Jacob must land on today is named Esau.

Let us read today's Scripture together, reading several key passages:

"And Jacob lifted up his eyes, and looked, and, behold, Esau came, and with him four hundred men. And he divided the children unto Leah, and unto Rachel, and unto the two handmaids. And he put the handmaids and their children foremost, and Leah and her children after, and Rachel and Joseph hindermost. And he passed over before them, and bowed himself to the ground seven times, until he came near to his brother." (Genesis 33:1-3, excerpt, KJV)

"And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept." (Genesis 33:4, KJV)

"And Jacob said, Nay, I pray thee, if now I have found grace in thy sight, then receive my present at my hand: for therefore I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God, and thou wast pleased with me." (Genesis 33:10, excerpt, KJV)

"And Jacob came to Shalem, a city of Shechem, which is in the land of Canaan... and pitched his tent... And he bought a parcel of a field... And he erected there an altar, and called it El-elohe-Israel." (Genesis 33:18-20, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, thank You; You touched our wound in the night, and You will also lead us by day to go see the people we have avoided for so long. Give us the courage to walk at the very front, and also give us the humility to bow down. Those things we owe, those things we fear, those people we have circled around for twenty years — lead us today to walk straight through toward them. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Groups Arranged, Walking at the Very Front, Bowing Seven Times (33:1-4)

First look at Jacob's arrangement. He lifted up his eyes and saw Esau coming, with four hundred men behind him. What kind of number is four hundred men? In that year Abraham went to rescue Lot with three hundred and eighteen trained men born in his own house. Esau coming with four hundred men looked, in Jacob's eyes, not like a party coming to welcome relatives; it looked like an army. What had Esau said twenty years ago? The Bible remembers it clearly: Esau said, the days of mourning for my father are at hand; then will I slay my brother Jacob. Jacob remembered that sentence for twenty years. This is how people are: words that wound us, we remember especially well; words with which we wound others, we tend rather to forget.

What did Jacob do? He divided the children into several groups: the two handmaids and their children foremost, Leah and her children after, and Rachel and Joseph hindermost. Frankly, this arrangement was not entirely honorable. Those placed furthest back were the ones his heart cherished most; those placed furthest front, if something went wrong, would suffer first. This was still the Jacob who knew how to calculate; only the night before he had received a new name, and today in arranging the line the old habit remained: the most beloved, hidden at the very back. The Bible neither covers for him nor paints him better than he was. A person who has received grace does not have his old temperament removed overnight. God had changed his name, but had not yet finished changing all his reactions. As we look at him today, let us not laugh at him. When we arrange seats, arrange order, arrange who goes first to take the blow, the ledger in our hearts differs little from Jacob's ledger.

But the next stroke is the bravest stroke of Jacob's life: "And he passed over before them." Please hear this sentence with its full weight. What kind of person had Jacob been before? A person who grasped the heel from behind, a person who hid behind his mother's skirt waiting for arrangements, a person who sent messengers ahead to scout and gifts ahead to cool anger, while he himself shrank at the very back of the procession. Last night, in crossing the river, he had also sent his wives and children over first and remained behind alone. But today it was different. He arranged the whole family, and then walked ahead of them all himself.

This is the mark left by the ford of the Jabbok. The limping man walked at the very front. Brothers and sisters, the first step of reconciliation is never winning the argument; it is stepping out oneself first. When a family is in a cold war, who speaks first? When a husband and wife are frozen, who walks over first? When there is a grievance in the church, who reaches out a hand first? We always want to let someone else go first — let the children go first, let time go first, let the other side admit fault first, and then we will consider it. Jacob gives us today an example: the person who bears the most responsibility walks at the very front; the person who is most afraid walks at the very front; the person whose leg is least steady walks at the very front. Not because he is no longer afraid, but because last night he saw the face of God, and today he dares to go see the face of a man. The grace you received before God last night must be spent before people today; only then does it count.

Then he "bowed himself to the ground seven times, until he came near to his brother." Bowing seven times was the great courtesy of that time for audience with a king or salutation to an elder: one step, one bow; one bow, one step nearer. Jacob walked very slowly. Why slowly? First, his leg was lame and he could not hurry; second, he was slow on purpose: to give his brother time to see clearly that the person coming bore no weapon, only bowing; to give his brother time for his anger to cool, and to give himself time to bend his waist down again and again. The action of bowing is more honest than the mouth. Saying sorry with the mouth is easy; bending the waist is hard. When we apologize to people, we often stand perfectly straight, chin lifted: the words are "I'm sorry," but the posture is "I did nothing wrong." Jacob was not like that; seven times he brought his face close to the dust. Twenty years ago, the blessing he stole from his father began with an impersonation; twenty years later, he returned the stolen dignity, one bow at a time. Reconciliation sometimes does not require a long speech; it requires bending the waist down and lowering the head, so the other person can see with his own eyes: I have changed; I have not come to grab; I have come to own up.

Just as he had bowed the seventh time and had not yet fully drawn near, the Scripture presents the first surprise of the whole chapter: "And Esau ran to meet him." The one who ran was Esau. In the Bible, a dignified elder did not run; the robes were long, and running was unseemly. But Esau ran. Not only did he run, he "embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept." Embraced him, fell on his neck, kissed him, wept — the Bible uses four actions in one breath, without a single sentence of explanation. Esau did not first ask: Why did you deceive our father in that year? Why did you seize my birthright? Where have you hidden these twenty years? He asked nothing; he embraced first.

Brothers and sisters, please note this order: forgiveness before explanation, an embrace before settling accounts. Our usual order is the reverse: first explain clearly, explain until I am satisfied, and then I will consider whether to forgive you; first admit fault deeply enough, and then I will consider whether to be reconciled with you. The conditions are laid out in a long string, the other person can never reach them, and reconciliation waits forever. Esau today was not like that. He ran first, embraced first, wept first, and did not mention a single word of the account. It is not that the account no longer existed; it is that grace ran ahead of the account. Later, the father of the prodigal son was also like this: the son's prepared confession had not yet been recited, and the father had already run out, fallen on his neck, and kissed him again and again. The most beautiful reconciliations in the Bible all have one point in common: the one who runs ahead moves his feet first and his mouth afterward; he gives the embrace first and hears the explanation afterward. If today we truly want to be reconciled with someone, let us first learn this lesson: do not make the other person pass an examination in your long string of questions; first let him know that this door of yours is open.

There is one more detail that must not be missed: they wept — both of them. It was not only Jacob who wept; Esau wept too. Twenty years of feud, twenty years of longing, twenty years of grievance were all in this one weeping. Some wounds, in places language cannot reach, tears can reach. This is especially true of men: a lifetime of words blocked in the throat — one good cry is truer than a hundred sentences. God did not make this pair of brothers hold a seminar first; He let them weep first. Brothers and sisters, do not belittle reconciliation in tears. Some families do not lack arguments — everyone can lecture with arguments; what they lack is one person who runs over first, embraces once, and weeps once. Arguments can be spoken slowly afterward; an embrace cannot wait.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Children Greet in Turn, and a Dialogue of Pressing a Gift Back and Forth (33:5-15)

After they had wept, Esau lifted up his eyes, saw the women and the children, and asked: "Who are those with thee?" Jacob answered: "The children which God hath graciously given thy servant." This sentence Jacob spoke fittingly, and also truthfully. He did not say: this is the household I have built up in these years. He said: God has graciously given them. Before last night, the tone of Jacob's speech was "I earned it with my hands"; after one fall in wrestling, he learned to say "God graciously gave." The same thing, with one sentence changed, and the whole state of heart is different. Brothers and sisters, one of the marks of grace is that when a person introduces everything he has, he begins to put God in the subject position of the sentence.

Next the children came forward in turn to greet him. First the two handmaids and their children came near and bowed themselves; then Leah and her children came near and bowed themselves; last, Joseph and Rachel came near and bowed themselves. This order was still the order of the groups just arranged: group by group they walked before Esau and bent down in salutation. What does this scene resemble? A family introduction ceremony twenty years late. The children had probably heard this uncle's name, and the way they had heard it was mostly in lowered voices: that is the one, the one your father offended back then. Children repaying their parents' debts of human obligation is a constant pattern in many families: a knot tied by the adults, and the children standing in the middle with apologetic smiles. But today it was different, because the father walked at the very front; the children bowed behind him with firm ground under their hearts: Father has already gone up to meet him, Father has already embraced him; we have not come to be scolded, we have come to acknowledge kin. Parents: if you walk in front and break open the road of reconciliation, your children will not have to hide for you all their lives; if you hide behind, your children will have to carry it for you all their lives. Think this through clearly today.

After the greetings, we enter the most thought-provoking dialogue of the whole chapter: the gift.

Esau asked: "What meanest thou by all this drove which I met?" Jacob said: "These are to find grace in the sight of my lord." It turns out that those herds of livestock sent ahead in batches had an intention that the Scripture stated early: I will send these gifts ahead, and perhaps they will cool his anger, and afterward I will see his face; perhaps he will accept me. The gift was Jacob's insurance, the last line of defense in his calculations.

Esau's answer was unexpectedly open-hearted: "I have enough, my brother; keep that thou hast unto thyself." Pause on this "enough." Esau said, in settling accounts the Hebrew way: what I have is sufficient. Twenty years ago, over a bowl of red stew and a blessing, the two brothers had contended as if life and death depended on it; twenty years later, the elder brother said: I have enough. What did time do? What did God do? In these twenty years Esau had made his home in Mount Seir, married, had children, hunted and traded; his life had gone on, and had gone on not badly. A man who in that year felt the sky had collapsed, looking back now, found the sky had not collapsed. Brothers and sisters, many passes in our hearts that we could not get over — at the time we felt we could not live without that thing — twenty years later may not be so. Much of what we clutch and will not let go of is not because it is truly that important, but because we cannot swallow the grievance. Esau's "I have enough" was a belated letting go: I do not live by seizing your blessing; God has given me my own days too.

Jacob's reply went one step further. He said that God had dealt graciously with him, "and because I have enough" (Gen 33:11). Note that in the Hebrew the two men used different words: Esau said he had rav — much, abundance; Jacob said he had kol — all, everything. The KJV renders both brothers' words with the single word "enough," but the contrast in the original is plain: one said much, the other said all. This sentence Jacob had learned on the road all the way back from Bethel: God had promised, and he had vowed to give a tenth back; today he dared to say "I have all," not as boasting of wealth, but as acknowledging the Lord: everything I have has come from You, and taking out a portion to give my brother does not grieve me. Brothers and sisters, whether one is willing to give things away is the measure of whether there is an "enough" in a person's heart. A person whose heart always feels it is not enough grasps everything dead tight, and even reconciliation must first have its cost calculated; a person whose heart knows that everything he has is grace is willing to use gifts to pave the road of reconciliation.

Then Jacob spoke the most moving sentence of the whole chapter — indeed, of the whole Book of Genesis: "Nay, I pray thee, if now I have found grace in thy sight, then receive my present at my hand: for therefore I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God, and thou wast pleased with me."

"As though I had seen the face of God." Last night Jacob had named the place of wrestling Peniel, meaning the face of God, and said: I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved. In less than a day, on his brother's face, he said this sentence again: seeing your face is like seeing the face of God. This was not politeness, not flattery. This face of Esau, Jacob had feared for twenty years; he would wake with a start from dreams of it; but today on this face there was no knife, only tears and an embrace. In his brother's forgiveness, Jacob recognized the grace of God. For a man who only last night had been let go by God, today every person who lets him go bears the shadow of God on his face. This is what a person who has been treated by grace is like: his way of looking at people has changed. Before, when he looked at Esau, he saw a threat; now, when he looks at Esau, he sees the face of God. Brothers and sisters, when you can look at the face of a person who once wounded you, or whom you once wounded, and see the face of God, your wound is more than half healed. It is not that the person has become holy; it is that your eyes have been washed by grace.

Jacob also said: "and thou wast pleased with me." "Pleased with / accepted," in the original ratsah (רָצָה, H7521), to accept with pleasure, to receive gladly [2]. This word in the Bible is mostly used of God accepting a person or accepting a sacrifice. Jacob used it to describe his brother: you accepted me, as God accepts a person. Being accepted is the deepest longing in the human heart. What we fear is not only punishment; we fear not being accepted: afraid that the face at home, when we return, will be cold; afraid that the seat in church, when we walk in, will be chilly; afraid that after explaining for half a day the other person says one sentence — "a person like you." Esau today said nothing at all, and only used an embrace to tell his brother: this person that you are, I receive. Twenty years of wandering — this was exactly what Jacob wanted. Brothers and sisters, if the church cannot let people be accepted, people will go elsewhere to find acceptance. In dealing with people, let us give acceptance first, and talk slowly about right and wrong afterward; this is the order this chapter today gives the church.

The pressing back and forth ended with Esau receiving the gift. There is one more word worth noting here: Jacob had called this gift "my present," and at the end he changed his words and called it my blessing — "Take, I pray thee, my blessing that is brought to thee" (Gen 33:11). The original word is berakah, which is blessing. What he had stolen twenty years ago was precisely a blessing; twenty years later, with his own hands he delivered a blessing back into his brother's hands, and the thing returned had not even changed its name. What was stolen, he returned; what was seized, he made up for. Only then has repentance landed on the ground. A verbal admission of fault is not the end; the hands must move, the goods must be returned, and face must be laid down — when these three are complete, reconciliation is real.

Having received the gift, Esau proposed they travel together: "Let us take our journey, and let us go, and I will go before thee." He also offered to leave some people to accompany Jacob. Jacob declined them all. This declining we will examine carefully in Part Three. Here, first note the close of the dialogue: Esau asked, Jacob answered; the elder brother generous, the younger brother courteous. A twenty-year feud — spoken open, embraced, wept out, the gift given — and hearts drew near. Reconciliation is not the victory of a negotiation; it is two people no longer treating each other as enemies. Jacob and Esau's encounter is the shadow of every reconciliation that came after.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Declining Seir, Booths at Succoth, an Altar at Shechem (33:16-20)

Now look at Jacob's declining. Esau invited him to travel together to Seir; what did Jacob say? "My lord knoweth that the children are tender, and the flocks and herds with young are with me: and if men should overdrive them one day, all the flock will die. Let my lord, I pray thee, pass over before his servant: and I will lead on softly, according as the cattle that goeth before me and the children be able to endure, until I come unto my lord unto Seir."

These words were half reality and half wisdom. The reality was: the children were small, the ewes were nursing their young, and the company truly could not move fast. A company in reconciliation cannot move fast. A newly repaired relationship cannot withstand being driven. Many reconciliations are ruined precisely by driving: right after the apology, the other person is required at once to be as intimate as before; right after returning home, the whole family is required at once to turn the page. Jacob understood this principle: walk slowly, walk at the strength of the weakest one in front of you. Brothers and sisters, the stretch of road after a family reconciliation should be walked this way: do not drive the children, do not drive the elderly, do not drive the one most deeply wounded. The speed of the company is decided by the slowest, not by the fastest. Whoever overdrives them for one day may snap off this breath that has only just been reconnected.

The half that was wisdom was this: Jacob did not go with Esau to Seir. Esau's warmth was real, but two men who had not lived together for twenty years, forced together in one place to live, would not necessarily do well. Esau was a hunter, a man of Mount Seir; Jacob was a shepherd, a man who was to go toward the promised land. They had embraced, the gift had been received, the feud had been resolved; they did not have to be bound into one household. Reconciliation does not equal merging, and forgiveness does not equal having no boundaries from now on. Some relationships, after reconciliation, are in their best state by blessing each other, going their own ways, and still being able to sit down and share a meal at festivals. Forcing people to live together, rubbing against each other every day, would instead waste today's tears. Jacob gives us here a very mature sense of proportion: draw near in feeling, be clear in direction. With his mouth he promised, "until I come unto my lord unto Seir," yet the Scripture records that he turned and went to Succoth. Scholars hold different views on this sentence: some say it was Jacob's evasion, others say whether he later went to Seir the text does not state clearly. We need neither whitewash him nor hasten to condemn him. The text places its weight on the direction he actually walked: he did not go south to Seir; he went toward the promised land. The most important thing for a person after receiving grace is that the direction be right. Courteous words may be spoken; the road must not be walked wrong.

"So Esau returned that day on his way unto Seir. And Jacob journeyed to Succoth, and built him an house, and made booths for his cattle." Succoth, Sukkot (סֻכּוֹת, H5523), means booths, shelters [4]. This place-name later became a place-name indeed, and also became the name of a feast Israel kept generation after generation: the Feast of Booths. What Jacob did at Succoth was very plain: he built a house, set up several booths, settled the livestock, and passed the winter. A man who had wandered for twenty years built a house for himself for the first time. Brothers and sisters, the fruit of reconciliation is often not in great matters but in small matters like these: being able to build a house in peace of mind, being able to sleep a whole night through, being able to settle the livestock. Before reconciliation, a person is taut: the luggage is never unpacked, the shoes are never taken off; after reconciliation, he dares to pitch the booth and settle down to live his days. If you ask what use reconciliation is, Succoth is the answer: a person can finally stop, and live.

But Jacob did not live at Succoth for the rest of his life. He journeyed on, and "came to Shalem, a city of Shechem, which is in the land of Canaan" (Gen 33:18). The KJV reads Shalem as the name of the city; the Hebrew word shalem also carries the sense of wholeness — of arriving whole and at peace. When Jacob left Haran, at Bethel he had made a vow: "If God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat, and raiment to put on, so that I come again to my father's house in peace" (Gen 28:20-21). More than twenty years later, the text settles the account: he arrived at Shechem whole, his household complete and the feud with his brother closed — the "in peace" of that vow, answered. God had not forgotten that vow made on a pillow of stone. Brothers and sisters, the vows you made to God in hardship when you were young, the tears you shed, the resolves you set — God has forgotten none of them. His way of settling accounts may be slow, so slow you think He has forgotten; but when the day comes, stroke by stroke, He settles them all before your eyes.

Arriving at Shechem, Jacob did one thing: he bought a parcel of land east of the city. "And he bought a parcel of a field, where he had spread his tent, at the hand of the children of Hamor, Shechem's father, for an hundred pieces of money." Buying land — this was what Abraham had done: Abraham bought the cave of Machpelah at Hebron to bury Sarah. Isaac had not bought land; Jacob bought. What does buying land mean? It means he was certain that this land was the land God had promised to give to his descendants, and he first paid out a hundred pieces of money as earnest. The fugitive had returned, no longer a passer-by, but a man setting up his stake. When faith walks to the step of buying land, it has landed on the ground: it is not saying with the mouth, "I believe God's promise," but taking out the money, pitching the tent, and building the altar.

Finally, the text lands on building an altar: "And he erected there an altar, and called it El-elohe-Israel." El Elohe Yisra'el (אֵל אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל) means: God, the God of Israel [5]. Please read this altar's name slowly. Jacob's grandfather Abraham built altars and called on the name of the LORD; his father Isaac built altars, likewise. When it came to Jacob, in the name he gave the altar he placed his own new name in it for the first time: the God of Israel. The name received only last night was today carved on the altar. This is not self-praise; this is claiming: the God who appeared at Bethel, who cared for him in Haran, who wrestled with him at the ford of the Jabbok — from now on He is my God, the God of Israel, the God I have acknowledged with my own person. When faith is passed to the third generation, it most easily becomes the faith of ancestors, the faith of parents, a faith heard from others. At Shechem Jacob completed this step: God was no longer only the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac; He was also the God of me, Jacob, of me, Israel, who built an altar with my own hands. Brothers and sisters, to which generation has your faith come? Have you built your own altar? At your own Shechem, have you called Him your God with your own mouth?

From the Jabbok to Shechem, Jacob's road is walked to its end, and looking back we discover that this chapter is quiet: there is no direct speech of God, no appearance of angels, no dream. There is only arranging groups, bowing, embracing, pressing a gift back and forth, pitching booths, buying land, and building an altar. Where is God? God is in the footsteps of Esau running over, in the tears of the two brothers, in the land bought for a hundred pieces of money, and in the name of that altar. Faith after daybreak looks like this: not in wonders, but in ordinary days; not at the ford, but at the dinner table, on the road, at home. Meeting God in the night is the beginning of grace; living out reconciliation by day is the completion of grace.

As for application, I will say only three concrete things. First: if there is a person in the family who has not spoken for twenty years, walk at the very front today. Do not wait for the other side, do not wait for the New Year, do not wait for a suitable occasion. Make that call first, write those few words first, say that sentence first: All these years, I was the one at fault. If it cannot come out of your mouth, learn from Jacob: first send a small gift, and let a thing knock on the door for you. Second: with people in the company or in the church with whom there is a grievance, stop passing messages through intermediaries. Passing messages cannot pass out a reconciliation; it can only pass out more suspicion. Go yourself, walk slowly, and have the heart of bowing seven times: do not drive, do not force, do not require the other person to declare their position on the spot. Third: if in your heart you have always remembered a sentence that person said in that year, as Jacob remembered "I will slay thee" for twenty years, today ask God to give you Esau's sentence: "I have enough." Enough means: I no longer live by holding grudges; God has given my days other things. Letting go does not let the other person off cheaply; it lets yourself off. 

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked a road after daybreak to its end: a limping man walking at the very front of his whole family, bowing seven times; an elder brother who had waited twenty years running over, embracing first and speaking afterward; a gift pressed back and forth that in the end was renamed a blessing; a house, several booths, a parcel of bought land, and an altar named "God, the God of Israel." This is what reconciliation looks like: it turns out to look like this — there was no reasoning first; there was an embrace first. No account was settled clear first; the face of God was seen first.

A person who has been treated by grace looks at the face of an enemy as though he had seen the face of God. This is not Jacob's lesson alone; it is the lesson of every one of us. The Lord Jesus taught us to pray: forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. He also said: before you offer your gift at the altar, first be reconciled to your brother. The order is always like this: reconciliation first, then sacrifice; an embrace first, then explanation; bowing down first, then opening the mouth.

Let us pray together: Lord, at the ford of the Jabbok You did not let Jacob go, and today You do not let us go either. Touch that limping place in our hearts, and make us dare to walk at the very front, dare to bow down, dare to reach out a hand first. Heal the sentences we have remembered for twenty years, and loosen the hands we have clutched for twenty years. Let us, on every face we have feared to see, see Your face again; and at our own Shechem, build an altar and say with our own mouths: You are my God. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] panim: "face," Hebrew פָּנִים (H6440), face, presence, countenance; Genesis 33:10 Jacob said "I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God," and "face" uses this word; see Genesis 32:30, where Jacob named the place of wrestling Peniel (the face of God), from the same root, as the scriptural basis for this manuscript's "recognizing the face of God on his brother's face"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "a person who has been treated by grace looks at the face of an enemy as though he had seen the face of God" is a homiletical exposition based on this word's correspondence before and after, and fact and exposition are kept distinct.

[2] ratsah: to accept with pleasure, to receive gladly, Hebrew רָצָה (H7521); Genesis 33:10 Jacob said "thou wast pleased with me," where "pleased" renders this word, whose basic sense is to accept with pleasure, to receive willingly; in the Old Testament this word is mostly used of God accepting a person or a sacrifice (e.g., Leviticus 1:4; Psalm 19:14); the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT.

[3] chen: grace, favor, Hebrew חֵן (H2580); Genesis 33:8 Esau asked the meaning of the gift and Jacob answered "to find grace in thy sight"; 33:10 "if now I have found grace in thy sight"; 33:15, where Jacob answers Esau's offer of an escort — "let me find grace in the sight of my lord" (Jacob's words, not Esau's) — all use this word, containing the sense of finding grace and being regarded with favor in the eyes of a superior; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[4] sukkot: Succoth, Hebrew סֻכּוֹת (H5523), the plural form of booths, shelters, also used as a place-name; Genesis 33:17 Jacob "made booths for his cattle," and the place was therefore named Succoth; the later Feast of Booths (Sukkot) shares this origin; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note.

[5] 'el 'elohe yisra'el: El-elohe-Israel, Hebrew אֵל אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, the name of the altar Jacob erected at Shechem in Genesis 33:20, translated literally "God, the God of Israel"; El is a name of God, Elohe means "the God of...," and Yisra'el is the new name Jacob received in Genesis 32:28; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT; this is a brief note on the altar's name.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "And he passed over before them, and bowed himself to the ground seven times, until he came near to his brother. And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept." (Genesis 33:3-4, KJV) "For therefore I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God, and thou wast pleased with me." (Genesis 33:10, KJV)

When morning came, the real test began. Jacob had wrestled with God in the night and received a new name, but by day he still had to face the brother he had avoided for twenty years. He did not send his family ahead; he went first himself, limping, bowing seven times. Esau ran to him, embraced him, kissed him, and wept, without settling a single account first. That is what reconciliation looks like: forgiveness before explanation, an embrace before arguments. We always want the other person to admit fault first and everything to be explained first before we reach out; God asks us to walk first, bow first, speak first. If there is a name you have been avoiding in your heart, stop avoiding it today. Make that call first, say those words first. A person who has been treated by grace can look at a once-feared face and see the face of God. Your Jabbok is behind you; it is time to walk to your Shechem: go slowly, do not drive the flock, be reconciled first, and then build the altar.

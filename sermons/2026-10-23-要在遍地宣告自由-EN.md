# Proclaim Liberty Throughout All the Land — A Sermon on Leviticus 25

- Text: Leviticus 25:1-55
- Author: Written by Guangyi Zhao from the biblical text and commentaries
- Status: Draft — awaiting cross-review (Sermon production batch 3, 2026-10-23; unpublished)
- English Bible base: King James Version (KJV). All Scripture quotations in this manuscript are embedded KJV text, checked verse by verse against the KJV of Leviticus 25; nothing is back-translated from the Chinese.
- Chinese original: Union Version, New Punctuation edition; quotations in the Chinese manuscript were checked verse by verse against that edition as carried on wd.bible, with no reverential substitutions inside quotations.
- Note on the title: The title is drawn from the KJV wording of Leviticus 25:10 itself, "proclaim liberty throughout all the land"; the Chinese title 要在遍地宣告自由 is likewise condensed from the Chinese base text at 25:10, 「在遍地给一切的居民宣告自由」, whose actual words "遍地" and "宣告自由" it preserves. The title is not built on any paraphrase from secondary retelling.
- Conventions: (Fact) marks the text and checkable facts; (Application) marks the preacher's exposition; (Summary) marks content paraphrased rather than quoted; (New Testament reading) marks the New Testament layer of reading; (Scholarly and historical discussion) marks questions on which scholarship and history remain unsettled. Fact and application are labelled separately throughout.

## Abstract

Leviticus 25 is the Old Testament's great charter of land and liberty. It enlarges the time-logic of the Sabbath to land and years: six years of sowing, and in the seventh year the land keeps a sabbath; count seven sabbaths of years, forty-nine years, and in the fiftieth year, on the Day of Atonement, the trumpet sounds, liberty is proclaimed throughout the land to all its inhabitants, and every man returns to his possession and to his family. This sermon does not expound every redemption price in detail; it follows the chapter's structure and theological thread. The land is the LORD's, and the people are the LORD's. What is sold in a land sale is only a number of harvest years, priced by the years remaining until the jubilee. The nearest kinsman is the redeemer. A poor brother may not be charged usury and may not be sold as a bondman; when the jubilee comes, land and man are released together. Rest and liberty are two policies of one and the same Owner.

## Sermon

### I. 00:00-08:00 Introduction: given on Mount Sinai — the Sabbath enlarged from the day to the land and the year

(Fact) The chapter opens by naming the place of its giving: "And the LORD spake unto Moses in mount Sinai, saying, Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, When ye come into the land which I give you, then shall the land keep a sabbath unto the LORD." (Lev 25:1-2, KJV) The location deserves notice: most of Leviticus is tied to the tabernacle and the altar, but this chapter is expressly said to have been given on Mount Sinai. The tense and the addressee deserve notice too: the people do not yet hold Canaan, the land is not yet in hand, yet the law legislates in advance for a land not yet possessed — and the subject of the first sentence is the land itself: the land shall keep a sabbath unto the LORD. It is not only the people who rest.

(Fact) Read this chapter alongside the one before it and the enlargement comes into focus: the festival calendar of chapter 23 takes the weekly Sabbath as its skeleton and enlarges rest from a day to the feasts; this chapter goes one step further and enlarges the same logic to the year and the land — the seventh year is the land's whole sabbath year, and beyond seven sabbath years stands the fiftieth year of jubilee. (Application) In Scripture the Sabbath never comes in only one size: it is first the rhythm of days, then the rhythm of years, and finally the rhythm of an entire social and economic order. A man who will rest his calendar but will not let his land and his debts rest has learned only half the Sabbath. This chapter preaches the enlarged half: the land must rest, the debtor must be released, the debt must stop, the servant must go home.

(Fact) The chapter's theological thesis is stated early, at verse 23, and this sermon nails it down before any detail: "The land shall not be sold for ever: for the land is mine, for ye are strangers and sojourners with me." (Lev 25:23, KJV) That sentence is the foundation under every rule of redemption, pricing, and release that follows: the land has a true Owner, and Israel entire — the landholder included — stands before that Owner only as strangers and sojourners. (Application) A sojourner's mind and a proprietor's mind handle property in opposite ways: the proprietor asks how to grip his holding tighter and bequeath it longer; the sojourner asks how to use it by the Owner's rules and hand it back when the term runs out. Every seemingly fussy statute later in the chapter grows out of this one sentence: since the land is not yours, what you sell can only be years; since the man was brought out of Egypt by God, what you buy can only be his labour, never the man himself.

(Fact) Structurally the chapter falls into four clear blocks: the sabbath year (vv. 1-7), the jubilee (vv. 8-17), the redemption of land and the rules of property (vv. 18-34, within which vv. 18-22, the promise to those who keep the statutes, are treated at the head of section IV of this sermon), and the rules for the poor brother (vv. 35-55). One hidden thread runs through all four: every block returns to the same ground — the land is Mine, and the people are My servants. (Application) Leave that thread and the sabbath year shrinks into a technique of fallow farming, the jubilee into utopian nostalgia, and redemption into a curiosity of ancient civil law. Hold the thread, and the four blocks are one whole: a property law written for sojourners.

### II. 08:00-24:00 The sabbath year: the land keeps its sabbath, and all eat together

(Fact) The sabbath-year legislation begins from the norm of cultivation: "Six years thou shalt sow thy field, and six years thou shalt prune thy vineyard, and gather in the fruit thereof; But in the seventh year shall be a sabbath of rest unto the land, a sabbath for the LORD: thou shalt neither sow thy field, nor prune thy vineyard." (Lev 25:3-4, KJV) Mark the wording of verse 4: it does not say that the people rest in the seventh year; it says the land has a sabbath of rest. Behind the KJV's "sabbath of rest" stands šabbāṯôn (Strong's H7677), the intensive form of the sabbath word, denoting complete, solemn cessation; verse 5 closes the unit with the same word, calling the year a year of rest for the land. Here the land is no passive means of production; it is itself a keeper of the sabbath — it too has a feast to keep unto the LORD.

(Fact) The concrete rules of the sabbath year come in two layers (v. 5): "That which groweth of its own accord of thy harvest thou shalt not reap, neither gather the grapes of thy vine undressed: for it is a year of rest unto the land." (Lev 25:5, KJV) Beyond neither sowing nor pruning, even the self-grown crop may not be harvested in the ordinary way, nor the grapes of the undressed vine gathered. In other words, what stops in the sabbath year is not labour only but the right to reap the land clean: what the land brings forth of itself is no longer the householder's private store. (Application) This is the layer most easily skipped, and it is where the chapter cuts deepest. To stop work for a year is to lose labour; to surrender the clean reaping is to surrender the consciousness of ownership. The first is an adjustment of schedule; the second is surgery of the heart. Israel must watch grain rise in the field and either fall back or be eaten by others, and learn the lesson: the land is not my cash machine; the land has its own sabbath to keep unto God, and my title deed must make way for that sabbath.

(Fact) What, then, is eaten in the sabbath year? The text's answer is not a storage scheme but a shared table: "And the sabbath of the land shall be meat for you; for thee, and for thy servant, and for thy maid, and for thy hired servant, and for thy stranger that sojourneth with thee. And for thy cattle, and for the beast that are in thy land, shall all the increase thereof be meat." (Lev 25:6-7, KJV) The list is ordered with care: the master, the manservant, the maid, the hired servant, the sojourning stranger, and then the cattle and the beasts of the land — from inside the house to outside it, from man to beast, all eating of what the one land yields of itself. On ordinary days master and servant are distinguished; in the sabbath year they sit at one table. On ordinary days the wild beast is the field's pest; in the sabbath year it is on the list too. (Application) Here rest shows its social shape: true rest is not the holiday of the propertied household alone, but the provisioning of the whole circle of life at once. The land rests a year, and no member of the circle — not the marginal sojourner, not the voiceless beast — is cut off from supply. A community that keeps the sabbath year must first learn rest in its distribution: grain is divided no longer by title but by shared presence.

(Fact) In form, the sabbath-year legislation belongs to the same class as the firstfruits command of chapter 23: both are laws paid in advance, effective only "when ye come into the land which I give you" (v. 2). At the moment of giving, the people are still below Sinai, far from entering Canaan. (Application) The sabbath year is therefore first a lesson in faith and only then an agricultural institution: before there is land, learn the land's rules; before there is a harvest, confess the harvest's Owner. Reverse the order — wait until the land and the yield are in hand — and a man will mostly say: this land I opened, this grain I sowed; why should it rest? The text sets the rules before the possessing precisely to block that "why." Covenant first, inheritance after: that is Leviticus's recurring order, and this chapter applies it to the land.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) How far the sabbath year was ever actually observed in Israel's history is a question to which neither the text nor scholarship gives a clean answer: no Old Testament narrative states in so many words that in such-and-such a year the whole territory kept a sabbath year, while Leviticus 26 interprets the desolate land of the exile as the land "enjoying her sabbaths" (Lev 26:34-35, Summary), a reading taken up in 2 Chronicles 36:21 (Summary). Milgrom and Hartley both discuss the observance-history of the sabbath year and the jubilee in their commentaries, and their conclusions are mostly cautious and unresolved. This sermon sets text and discussion side by side and does not claim a golden age of complete observance; to preach ideal law as accomplished fact would go beyond what this text can bear.

### III. 24:00-42:00 The jubilee: the trumpet on the Day of Atonement, liberty proclaimed throughout the land

(Fact) The reckoning of the jubilee is stacked up from the sabbath years: "And thou shalt number seven sabbaths of years unto thee, seven times seven years; and the space of the seven sabbaths of years shall be unto thee forty and nine years." (Lev 25:8, KJV) Seven sevens, forty-nine — the method of counting belongs to the same tradition as the counting from the sheaf to the fiftieth day in chapter 23: a holy year is not printed on the calendar; it is counted out. The fifty days' counting ends at the harvest; the seven-times-seven years' counting ends at release. The one is measured in days, the other in generations: a person meets a jubilee at most once or twice in a lifetime, and so every counting carries the weight of hope.

(Fact) The opening moment of the jubilee is fixed with precision: "Then shalt thou cause the trumpet of the jubile to sound on the tenth day of the seventh month, in the day of atonement shall ye make the trumpet sound throughout all your land." (Lev 25:9, KJV) The "trumpet" here is the šôpār (Strong's H7782), an animal's horn used as a trumpet, most often a ram's horn; BDB gives horn and trumpet-horn as its core senses. That the opening day is chosen to be the Day of Atonement is no accident: atonement first, release after; the account of sin is settled before God, and only then are the accounts of land and persons settled throughout the land. (Application) Reverse the order — release the debts and return the land first, and speak of atonement afterwards — and release decays into economic technique. By tying the trumpet to the Day of Atonement, the text declares that every social release begins at the altar. Liberty without atonement is cheap; atonement without liberty hangs in the air. On the tenth day of the seventh month, both happen in a single blast of the horn.

(Fact) What the trumpet proclaims is the chapter's title sentence: "And ye shall hallow the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof: it shall be a jubile unto you; and ye shall return every man unto his possession, and ye shall return every man unto his family." (Lev 25:10, KJV) Three words must be handled by the original text. First, "liberty," Hebrew dərôr (Strong's H1865), freedom, release, free outflow; Levine, in the JPS commentary, notes that the word belongs to the same tradition as the release edicts promulgated by kings in the ancient Near East — its core is not a feeling of relief but the legal restoration of status and property. Second, "jubile," Hebrew yôḇēl (Strong's H3104), whose base sense is bound up with the ram and the ram's horn: the year is named for the blast that opens it — a year named by a sound, and the sound carried by the year. Third, "possession," Hebrew ʾăḥuzzāh (Strong's H272), the landed holding, the ancestral allotment each household received by tribe.

(Fact) The observance of the jubilee itself follows the sabbath-year pattern (vv. 11-13, Summary): in the fiftieth year there is no sowing, the self-grown crop is not reaped, and the grapes of the undressed vine are not gathered, for it is the jubilee and is holy; the people eat the increase of the field as it comes; and in that year every man returns to his possession. The jubilee, that is, is first of all still a sabbath year — one year added on top of seven sabbath years — and only then is release laid over the rest: return to possession, return to family. (Application) The layering is deliberate: liberty is not a new regime started from scratch; it is the fruit that rest grows into. A society that cannot rest will not release either; only a society willing to let the land rest and the debts stop is qualified to talk about sending men home. When we speak of liberty today we usually mean more choices and fewer constraints; the liberty of Leviticus is three concrete things — land to return to, a family to return to, and debts that can be cleared. Liberty that cannot land on those three is only a slogan.

(Fact) The jubilee's economic rule follows at once (vv. 14-17): in buying and selling land the parties shall not oppress one another; the buyer buys according to the number of years after the jubilee, and the seller sells according to the number of years of crops; the more years, the higher the price, the fewer years, the lower the price, because what is sold is a number of harvests (Summary). The verb behind "oppress" is the causative stem of yānāh (Strong's H3238), to oppress, to maltreat, to take unfair advantage; BDB lists Leviticus 25:14 and 25:17 expressly among its occurrences, where it denotes the strong taking advantage of the weak in a transaction. (Application) This paragraph is the most "modern" in the chapter: it dismantles the truth about land sales in public — what you buy is never the land; you buy the harvest years remaining until the next jubilee. Since the land cannot be sold for ever, every "land price" among men is only a rent; the nearer the jubilee, the lower the rent, because the years remaining are fewer. Think that through, and greed loses one of its hiding places: no one can hoard land into a perpetual family fortune, for every fifty years the board is reset. The jubilee is not charity law; it is property law. It does not rest on the kindness of the rich; it rests on the reset built into the system.

(Fact) The jubilee has its own rule for eating, and the text states it again: "For it is the jubile; it shall be holy unto you: ye shall eat the increase thereof out of the field." (Lev 25:12, KJV) "Ye shall eat the increase thereof out of the field" answers the shared table of verse 6 in the sabbath-year section: no cultivation, no reaping crews, no storehouses being filled — a man walks into the field and eats what he finds, as much as there is. The jubilee's table stands in the open air. (Application) For people who live in the age of warehousing and logistics this is a strange picture: security lies not in the storehouse but in the hand of the Lord who blesses. The sixth year's three years of produce (v. 21) already supply the ground of confidence, and the jubilee goes one step further: even what grows of itself is enough. Step by step the text dismantles man's reliance on stockpiles and trains Israel into a people that can wait. A man who cannot wait cannot keep a sabbath; a society that cannot wait cannot hold a jubilee.

(Scholarly and historical discussion) The numbering of the jubilee carries a long-standing dispute: is the fiftieth year a separate year following immediately upon the forty-ninth — the seventh sabbath year — or does it coincide with the forty-ninth year, "the fiftieth" being only a manner of counting? On the separate-year reading, the forty-ninth and fiftieth years are two fallow years in a row, an enormous pressure on subsistence — which is precisely why scholars have defended both readings; Milgrom argues for the fiftieth year as a distinct year, while others argue for the coincident reading. This sermon sets the two side by side and decides neither; its statement that the counting runs "from forty-nine years to the fiftieth year" follows only the counting order of verses 8-10 themselves and does not presuppose an answer to the dispute. Likewise, whether the jubilee was ever observed in full in history has no narrative attestation in the Old Testament; that discussion was set out in section II and is not rehearsed again here.

### IV. 42:00-56:00 Redemption and the poor: the land may be redeemed, and a man may not be made a slave

(Fact) The fourth block first sets verses 18-22, the promise passage, in place: "Wherefore ye shall do my statutes, and keep my judgments, and do them; and ye shall dwell in the land in safety." (Lev 25:18, KJV) The text then rehearses in advance the question in the people's hearts: in the seventh year we neither sow nor gather — what shall we eat? The answer: "Then I will command my blessing upon you in the sixth year, and it shall bring forth fruit for three years." (Lev 25:21, KJV) In the eighth year they sow and eat of the old store until the produce of the ninth year comes in (Summary). (Application) To place this passage before the detailed rules of redemption is itself an act of interpretation: settle the fear of "will there be enough to eat?" first, and only then talk about the ethics of "will you let go?" The sabbath year and the jubilee are hard to keep, and the difficulty was never technical; it is fear — fear that one year's rest breaks the food supply, fear that a released debt never comes back. The text does not dodge the fear; it answers it head-on: the sixth year's produce covers three years. The arithmetic of faith differs from the arithmetic of fear: fear counts the stockpile; faith counts the Lord who blesses.

(Fact) The rules of redemption open with a general principle: "And in all the land of your possession ye shall grant a redemption for the land." (Lev 25:24, KJV) Then comes the order of redemption (vv. 25-28, Summary): if a brother has grown poor and sold part of his possession, his nearest kinsman shall come and redeem what his brother sold; if there is no redeemer, and the man himself has prospered and is able to redeem it, he shall count the years of the sale and restore the price of the remaining years to the buyer, and return to his possession; if he cannot redeem it at all, what was sold remains in the buyer's hand until the jubilee, and in the jubilee it goes out and the man returns to his possession. The Hebrew behind "redeem" and "redeemer" is the verb gāʾal (Strong's H1350) and its participle gōʾēl, denoting the nearest kinsman who bears the family obligation to buy back property and persons. (Application) The institution of the gōʾēl writes mercy into kinship duty: rescuing the poor is not an optional act of charity but the charge of the nearest relative — the closer a man stands, the sooner the duty of redemption lands on his shoulders. And the jubilee is the final safety net: if kin cannot redeem and the man cannot redeem himself, when the term arrives God Himself redeems the land back by means of the calendar.

(Fact) The rules for houses come in three grades (vv. 29-34, Summary): a man who sells a dwelling house in a walled city may redeem it within a whole year after it is sold; if it is not redeemed within the full year, the house in the walled city is established for ever to the buyer throughout his generations and does not go out in the jubilee. But the houses of villages with no wall round about are counted as the fields of the country: they may be redeemed, and they go out in the jubilee. The houses in the cities of the Levites may be redeemed at any time; if not redeemed, they go out in the jubilee, for the houses of the cities of the Levites are their possession among the children of Israel; and the field of the suburbs of their cities may not be sold, for it is their perpetual possession. (Application) The three grades draw the line between "dwelling" and "livelihood": the walled-city house has been absorbed into the urban economy and may be sold for ever; the village house stands together with its fields and is livelihood itself, and may not be severed for ever; the Levites received no tribal allotment of land, so their city houses are their whole possession, and accordingly their right of redemption is the widest and their suburbs may never be sold. The system here is not one-size-fits-all; it protects by the weight of livelihood — the nearer a thing lies to the root of a man's living, the less it may be cut off for good.

(Fact) The rules for the poor brother begin with support (vv. 35-37): if your brother has grown poor and his means fail with you, you shall relieve him, so that he may live with you like a stranger and a sojourner (Summary). The red line on lending is drawn with great concreteness: "Take thou no usury of him, or increase: but fear thy God; that thy brother may live with thee. Thou shalt not give him thy money upon usury, nor lend him thy victuals for increase." (Lev 25:36-37, KJV) Two words must be handled by the original text: "usury" is nešekh (Strong's H5392), derived from the verb "to bite" — interest is named by the text as a wound bitten out; "increase" is tarbîṯ (Strong's H8636), the increment charged over and above the principal. Verses 36 and 37 use the two words together, covering money and food alike, and block every loophole of renamed interest. (Application) Mark the purpose clause of this law: not "that your money may make money," but "that thy brother may live with thee." In Scripture, lending is first an art of living together, not a tool for breeding profit; to take interest from a poor brother is to make his poverty into your business. The name the text gives that business is unsparing: a bite. The man who bites his brother bites at the heart of the Lord who said, "that he may live with thee."

(Fact) The lowest depth of a brother's poverty is the selling of himself (vv. 39-46): "And if thy brother that dwelleth by thee be waxen poor, and be sold unto thee; thou shalt not compel him to serve as a bondservant:" (Lev 25:39, KJV) He shall be with the householder as a hired servant and as a sojourner, and serve until the year of jubilee; in the jubilee he departs with his children, returns to his own family, and to the possession of his fathers (Summary). The ground is stated in verse 42: "For they are my servants, which I brought forth out of the land of Egypt: they shall not be sold as bondmen." (Lev 25:42, KJV) The word "servants" is ʿeḇeḏ (Strong's H5650), the Old Testament's commonest word for servant and slave; the text's point is that Israel's ʿeḇeḏ-status has already been claimed by God — they are already His servants, and therefore cannot be resold as any man's bondmen. The master shall not rule over him with rigour, but shall fear God (Summary). (Fact) The second half of this passage, the provisions for bondmen and bondmaids bought from the nations round about and from the families of sojourners, held and inherited as a possession (vv. 44-46, Summary), is stated in this sermon at the (Fact) level exactly as it stands: the text expressly permits the holding and bequeathing of foreign bond-servants alongside the release rules for Israelite brothers, and the boundary between the two is drawn plainly in the text itself. This sermon neither whitewashes that coexistence nor rewrites the text by later standards; it notes only that the ground of release given within this text — "which I brought forth out of the land of Egypt" — runs, within the scope of this text, first to the Israelite brother, and that how the later church extended that ground to all men belongs to the (New Testament reading) layer and to church history, and is not read back into this passage.

(Fact) The last case is the brother who has sold himself to a sojourner who has grown rich (vv. 47-55, Summary): after he is sold, he may be redeemed; one of his brethren, his uncle, his uncle's son, or any near kinsman of his family may redeem him, or he may redeem himself if he is able; the price of his redemption is reckoned with the buyer from the year he was sold to the year of jubilee, according to the number of years, at the rate of a hired servant's time; if many years remain, he repays in proportion, and if few years remain, in proportion; he shall be with the buyer as a yearly hired servant, and the buyer shall not rule with rigour over him in your sight; and if he is not redeemed by these means, he goes out in the year of jubilee, he and his children with him. (Application) Note the basis on which the redemption price is calculated: not the price of a body, but the wages of labour — this man was never priced merchandise; he is a worker whose years are reckoned as hired time. Even in the most extreme case, the law keeps this line in the ledger: an Israelite may sell his labour; he may not sell himself. He may owe a debt; he may not owe his life. The more finely the account is kept, the more steadily the man is kept.

### V. 58:00-60:00 Conclusion: unto Me the children of Israel are servants

(Fact) The chapter's last sentence is its seal: "For unto me the children of Israel are servants; they are my servants whom I brought forth out of the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God." (Lev 25:55, KJV) The word "servants" is again ʿeḇeḏ (H5650); the sentence answers verse 42 across the chapter, just as verse 23 declares that the land is Mine. Two declarations frame the whole: the land may not be sold for ever, for the land is Mine; a man may not be sold for ever, for the man is My servant. Land and persons — the two assets Israel valued most — are both registered in the books of one and the same Owner. (Application) Let this sermon gather the chapter into three sentences. First, the land is the LORD's: we are strangers and sojourners, every holding is a tenancy, and every price is reckoned in years only. Second, rest is the root of release: from the day to the year, and from the year to the fiftieth year, a society that will not stop will not set free, and a land that is never rested grows no liberty. Third, liberty is God's own property policy: redemption has its order, usury has its red line, self-sale has its term, and the jubilee has its trumpet — liberty is not the absence of a master but a change of masters; Israel is free precisely because Israel is God's servant.

(New Testament reading) The New Testament carries one loud echo of this chapter, labelled as throughout as a layer of reading and not read back into the text: in Luke 4, Jesus in the synagogue at Nazareth unrolls the scroll of Isaiah and reads the passage of the captives' release, liberty for the bruised, and the acceptable year of the Lord (Luke 4:18-19, citing Isa 61:1-2, Summary) — "To preach the acceptable year of the Lord." (Luke 4:19, KJV) The "liberty" proclaimed in Isaiah 61 is, in the Hebrew, this chapter's very word dərôr (H1865), rendered aphesis in the Septuagint, the word-stream Luke's Greek continues. This sermon labels the layer explicitly as a New Testament reading: it is the church's faithful interpretation from the apostolic age onward, with its own New Testament textual basis; but Leviticus 25 is first of all itself — a sabbath year, a jubilee, redemption prices and hired wages, and the duty of the nearest kinsman — and it must not be flattened into the mere shadow of a type. To let the New Testament reading light up this text, and to let this text hold the reading steady: both must be done.

(Application) One last word on the temper required to preach this chapter. The least ambiguous thing about Leviticus 25 is its bookkeeping: forty-nine years, the fiftieth year, the limit of one full year, prices increased by the years and diminished by the years — the text reckons to the last fraction. Whoever preaches this chapter must first be trained by that bookkeeping: quotations according to the base text, years according to the text, disputes set side by side as they stand, and the foreign-bondmen provisions stated as they stand, unwhitewashed. And may those who hear this chapter begin from its smallest account: is there in my hand a piece of land "sold for ever" — a debt I will never hand back, a relationship I will never release, a brother I am still biting and will not let go? The jubilee trumpet has already sounded within this text. For our generation, may we be the people who return the land, release the man, and settle the account — because the land is His, and the man is His, and we ourselves are servants He brought forth out of Egypt first of all.

## Devotional Excerpt

Leviticus 25 takes from me the two things I clutch hardest — my land and myself — and tells me neither was ever mine. "The land shall not be sold for ever: for the land is mine; for ye are strangers and sojourners with me." And I, who thought I owned my years, hear at the chapter's end that the children of Israel are His servants, whom He brought forth out of the land of Egypt. The seventh year teaches my hands to open: the sabbath of the land is meat for me, and for my servant, and for the stranger, and for the cattle and the beast — no one is fed alone. The fiftieth year teaches my ears to listen for the trumpet: "proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof." Liberty here is not a feeling; it is a man returning unto his possession and unto his family, a brother living with me without usury, a servant going out with his children. This morning I will practise the smallest obedience first: loosen my grip on one thing I have been holding as mine for ever; refuse one gain that would bite — neshek — into a brother's poverty; and release one old debt of kindness I have been carrying on my own books. The trumpet has already sounded in the text. My part is only to live as if I heard it.

2. On the title: Lev 25:10 in the Chinese base text reads 「在遍地给一切的居民宣告自由」; the Chinese title 「要在遍地宣告自由」 gathers its wording "遍地" and "宣告自由" from that sentence in condensed form and is not a verbatim quotation of the whole clause, as its note in the Chinese manuscript states.
3. šabbāṯôn (H7677): an intensive formation from šabbāṯ, denoting complete, solemn rest. BDB lists Lev 25:4 and 25:5 (šənaṯ šabbāṯôn, a year of sabbath observance) expressly under its sabbatical-year sense; the word is the same one that threads the rest theme through Leviticus 23.
4. šôpār (H7782): the horn-trumpet, most often a ram's horn. The Hebrew phrase at Lev 25:9 is šôpār terûʿāh; the Chinese base text renders it 「大发角声」, and the KJV "the trumpet of the jubile." BDB cites Lev 25:9 among the word's sacred uses.
5. dərôr (H1865): freedom, release. It occurs eight times in the Old Testament, with Lev 25:10 as the core text of proclaimed release; Isaiah 61:1 uses the same word, rendered aphesis in the Septuagint, the word-stream continued in the Greek of Luke 4:18-19 (see the New Testament reading layer in the sermon).
6. yôḇēl (H3104): in its base sense the ram and the ram's horn, by extension the year proclaimed by the horn-blast, the jubilee. In this chapter it occurs more than ten times from 25:10 onward and is the pivot word of the chapter's counting and returning.
7. ʾăḥuzzāh (H272): a holding, landed possession. The Chinese base text renders it 「产业」 at 25:10, 「地业」 at 25:13, and 「为业」 at 25:24 — one Hebrew word under differing renderings of the Chinese edition, as the Chinese manuscript notes verse by verse.
8. "Oppress" at 25:14 and 25:17: the causative stem hônāh of yānāh (H3238), to oppress, to maltreat. BDB lists Lev 25:14, 17 expressly as the transactional occurrences of wronging a brother; the KJV renders both with "oppress."
9. Placement of vv. 18-22: this passage — dwell in safety by keeping the statutes, and the sixth year's produce for three years — stands in the text's own order between the jubilee pricing rules (vv. 14-17) and the general redemption principle (v. 23). This sermon treats it at the head of section IV as the faith-premise of the redemption and poor-brother rules; the text's own order is not altered, as the Chinese manuscript likewise notes.
10. gāʾal (H1350) and gōʾēl: the verb denotes redeeming, buying back property; its participle gōʾēl denotes the nearest kinsman charged with the family's duty of redemption. "His kin" / "any of his kin" at Lev 25:25 renders this word; rights and prices of redemption recur throughout the chapter as its verbal skeleton.
11. nešekh (H5392) and tarbîṯ (H8636): nešekh derives from nāšak, "to bite," denoting interest bitten off in advance; tarbîṯ derives from rāḇāh, "to increase," denoting the increment charged at repayment. The two stand together at 25:36-37, covering money and food alike; the KJV renders them "usury" and "increase."
12. ʿeḇeḏ (H5650): servant, bondman, occurring some eight hundred times in the Old Testament. Lev 25:42 and 25:55 twice call Israel "my servants," which with 25:23, "the land is mine," frames the chapter with a double declaration of ownership.
13. The numbering dispute: the coincident reading and the distinct-year reading of the forty-ninth and fiftieth years both persist in scholarship, Milgrom arguing for the distinct year; and for the observance history of the sabbath year and jubilee the Old Testament offers no narrative of complete observance, Lev 26:34-35 and 2 Chr 36:21 belonging to the exile-interpretation layer. The sermon sets both matters side by side and decides neither.
14. The foreign bondmen provisions (25:44-46): the text expressly permits buying bondmen and bondmaids from the surrounding nations and from sojourners' families and holding them as an inheritable possession, alongside the rules for Israelite brothers at 25:39-43. This sermon states their content and boundary at the (Fact) level, neither rewriting the text by later ethics nor preaching the text's permission as a command.

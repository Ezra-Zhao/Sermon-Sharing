# By the Mouth of Two or Three Witnesses — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 19

> Status: First translation — pending proofreading (初译待校). Base text for all Scripture quotations in this English version: the King James Version (KJV). The Chinese source sermon was based on the CUNPSS (New Punctuation Union Version, Simplified, "Shen" edition); where the Chinese manuscript discusses readings, punctuation, or quotation marks of that Chinese base text, this translation reports those facts as facts about the Chinese base text, while every Scripture quotation itself is reproduced verbatim from the KJV (see the Revision Record at the end).

> Verification of the title: Four candidates were considered. (1) "Thine Eye Shall Not Pity," taken from the identical sentence in verses 13 and 21 — weighty, but too severe in edge; it shows only the severity and not the chapter's merciful side, which reserves cities for the unintentional slayer. (2) "Innocent Blood," taken from verses 10 and 13 — it can gather up the blood and the land of the first half of the chapter, but it cannot gather up the witness of the second half. (3) "Separate Three Cities," taken from the command to "separate" in verses 2 and 7 — it reaches only the matter of the cities of refuge, not the landmark and not the witness. (4) "By the Mouth of Two or Three Witnesses," taken from verse 15 — verse 15 is the sentence of this chapter directly quoted twice in the New Testament (Matt 18:16; 2 Cor 13:1; registered only — see the New Testament Reading layer in the body below); and the second half of the chapter (the law of witness) is the procedural foundation of the first half (the cities of refuge): without reliable witness, unintentional and intentional killing cannot be distinguished, and neither asylum nor extradition can be adjudged. Candidate (4) is adopted: "By the Mouth of Two or Three Witnesses." The English title is taken from the original wording of KJV 19:15: "By the Mouth of Two or Three Witnesses" (KJV 19:15: "at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established.").

> Note on the base text and collation: The quotations of the Chinese source sermon follow the CUNPSS (New Punctuation Union Version, Simplified, "Shen" edition) as its fixed base text, collated verse by verse against the BHS Hebrew and the KJV English; all 21 verses of the chapter were checked first-hand. The numbering agrees in all three: Deuteronomy 19 has 21 verses in total. The BHS paragraph markers, checked directly: *samekh* occurs five times, at the end of verses 7, 10, 13, 14, and 21; *petuhah* does not occur in this chapter. Three entries on the fixed base text: (1) At 19:15 the Chinese base text reads "作见证" (with 作), where the modern punctuated Union Version reads "做见证"; the Chinese quotations follow the base text's 作. (2) At 19:18 the Chinese base text runs the sentence on with a comma — "the judges shall make diligent inquisition, and, behold, if the witness…" in sense — where the modern punctuated Union Version breaks it with a full stop; the Chinese quotations follow the base text. (3) Throughout this chapter the Chinese base text calls these cities simply "cities," with no proper name; the proper term "cities of refuge" (逃城) appears in the base text at Num 35:6 ("there shall be six cities for refuge" in the corresponding Chinese wording). Account of the quotation marks — as measured in the fixed text: in the CUNPSS base text, opening quotation marks occur five times, at the head of verses 1, 4, 11, 14, and 15, and closing quotation marks occur three times, at the end of verses 13, 14, and 21 — five openings and three closings in all. In group terms: verses 11–13 form one group (opening mark at the head of verse 11, closing mark at the end of verse 13); verse 14 is one group, opening and closing within itself; verses 15–21 form one group (opening mark at the head of verse 15, closing mark at the end of verse 21); and the opening marks at the head of verses 1 and 4 are not closed within this chapter (the paragraph quotation-mark style of Moses' continuing discourse across chapters). Verses 1–10 are therefore not without quotation marks: verses 1 and 4 each carry an opening mark. All quotations in the Chinese source follow the fixed base text letter for letter, including the opening and closing of quotation marks; all quotations in this English version are reproduced from the KJV (the KJV itself does not mark this discourse with quotation marks, and none have been added beyond the quotation marks that frame each citation as a citation).

Deuteronomy 19 is the chapter in the detailed legislation (chapters 12–26) devoted to "protecting life, protecting property, and protecting truth." Chapters 16–18 have just established the offices — judges and officers, the king, the priests and Levites, the prophet; chapter 19 then immediately gives the substantive law those offices are to execute: the first 13 verses treat blood and cities, separating out cities of refuge for the unintentional slayer while strictly forbidding the cities of refuge to shelter the intentional murderer; verse 14, in a single sentence, treats the landmark, protecting the neighbour's property; verses 15–21 treat witness, laying down the general rule for establishing a matter, the investigation of a false witness, and the penalty in kind. The logic of the chapter is layered support from below: the city protects the life of the unintentional slayer; the hearing by the elders and judges decides who deserves that protection; and the foundation of the hearing is witness — "at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established" (19:15). "Blood" occurs five times in this chapter (verse 6; twice in verse 10; verse 12; verse 13), and "thine eye shall not pity" answers itself at the head and tail of the chapter, in verses 13 and 21: no pity for the intentional murderer, no pity for the false witness; the opposite of pity here is not cruelty, but that the blood of the innocent and the name of the falsely accused should no longer be shed in vain.

> Note on form: The chapter is divided into four sections. Section One, verses 1–7: separating the three cities and the rule for unintentional killing. Section Two, verses 8–13: three more cities when the border is enlarged, and no asylum for the intentional murderer. Section Three, verses 14–15: the landmark and the general rule of witness — two fences protecting the neighbour. Section Four, verses 16–21: the investigation of a false witness and "life shall go for life." Time codes: Introduction 00:00–05:00; Section One 05:00–20:00; Section Two 20:00–33:00; Section Three 33:00–45:00; Section Four 45:00–57:00; Conclusion 57:00–60:00. Each section has five layers: [Factual Layer] states only the facts of the text; [Theological Layer] theological inferences, each marked as such; [Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] registers discussion only and does not decide in place of the text; [New Testament Reading Layer] registers and reports New Testament citations of this chapter only, and does not use the New Testament to rewrite the chapter's original meaning; [Summary Layer] gathers the section in one paragraph. Two rules of adjudication are declared in advance: first, as to the differences among the accounts of the cities-of-refuge system in Numbers 35, Deuteronomy 4, Deuteronomy 19, and Joshua 20 (number of cities, appointment, and hearing procedure), this sermon sets them side by side only — it does not harmonize them and does not adjudicate them; second, the echoes among 19:15–21 and Deut 17:6, Deut 5:20 (the ninth commandment of the Decalogue), Matt 18:16, and 2 Cor 13:1 are registered in the Reading layer only; no one of them is used to rewrite the original meaning of this chapter, and this sermon does not adjudicate their normative scope. ## Introduction (00:00–05:00)

[Factual Layer] The chapter opens by setting the scene: "When the LORD thy God hath cut off the nations, whose land the LORD thy God giveth thee, and thou succeedest them, and dwellest in their cities, and in their houses;" (19:1) — the cities-of-refuge system was not made for marching through the wilderness; it was made for settled life: after moving into the cities and houses others left behind, the first civil arrangement commanded is to separate three cities (19:2). Those commanded in this chapter are the whole people ("Thou shalt prepare thee a way," 19:3); those who execute it include the elders of his own city (19:12) and the priests and judges (19:17); no king and no prophet appears in this chapter — it is the first purely civil and judicial law after Deuteronomy's series on the offices.

[Theological Layer] The order of this chapter is itself a theological statement (this is an inference): God treats first the survival of the unintentional slayer (the bulk of verses 1–10), then the death of the intentional murderer (verses 11–13), then the landmark (verse 14), and finally witness (verses 15–21). The weight of the law falls first on "how the person who does not deserve to die may live," and then on "how the sin that deserves death is to be put away"; and the last seven verses of the chapter do not speak of killing at all, but of bearing witness — because every judgment in the first thirteen verses finally rests on whether witness is true or false. This is an inference drawn from the structure of the text, marked as such in this sermon; it is not presented as the text's own self-description.

[Summary Layer] The chapter in one sentence: God leaves a city for the person who killed unintentionally, leaves a procedure for the person falsely accused, and leaves a landmark stone for the neighbour's property; and what He refuses to pity is the life of the intentional murderer and the evil of the false witness. In the four sections that follow, we look at these four arrangements one by one.

## I. Separating the Three Cities and the Rule for Unintentional Killing (Deut 19:1–7; 05:00–20:00)

[Factual Layer] This section has seven verses. Verse 1, the setting (already seen in the Introduction). Verse 2, the command: "Thou shalt separate three cities for thee in the midst of thy land, which the LORD thy God giveth thee to possess it." (19:2) — the verb *bāḏal* (H914) means "to separate, to set apart," of the same root usage as God's separating light from darkness in Genesis and the separating of holy from common in Leviticus (registered here as a lexical entry). Verse 3, three works: "Thou shalt prepare thee a way, and divide the coasts of thy land, which the LORD thy God giveth thee to inherit, into three parts, that every slayer may flee thither." (19:3) — prepare a way (*kûn*, H3559, to establish, to make firm), divide the land into three parts (*šālas*, H8027, to divide in three), with the purpose that the slayer may flee there. Verses 4–5, the rule: "And this is the case of the slayer, which shall flee thither, that he may live: Whoso killeth his neighbour ignorantly, whom he hated not in time past;" (19:4); the illustration is a woodcutting accident: "As when a man goeth into the wood with his neighbour to hew wood, and his hand fetcheth a stroke with the axe to cut down the tree, and the head slippeth from the helve, and lighteth upon his neighbour, that he die; he shall flee unto one of those cities, and live:" (19:5) — the head (*barzel*, H1270, iron) of the axe (*garzen*, H1631) slipping from the helve (*nāšal*, H5394) is an accident of tool failure, and the text takes it as the paradigm of "ignorantly." Verse 6, the danger: "Lest the avenger of the blood pursue the slayer, while his heart is hot, and overtake him, because the way is long, and slay him; whereas he was not worthy of death, inasmuch as he hated him not in time past." (19:6) — the avenger of the blood (*gōʾēl haddām*, H1350) is a relative of the dead man; "whereas he was not worthy of death" is the text's own verdict sentence on the unintentional slayer. Verse 7, the restatement: "Wherefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt separate three cities for thee." (19:7) In the whole chapter, the fleeing word-group occurs four times (verses 3, 4, 5 of this section and verse 11 of the next), and "live" twice (verses 4, 5).

[Theological Layer] Four inferences, each marked. First, the reachability of asylum is part of the system itself (this is an inference): the law does not merely announce "you may flee"; it commands roads to be prepared, the land to be divided, and cities to be set near at hand (19:3) — if the city were too far, the avenger of blood would "overtake him, because the way is long" (19:6), and asylum would be a dead letter; justice that cannot be reached is not justice. A further factual observation: the "prepare thee a way" and "divide … into three parts" of verse 3 are a spatial arrangement setting one city in each of the three parts of the whole land, so that from any place the unintentional slayer's road to the nearest city is roughly equal; asylum is not one isolated city set up as a symbol, but a net spread over the whole territory — and the density of the mesh is the sincerity of the system (this is an inference drawn from the engineering details of the text). Second, distinguishing intent from ignorance is the moral foundation of all the law in this chapter (this is an inference): the rule has two requirements — "hated not in time past" examines the history of motive, and "ignorantly" (*biḇlî-ḏaʿaṯ*, without knowledge, without awareness) examines the state of mind at the moment of the act; in a lethal case, intent and ignorance are distinguished first, and only then is punishment discussed. Third, the subject of the verb for setting up the cities is the people (this is an inference): "Thou shalt separate," "Thou shalt prepare thee a way" — God commands, and human beings establish; a city of refuge is not a sanctuary dropped from heaven, but a way of life the community prepares with its own hands for its neighbour. Fourth, the rule gives only one illustration, yet thereby sets the form of a precedent (this is an inference): lethal circumstances vary endlessly, and the rule writes only the one woodcutting case (19:5); what the law gives is not a list but a criterion — whoever is "without hatred beforehand and ignorant" is to be measured by this case; the case is finite, the criterion unbounded, and it is precisely by this one case and this one criterion that later judges distinguish intent from ignorance in concrete cases.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two registrations. First, blood vengeance (kin vengeance) was a widespread duty of kin in the ancient Near East, and the same word *gōʾēl* in Ruth and elsewhere denotes the "near kinsman" and his duty of redemption (the redeemer). This chapter confines the action of the avenger of blood within the cities-of-refuge system — once the unintentional slayer has entered a city, the avenger of blood may not lay a hand on him; where the intentional murderer is extradited, he is "deliver[ed] … into the hand of the avenger of blood" (19:12). The system's treatment of vengeance is incorporation rather than abolition; this is the standard scholarly description of the institution, registered here only and not developed. Second, a side-by-side registration of the cities-of-refuge accounts (the full side-by-side account is in Section Two; the part belonging to this section is registered here first): Deuteronomy 4 records that Moses had already separated three cities east of the Jordan, and at 4:42 the rule reads, "That the slayer might flee thither, which should kill his neighbour unawares, and hated him not in times past; and that fleeing unto one of these cities he might live:" (Deut 4:42; KJV) — in wording almost identical to 19:4 of this chapter; the two passages both belong to Deuteronomy, one already done (east of the Jordan), the other yet to be done (west of the Jordan). The difference is set side by side only, not adjudicated.

[New Testament Reading Layer] Only one traditional reading is registered: in the history of interpretation, Hebrews 6:18 — "who have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us" (Heb 6:18, KJV, fragment) — is often read alongside the cities-of-refuge system, and Christ is likened to the refuge of the believer. This is a parallel reading of later interpretation; its existence is registered here only. Hebrews 6 itself does not cite this chapter, and this sermon does not use that parallel reading to rewrite the original meaning of chapter 19; the "cities" of this chapter, within the text, are three real cities in the land of Canaan.

[Summary Layer] Section One: God commands the people to separate three cities in the promised land, to prepare the way, and to divide the land into three parts, so that a person who killed without prior hatred and ignorantly may flee there and live; the system acknowledges that the avenger of blood's rage is real ("while his heart is hot," 19:6), yet stops it with distance and roads, waiting for a hearing to distinguish intent from ignorance.

## II. Three More Cities, and No Asylum for the Intentional Murderer (Deut 19:8–13; 20:00–33:00)

[Factual Layer] This section has six verses: first a promise, then a prohibition. Verses 8–9: "And if the LORD thy God enlarge thy coast, as he hath sworn unto thy fathers, and give thee all the land which he promised to give unto thy fathers;" (19:8) "If thou shalt keep all these commandments to do them, which I command thee this day, to love the LORD thy God, and to walk ever in his ways; then shalt thou add three cities more for thee, beside these three:" (19:9) — the covenant to add cities carries a condition: keep and do, love the LORD thy God, and walk ever in his ways. The three verbal phrases of the condition clause tighten step by step — "keep all these commandments to do them" is obedience acted out, "to love the LORD thy God" is the inward motive, "to walk ever in his ways" is continuance in time; the adding of cities is not a one-time reward but an arrangement that grows in step with continuing obedience (this is an observation drawn from the syntactic structure). Verse 10, the purpose clause: "That innocent blood be not shed in thy land, which the LORD thy God giveth thee for an inheritance, and so blood be upon thee." (19:10) — the blood of the "innocent" (*nāqî*, H5355), if shed in the land of inheritance, brings guilt upon the people of the whole land. Verses 11–13, the counter-case: "But if any man hate his neighbour, and lie in wait for him, and rise up against him, and smite him mortally that he die, and fleeth into one of these cities:" (19:11) "Then the elders of his city shall send and fetch him thence, and deliver him into the hand of the avenger of blood, that he may die." (19:12) "Thine eye shall not pity him, but thou shalt put away the guilt of innocent blood from Israel, that it may go well with thee." (19:13) — the three requirements of intentional murder: hate (*śānēʾ*, H8130, hatred in the heart), lying in wait (*ʾāraḇ*, H693, ambush in the act), and smiting to death; the procedure has a single action: the elders of his own city (*zāqēn*, H2205, plural in the original) send men to extradite him. In this section the BHS *samekh* stands at the end of verses 10 and 13, corresponding respectively to the closing of the purpose sentence about "innocent blood" and of the prohibition to "put away the guilt of innocent blood" (this is a textual observation).

[Theological Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, asylum has a boundary, and the boundary is intent (this is an inference): the same city is a way of life for the unintentional slayer and is nothing at all for the intentional murderer; the city of refuge protects "ignorance," not the act of "killing" itself — the man of verse 11 "fleeth into one of these cities," the gate opens all the same, and the hearing brings him out all the same. The existence of the asylum system never abolishes judgment. Second, the covenant to add cities binds territory to obedience (this is an inference): the enlargement of the border is God's gift according to the oath (19:8), while the adding of three more cities is the human response conditioned on "keep all these commandments to do them … to love the LORD thy God" (19:9) — the larger the land, the more the cities; if the expansion of territory is not accompanied by the reachability of the way of escape, the enlarged land becomes a land where innocent blood is shed (19:10). Third, "that it may go well with thee" (19:13) joins justice and well-being directly (this is an inference): putting away the guilt of shedding innocent blood is not the satisfaction of a vengeful heart, but the condition of the whole land's well-being; conversely, to let the intentional murderer hide inside the system leaves the guilt of blood to "be upon thee" (19:10) — upon the community that did not carry out the extradition.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] This section sets side by side, in concentrated form, the differences among the four accounts of the cities-of-refuge system — side by side only, not harmonized, not adjudicated. Number of cities: Num 35:6 fixes six, and they belong to the Levites' cities — "And among the cities which ye shall give unto the Levites there shall be six cities for refuge, which ye shall appoint for the manslayer, that he may flee thither: and to them ye shall add forty and two cities." (Num 35:6, KJV). Deut 4:41–43 records three cities already separated east of the Jordan; this chapter commands three cities to be separated west of the Jordan (19:2, 7) and, on condition of obedience, promises in advance "three cities more" (19:9) — if one counts by this chapter's arithmetic alone, three plus three is six, and together with the three east of the Jordan the total could reach nine cities; within the text of this chapter, no carrying out of the "three cities more" is recorded. Joshua 20 settles on six cities and names them one by one: west of the Jordan, three cities — "And they appointed Kedesh in Galilee in mount Naphtali, and Shechem in mount Ephraim, and Kirjatharba, which is Hebron, in the mountain of Judah." (Josh 20:7, KJV); east of the Jordan, three cities — "And on the other side Jordan by Jericho eastward, they assigned Bezer in the wilderness upon the plain out of the tribe of Reuben, and Ramoth in Gilead out of the tribe of Gad, and Golan in Bashan out of the tribe of Manasseh." (Josh 20:8, KJV) — the names of the three eastern cities appear at Josh 20:8; Deut 4:41–43 likewise records that Moses separated three cities east of the Jordan, but their names were not rechecked verse by verse for this sermon, and whether the names at the two places are all identical is not asserted here. Procedure: Josh 20:4 records the arrival — "And when he that doth flee unto one of those cities shall stand at the entering of the gate of the city, and shall declare his cause in the ears of the elders of that city, they shall take him into the city unto them, and give him a place, that he may dwell among them." (Josh 20:4, KJV) — there is a declaration at the city gate and reception by the elders; Josh 20:6 further records, "And he shall dwell in that city, until he stand before the congregation for judgment, and until the death of the high priest that shall be in those days: then shall the slayer return, and come unto his own city, and unto his own house, unto the city from whence he fled." (Josh 20:6, KJV) — there is judgment before the congregation and release after the death of the high priest; and Josh 20:9 sums up, "These were the cities appointed for all the children of Israel, and for the stranger that sojourneth among them, that whosoever killeth any person at unawares might flee thither, and not die by the hand of the avenger of blood, until he stood before the congregation." (Josh 20:9, KJV) — in its express wording the beneficiaries include the sojourning stranger. This chapter, by contrast, says not a word about a declaration at the city gate, no judgment before the congregation, no release at the high priest's death, and no express mention of the sojourning stranger; its procedural line is single: the elders of his own city extradite the intentional murderer (19:12). The wording also differs: for "appointed/separated" Josh 20:7 uses *qāḏaš* (H6942, to make holy, to set apart), while this chapter uses *bāḏal* (H914); and Numbers 35 confines the witness principle to murder cases: "Whoso killeth any person, the murderer shall be put to death by the mouth of witnesses: but one witness shall not testify against any person to cause him to die." (Num 35:30, KJV). These differences are set side by side here: number of cities, ownership, procedure, scope of beneficiaries, and wording — all five differ; this sermon does not adjudicate which is the original intent of the fixed text, and still less does it use one passage to rewrite another. One further Old Testament cross-reference is registered: Exodus 21:13–14 already has the same distinction in the same structure — "And if a man lie not in wait, but God deliver him into his hand; then I will appoint thee a place whither he shall flee." (Exod 21:13, KJV); "But if a man come presumptuously upon his neighbour, to slay him with guile; thou shalt take him from mine altar, that he may die." (Exod 21:14, KJV) — there the asylum is "a place," "mine altar"; here it is the separated cities; there the intentional murderer is taken from before the altar, here he is brought out of the city by the elders. Sameness of structure and difference are set side by side, not adjudicated.

[New Testament Reading Layer] No registration of a direct New Testament citation of this section's text exists for this section. The echo in the New Testament (e.g., in 1 Corinthians 5) of the "put away the evil" formula of this section (*bāʿar*, 19:13, the same formula as in Deuteronomy 13, 17, and 18) belongs to intertextuality at the level of the whole book of Deuteronomy and is not tied to a single verse of this section; this sermon notes its existence here only, without developing it and without quoting it word for word.

[Summary Layer] Section Two: if the border is enlarged, cities must be added; if innocent blood is shed in the land of inheritance, the guilt falls on the whole land. The intentional murderer — he hated him, lay in wait for him, and killed him — gains nothing by fleeing into a city: the elders of his own city will bring him out and deliver him to the avenger of blood; thine eye shall not pity him; put away the guilt of innocent blood, and it may go well with thee.

## III. The Landmark and the General Rule of Witness (Deut 19:14–15; 33:00–45:00)

[Factual Layer] This section has two verses, each establishing one general rule. Verse 14: "Thou shalt not remove thy neighbour's landmark, which they of old time have set in thine inheritance, which thou shalt inherit in the land that the LORD thy God giveth thee to possess it." (19:14) — the landmark (*gəḇûl*, H1366) is the boundary of each family's property, set by "they of old time" when the land was divided; the verb of the prohibition is *sûg* (H5253, Hiphil, to remove, to make retreat). In this chapter the BHS *samekh* stands at the end of verse 14, and this single verse forms a paragraph by itself (textual observation). Verse 15: "One witness shall not rise up against a man for any iniquity, or for any sin, in any sin that he sinneth: at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established." (19:15) — the scope is "any iniquity, or … any sin," that is, all cases; the rule has a double limit: one witness does not establish a matter (the negative sentence), and only at the mouth of two or three witnesses is the matter established (*yāqûm dāḇār*; *qûm*, H6965, the matter shall stand established). Read against Deut 17:6 in the same book: there it is limited to a capital offence — "At the mouth of two witnesses, or three witnesses, shall he that is worthy of death be put to death; but at the mouth of one witness he shall not be put to death." (Deut 17:6, KJV); read against Num 35:30: there it is limited to a murder case (see the quotation in Section Two). The three passages are of the same wording family, with scope widening step by step: murder case, capital offence, every iniquity and sin. This verse is the broadest general statement of the three; this is a textual fact from reading the three fixed texts against one another.

[Theological Layer] Two inferences are announced in the source, and three follow, each marked. First, verses 14 and 15 set side by side are two fences "protecting the neighbour" (this is an inference): verse 14 protects the neighbour's property — move a landmark, and a man's land is taken from him in silence; verse 15 protects the neighbour's name and life — a single untested mouth, and a man's innocence is taken from him without evidence. Removing a landmark is encroachment in slow motion; establishing a case by one mouth is encroachment in haste; the law places the two side by side in adjacent verses, and puts both under "thou shalt not." Second, the position of the witness rule in the whole chapter is a hinge (this is an inference): it stands between the cities of refuge (verses 1–13) and the hearing of the false witness (verses 16–21); every extradition and every grant of asylum in the first half, and every inquisition in the second half, must first pass this verse — without the mouth of two or three witnesses, the "intentional murder" of the earlier text cannot be established, and the "false witness" of the later text cannot be inquired into either. Third, the harm of a single untested testimony is not confined to the courtroom (this is an inference): a word passed on without cross-witness, circulating in the community, destroys a man's name almost as surely as a sentence in court; verse 15 establishes "at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses" as the general rule for all cases, which amounts to requiring the whole community, before it opens its mouth in judgment, to learn first to wait for the second and the third mouth — to speak a beat more slowly is the way of life the law leaves the neighbour.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two registrations. First, boundary stones in the ancient Near East were markers under the double protection of religion and royal law, and removing a boundary stone is listed as a grave offence in several ancient law codes and works of wisdom literature; within Deuteronomy itself, 27:17 contains a separate curse on the one who removes a landmark (this sermon registers its existence only and has not checked that verse's fixed text verse by verse). Second, "two or three" is the customary Hebrew idiom for "two or three"; the lower limit of the rule is two: even if the single witness's words are true, the case is not established — what the rule protects is not only the accused, but also the witness system itself against manipulation by one side. This is the standard scholarly explanation of this verse, registered here only.

[New Testament Reading Layer] This verse is the only verse of the chapter directly quoted in the New Testament — two places, registered only, not adjudicated. Matt 18:16: "But if he will not hear thee, then take with thee one or two more, that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established." (Matt 18:16, KJV) — in the steps for winning back an erring brother in the church, this verse's principle is cited. 2 Cor 13:1: "This is the third time I am coming to you. In the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established." (2 Cor 13:1, KJV) — Paul cites this verse in quotation form, introducing his own third coming to Corinth. The corresponding KJV wording at the two places, "that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established" and "In the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established," belongs, with Deut 19:15 KJV's "shall the matter be established," to the sentence pattern of the Hebrew original *yāqûm dāḇār* (registered). This sermon registers only the fact of citation and the correspondence of wording; it does not here adjudicate the normative scope of this principle in the contexts of church discipline and apostolic authority.

[Summary Layer] Section Three: one landmark stone, one count of mouths. Thou shalt not remove the landmark set by them of old time, and thou shalt not establish any man's sin at the mouth of one witness; only at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established — these two verses are the guardrail of the first half of the chapter and the threshold of the second half.

## IV. The Investigation of a False Witness and Life for Life (Deut 19:16–21; 45:00–57:00)

[Factual Layer] This section has six verses and writes out a complete hearing of a false witness. Verse 16, the suit is raised: "If a false witness rise up against any man to testify against him that which is wrong;" (19:16) — in the original, "a false witness" is *ʿēḏ-ḥāmās* (*ʿēḏ*, H5707; *ḥāmās*, H2555, violence, wrong); "that which is wrong" (*sārâ*, H5627) denotes turning aside, transgression. Verse 17, appearance in court: "Then both the men, between whom the controversy is, shall stand before the LORD, before the priests and the judges, which shall be in those days;" (19:17) — the two parties to the controversy (*rîḇ*, H7379) stand together; those who receive the case are the priests and the judges; the venue is "before the LORD"; this verse is the last occurrence of the divine name in the chapter, and the only one without the possessive "thy God" (for the count, see the Notes). Verse 18, the inquisition: "And the judges shall make diligent inquisition: and, behold, if the witness be a false witness, and hath testified falsely against his brother;" (19:18) — to make inquisition (*dāraš*, H1875), with the adverb *hêṭêḇ* (H3190), "diligently, thoroughly"; the falsehood word-group (*šeqer*, H8267) occurs twice in this verse in the Chinese base text's count ("false witness … falsely" — in the KJV wording, "a false witness" and "testified falsely"); the witness (*ʿēḏ*) "testified" (*ʿānâ*, H6030) against his brother. Verse 19, the sentence: "Then shall ye do unto him, as he had thought to have done unto his brother: so shalt thou put the evil away from among you." (19:19) — the measure of the sentence is *zāmam* (H2161, what he had thought, purposed to do to his brother); the put-away-the-evil formula (*bāʿar*) occurs again in the chapter, answering verse 13. Verse 20, publication: "And those which remain shall hear, and fear, and shall henceforth commit no more any such evil among you." (19:20) — the sentence has a public, deterrent function. Verse 21, the general principle: "And thine eye shall not pity; but life shall go for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot." (19:21) — five items: life (*nepeš*, H5315), eye (*ʿayin*, H5869), tooth (*šēn*, H8127), hand (*yāḏ*, H3027), foot (*regel*, H7272); "thine eye shall not pity" (*ḥûs*, H2347) is the same sentence as in verse 13 and closes the whole chapter; the BHS *samekh* stands at the end of this verse.

[Theological Layer] Three inferences, each marked. First, the sin of false witness is sentenced according to its intent (this is an inference): what verse 19 punishes is not the completed harm the lie has already caused, but the harm the witness "had thought to have done" to his brother — what penalty he schemed for his brother to receive, that penalty he receives himself; that the scheme did not succeed does not lessen the sin, because had the court sentenced according to his words, the penalty would already have been accomplished. Second, the duty of inquisition lies on the judges (this is an inference): the subject of verse 18 is the judges — "the judges shall make diligent inquisition"; the exposure of a false witness does not depend on public opinion, nor on the accused defending himself, but on the diligence of those who receive the case in checking item by item; verse 15 fixed the count of mouths for witness, verse 18 fixed the fineness of the inquisition — the two verses together are the complete rule of evidence. Third, the venue of the hearing decides the attitude of the testimony (this is an inference): the two parties "stand before the LORD" (19:17) — bearing witness is not arranging words before men, but stating facts before God; the evil of false witness is not only framing a brother, but also despising this venue.

[Scholarly and Historical Discussion Layer] Two registrations. First, the formula of likeness in verse 21 (in scholarship commonly called *lex talionis*) has parallel provisions in ancient Near Eastern law codes (the Code of Hammurabi and others); the standard scholarly readings of this formula in Deuteronomy are two, registered only and not adjudicated: one reads it as a scale limiting vengeance — the penalty may not exceed the harm received, and private vengeance stops here; the other reads it as a principle of equivalence in sentencing, whose concrete mode of execution (literal execution or commutation) was also long discussed within later Jewish tradition. The quotations in this sermon are for understanding within this chapter only: in this chapter the formula directly modifies the measure of sentence in the false-witness case (verse 19), and this sermon does not extrapolate its literal scope of application. Second, "those which remain shall hear, and fear" of 19:20 is of the same form as the publication wording at many put-away-the-evil provisions in Deuteronomy (such as the related provisions in chapters 13 and 17); it is a fixed rhetoric of Deuteronomic law, whose function lies in the community's collective memory and deterrence; this sermon registers the sameness of form only.

[New Testament Reading Layer] First, two echoes within the Old Testament are registered — registered only, not read backwards. First, the ninth commandment of the Decalogue: Deut 5:20 reads, "Neither shalt thou bear false witness against thy neighbour." (Deut 5:20, KJV) — the Chinese base text at that verse has no word corresponding to "thy neighbour" (it reads, in sense, "thou shalt not bear false witness to frame a man"), while the original has *rēaʿ* (H7453), which the KJV renders "thy neighbour"; and its word for the false witness in the original is *ʿēḏ šāwʾ* (*šāwʾ*, H7723, vain/false), a near-synonym but different word from this chapter's *šeqer* (H8267) at 19:18, the Chinese base text rendering both as "false witness" (this account agrees with the base-text entries of the Deuteronomy 5 sermon in this series). The correspondence of the two is registered here in this form: the commandment forbids "bearing false witness to frame," and this section establishes the courtroom inquisition and the penalty in kind; which comes first and which later, and how their norms correspond, this sermon sets side by side only and does not adjudicate. Second, the relation between the witness rule of Deut 17:6 and verse 15 of this chapter was registered by side-by-side reading in the Factual Layer of Section Three, and is gathered here under the general register of the Reading layer (see Note 6). One New Testament place: Matt 5:38–42 records, in the Sermon on the Mount, the Lord Jesus speaking of the ancient saying "eye for eye, tooth for tooth" and giving his teaching. This sermon has not checked that passage's Chinese fixed text verse by verse, so it registers the existence of the citation relation only, does not quote it word for word, and does not here adjudicate the normative relation between that teaching and this chapter's formula. Of this section's procedure for investigating a false witness, no registration of a direct New Testament citation of this section's wording has been found.

[Summary Layer] Section Four: a witness of violence rises up to frame his brother; the two parties stand before the LORD, and the priests and judges make diligent inquisition; once it is established as false witness, he is done unto as he had thought to have done unto his brother, and the evil is put away from among you; those who remain hear, and fear. Thine eye shall not pity — life shall go for life, eye for eye: the whole chapter closes with the measure of justice, and with that same measure it protects every person who stands in the court.

## Conclusion (57:00–60:00)

[Factual Layer] A re-reading of the whole chapter: 21 verses; eight commands in all — separate three cities (verses 2, 7), prepare the way (verse 3), the covenant to add three cities (verse 9), extradite the intentional murderer (verse 12), do not remove the landmark (verse 14), establish the matter by two or three witnesses (verse 15), make diligent inquisition into the false witness (verse 18), and life shall go for life (verse 21). The divine name "the LORD" occurs nine times in the chapter, its last occurrence at verse 17, "shall stand before the LORD" — the chapter's litigation, hearing, and sentence are all finally gathered under this one sentence.

[Theological Layer] The Conclusion makes a single theological statement (this is an inference): this chapter places mercy and justice in one and the same law — to leave a city for the unintentional is mercy; to let neither the intentional murderer hide in the city nor the false witness hide in his mouth is justice. If mercy has no justice, the city of refuge becomes the murderer's shield; if justice has no mercy, the hot heart of the avenger of blood will swallow the unintentional. In this chapter God refuses to let go of either: the city gate stands open for the unintentional slayer, the court stands open for the falsely accused, the landmark stone stands for the neighbour's land, and the witness's mouth — only at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall every matter be established.

## Devotional Extract

Few moments are lonelier than discovering that your own hands have caused a death you never intended: the avenger is behind you, his heart hot, and the blood on your hands is real. Deuteronomy 19 says God prepared in advance for such a person — the cities were already separated, the way already prepared, the land already divided into three parts, so that he could make it there and live. "And this is the case of the slayer, which shall flee thither, that he may live: Whoso killeth his neighbour ignorantly, whom he hated not in time past;" (Deut 19:4, KJV). Here mercy is not a word of comfort but an arrangement made beforehand: God's compassion often finishes building the city and paving the road long before our disaster strikes. The same chapter also keeps a procedure for the innocent: no one may be established guilty at the mouth of one witness alone; inquisition must be diligent, so that no one perishes by a false word. Today, when we are quick to judge a person, it is worth pausing first: have I heard the mouth of two or three witnesses, or the mouth of one person only? Do I rest on facts diligently inquired into, or on a heart hot with emotion? In the footsteps of the avenger of blood there is the pain of bereavement, and also the danger of killing an innocent person by mistake; the law is considerate even of his feelings, yet does not permit his rage to take the place of judgment. May we both flee to the city that has been prepared and find refuge there, and learn from the God who separated the cities — to leave people a way to live first, and to settle every matter on solid evidence.

> "One witness shall not rise up against a man for any iniquity, or for any sin, in any sin that he sinneth: at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established." — Deuteronomy 19:15 (KJV)

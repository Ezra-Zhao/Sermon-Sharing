# Commentary on the Gospel of John, Volume One, Chapter 7: The Unspoken Thirst in the Heart

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «心里那口说不出的渴».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

- Volume One, Chapter 7
- Author: Guangyi Zhao (Guangyi Zhao)
- Scripture: John 7:25-52

---

In the previous chapter, by the Sea of Galilee, Jesus said that He is the bread of life, and the people who had eaten their fill came looking for Him again the next day. This chapter changes the feast, and it also changes the word: the bread has been spoken of, now it speaks of water; hunger has been spoken of, now it speaks of thirst.

The events took place at the Feast of Tabernacles. This feast falls in autumn, when every household sets up booths and lives in them for several days, in remembrance of their ancestors leaving Egypt and dwelling in tents in the wilderness. Jerusalem was full of people, yet the atmosphere was not peaceful: some were discussing Him, some believed in Him, and some had already sent men to arrest Him. In this air, half welcoming and half hostile, on the last day of the feast, He stood up and cried out one sentence, and that cry has held readers two thousand years later as well: Are you thirsty, or not?

## I. The Scripture

First, look at a passage from the middle of the feast. Jesus was teaching in the temple, and the people of Jerusalem recognized Him, muttering to themselves in their hearts:

> Now some of them from Jerusalem said, "Is this not He whom they seek to kill? But look! He speaks boldly, and they say nothing to Him. Do the rulers know indeed that this is truly the Christ? However, we know where this Man is from; but when Christ comes, no one knows where He is from." (John 7:25-27, NKJV)

Jesus took up this remark on the spot:

> Then Jesus cried out, as He taught in the temple, saying, "You both know Me, and you know where I am from; and I have not come of Myself, but He who sent Me is true, whom you do not know. But I know Him, for I am from Him, and He sent Me." (John 7:28-29, NKJV)

Some wanted to arrest Him, but no one laid a hand on Him. Among the multitude, however, many believed in Him (see John 7:30-31). The chief priests and the Pharisees sent officers to arrest Him. Jesus said that He would be with them a little while longer, and then He would go to Him who sent Him; they would seek Him and not find Him (see John 7:32-34). The crowd did not understand these words and guessed back and forth among themselves. Just at that time, the feast reached its last day.

> On the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried out, saying, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink. He who believes in Me, as the Scripture has said, out of his heart will flow rivers of living water." (John 7:37-38, NKJV)

John immediately added a sentence of his own explanation — this is his usual way of writing, for fear that readers would misunderstand:

> But this He spoke concerning the Spirit, whom those believing in Him would receive; for the Holy Spirit was not yet given, because Jesus was not yet glorified. (John 7:39, NKJV)

As soon as this cry went out, the crowd split on the spot:

> Therefore many from the crowd, when they heard this saying, said, "Truly this is the Prophet." Others said, "This is the Christ." But some said, "Will the Christ come out of Galilee? Has not the Scripture said that the Christ comes from the seed of David and from the town of Bethlehem, where David was?" So there was a division among the people because of Him. (John 7:40-43, NKJV)

The officers who had been sent to arrest Him returned empty-handed in the end. When their superiors asked them why they had not brought the man, their answer was only one sentence:

> The officers answered, "No man ever spoke like this Man!" (John 7:46, NKJV)

In the council there was one more man who spoke up — Nicodemus, whom the reader met in chapter 3, the teacher who came to Jesus by night. He spoke a fair word on Jesus' behalf:

> "Does our law judge a man before it hears him and knows what he is doing?" (John 7:51, NKJV)

A few words need to be made clear first.

One is the last day of the Feast of Tabernacles. The Feast of Tabernacles lasted seven days in succession, and the eighth day was a sacred assembly, the climax of the whole feast, so John calls it that great day (see Leviticus 23:36). During the feast, every morning the priests went down in procession to the Pool of Siloam, drew water with a golden pitcher, blew trumpets and sang psalms, carried it back to the temple and poured it beside the altar, while the crowd sang, "Therefore with joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation" (Isaiah 12:3, NKJV). This ceremony commemorated the ancestors receiving water from the rock in the wilderness (see Exodus 17:1-7), and it also looked forward to the day when, as the prophet said, living water would flow out from Jerusalem (see Zechariah 14:8). For seven days in succession, everything the whole city saw and heard was water. When Jesus stood up at this moment and said, If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink, He was speaking standing right in the middle of the ceremony.

Another is thirst. In the original language, this word is simply the thirst of a parched throat, the kind of thirst where one's throat is dry and smoking. Used of a person, it never refers only to the body. Long ago the Old Testament prophet Isaiah had cried out almost the same words on God's behalf: "Ho! Everyone who thirsts, come to the waters; and you who have no money, come, buy and eat" (Isaiah 55:1, NKJV). As soon as Jesus uttered this sentence, in the ears of those familiar with Scripture, the prophet's voice should have sounded at the same time.

Another is living water. Living water is not a pool of stored water; it is flowing water, spring water, river water, the kind that cannot be exhausted and still flows outward. In chapter 4, Jesus said to the Samaritan woman that the water He gave would become in a person a fountain of water springing up into everlasting life (see John 4:14). By this chapter, the words take another step forward: not only does it spring up within you, it must also flow out from your heart, and flow into rivers.

This word rendered "heart" — in the original it refers to the innermost part of a person's body, put into plain language, that deepest place in a person that one oneself cannot quite clearly touch. When the Lü Zhenzhong Translation handles words of this kind, it also deliberately brings out the meaning of what is inside: the water does not flow out from a person's surface, nor from a person's own ability; it flows out from that deepest place where the Holy Spirit has come to dwell. As for the Holy Spirit, for now you may understand Him in this way: He is the Spirit of God; He is the One whom, after Jesus departed from this world, God sent to dwell in those who believe in Him and to walk with them all their lives. John says He had not yet been given, because Jesus was not yet glorified. In the Gospel of John, being glorified often refers to Jesus completing the whole road of the cross, the resurrection, and the ascension; once that road was completed, the Holy Spirit was poured out in abundance on the day of Pentecost (see Acts chapter 2).

## II. Exposition

The atmosphere of this chapter must be read out first. Throughout the whole chapter there are voices of discussion everywhere: Who exactly is this man? Have the rulers privately believed? When Christ comes, should His whereabouts be mysterious? Hidden within the discussion is a very interesting misalignment. The people of Jerusalem said, We know where this man is from. They said this very confidently: He grew up in Nazareth, He has a Galilean accent, everyone in the neighborhood knows His family's circumstances. But it was precisely here that they misjudged Him. As for Jesus' origin, they knew the surface layer; of the deeper layer they knew nothing at all. So Jesus' reply carried a slight edge: You both know Me, and you know where I am from. This sentence, rather than being an admission, gently pushes their confidence back. You think you know, but in fact you do not know Him who sent Me. The time when a person is hardest to persuade is when he feels he has known it all along. The root of all the disputes later in this chapter lies here.

Look again at the posture in which He cried out. When Jesus ordinarily taught people, He mostly sat down to speak; in the first half of chapter 7, He was teaching in the temple, also in ordinary discourse. On this last day, John specifically records two actions: He stood, and cried out. Standing is the posture of a formal proclamation; crying out is so that everyone in the courtyard would hear. This was not a private chat, nor a special lesson given only to the disciples; this was a public invitation issued before the whole city gathered for the feast, and before those who wanted to arrest Him. The content of the invitation is also worth chewing over slowly. If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink. There is only one condition: thirst. It does not say, if anyone understands, if anyone is good enough, if anyone passes an examination on Scripture. First admit thirst, first come — this order, from Isaiah to Jesus, has never changed.

There is another layer: He placed Himself in the position of the ceremony. For seven days in succession, the priests poured the water of Siloam on the altar; that water commemorated the provision of the past and sought the rain of the future. Now Jesus stood in the temple and said, Come to Me and drink — which is to say: The Provider you commemorate year after year, the living water you hope for year after year, is now standing right before you. No wonder the crowd was divided on the spot. These words were either madness or true; there was no middle ground. Those who said, This is the Christ, and those who said, Will the Christ come out of Galilee, were in fact arguing about the same thing: Did He deserve to say this sentence?

The reason brought forward by those who opposed Him sounded very solid: Scripture says Christ is the seed of David and comes from Bethlehem; this man, however, is a Galilean, so it does not match. Their Scripture was not quoted wrongly; the prophet Micah had indeed prophesied concerning Bethlehem (see Micah 5:2). What was wrong was that the information in their hands was incomplete: Jesus was in fact born in Bethlehem; only, John does not recount this in detail in this Gospel, and his readers already knew it from the other Gospels. And so a debate presented the most lamentable scene: those who opposed Him held the right Scripture and reached the wrong conclusion, because before drawing their conclusion, they had not gone and checked all the facts about the man before their eyes. That fair word of Nicodemus points precisely at this disease: To judge a man before hearing him and knowing what he is doing — even the law does not permit that. In the end he was pushed back by a single sentence from his colleagues: Search and look, for no prophet has arisen out of Galilee. In this too they spoke wrongly: the hometown of the prophet Jonah, Gath Hepher, was in the region of Galilee (see 2 Kings 14:25). Those most particular about searching searched most carelessly. Reading this passage, do not be in a hurry to laugh at them. Once a person has already settled a case in his heart, whether it is Scripture or facts, they are often merely brought in to embolden the conclusion.

The passage about the officers is the most moving stroke in the whole chapter. These men were ordered to arrest Him; they listened to one discourse, yet returned empty-handed, for this reason: No man ever spoke like this Man. What held them was the words themselves. Even those who came to arrest Him were first held by His words. Over two thousand years, countless people have first been tripped up by just such a sentence, and only then slowly walked inward.

Finally, return to the promise itself. He who believes in Me, out of his heart will flow rivers of living water. Jesus said, as the Scripture has said, yet in the Old Testament there is no verse that matches this sentence word for word. It is Isaiah's invitation to the thirsty to come to the waters (see Isaiah 55:1; 58:11), Ezekiel's vision of a great river flowing out from under the threshold of the temple (see Ezekiel 47:1-12), Zechariah's prophecy that living water would flow out from Jerusalem (see Zechariah 14:8) — several waters flowing together into this one sentence. Note also that the rivers are plural, and that they flow outward from the heart. What Jesus promised was never a small cup of water enough to quench one's own thirst; it is that, after a person's inside has been connected to the source, there is so much that it must flow outward, and flow to other people. John immediately makes clear that this water is the Holy Spirit. That is to say, the date for the fulfillment of this promise was not the day of the Feast of Tabernacles itself; it would have to wait until after Jesus was glorified. But the invitation was issued that very day: If you thirst, you may come today. How the water would become rivers within a person — that was a later story; whether one is willing to admit thirst and willing to come was the matter of that day.

Looking at the whole chapter together: the crowd asked who He was, the officers were held by His words, Nicodemus asked that a man be heard fully before being judged, and Jesus' way of asking was the most direct: Are you thirsty? Arguing for a whole day about the question concerning Him is not as good as first honestly answering this one question concerning oneself.

## III. Application

First, speak of this thirst — why is it hard to speak of it?

When the body is thirsty, a person will find water to drink himself and does not feel ashamed. The thirst in the heart is different; many people will not admit it all their lives. When work goes badly, one can say it is luck; when money is not enough, one can say it is the market; but that empty, dry feeling in the heart in the middle of the night when one cannot sleep, vague and impossible to explain, cannot be explained to others. Tell a friend, and everyone laughs and it passes; tell family, and one fears they will worry; for the rest of the time, one just bears it oneself and props it up with other things first. Scrolling on the phone props it up for a while, liveliness props it up for a while, being busy props it up for a while, buying a few things props it up for a while. It holds up in the daytime; it does not hold up in the deep of night. The first layer of meaning in Jesus' words is that He speaks out for you this word you are embarrassed to say: thirst. He did not wait for you to organize your language; He asked first.

Next, we often mistake thirst for something else.

There is a kind of person who is clearly thirsty in his heart, yet desperately exerts himself toward achievement. He thinks that if he is promoted one more level, earns a little more, is recognized by a few more people, that dryness will disappear. When achievement really comes, he is happy for a few days, and the dryness returns, and more clearly than before, because he has already tried it and knows that cup of water does not suit his taste. Another kind of person looks for water in relationships. He places all his hope on one person, demanding that the other understand him, fill him, revolve around him, pressing the other until he can hardly breathe — and the water is still not enough to drink, and in the end both of them thirst together. There is yet another kind, who blocks thirst with doctrines. He has read many books and heard many lessons, and can produce set after set of answers about life; but when he wakes in the middle of the night, his heart is still dry. Knowing where there is water and drinking water are two different things. That Samaritan woman in chapter 4 had had several husbands, came out at noon every day to draw water, avoiding people and avoiding herself; those people in chapter 6 who had eaten their fill chased across the sea the next day, wanting yet another meal. By chapter 7, Jesus made the matter plain: what you are looking for is not another bucket of water, nor another meal, but the source. A bucket will empty and a meal will be digested; only when a person is connected to the source does thirst have somewhere to land.

A fair word should also be said for religion. In this chapter, the ceremony itself was not wrong; the Feast of Tabernacles was a feast appointed by God Himself. The problem was that a person could keep the ceremony in great liveliness while his heart did not move at all: after seven days of pouring water, he went home still thirsty. It is the same today: a person can take part in activities very regularly, and after the gathering disperses his heart is still dry. Jesus stood up and cried out at the climax of the feast, not to wreck the occasion, but to remind those keeping the feast: Do not embrace the golden pitcher and forget the source. If you have an inexplicable weariness toward religious activities, what you are weary of may not be faith itself, but those days of drawing water year after year while never having drunk a single mouthful oneself.

This promise has another half, often skipped over by people: the water not only comes in, it also goes out.

At first, those who come to drink mostly think of quenching their own thirst; there is nothing wrong with that, and Jesus' invitation begins right there. But the picture He promised is larger than quenching thirst: rivers flowing out from the heart. That is to say, a person truly connected to the source slowly changes. Before, he poured out complaints to everyone he met; later, he can quietly listen to others pour out their complaints and can give a sentence or two that is truly useful. Before, when something happened at home he flared up first; later, he became the one who settles the whole family's heart. Before, he calculated so as not to suffer loss; later, his hand loosened, and his time, money, and attention he became willing to share with others. This is not produced by gritting one's teeth to be a good person; what is produced that way will sooner or later tire, keep accounts, and feel wronged. The picture of rivers speaks of something else: when there is much water, it flows by itself. A person is first filled, and overflowing is a natural thing. If you feel that you are cold toward others and cannot give love, do not be in a hurry to scold yourself first; perhaps it is not that you are bad, but that you are dry. A dry person cannot give water — this principle is not hard to understand. The order is still the same one: come and drink first.

Finally, a word for those still watching and waiting. The people in this chapter reacted in all different ways; the one thing absent is anyone who heard and had no reaction at all. John records that sentence of the officers, and is also asking today's readers: You have heard many people speak; has there been anyone like Him, who with one sentence speaks right to the driest place in you, and also gives you an invitation to come? If there is a slight feeling of being spoken to, there is no need to be in a hurry to attach a label; that may be your thirst itself acknowledging this sentence. Admitting thirst is not shameful; in this chapter, admitting thirst is the only ticket written out plainly.

## IV. Response

Having read this far, you might pause and think about a few questions.

- When was the last time you clearly lacked neither food nor drink, yet your heart was uncomfortably dry? What did you use to prop it up at the time, and how long did it hold?
- The people of Jerusalem said, We know where this man is from. Concerning Jesus, concerning faith, is there any conclusion you reached long ago that you have in fact never seriously examined?
- Nicodemus sought only one thing: hear a man fully before judging him. Are you willing to treat this book, and to treat Jesus, with the same fairness — first hear His words fully, and then draw your conclusion?
- If rivers of living water flowing from the heart are real, to whom would you most want this water to flow first? To someone in your family, or to some corner in your own heart that has been dry for a long time?

If you are willing, you may follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, I admit that in my heart there is an unspoken thirst. I have propped it up with many things, and none of them held. You said that if anyone thirsts, he may come to You and drink; today I come according to this word. Please first let me drink one mouthful of Your water, and then slowly teach me what rivers of living water mean. Amen."

There are also entrances for continuing onward. In the previous chapter, He said He is the bread of life; in this chapter, He said He is the source of living water. In the next chapter we will read John chapter 8, where a woman dragged before the crowd in shame met Him, and He also declared publicly: I am the light of the world. The water has been spoken of; next it speaks of light. You may also find a trusted Christian friend — no need to speak great doctrines — just tell him, exactly as it is, about that thirst in your heart. Speaking it out is itself the first step toward the source.

## Summary (about 100 words)

After crossing the Red Sea, Moses and the Israelites sang a song of victory unto the LORD: He hath triumphed gloriously; He is my strength, my song, and my salvation; He is a man of war, the God who will lead His redeemed people into His sanctuary; Miriam took a timbrel and answered with song. Yet three days later, the bitter water of Marah turned the singing into murmuring, and in the test God declared: I am the LORD that healeth thee. The song of victory and the water of the wilderness are one and the same lesson.

## (00:00 - 08:00) Opening · Scripture Reading · Prayer

Brothers and sisters, peace to you all!

At the end of the previous chapter, the Israelites stood on the far shore of the Red Sea, behind them the waters churning and closing again, beneath their feet the dry ground they had just walked through. They looked back: the pursuers were gone, the chariots had sunk, and the empire that had enslaved them for hundreds of years had been swallowed by the sea in a single night. The Bible says they feared the LORD. When reverent fear reaches its fullness, a person does not stay silent; the mouth looks for an outlet. At the opening of this chapter, that outlet arrives — singing.

Please note: singing was not something the Israelites happened to do after crossing the sea. Singing is the completion of salvation. A person who has been saved trembles, weeps, and at last it must settle into one action: open the mouth, sing, and sing what has just happened, line by line, into memory. The song of Exodus chapter 15 is one of the earliest full-length poems in the Bible; scholars commonly regard its language and style as archaic, close to the early poetic traditions of Canaan. This is a common scholarly judgment, and its details remain debated; we do not state it as settled, but one thing is undisputed: this song is very near to the Red Sea — so near that the salt of the sea is still in its words, and the splash of war horses falling into the water can still be heard. [1]

But this chapter is not only a song. After the singing, the people journeyed on; three days, and no water was found; water was found, and the water was bitter and could not be drunk. So the same people who had just sung "I will sing unto the LORD," three days later murmured against Moses. Brothers and sisters, this chapter sets before us the truest rhythm of human life: between the summit and the valley there are often only three days; between a song of victory and a murmur there is often only a mouthful of bitter water. Today let us walk along this chapter: first listen to the song by the sea, then to the sound of water in the wilderness, and last to the sentence God spoke beside the bitter water — I am the LORD that healeth thee.

Let us read several key passages together:

"Then sang Moses and the children of Israel this song unto the LORD, and spake, saying, I will sing unto the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously: the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea. The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation: he is my God, and I will prepare him an habitation; my father's God, and I will exalt him. The LORD is a man of war: the LORD is his name." (Exodus 15:1-3, excerpt, KJV)

"Thou in thy mercy hast led forth the people which thou hast redeemed: thou hast guided them in thy strength unto thy holy habitation." (Exodus 15:13, excerpt, KJV)

"The LORD shall reign for ever and ever." (Exodus 15:18, KJV)

"And when they came to Marah, they could not drink of the waters of Marah, for they were bitter: therefore the name of it was called Marah. And the people murmured against Moses, saying, What shall we drink?" (Exodus 15:23-24, excerpt, KJV)

"And he cried unto the LORD; and the LORD shewed him a tree, which when he had cast into the waters, the waters were made sweet: there he made for them a statute and an ordinance, and there he proved them, and said, If thou wilt diligently hearken to the voice of the LORD thy God, and wilt do that which is right in his sight, and wilt give ear to his commandments, and keep all his statutes, I will put none of these diseases upon thee, which I have brought upon the Egyptians: for I am the LORD that healeth thee." (Exodus 15:25-26, excerpt, KJV)

Let us pray: Lord, You led Your people out from the sea, and You also led them through a wilderness without water. Teach us two things today: in the hour of victory, how to sing unto You; and in the hour of thirst, how to wait for the water You prepare. In the name of the Lord Jesus, Amen.

## (08:00 - 24:00) Part One: The Song of Victory by the Sea — He Hath Triumphed Gloriously (15:1-12)

First, see how this song begins. "I will sing unto the LORD." "Sing / song," in the original shir (שִׁיר, H7892), is a song or poem, and used as a verb, to sing [1]. Moses did not say "I will speak unto the LORD," nor "I will set up a stone to remember"; he said "I will sing." Brothers and sisters, there is a deep layer here: some facts cannot be held by narration alone. If the crossing of the Red Sea were written only as a battle report — so many enemy troops, so many sunk in the sea, none of ours wounded — every word would be true, yet the most important thing would be missing: the gratitude and wonder that burst out of the people's hearts. Poetry can hold what narration cannot. By what is the memory of faith preserved the longest? Not by archives, but by songs. A person may forget the outline of a sermon, yet never forget a song sung from childhood to old age. The Israelites wrote their salvation into a song, and this song was sung generation after generation with the congregation, sung into the Psalms, sung into the Prophets, and sung until, in Revelation, it echoes again on the sea of glass. If the saved do not sing, the story of salvation quickly becomes someone else's story; once they sing it, it becomes their own story, and every time they sing it, they are saved afresh.

Why sing? "For he hath triumphed gloriously." The original of this phrase is very special: ga'oh ga'ah (גָּאֹה גָּאָה, H1342), the same root repeated twice, meaning "He is most highly exalted, He has won the utter victory" [2]. The root sense of this word is bound up with rising high and self-exaltation; used of human beings it mostly means proud lifting up; used of God, it is the manifestation of His glory — not a contest between man and God over who is higher, but God showing, at the very moment Pharaoh thought himself highest, that He is far higher than all who exalt themselves. Pharaoh had once said: Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice? Pharaoh lifted himself extremely high and counted God as nothing. This song answers him with the same word "high": the one truly exalted is not the king seated on the chariot, but the God who parted the waters and closed them again. Moses did not say "for we have triumphed gloriously," nor "for we fled swiftly." The first line of the song nails down the ownership of the credit: the Victor is He, not we. What did the Israelites do in this battle? They walked, and they watched. They walked across the seabed and stood on the shore and watched. Even Moses' lifted rod was lifted because God told him to lift it. Brothers and sisters, if a song of victory is sung with the wrong subject, it spoils after two singings: the first time it is gratitude, the second time it is boasting. This song has not spoiled for more than three thousand years, because its subject has been right from the first line.

Next comes the heart of the whole song, verse 2: "The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation." "My strength" in the original is 'ozzi (עָזִּי, H5797), and "my song" is vezimrat (וְזִמְרָת, H2176); the two words together, 'ozzi vezimrat, my strength and my song [3]. Please note the order: He is first my strength, then He becomes my song, and finally He becomes my salvation. That is to say, God does not only save me from outside; He first enters into me and becomes my strength; when there is strength, there is a song in the mouth; and when the song is sung, the whole person rests in salvation. There is also a small word of great weight — "my." Not a vague "the LORD is strength," but "my strength"; not "He is Israel's salvation," but "he is become my salvation." Moses sang the salvation of the whole nation into a salvation that every person can take hold of with his own hands. This sentence was later sung again and again: Psalm 118 says, "The LORD is my strength and song, and is become my salvation," almost word for word; and the song for the day of salvation in Isaiah chapter 12 is this same sentence. David sang it and Isaiah sang it, showing that this is not Moses' private feeling but the common grammar of all the saved: first confess Him as mine, then declare to others that He is ours. If faith always stays at "ours," it may be lively, but its root is shallow; once it enters into "my," the song becomes solid.

Verse 3 advances another layer: "The LORD is a man of war: the LORD is his name." "Man of war," in the original ish milchamah (אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, H376 and H4421), literally "a man of war, one who fights battles" [4]. Placed before hearers today, this sentence may make some frown: how can God be a man of war? But for the people who had just walked across the seabed, this sentence spoke exactly to their deepest need. They were unarmed, facing the strongest army in the world of that time; what they needed was not weapons handed to them — they would not even have known how to use weapons; what they needed was God Himself fighting for them. Here the Bible declares without evasion: some battles people cannot fight, and need not fight; they are to be handed to the God who fights for His people. Pharaoh's name was carved everywhere in Egypt — on chariots, on temples, on stelae, all of it Pharaoh's name. But this song says the name truly worth remembering is the LORD. Egypt's stelae will weather away, and Pharaoh's name has become characters in a museum; the name of the LORD was carried by a song out of Egypt, across the wilderness, down to today, and into our assembly hall. In the end, the victory of a reputation is not on stone, but in song.

In verses 4 to 10 the poem replays the scene on the sea line by line: Pharaoh's chariots and his host He cast into the sea; the depths covered them, they sank like a stone into the deep; with the blast of Thy nostrils the waters were gathered together; Thou didst blow with Thy wind, and the sea covered the enemy. There are two pictures set in sharp contrast here. With what did God deal with Egypt's great army? With breath. The breath of His nostrils made the waters stand in a heap; the wind of His mouth made the sea flow back. The strongest army among men, before God, was a matter of a single breath. This is not to say that God is cruel; it is to say that the fear that had occupied the people's hearts for hundreds of years was blown away with one breath: it turned out that the empire which had pressed on us for centuries was, before God, this light. In verse 11 the poem reaches its summit: "Who is like unto thee, O LORD, among the gods? who is like unto thee, glorious in holiness, fearful in praises, doing wonders?" Please note, Moses did not deny that the nations had their "gods" whom they worshiped; he stood in the middle of the arena of the gods and asked one question: Who is like unto Thee? Pharaoh's magicians could perform a few tricks, and Egypt's temples were fragrant with incense, but not one of them could open a road in the sea, or make slaves into a free people in a single night. The monotheism of this song grew out of experience: it was not first having a set of doctrines and then fitting experience into them; it was first being saved, and then, looking back, recognizing that the One who saved them is incomparable.

I close the first part with one reminder: the people who sang this song still had wet feet and sand in their shoes. The most living songs of faith are often not sung after everything has settled down; they are sung just after escape, while the soul is still trembling. Do not wait until your life is entirely sorted out to sing unto God; once it is sorted, you will most likely credit it to your own account. While the seawater is still dripping, while you still remember that you were the one who was saved, sing first. Singing is the necessary outlet of salvation: where the water flows, there must be an outlet; where grace leads, there must be a song.

## (24:00 - 42:00) Part Two: The Song Looks toward the Sanctuary — From the Red Sea to Zion (15:13-21)

At verse 13 the song suddenly lifts its head and looks far away: "Thou in thy mercy hast led forth the people which thou hast redeemed: thou hast guided them in thy strength unto thy holy habitation." "Holy habitation," in the original neveh qodesh (נְוֵה קֹדֶשׁ, H5116 and H6944), is the holy dwelling place, the place where God resides [5]. Please think about how astonishing this sentence is: where were the people standing at that moment? On the east shore of the Red Sea, with the border of Egypt still beneath their feet; ahead of them lay the wilderness, Sinai, and decades of wandering, and they had never even seen the face of their homeland; still less the sanctuary — the tabernacle had not yet been made, the temple would wait hundreds of years more, and Mount Zion was then inhabited by foreigners. Yet this song, as soon as it opens its mouth, sings out the destination: Thou leadest us, not only as far as the wilderness, but unto Thy sanctuary, unto the dwelling Thou hast established. Verse 17 says it even more fully: "Thou shalt bring them in, and plant them in the mountain of thine inheritance, in the place, O LORD, which thou hast made for thee to dwell in, in the Sanctuary, O Lord, which thy hands have established." Brothers and sisters, this is the prophetic vision of the song: if salvation does not look toward its destination, it scatters after two lines. God never saves people by halves: He saves you out of Egypt in order to bring you into His dwelling; however long the wilderness in between may be, it is a journey, not a home. Later, in the wilderness, the people wanted again and again to turn back precisely because they took the journey for the destination and the tent for the end. On the very first day of departure, this song said it in advance: our destination is the sanctuary, the place His own hands have established. When we sing today, we also need this vision that looks toward the destination: your present condition — the illness not yet healed, the child not yet returned, the matter not yet resolved — is only the journey. The One who saved you has already sung the destination to you in the song.

This part of the song also writes the reaction of the nations: the people shall hear and be afraid; sorrow shall take hold on the inhabitants of Palestina; the dukes of Edom shall be amazed; the mighty men of Moab, trembling shall take hold upon them; all the inhabitants of Canaan shall melt away. These place names are the neighbors along the road the people were about to walk. This song was, in effect, giving the people a pill of reassurance in advance: you fear the people on the road, but the people on the road first fear the God you have experienced. What happened at the Red Sea would be carried abroad, carried to places you have not yet reached, and open the way for you first. Later it indeed happened so: Rahab said to the spies in Jericho, we have heard how you crossed the Red Sea, and our hearts melted. The gospel spreads in the same way: what God has done in one person often arrives, before the person himself, at the places he is about to go. Your testimony sometimes arrives before you do.

Verse 18 is the crown of the whole song: "The LORD shall reign for ever and ever!" Pharaoh reigned in Egypt until the earth shook, and at last sank to the bottom of the sea. But this song declares a King who reigns forever. Please note: in the whole Bible's teaching that "the LORD reigns," this song is one of the sources. In an age when Israel had no king, Moses had already sung it clearly: our King is not the one who will be set up in the future; our King is this One now, and this One forever. Later kings would corrupt, be carried captive, and be cut off; only this One reigns for ever and ever. This is also why, in Revelation chapter 15, those who stand victorious on the sea of glass still sing "the song of Moses the servant of God": from the Red Sea to the sea of glass, it is the same reigning God who is sung.

When the singing ends, the camera turns to a woman. Verse 20: "And Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, took a timbrel in her hand; and all the women went out after her with timbrels and with dances." Miriam was the elder sister of Moses and Aaron; the one who had watched by the Nile, watching over the ark of bulrushes that held her baby brother, was she. Now she stood by the Red Sea with a timbrel in her hand, leading the women in dance and answering song. What she sang was only one sentence, the echo of the whole song: "Sing ye to the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea." [1][2] One sentence was enough. Miriam did not write another song; what she did was take up Moses' song and let it sound once more among the women. Brothers and sisters, the singing of a congregation is passed on in just this way: one person sings first, and the multitude answers; the one who leads sings experience into a song, and those who follow sing the song into their own experience. In the church, do not despise those who answer the song: without a congregation that answers, even the finest song is only a solo. The picture of Miriam with the timbrel also tells us that worship was never only a matter of the throat; it is a matter of the whole body: hands striking the timbrel, feet dancing — grace remembered by the body is deeper than grace remembered by the mind alone. The backs that were bent making bricks in Egypt stood upright today in song; that itself is part of salvation.

But I must remind you of one honest sentence: the singers of this song, sung until it shook the heavens, were, three days later, the very same crowd who murmured against Moses. It was not a different crowd; it was the same people. The summit of singing was real, and the murmuring was also real, and between the two there were only three days of journey. The second half of this chapter is precisely where the Bible, without sparing anyone's face, records this for us to see.

## (42:00 - 56:00) Part Three: Three Days to Marah — Bitter Water, Testing, and the LORD Who Heals (15:22-27)

"So Moses brought Israel from the Red sea, and they went out into the wilderness of Shur; and they went three days in the wilderness, and found no water." Three days. On the third day after the singing, the water skins were empty. The Bible deliberately records this number to make us recognize a spiritual rhythm: between the summit of the spirit and the daily life of the wilderness there are only three days. Singing on Sunday in the assembly hall with hot tears, and by Wednesday, in the office, in the hospital ward, in the kitchen, the throat is dry and smoking — this is not your experience alone; it is the experience of the Exodus generation, and the experience of the saints of every age. The experience of the summit cannot be drunk as water; yesterday's emotion cannot quench today's thirst. If God gave only summits and no daily life, people would die of thirst between two summits; God's leading is that after the summit one still walks step by step, and still receives day by day.

At Marah they saw water, but could not drink it: "for they were bitter: therefore the name of it was called Marah." Marah, in the original Marah (מָרָה, H4785, from mar, מַר, H4751, 'bitter'), simply means bitterness [6]. The original plays a wordplay here: the place is named by bitterness, the water tastes of bitterness, and the human heart also sounds with bitterness — the first sentence out of the people's mouths was no longer a song but a complaint: "What shall we drink?" Please compare: three days earlier, the first sentence out of their mouths was "I will sing unto the LORD"; three days later, the first sentence was "What shall we drink?" The same mouth, two voices. When people are parched, the thing most easily forgotten is the song they themselves sang three days before. This is not to say the thirst was unreal — three days without water is a matter of life and death, and God never rebuked them for being thirsty. The problem was not that they had a need; the problem was that their way of facing the need had changed: those who sang had learned trust, while those who murmured were left with only accusation. When a need brings a person before God, it is prayer; when a need drives a person to blame others, it is murmuring. The lesson of Marah is not that we should pretend we are not thirsty; it is that when we are thirsty, we should still remember that the God who parted the sea three days ago is here today as well.

How did Moses respond? He did not argue with the people; he cried unto the LORD. "And the LORD shewed him a tree, which when he had cast into the waters, the waters were made sweet." Please note this verb: "shewed." God did not send down pure water from heaven, nor tell Moses to lift the rod and part the water again; He shewed him a tree that was right nearby. The material for healing the bitter water was, all along, beside the bitter water. Very often we pray and ask God to remove the bitter situation, and God instead opens our eyes to see the tree He has long prepared beside the bitter situation. The tree was cast into the water, and bitterness became sweetness — this sign is itself a small sermon: that piece of wood cast into the bitter water made the bitter water sweet. Many years later, at Golgotha, One who was hung on a tree entered the bitter water of mankind more completely than that tree had, and turned the water of the curse into the water of blessing. This is a shadow that this chapter may legitimately let us glimpse; we touch on it and pass on, for the text itself first speaks of the healing of that generation in its own day.

What follows is the weightiest, and most easily misread, passage of the whole chapter: "there he made for them a statute and an ordinance, and there he proved them." Proving is not tempting a person to fall; it is testing and verifying, as gold is assayed. At Marah God drew a line: the Red Sea was grace; Marah was the lesson; having crossed the sea, they must now begin to learn obedience. The words of verse 26 must be read as they stand, without letting a single word of the condition slip away: "If thou wilt diligently hearken to the voice of the LORD thy God, and wilt do that which is right in his sight, and wilt give ear to his commandments, and keep all his statutes, I will put none of these diseases upon thee, which I have brought upon the Egyptians: for I am the LORD that healeth thee." "That healeth thee," in the original rofe'ekha (רֹפְאֶךָ, H7495), is "the One who heals you, who makes you whole" [7]. Brothers and sisters, this must be stated precisely: this promise is a promise with a conditional sentence. God says "If thou... then I..." — it is not an unconditional grant of wishes, not a check that can be cut out, pasted on the refrigerator, and automatically cashed regardless of everything. Its structure is the structure of a covenant: on one side, the people diligently hearkening and doing what is right in His sight; on the other side, God Himself being their Healer. Hearkening comes first; the experience of healing follows. This is not to say that God's healing must be bought by human beings — salvation is entirely grace, and the Red Sea is the proof: they did nothing, and the sea parted; but in the life after salvation, God wills that His people walk together with His healing in obedience. To erase the condition and keep only "I am the LORD that healeth thee" is to read the Bible thin; to erase the promise and keep only the condition is to read the Bible cold. Both must be held: He is the Lord who heals — that is His name; He wills that we hearken — that is His way. James says that the one who is sick should call upon the Lord; prayer and obedience have always been two sides of one coin.

And do not forget: the place where God declared Himself to be the Healer was at Marah, not in the sanctuary. The name was given beside bitter water. God's most precious names are often not given in comfortable circumstances, but in bitter ones: Abraham knew "Jehovah-jireh" on Mount Moriah, and Israel knew "the LORD that healeth thee" at Marah. The mouthful of bitter water where you are today may well be the address at which you come to know a new name of God. Do not rush to flee Marah so fast that you do not even learn the lesson; first ask one question: Lord, which side of You do You want me to know here?

The chapter ends with a quiet picture: "And they came to Elim, where were twelve wells of water, and threescore and ten palm trees: and they encamped there by the waters." Twelve wells of water, for the twelve tribes; seventy palm trees, a broad canopy of shade. From Marah to Elim, bitterness and sweetness turned out not to be far apart. God did not let the people live long at Marah, nor did He let them skip Marah. The order cannot be reversed: first Marah, then Elim; first the proving, then the rest; first learn hearkening beside bitter water, then encamp beneath the palm trees. Please note one more detail: the springs and palm trees of Elim were not earned by the people; they met them as they walked on. When the lesson of Marah had been learned, and they walked a stretch further, rest appeared by the roadside of itself. Many brothers and sisters are stuck at Marah in their lives, not because God does not give an Elim, but because they sat down beside the bitter water, unwilling to walk further and unwilling to listen to Moses, only recounting again and again how bitter that water is. Bitter water is not for living in; it is for passing through. Finish the lesson of hearkening at Marah, and Elim is at the next stop. There is one more layer of comfort: Elim had twelve wells, not one. God's supply is always measured according to the need of the whole congregation: twelve tribes, one well for each tribe, everyone with a share, and no tribe having to drink what another tribe left behind. Your share, too, God has kept separately for you. Today's application I gather into three things.

First, after victory, sing the song out at once, and also store the song up. Write your salvation down in words, sing it into a song, tell it to your children. Memory is unreliable; songs are reliable. What you sing today will, in your days of thirst, turn around and sing to you.

Second, prepare in advance for the thirst of the third day. Do not take Sunday's emotion to cover a whole week of wilderness. The water for each day must be received each day; devotion, Scripture reading, and prayer are the daily filling of the water skin in the wilderness. The summit gives direction; the daily life gives strength.

Third, beside bitter water, first hearken, then wait for the sweetness. Let God's word enter first, do first what is right in His sight, and the Lord who heals will, in His own time, show Himself to be the Healer. Obedience is not a bargaining chip; it is the road by which children come home.

## (58:00 - 60:00) Conclusion · Prayer of Response

Today we have walked through Exodus chapter 15: a song of victory by the sea, which saw through the wilderness and saw all the way to the sanctuary; a three-day stretch of dry road, which entered Marah and, in the proving, heard a sentence that carries both a condition and grace — I am the LORD that healeth thee; and at the end, the twelve wells and seventy palm trees of Elim. Singing is the necessary outlet of salvation, testing is the daily life three days after victory, and the One who saved us is not only King beside the sea; beside bitter water He is also the Healer.

Let us pray together: LORD our God, You have triumphed gloriously; You are our strength, our song, and our salvation. Thank You for leading us across the sea, and thank You also for not deleting Marah from our road. Teach us to sing songs with the right subject in victory, to not murmur in thirst, and to learn hearkening first beside bitter water. You are the Lord who heals us; into Your hands we place the sickness of our bodies and the bitterness of our hearts. May our destination be exactly what the song sang: the sanctuary Your own hands have established. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, Amen.

## Endnotes

[1] shir: Hebrew שִׁיר (H7892), song, poem; as a verb, to sing; Exodus 15:1 "Then sang Moses and the children of Israel this song unto the LORD," and 15:21 Miriam's answering song "Sing ye to the LORD," both use this word family; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's sentence "the memory of faith is preserved by songs" is a homiletical exposition based on this word and the transmission of the poem (see Psalm 118, Isaiah 12, Revelation 15), and is marked separately from the lexical facts.

[2] ga'oh ga'ah: Hebrew גָּאֹה גָּאָה (H1342, root ga'ah, to rise high, exalt oneself, be gloriously triumphant), the repetition of the same root forming an intensive construction; used in Exodus 15:1, 15:7, and 15:21, rendered in the Chinese Union Version as "hath greatly triumphed / triumphed gloriously"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Scholars commonly regard the language and style of the Exodus 15 poem as archaic (close to early Hebrew poetry); this is a common scholarly judgment whose details remain debated, and it is not treated as settled. The text's "contrast between those who exalt themselves" is a homiletical exposition based on the word's meaning.

[3] 'ozzi vezimrat: Hebrew עָזִּי וְזִמְרָת (H5797 'oz, strength, with the first-person suffix, "my strength"; H2176 zimrah, song, melody of praise), Exodus 15:2 "The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Intertextual echoes: Psalm 118:14 and Isaiah 12:2 repeat this sentence almost verbatim, serving as the scriptural basis for this manuscript's "common grammar of salvation."

[4] ish milchamah: Hebrew אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה (H376 ish, man; H4421 milchamah, war, battle), literally "a man of war," Exodus 15:3 "The LORD is a man of war," meaning that the LORD Himself fights for His people; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "some battles are to be handed to the God who fights for His people" is a homiletical exposition based on the context (see Exodus 14:14, "The LORD shall fight for you").

[5] neveh qodesh: Hebrew נְוֵה קֹדֶשׁ (H5116 naveh, dwelling, habitation, pasture, resting place; H6944 qodesh, holy, holiness), Exodus 15:13 "thou hast guided them in thy strength unto thy holy habitation"; see also 15:17, "the Sanctuary, O Lord, which thy hands have established"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the song's prophetic vision toward Zion and the sanctuary" is an exegetical judgment and exposition based on the destination pointed to by 15:13 and 17, and is marked separately from the lexical facts.

[6] marah: Hebrew מָרָה (H4785, the place name Marah, from מַר, mar, H4751, bitter, bitterness, by extension anguish), Exodus 15:23 "they could not drink of the waters of Marah, for they were bitter: therefore the name of it was called Marah"; the place name shares the sound and root of "bitter," forming a wordplay in the original; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. This manuscript's "the place is named by bitterness, and the heart sounds with bitterness" is a homiletical exposition based on this wordplay.

[7] rofe'ekha: Hebrew רֹפְאֶךָ (H7495 rapha', to heal, to make whole, with the second-person suffix, "the One who heals you"), Exodus 15:26 "for I am the LORD that healeth thee"; the meanings are according to BDB and HALOT. Exodus 15:26 has a conditional structure ("If thou wilt diligently hearken... I will..."); this manuscript presents the structure of its covenant as it stands and does not read it as an unconditional promise; see James 5:14 on calling upon the Lord in sickness.

## Devotional Excerpt

Today's Scripture: "I will sing unto the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously: the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea. The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation." (Exodus 15:1-2, KJV) "And when they came to Marah, they could not drink of the waters of Marah, for they were bitter." (Exodus 15:23, KJV) "For I am the LORD that healeth thee." (Exodus 15:26, KJV)

The first thing Israel did after crossing the Red Sea was to sing; on the third day after the singing, there was no water. Does your life also have such a third day, when the warmth of grace is still there but hardship is already at the door? Notice the order of this chapter: first the Red Sea, then Marah; first they knew the God who triumphed gloriously, then at the bitter water they knew the God who heals. God did not make Marah's water disappear; He made it sweet. In many prayers God does not remove the situation; He changes its taste, and the door to that change is hearkening: if you will diligently hearken, He is the LORD who heals you. Today, do not hurry to murmur over Marah. Sing again the song of three days ago: He is my strength, my song, and my salvation. As you sing, you may find that beside the bitter water there has been a tree all along. And walk a stretch further: the wells and palm trees of Elim are waiting for you. God never saves you by halves, leaving you in the wilderness to live or die on your own; He who led you out of Egypt will surely lead you into His sanctuary. So do not fear the days of thirst, and do not drive your tent pegs down at Marah: drink a mouthful of the sweetened water, remember the name of the Healer, then pack up and follow the pillar of cloud onward. Your Elim will not be far.

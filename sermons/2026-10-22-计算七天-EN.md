Summary: Leviticus chapter 15 closes the purity legislation of chapters 11-15 with the bodily discharges. A man's abnormal discharge, a woman's monthly period and abnormal discharge, and the brief uncleanness of a seminal emission and of marital intercourse are all placed within one scheme of discernment: who is clean after washing until the evening, and who must number seven days and offer sacrifice on the eighth day. The final purpose is stated plainly: to separate the children of Israel from their uncleanness, lest they defile His tabernacle.

## I. The Close of the Purity Legislation: Even the Body's Discharge Must Be Discerned 00:00-08:00

Brothers and sisters, by the time we reach Leviticus chapter 15, the purity legislation has come to its last station. From chapter 11 to chapter 15 Scripture spends five whole chapters on cleanness and uncleanness: chapter 11 treats food, what may and may not be eaten, and how the carcasses of animals defile; chapter 12 treats childbirth, the mother's uncleanness and her period of purification; chapters 13 and 14 treat diseases of the skin, mould in garments and houses, and the elaborate, solemn procedure by which one with a serious skin disease is cleansed; in chapter 15 the text turns its gaze back upon the human body itself, and treats the body's discharges. In thirty-three verses it addresses a man's abnormal discharge, a seminal emission and marital intercourse, a woman's monthly period, and a woman's abnormal bleeding outside her period. The subject is private; the writing is anything but vague: symptom, duration, defilement and cleansing are set down item by item. This is the closing chapter of the purity legislation — like the closing of a ledger: the categories, vocabulary and procedures established in the preceding chapters undergo here their final, and most intimate, test. (Structural observation)

Why call it a closing? Consider its place and form. The first four chapters each govern one sphere of life — food, childbirth, skin, houses — visible spheres that a person can see and handle. This chapter governs the sphere a person finds hardest to speak of and least able to escape: his own body. A man can change his diet; he can deal with garments and houses; he cannot leave his body. When the body discharges, there is nowhere to hide. In bringing the legislation to this point, God makes His meaning very clear: the lesson of separating uncleanness does not govern only the table and the walls; it governs the body's most private sphere as well. No corner of life, because it is hard to speak of, may be placed outside God's teaching. The opening formula is the same as in the earlier chapters: verses 1-2 record that the LORD spoke to Moses and Aaron, charging them to instruct Israel, and verse 2 states the chapter's definition: "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, When any man hath a running issue out of his flesh, because of his issue he is unclean" (Lev 15:2, KJV). The words are still addressed to Moses and Aaron; those commanded to obey are still all Israel. Even private matters are to be taught openly and discerned clearly; vagueness and shame have never been God's way of dealing with the body. The ordinances name the symptoms, fix the days, and explain the washing — and that in itself is a mercy: the sufferer need not guess at his condition, and his family and neighbours need not exclude him out of fear. (Fact)

Before reading this chapter, one governing principle must be fixed, or its thirty-three verses will be misread as a list for shaming people. We learned this principle in chapter 11, and it must be repeated here, because this chapter invites the error even more: ritual uncleanness is not moral guilt. A woman in her period has committed no offence; a man with a seminal emission has committed no offence; husband and wife together in marriage are living in what God Himself instituted and blessed — it is in no way a filthy sin. The sufferer from an abnormal discharge is a person enduring sickness, not a person doing evil. Ritual uncleanness names a state of temporary unfitness to approach the sanctuary; it has a beginning and an end, a fixed duration, and a procedure of cleansing. Moral sin names man's rebellion against God and wrong against his neighbour, and it requires confession and atonement. Confuse the two levels, and this chapter will be read wrongly: periods and discharges will be misread as punishments, sufferers as sinners, and a brief washing as atonement. The text means nothing of the kind. Leviticus 15 treats discharges throughout with the attitude of healing and return: the range of uncleanness is defined in order to protect the camp and the tabernacle; the procedure of cleansing is defined in order to give the sufferer a way back. There is not one word in the chapter rebuking the sufferer, not one inference blaming his discharge on his personal sin. As we preach this chapter, we must keep the same restraint: solemn, accurate, neither prurient nor evasive, and never using the text to convict any person. (Fact)

The chapter also has a key, and I place it in your hands now: the distinction between two grades of uncleanness. Read all thirty-three verses and the ordinances in fact fall into two grades, clearly weighed. The first grade is brief uncleanness: a man's seminal emission, marital intercourse, and a woman's normal period. Its cleansing is simple — washing the body and the clothes; uncleanness lasts until the evening, or the period runs its seven days and ends; no sacrifice is required, and no priestly inspection. The second grade is the uncleanness of an abnormal discharge: a man's chronic abnormal discharge and a woman's prolonged bleeding outside her period. Its uncleanness is deeper and its range of defilement wider; after healing, the sufferer must still number seven days, wash his clothes and bathe, and on the eighth day bring two birds to the door of the tabernacle, where the priest offers them as a sin offering and a burnt offering, before he is fully clean. The boundary between the two grades is drawn with complete clarity: normal physiology and abnormal sickness are not to be treated alike. What one night can settle must not be dragged out into a heavy burden; what is long and hard to heal must not be made light of. God's ordinances have a sense of proportion: the light is handled lightly, the heavy heavily. That proportion is itself teaching: He neither exaggerates everyday physiology nor makes light of chronic suffering; He prepares for each person a way back proportioned to his case. We follow the text's own order: first the man's abnormal discharge, then the brief uncleanness and the woman's period, then the symmetrical provision for the woman's abnormal discharge, and finally the statement of purpose in verse 31. (Fact)

## II. The Man's Discharge: A Chain of Defilement and the Hope of Healing 08:00-24:00

The text treats the man first. The definition of verse 2 has been quoted in full above: "When any man hath a running issue out of his flesh, because of his issue he is unclean" (Lev 15:2, KJV). The sufferer here is called in Hebrew the one with a discharge — a man with an abnormal secretion or flow, a chronic, pathological discharge of the male organ, different from a passing seminal emission. The ordinance first fixes the classification: whatever form the discharge takes, the sufferer is unclean. Verse 3 says: "And this shall be his uncleanness in his issue: whether his flesh run with his issue, or his flesh be stopped from his issue, it is his uncleanness" (Lev 15:3, KJV). Flowing, he is unclean; temporarily stopped, seemingly improving, he still counts as unclean until he is truly healed and has completed the procedure of cleansing. That sentence may look severe; in fact it is protective caution. A sickness whose symptoms come and go cannot be pronounced clean on the strength of a temporary stop, or the sufferer will return to the camp, relapse, and the chain of defilement will have to begin all over again. The ordinance would rather wait longer than release too soon — for the sake of the sufferer himself and of the whole camp. (Fact)

Verses 4-12 then unfold a chain of defilement, the chapter's most striking literary feature. The bed the sufferer lies on becomes unclean; whatever he sits on becomes unclean; one who touches his bed must wash his clothes and bathe, and is unclean until the evening; one who sits on what he sat on does the same, and is clean at evening; one who touches the sufferer's body is treated likewise. Whatever saddle he rides on defiles whoever sits on it; one who touches anything that was under him, or carries his things, must also wash. The most distinctive item stands in verse 8: if the sufferer spits on a clean person, that person must wash his clothes and bathe, and is unclean until the evening — even saliva is listed among the routes of defilement, a special case found nowhere in chapters 13-14. Verses 10-12 turn to vessels: an earthen vessel the sufferer has touched must be broken; a wooden vessel must be rinsed in water. Earthenware is porous; once defiled it cannot be washed clean, and can only be broken; wood can be washed and used again. By this point the weight of the ordinance is plain: the defiling power of an abnormal discharge is no symbolic gesture; it is an actual chain running along body, bed, seat, saddle and vessels. (Fact)

The sermon takes the main points; the chain need not be unrolled link by link. But three things must be seen clearly. First, defilement is transmitted by grades. The sufferer himself is the source of uncleanness, and his grade is the deepest: he is not wholly clean until he is healed and has offered sacrifice. The bed and the seat receive his uncleanness; one who touches the bed or sits on the seat is defiled for one day only, and is clean after washing, at evening. The source is heavy, the outskirts light, and the ordinance distinguishes them without a hair's confusion. God's teaching is never that contact means equal guilt or equal grade; it weighs according to distance and degree of contact. Second, the things on the chain are the everyday things. The bed is where a man rests, the seat where he stays, the saddle the means of his travelling, the vessels the tools of his eating and drinking. What the discharge defiles is precisely the core of life: sleeping, sitting, travelling, using. This shows plainly what abnormal sickness injures — the very foundations of life: the sufferer cannot sleep in peace, cannot sit at ease, cannot travel normally, and even the vessel in his hand has become a problem. The ordinance writes this hardship down as it is, without cosmetic. Third, washing always follows defilement. Every announcement of defilement is followed at once by its washing: wash the clothes, bathe the body, and at evening be clean. The ordinance never names a problem without giving a way; behind every declaration of defilement stands a road of return. That is the fundamental difference between purity legislation and a writ of condemnation: the writ drives a man out; the ordinance leads him back. (Fact; Application) To say that the sufferer's days are hard and the foundations of his life are injured is a factual summary drawn from the objects the ordinance lists; to say that this hardship should move today's hearers to compassion for the sick is the sermon's application. The two levels are kept apart, and the summary does not masquerade as the text's own words.

Verses 13-15 suddenly turn toward hope. Verse 13 says: "And when he that hath an issue is cleansed of his issue; then he shall number to himself seven days for his cleansing, and wash his clothes, and bathe his flesh in running water, and shall be clean" (Lev 15:13, KJV). "Number" — in the Chinese original, the verb rendered "calculate" — is the word from which this sermon's title is taken, in the text's own wording. Read the verse slowly: healing comes first, numbering after. The sufferer is healed first — the discharge stops, the body recovers — and then he begins to number seven days. When the seven days are full, on the eighth day he takes two turtledoves or two young pigeons, comes before the LORD to the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, and gives them to the priest (v. 14, summary); and verse 15 says: "And the priest shall offer them, the one for a sin offering, and the other for a burnt offering; and the priest shall make an atonement for him before the LORD for his issue" (Lev 15:15, KJV). The healed man, with two small birds that even the poorest can provide, can walk the whole road of return. The scale of this offering is plainly lighter than the procedure for one cleansed of a serious skin disease in chapter 14: there there are lambs, oil and the ceremony of waving; here there are only two birds. Lighter does not mean lighter esteem; it means proportion. One with a discharge is usually a man long sick, whose household has gone from bad to worse; the price of return that God fixes for him is a price the poor can pay. The measure of the offering is weighed against the man's condition, and the door of return stands open to the weakest. (Fact)

Note also the order and the names of the offerings: first the sin offering, then the burnt offering. The sin offering deals with the state of uncleanness, so that he may approach the sanctuary again; the burnt offering, wholly given up, declares that, once cleansed, he gives himself to God afresh. Two birds: one closes the days of sickness behind him, the other opens the cleansed life before him. The priest's role here is that of agent and witness: he does not heal the sufferer — healing precedes his work; what he does is solemnly to establish and accomplish this man's return before God. The door of the tabernacle is a public place: the sufferer's cleanness is not a private feeling of his own, but a fact established through the priest, through sacrifice, before God and the congregation. From being separated out in sickness to being received back at the door of the tabernacle — when that road is walked to the end, the man stands again within the worshipping life of the camp. The section on the man ends at that open door. What the text wants us to remember is not only how long the chain of defilement is, but that the road of healing truly exists, and that it has been prepared for the poorest and the weakest. (Fact; Application)

## III. Clean by Evening: Brief Uncleanness and a Woman's Period 24:00-42:00

In verses 16-18 the pen turns, and treats three matters of wholly different weight from the abnormal discharge just described: a man's seminal emission, and husband and wife together. In summary (not verbatim quotation): the three verses treat in turn a man's emission in sleep, the washing of any garment or skin on which the emission falls, and a man lying with a woman; in each case the provision is the same — bathe in water, wash what must be washed, and be unclean until the evening (Lev 15:16-18, summary). Verse 16 in the King James Version reads: "And if any man's seed of copulation go out from him, then he shall wash all his flesh in water, and be unclean until the even" (Lev 15:16, KJV). Set these three verses beside the fifteen before them, and the difference is visible at a glance: no long chain of defilement, no item-by-item tracing through beds and seats, no numbering of seven days, no sacrifice, and no priest appears. There are only three things: bathe, wash, and wait for the evening. When evening comes, the person is clean. With the most economical strokes the ordinance draws the lower boundary of the two grades: this is uncleanness that is temporary, light, and settled by each person himself. A seminal emission is the body's natural discharge, possibly in sleep, not subject to the will; intercourse is the rightful life of marriage, a union God Himself instituted when He ordained marriage and blessed it. That these two things are classed as unclean never means that they are sinful; it means only that even a rightful, natural bodily matter leaves a person, for that day, temporarily unfit to approach the sanctuary. A change of ritual state carries no moral judgement. The sermon must say this with complete clarity, especially on the verse about husband and wife: Scripture has never called marital union filthy. On the contrary, Genesis teaches that the two becoming one flesh is God's institution, and Hebrews teaches that marriage is honourable in all. What this chapter fixes is only a one-day pause in fitness for worship, removed by washing, with no disparagement whatever. Whoever uses these three verses to shame marriage or condemn a spouse is misusing the text. (Fact)

The teaching power of these three verses hides precisely in their lightness. (Application) If God's ordinances had only the heavy grade, men would conclude that the body itself is a calamity, and that the further one flees from it the more spiritual one is. With this light grade in place, the people learn that bodily matters have their degrees: normal physiology is not sickness, a rightful union is not sin; there is no need to panic at the mention of the body, nor to carry guilt for what is natural. Temporary uncleanness is like a small threshold: it reminds a man that approaching God requires preparation, washing, and reverence, without building the threshold into a high wall that shuts ordinary people out. "Unclean until the evening" — that sentence will recur many times in this chapter; it is the standard formula of brief uncleanness. The sun sets, the day turns its page, and the one who has washed is clean again. God's days are bounded by evening, and man's return is appointed for evening: not dragged out, not accumulated, not letting a man carry yesterday's defilement overnight. That rhythm is peace for the whole camp: uncleanness has a term, and when the term comes, the road opens.

Verses 19-24 treat a woman's normal period. In summary: verse 19 fixes that a woman in her period is unclean for seven days, and whoever touches her is unclean until the evening; the verses that follow provide that the bed she lies on and whatever she sits on in her uncleanness are unclean, that one who touches her bed or seat must wash his clothes and bathe and is unclean until the evening, and that a man who lies with her in her period is unclean for seven days, and the bed he lies on is unclean (Lev 15:19-24, summary). Three levels must be set out as they stand. First, the period's uncleanness runs for seven days — longer than the one day of an emission or of intercourse, shorter than the indefinite term of an abnormal discharge; seven days is a complete cycle; when it is full, the period itself ends, and no sacrifice is separately required — formally corresponding to the period of purification after childbirth in chapter 12. Second, the period's defilement runs parallel to the man's discharge section: bed and seat transmit defilement in the same way, and the one who touches is clean after washing, at evening. The parallel shows that the period is handled within a framework comparable to the discharge, yet plainly lighter in term and in the manner of its ending: there are no detailed rules about saliva, saddles, or the breaking of vessels, and no sacrifice on the eighth day. This is another instance of the two grades: the same kind, differently weighed; the same framework, a simpler procedure. Third, the provision about lying with a woman in her period must be read together with the prohibitions of chapters 18 and 20 for completeness: this chapter states only its ritual consequence, uncleanness for seven days; the ethical prohibition stands in chapter 18, which forbids approaching a woman in her period, and the penalty in chapter 20. This chapter is purity legislation; it governs state and term. The division of labour differs, and no one may read this passage alone and conclude that the chapter settles the matter with seven days. (Fact)

On the period, one even-handed word is also due. (Application) A woman's monthly period is the body's normal course, the mark of her capacity to bear children. The ordinance gives her seven days under the name of uncleanness, and at the same time gives her seven days of being set apart and of rest: in those seven days she withdraws from the camp's worship and public contact, and her household, by rule, does not touch her bed or sit on her seat. Viewed with today's eyes, the arrangement has its restrictive side; viewed within the text's own world, it has a clear function: her state is openly acknowledged, with no need to hide and pretend nothing is happening; her term is clearly bounded, and when the day is full she returns, subject to no indefinite exclusion. The text neither romanticises those seven days nor stigmatises the menstruating woman; it simply brings the normal course into the ordinance, just as it brings childbirth and seminal emission into the ordinance: every season of the body stands under God's teaching, and every season carries the guarantee of return when its term is full. Reading this passage, what we are to learn is this even-handedness: no exaggeration, no evasion; to weigh it as the text weighs it, acknowledging the boundary while guarding the dignity.

## IV. A Woman's Discharge: An Arrangement Fully Symmetrical with the Man's 42:00-56:00

Verses 25-30 treat a woman's abnormal discharge outside her period (what follows is summary, not verbatim quotation): if a woman has a discharge of blood for many days outside the time of her period, or her period runs beyond its time and the bleeding does not stop, all the days of her discharge are unclean, as the days of her period; every bed she lies on and everything she sits on is unclean, and whoever touches those things must wash his clothes and bathe, and is unclean until the evening. Verse 28 says: "But if she be cleansed of her issue, then she shall number to herself seven days, and after that she shall be clean" (Lev 15:28, KJV). On the eighth day she takes two turtledoves or two young pigeons and brings them to the priest at the door of the tabernacle, and the priest offers one as a sin offering and the other as a burnt offering, making atonement for her (Lev 15:29-30, summary); verse 30 in the King James Version reads: "And the priest shall offer the one for a sin offering, and the other for a burnt offering; and the priest shall make an atonement for her before the LORD for the issue of her uncleanness" (Lev 15:30, KJV). Reading this passage, what strikes one most is not its detail but its familiarity: the defilement of bed and seat, the formula of washing and cleanness at evening for the one who touches, healing, the numbering of seven days, two birds on the eighth day, one a sin offering and one a burnt offering — step for step, the backbone matches the ordinance for the man's discharge at the chapter's opening, joint by joint. One small difference must be stated as it stands: in the man's section, verse 13 expressly requires washing the clothes and bathing in running water; in the woman's section, verse 28 says only that she shall number seven days and after that be clean, without restating the washing of clothes and bathing in running water. The symmetry belongs to the backbone of the procedure; it is not a word-for-word copy, and preaching must say so honestly. (Structural observation)

The weight of the symmetry must be spoken out in full. (Structural observation; Application) In the ancient world a woman's menstruation and bleeding were often shrouded in deeper taboo and fear, readily classed as ill-omened and treated more heavily. This chapter sets up no stricter, more shaming ordinance for the woman: the range of her defilement is the same as the man's, her procedure of cleansing is the same as the man's, the offering she presents is the same as the man's, and she returns to the same door of the tabernacle. Before the ordinance, the condition of male and female sufferers is equal: alike persons enduring sickness, alike walking the road of numbering seven days, alike at the price of two small birds standing again before God. The symmetry is a fact of the text — the two sections can be verified item by item; to say that this symmetry shows God regarding sufferers without distinction of sex, and the road of return without distinction of rank or weight, is the sermon's application standing upon that fact. The application fits the text, because nowhere does the text lay one link heavier on the woman. The woman long afflicted bears one burden more than the male sufferer: her sickness is entangled with her period, and her days have no fixed term; the ordinance's answer to her is not a longer sentence, but the same road with an end to it: healing, then numbering seven days, then the eighth day — and the door opens.

The description of the sickness in verse 25 also deserves a pause: whether outside the time of her period, or beyond the time of her period, the ordinance covers both cases and does not argue with anyone about which class the cause belongs to. The sick woman does not first have to prove that her bleeding fits some definition before she qualifies to walk the procedure of cleansing; everyone whose discharge is long and unstopped stands within this ordinance's covering. The breadth of the ordinance is consideration for the sufferer: classification exists for the sake of handling, not for the sake of fault-finding; eligibility to return is never made to rest on an examination of causes. As we read Scripture and deal with people today, we should keep this in mind: the text's concern is how the sufferer comes back, not with interrogating the sufferer first. This consideration runs in one line with the fine flour of chapter 2 and the reduced offering-price for the poor in chapter 14: the deeper one reads into the ordinances of Leviticus, the more their character of leaving a road for the weak comes into view. (Fact; Application)

One section for the man, one for the woman, with brief uncleanness and the period between them — the chapter's skeleton now stands fully revealed: the abnormal at the two ends, the normal in the middle; the two ends answering each other across the distance, the middle passing over lightly. With this skeleton in view, we can understand why the summary in verses 32-33 is written so briefly: it gathers the whole chapter as the law for the one with a discharge, for the one with a seminal emission, for man and woman together, and for the woman in her period. This chapter is no heap of miscellaneous items, but a building of ordered structure: it divides by sex into two wings, by weight into two grades, and by term into three ways of ending — until the evening, the full seven days, and numbering seven days with sacrifice. Its orderliness is not literary decoration; it is the method of its teaching: the people hear it once, and can remember which road their own case must walk. If God's ordinances are to be practicable, they must first be memorable, understandable, and followable. Vague law oppresses; clear ordinance builds up. The clarity of chapter 15 is itself part of its mercy. (Structural observation)

## V. Lest They Defile My Tabernacle: The Purpose Clause and a Voice in the New Testament Calling Her Daughter 58:00-60:00

All the chapter's ordinances finally gather into one sentence of purpose. Verse 31 says: "Thus shall ye separate the children of Israel from their uncleanness; that they die not in their uncleanness, when they defile my tabernacle that is among them" (Lev 15:31, KJV). (Fact) Note three things. First, the verb is "separate": the whole chapter's washings, numberings and offerings aim not at pushing the sufferer away for ever, but at separating the person from the uncleanness, so that the person can come back. Second, the tabernacle has an owner: the text says "my tabernacle." That tabernacle stood in the midst of the camp, just as Exodus records that God commanded a sanctuary to be made so that He might dwell among them; and the opening of Numbers in turn commands that those unclean with a discharge and the like be put out of the camp — answering this chapter across the distance: not because God fears dirt, but because the God who dwells with His people is holy, and approach requires separation. Third, the consequence is stated with full weight: the one who defiles the tabernacle is to die in his uncleanness. The purity legislation has run for five chapters, and its concern was never merely hygiene and ritual; it is whether a camp can have God dwelling in its midst. If a man treats separation lightly and brings uncleanness before God, what is damaged is the whole camp's life of dwelling with God.

(New Testament reading; homiletical use) Many years later, the Gospel records a woman who had suffered a discharge of blood for twelve years: her condition is precisely the condition of the woman of verse 25 of this chapter — bleeding for years, unclean by rule, unable freely to touch others. She came behind Jesus and touched His garment, and her sickness abated; but Jesus stopped, asked who had touched Him, and publicly called her "Daughter," spoke to her, and sent her away in peace. In homiletical use, this scene yields only two points: first, one who under the law stood separated outside receives, before Jesus, a name — daughter; second, Jesus is not afraid of her touch; instead, He makes the touch an encounter of cleansing and restoration. This is not the original meaning of Leviticus chapter 15 — in its original meaning, the chapter is the purity legislation of camp and tabernacle — but the New Testament narrative's response to that condition, at the level of a New Testament reading. Nor may preaching draw from it any promise of present-day healing effect: the text records that one woman's encounter on that one occasion; it is not a guarantee of cure for sufferers today. The sick should seek treatment, should pray, should wait; the church should accompany and show compassion — as this chapter leaves a road for the sufferer, so it leaves for those who suffer a road, a seat, and a door of return. The days of numbering will one day be full, and the door of the eighth day will one day open: may we learn both to be separated from uncleanness, and never to be separated from the sufferer.

## Notes

1. zav and zov: the sufferer is called zav, Hebrew זָב, transliterated zav, a participial form, one who discharges, one who has a flow, Strong's number H2100; what he discharges is called zov, Hebrew זוֹב, transliterated zov, noun, a discharge, a flowing, Strong's number H2101. Lexical data from BDB: under zav, one that has a discharge; under zov, a flowing, discharge; HALOT: under zov, discharge. The definition in Lev 15:2 is built on these two words: "When any man hath a running issue out of his flesh, because of his issue he is unclean." The zav of this chapter is a chronic abnormal discharge, distinct in wording and in handling from the passing seminal emission of vv. 16-18; the two must not be read together.

2. tame and tumah: "unclean," Hebrew טָמֵא, transliterated tame, adjective, Strong's number H2931; its noun tumah, Hebrew טֻמְאָה, transliterated tumah, Strong's number H2932. BDB: under tame, unclean; HALOT: ritually unclean. The usage in Leviticus 11-15 is chiefly the ritual category: a state of temporary unfitness to approach the sanctuary, with a fixed term and a procedure of cleansing, distinct from moral sin. The word rendered "uncleanness" in 15:31 corresponds to tumah in its plural or collective use, the sum of the uncleannesses treated in this chapter.

3. shikhvat zera (seminal emission): Hebrew שִׁכְבַת־זֶרַע, transliterated shikhvat zera. shikhvah, Hebrew שִׁכְבָה, a lying, a layer poured out, hence an emission, Strong's number H7902; zera, Hebrew זֶרַע, seed, offspring, Strong's number H2233. BDB notes under shikhvah: emission, copulation; HALOT likewise. Verses 16-18 use this expression for the emission in sleep and for intercourse, and fix only washing the body and the garments and uncleanness until the evening — the brief grade among this chapter's two grades of uncleanness, with no sacrifice and no priestly inspection. The sermon's division into grades rests on the handling prescribed in these three verses.

4. niddah (menstrual impurity): Hebrew נִדָּה, transliterated niddah, noun, impurity, menstrual uncleanness, Strong's number H5079. BDB: impurity, filthiness, specifically menstrual impurity; HALOT: menstrual impurity. Verses 19-24 fix by this word the seven days of a woman's period. Intertexts: Lev 12:2 uses cognate wording for the mother's days after childbirth; Lev 18:19 forbids approaching a woman in her impurity, at the level of ethical prohibition; Lev 20:18 fixes the penalty for the offender, at the level of penal law. This chapter governs only ritual state and term; its division of labour from chapters 18 and 20 is the basis of the sermon's combined reading.

5. zavah (a woman with a discharge): the feminine participial form of zav, Hebrew זָבָה, transliterated zavah. Verses 25-30 answer Lev 15:1-15 item by item: the defilement of bed and seat is the same; the washing and cleanness at evening of one who touches are the same; numbering seven days after healing is the same (15:28); two birds on the eighth day, one a sin offering and one a burnt offering, are the same (15:29-30). The one item not restated is the washing of clothes and bathing in running water of 15:13 in the man's section; the woman's section, 15:28, says only that she shall number seven days and after that be clean. The symmetry belongs to the backbone of the procedure, not to a word-for-word copy; both the sermon and this note state the difference as it stands.

6. roqq (saliva): Hebrew רֹק, transliterated roq, noun, spittle, Strong's number H7536. BDB: spittle; HALOT: spittle. Verse 8 provides that if the one with a discharge spits on a clean person, that person must wash his clothes and bathe, and is unclean until the evening. This is the special case in this chapter's chain of defilement: saliva passes directly from the sufferer to another's body, without bed, seat or vessel; the skin-disease legislation of chapters 13-14 has no such item. Milgrom notes that this item shows the notion of defilement covering the direct transmission of bodily fluids; Wenham likewise treats it as the clearest instance of the discharge's defiling power.

7. Words for the objects in the chain of defilement: bed, mishkav, Hebrew מִשְׁכָּב, Strong's number H4904; seat, moshav, Hebrew מוֹשָׁב, Strong's number H4186; saddle, merkav, Hebrew מֶרְכָּב, Strong's number H4817, used in 15:9; vessel, keli, Hebrew כְּלִי, Strong's number H3627; the earthen vessel is expressed with the word for pottery clay, Strong's number H2789. Verse 12 provides that the earthen vessel must be broken and every wooden vessel rinsed in water. Hartley explains the breaking by earthenware's porous material, which takes in defilement beyond washing. The chain is taken in its main points; the sermon sets out only five ends — bed, seat, saddle, vessels and saliva — and does not unroll the chain verse by verse.

8. Running (living) water: Hebrew מַיִם חַיִּים, transliterated mayim chayyim, living water, running water. mayim, Hebrew מַיִם, water, Strong's number H4325; chayyim is the plural form of chay, Hebrew חַי, living, Strong's number H2416. Verse 13 fixes that the healed man shall bathe his flesh in running water. Comparison with chapter 14: the one cleansed of a serious skin disease also uses living water (14:5-6, 50-52), there joined with the slaying of a bird and the sprinkling of blood; in this chapter living water serves only for bathing, and the procedure is simpler. The comparison belongs to the level of the notes; the sermon argues only from the scale of the offerings and does not unfold the rites of sprinkling.

9. Numbering seven days and the two birds of the eighth day: the "number" of 15:13 (KJV) corresponds to the Hebrew verb of counting; the Chinese original's title takes its wording, "calculate/number seven days," from 15:13 and 15:28 in its base text, and not from the wording of any other version. The eighth day's offering is two turtledoves or two young pigeons: tor, Hebrew תֹּר, turtledove, Strong's number H8449; "young pigeons" renders the Hebrew for sons of the dove. Names of the offerings: sin offering, chattat, Hebrew חַטָּאת, Strong's number H2403; burnt offering, olah, Hebrew עֹלָה, Strong's number H5930. Comparison with chapter 14: there there are a guilt offering, lambs, the waving of oil, and rites of anointing with oil and blood, with a reduced provision for the poor (14:21-32); in this chapter male and female sufferers alike bring two birds, with no guilt offering and no anointing rite — a plainly lighter scale. This difference belongs to the level of the notes; the sermon's argument about lightness rests on it.

10. The tazzir of 15:31 and the defiling of the tabernacle: the verb rendered "separate" is the causative stem of nazar, Hebrew נָזַר, transliterated nazar, to separate, to keep apart, Strong's number H5144. BDB notes under this word, for the causative stem: make separate, keep apart; HALOT likewise. Tabernacle, mishkan, Hebrew מִשְׁכָּן, Strong's number H4908. The verb rendered "defile" is the causative stem of tame, to make unclean. Milgrom stresses that 15:31 is the purpose sentence of the whole block, chapters 11-15: if uncleanness is brought into the tabernacle, the presence of the indwelling God is itself threatened; hence the conclusion in the language of death, whose severity must not be softened into general exhortation. Intertexts: Exodus 25:8, God will dwell among them; Numbers 5:1-4, those unclean are put out of the camp, lest they defile the camp in whose midst God dwells.

11. The word "law" in the summary: verses 32-33 close with the summary formula, "This is the law of…". Law, torah, Hebrew תּוֹרָה, transliterated torah, teaching, ordinance, Strong's number H8451. The items listed in the summary answer the chapter's four sections one by one — the one with a discharge, the one with a seminal emission, the one unclean in her period, and the one who lies with an unclean woman — showing that the chapter's structure is deliberate arrangement from beginning to end, not a heap of items. The sermon's structural observation rests on this summary's backward reference to the whole chapter.

12. Sources for the New Testament reading level: Mark 5:25-34, the woman with a discharge of blood for twelve years touches Jesus' garment and is healed, and Jesus calls her "Daughter"; parallel accounts in Matthew 9:20-22 and Luke 8:43-48. The form of the woman's sickness answers to the female discharge sufferer of 15:25; that answer is a correspondence at the narrative level, and does not amount to the Gospel writer's expressly citing this chapter, still less to the original meaning of this chapter. Homiletical use is limited to two points: the naming of one who had been separated, and the restoration of encounter; no promise of present-day healing effect is drawn from it. Wenham and Hartley, in treating this chapter, both mention this Gospel narrative as the passage most often cited in later reading; its placement is likewise at the level of application.

## Devotional Excerpt

Reading again today the words about numbering seven days, my heart slowly grew quiet. The one who is healed does not rush straight back into the crowd; he counts, day by day: the first day, the second day, until the seventh day. Those seven days are not a punishment; they are the corridor God leaves for return. My own life has known days that would not stop leaking: strength leaked, hope leaked, prayer leaked, and though I longed to draw near to God, I felt unfit to stand before Him. This chapter reminds me that God never told the sufferer to stand outside forever. First He stops the discharge, then He gives the days, then the washing, and on the eighth day He opens the door of the tabernacle. Return has an order, and it has an assurance: it does not rest on whether I feel clean enough, but on walking, day by day, the days He has appointed, until they are full. I am learning neither to declare myself well too quickly nor to despair that I cannot go back. I will simply live this one day well — wash what must be washed, wait where I must wait, count what must be counted. And when His appointed day comes, the door will open, and I will stand before Him again and offer Him, once more, my empty-handed self. May I also learn to treat others this way: toward the one who is still numbering his days, not to hurry him, not to avoid him, and not to speak needless words, but simply to walk with him day by day until he returns to worship and sits quietly beside me, and together we give thanks to the God who opens the door. (Scripture wording in this excerpt follows the King James Version where quoted in substance: "he shall number to himself seven days for his cleansing," Lev 15:13, KJV.)

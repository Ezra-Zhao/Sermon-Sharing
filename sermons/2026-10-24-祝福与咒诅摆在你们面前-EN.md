# I Set Before You This Day a Blessing and a Curse — A Sermon on Deuteronomy 11

> English first translation · Initial translation pending proofreading (英文首译·初译待校). Source: the Chinese final text, strongly re-verified and corrected on 2026-10-25. All Scripture quotations in this translation are taken verbatim from the King James Version (KJV), fetched directly from BibleGateway KJV / equivalent primary KJV pages; no quotation has been translated from the Chinese.

> Title determination: Five candidates were considered — (1) "I Set Before You This Day a Blessing and a Curse," from v. 26, "Behold, I set before you this day a blessing and a curse" (KJV 11:26); (2) "A Land Which the LORD Thy God Careth For," from v. 12, covering only the middle of the chapter; (3) "But Your Eyes Have Seen," from v. 7, covering only the first section; (4) "Every Place Whereon the Soles of Your Feet Shall Tread," from v. 24, covering only the fourth section; (5) "Drinketh Water of the Rain of Heaven," from the image in v. 11, covering only the second section. Candidate (1) is adopted: the argument of all thirty-two verses has a single axis. The first half (vv. 1–25) sets out history, land, and promise item by item; the second half (vv. 26–32) gathers all of it into one act — setting before. God first sets the great acts before their eyes (vv. 1–7), then sets the land before their feet (vv. 8–25), and finally sets the blessing and the curse before them (v. 26), and waits for them to choose for themselves. English title: I Set Before You This Day a Blessing and a Curse (from KJV 11:26, "Behold, I set before you this day a blessing and a curse").

## Main Body

Introduction (00:00–05:00). [Factual layer] The chapter's place in the book is registered first: together with chapters 6–10 it belongs to Moses' second great discourse, and it is the closing chapter of that discourse — chapter 6 issues the Shema, chapter 10 asks "what doth the LORD thy God require of thee," and chapter 11 sets out the grounds and the choice all at once: first history is set out (vv. 1–7), then the land (vv. 8–12), then the daily working of the covenant (vv. 13–21, the second paragraph of the Shema), then the borders of the promise (vv. 22–25), and finally the two ways (vv. 26–32). The chapter has thirty-two verses, structured as registered: the generation that saw with its own eyes (vv. 1–7, 7 verses), the land that drinks rainwater (vv. 8–12, 5 verses), the second paragraph of the Shema (vv. 13–21, 9 verses), taking heed and keeping, and the land of the soles of the feet (vv. 22–25, 4 verses), the blessing and the curse set forth (vv. 26–32, 7 verses) — the groups total thirty-two verses (7 + 5 + 9 + 4 + 7 = 32, Note 1; the main body follows these five sections). Throughout the chapter only one side's voice is quoted: from beginning to end it is God's words and Moses' exhortation within Moses' discourse; the people do not speak — there is only one thing for them to do: hear, and choose. [Theological-inference layer] The chapter's order is itself doctrine: the choice comes last, the witness first. God does not demand a decision from people who know nothing: He first lets this generation run through the great events of forty years before their eyes, and only then asks them which way they will go. "This day" occurs eight times in this chapter in the KJV as in the Chinese account (Note 2, KJV recount) — history is not nostalgia; history is the ground for today's decision. [Summary layer] The chapter's governing statement can be put this way: You saw with your own eyes how He dealt with Egypt, how He dealt with the rebels, and how He dealt with you; you are about to live in a land that drinks the rain of heaven, a land under His eyes from the beginning of the year to the end of the year; therefore, behold — the blessing and the curse are both set before you this day: obey, and be blessed; turn aside, and be cursed; two mountains are already waiting across the river to bear witness. God has set everything out; only one action remains: choose.

### I. The Generation That Saw With Its Own Eyes (11:1–7; 05:00–16:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 1, quoted in full from the KJV — the chapter's opening sentence: "Therefore thou shalt love the Lord thy God, and keep his charge, and his statutes, and his judgments, and his commandments, alway." Registered item by item: (1) The chapter does not open with narrative; it opens with a command: love + keep always. Four names for the law stand in a row: charge, statutes, judgments, commandments (miṣwâ, H4687, six occurrences in this chapter, Note 3). (2) The word "alway" is registered: the Hebrew is kol-hayyāmîm, literally "all the days" — the time limit for keeping the commandments is not the feasts; it is every day. (3) "Love" (ʾāhaḇ) in this verse occurs three times in the chapter (vv. 1, 13, 22), and all three are in the direction of man's love for God (Note 4), standing in exact counterpart to the narration in chapter 10 of God's prior love for the fathers (registered in that sermon): that chapter spoke of His love; this chapter speaks of your love. [Factual layer] Verse 2, quoted in full: "And know ye this day: for I speak not with your children which have not known, and which have not seen the chastisement of the Lord your God, his greatness, his mighty hand, and his stretched out arm," Registered item by item: (1) The qualification of the hearers is drawn here: this sermon is not spoken to the children; it is spoken to those who experienced it themselves — the verb account: "know" set against "have not known," yādaʿ (know) occurring, in the English know-family, three times in the chapter (Note 5, KJV recount). (2) Four items seen stand in a row: chastisement (mûsar, H4148), greatness (the Hebrew ûgəḏullô, literally "his greatness"), his mighty hand, and his stretched out arm — the last two are the fixed Exodus vocabulary (Deut 4:34; 9:26, checked directly in the cross-reference column of this verse's per-verse page). Deut 4:34 (KJV): "Or hath God assayed to go and take him a nation from the midst of another nation, by temptations, by signs, and by wonders, and by war, and by a mighty hand, and by a stretched out arm, and by great terrors, according to all that the Lord your God did for you in Egypt before your eyes?" (3) "Chastisement" heads the four items: the first of God's acts that this generation saw was not deliverance but chastisement; the same word is used in Deut 8:5 (checked directly in the same column): "Thou shalt also consider in thine heart, that, as a man chasteneth his son, so the Lord thy God chasteneth thee" (KJV) — registered on record. [Factual layer] Verse 3, quoted in full: "And his miracles, and his acts, which he did in the midst of Egypt unto Pharaoh the king of Egypt, and unto all his land;" Registered item by item: (1) The Hebrew behind "miracles and acts" is two words: ʾōtōtāyw (his miracles/signs, ʾôt, H226) and ûmaʿăśāyw (and his acts, maʿăśeh, H4639) — the KJV renders "his miracles, and his acts"; the Chinese base combines them into one phrase; registered by entry. (2) The object account: Pharaoh + all his land — the scope of the acts is all Egypt, not one corner of the court. (3) The word "Egypt" occurs four times in this chapter (twice in v. 3, once in v. 4, once in v. 10, Note 6, KJV recount identical). [Factual layer] Verse 4, quoted in full: "And what he did unto the army of Egypt, unto their horses, and to their chariots; how he made the water of the Red sea to overflow them as they pursued after you, and how the Lord hath destroyed them unto this day;" Registered item by item: (1) The verb account of this verse: "made … to overflow" (Hebrew hēṣîp, made the water flow over, overflow; root number pending verification, Note 12) and "destroyed" (ʾāḇad, H6, two occurrences in this chapter, the other at v. 17). (2) "Unto this day" (ʿad hayyôm hazzeh) is registered: the effect of the Red Sea event is spoken of by Moses as lasting to the very day of the discourse — forty years on, that army has never come back. (3) The cross-references of this verse, checked directly in the cross-reference column of its per-verse page: the Red Sea narrative of Exodus 14–15, and Heb 11:29 in the "by faith they passed through the Red sea" form (KJV: "By faith they passed through the Red sea as by dry land: which the Egyptians assaying to do were drowned"), registered there at the reading layer. [Factual layer] Verse 5, quoted in full: "And what he did unto you in the wilderness, until ye came into this place;" Registered item by item: (1) The sentence pattern of this verse is registered as neutral: the Hebrew ʿāśâ lākem, literally "what he did unto you," carries no praise or blame in itself — the forty wilderness years contained both manna and quails and also plague and fiery serpents, and this verse gathers them all into "what he did unto you"; praise and blame are borne by v. 6 below and by the context of the whole chapter. (2) The result clause is registered: "until ye came into this place" — the end point of the wilderness narrative is survival: the chastised generation, after all, stood east of the Jordan. [Factual layer] Verse 6, quoted in full: "And what he did unto Dathan and Abiram, the sons of Eliab, the son of Reuben: how the earth opened her mouth, and swallowed them up, and their households, and their tents, and all the substance that was in their possession, in the midst of all Israel:" Registered item by item: (1) The name account: this verse names only Dathan and Abiram, not Korah — differing in fullness from the narrative of Numbers 16, where the three are named together, and matching the form of Ps 106:17, which likewise names only Dathan and Abiram (KJV: "The earth opened and swallowed up Dathan and covered the company of Abiram"); the divergence is set side by side at the discussion layer. (2) The swallowed account, four items: the men themselves, their households, their tents, and all the substance that was in their possession (Hebrew kol-hayqûm, everything that subsists; meaning and number pending verification). (3) "The earth opened her mouth" (Hebrew pāṣətâ hāʾāreṣ ʾet-pîhā, the earth opened her mouth) is a personifying sentence: the executor of the judgment is here written as the earth itself. Read against Num 26:9–10 (checked directly in the cross-reference column of this verse's per-verse page): "And the sons of Eliab; Nemuel, and Dathan, and Abiram. This is that Dathan and Abiram, which were famous in the congregation, who strove against Moses and against Aaron in the company of Korah, when they strove against the Lord: And the earth opened her mouth, and swallowed them up together with Korah, when that company died, what time the fire devoured two hundred and fifty men: and they became a sign" (KJV) — there too only Dathan and Abiram are named in the heading, with a form of words about the earth opening and swallowing them and Korah; agreements and differences registered. [Factual layer] Verse 7, quoted in full — the close of the first section: "But your eyes have seen all the great acts of the Lord which he did." Registered item by item: (1) "But" draws the line: the children have not known and have not seen (v. 2); your eyes have seen — the same verb field (seeing) is set in contrast at the head and tail of this section. (2) "Your eyes" in the Hebrew is ʿênêkem hārōʾōt, "your eyes which saw" — eye (ʿayin, H5869) is registered in the source account at two places in this chapter, the other being God's eyes in v. 12: men's eyes have seen His acts, His eyes look upon the land; the two pairs of eyes face each other across the chapter, registered on record. In the English KJV text the word "eyes" in fact occurs a third time, at v. 18 ("frontlets between your eyes"); see the KJV count-difference register in the Notes. (3) "All the great acts" (maʿăśêh YHWH haggādōl, the great acts of the LORD) closes the list of vv. 2–6: chastisement, the Red Sea, the wilderness, the swallowing of the rebels — even judgmental events are counted among the "great acts," not one deleted. [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: (1) Responsibility is proportional to witness — this is an account the section itself writes out: Moses does not demand this kind of obedience from people who have not seen. This generation's privilege is not bloodline but eyewitness: they are required to love and required to keep always because they saw with their own eyes. (2) "Chastisement" standing at the head of the list of things seen is worth recording on its own: Israel's memory of faith is not whitewashed — God's acts include His strokes against His own people, including the earth opening to swallow rebels from the congregation. A faith that deletes judgment from God's record cannot sustain the blessing and the curse that follow in this chapter. This is an inference, marked as such here. [New Testament reading layer] The echoes of this section in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: Hebrews 11:29 places the passing through the Red Sea in the catalogue of faith (checked directly in the cross-reference column of v. 4's per-verse page; KJV quoted above), a motif echo of this section's eyewitness structure; 1 Corinthians 10's treatment of the wilderness generation as "examples" (registered as report) is in the same key as this section's use of history as the ground of choice. Both are registered only, and are not read back as this chapter's original meaning. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters set side by side. (1) The fullness and brevity concerning Dathan, Abiram, and Korah, set side by side: (a) The focus reading: this chapter takes only the persons directly connected with "the earth opened"; Korah's company has its own end by fire in Numbers 16, omitted here in excerpt. (b) The coexisting-traditions reading: Numbers 16 is itself woven from two rebellion narratives (Korah's censers; the earth's swallowing of Dathan and Abiram), and this chapter, with Ps 106:17, carries only the earth-swallowing line. The two readings are set side by side; what can be fixed is only the textual fact: this chapter and Ps 106:17 share the same form, Num 26:9–10 is close to it, and Numbers 16 names the three together. (2) Praise or blame in v. 5's "what he did unto you," set side by side: (a) The neutral reading: the sentence pattern itself judges neither praise nor blame; the wilderness acts contain both grace and judgment. (b) The chastisement reading: following the mûsar framework of v. 2, this verse refers chiefly to chastising acts. The two readings are set side by side, without adjudication. [Summary layer] One governing sentence for the section: This sermon is not spoken to your children; it is spoken to those who saw with their own eyes. Chastisement, the Red Sea, the wilderness, the earth opening — all the great acts, not one deleted. Those who have seen all this have no standing to say they did not know; their obedience begins from memory.

### II. The Land That Drinks Rainwater (11:8–12; 16:00–26:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 8, quoted in full: "Therefore shall ye keep all the commandments which I command you this day, that ye may be strong, and go in and possess the land, whither ye go to possess it;" Registered item by item: (1) "All the commandments" (kol-hammiṣwâ) is in the Hebrew a singular collective form: the commandment is here kept as one whole, not picked and chosen; this form occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 8, 22, Note 3). In English the KJV reads the plural "all the commandments" / "all these commandments," and the singular-collective form is not preserved; see the KJV count-difference register. (2) "That ye may be strong" (ḥāzaq, H2388) is registered: keeping the commandments and courage are directly linked here — courage is not innate; it is kept into being. (3) "The land, whither ye go to possess it": the land is still in the future tense here; entering the land takes keeping the commandments as its preceding course; the causal order of this verse is registered on record. [Factual layer] Verse 9, quoted in full: "And that ye may prolong your days in the land, which the Lord sware unto your fathers to give unto them and to their seed, a land that floweth with milk and honey." Registered item by item: (1) The oath account: sware (šāḇaʿ, H7650, niphal form) occurs twice in the chapter (vv. 9, 21), in both cases with the fathers as its object; seed (zeraʿ, H2233) is here: the beneficiaries of the promise run from the fathers down to the seed, and the hearers themselves stand on this line. (2) "A land that floweth with milk and honey" occurs only this once in the chapter: this signature Exodus phrase (its group of sources registered as report) appears only once at the close of the Deuteronomy discourse — Moses has another way of speaking about this land; see vv. 10–12. (3) The condition of long days follows v. 8: long days rest not on military force but on keeping the commandments, in the same form as the long-life promises of Deut 4:40 and 5:16 (registered). [Factual layer] Verse 10, quoted in full (the base text divides it into two sentences; Entry (iv)): "For the land, whither thou goest in to possess it, is not as the land of Egypt, from whence ye came out, where thou sowedst thy seed, and wateredst it with thy foot, as a garden of herbs:" Registered item by item: (1) The basis of the comparison comes first: "is not as" — before entering the land Moses dismantles expectations: do not fit Canaan with Egyptian experience. (2) "Wateredst it with thy foot" (Hebrew hišqîtā bəraglekā, you caused it to drink with your foot) is registered: Egypt's irrigation drew Nile water by human labour, the foot being the tool; "as a garden of herbs" (kəgan hayyārāq) — a herb garden must be watered day by day; the figure is registered. (3) This verse's cross-reference: Zech 14:18, where Egypt too, if it does not go up to keep the feast, has no rain (checked directly in the cross-reference column of this verse's per-verse page; KJV: "And if the family of Egypt go not up, and come not, that have no rain; there shall be the plague, wherewith the Lord will smite the heathen that come not up to keep the feast of tabernacles") — registered on record: in the prophets, Egypt too is placed inside the account of "rain." [Factual layer] Verse 11, quoted in part by entry: "(the land is) a land of hills and valleys"; the second half of the verse in literal rendering of the Hebrew is registered: "(the land) drinketh water of the rain of heaven" (limṭar haššāmayim tišteh-māyim). The KJV in full: "But the land, whither ye go to possess it, is a land of hills and valleys, and drinketh water of the rain of heaven:" Registered item by item: (1) The terrain account: hills and valleys — not a flat river-valley plain, but hill country; hill country cannot retain river water and can only wait for rain. (2) The verb account: the subject of the drinking is the land: the land of Canaan itself "drinketh" the rain of heaven — rain (māṭār, H4306) occurs, as a Hebrew word, three times in the chapter (vv. 11, 14, 17, Note 7): once as the norm (v. 11), once as promise (v. 14), once as withholding (v. 17); the three verses form the chapter's whole weather account. In the English KJV text the word "rain" occurs five times across those same three verses, v. 14 alone naming it three times ("the rain of your land … the first rain and the latter rain"); see the KJV count-difference register. (3) This verse read against v. 10: the land of Egypt is a land watered by man; the land of Canaan is a land watered by heaven — two lands, two ways of living. [Factual layer] Verse 12, quoted in full: "A land which the Lord thy God careth for: the eyes of the Lord thy God are always upon it, from the beginning of the year even unto the end of the year." Registered item by item: (1) "Careth for" (Hebrew dōrēš ʾōtāh, dāraš, H1875, seeks after, cares for) is registered: this land is a land actively visited and inquired after by God — it is not that the land is fertile, but that its Lord is diligent. (2) The time account is in full-measure form: "from the beginning of the year even unto the end of the year" (mērēšît haššānâ wəʿad ʾaḥărît šānâ) plus "always" (tāmîd) — the care has no off-season. (3) The eye account, read against v. 7 (registered there): the people's eyes have seen His acts; His eyes look upon the people's land — the "seeing" in this chapter runs in both directions. [Theological-inference layer] The inferences of this section: (1) Verses 10–12 are one of the Old Testament's most important lessons in spiritual geography: God deliberately moved His people from a "land by a river" to a "land by heaven." The Egyptian mode of faith is the water-carrying mode: resources visible, graspable, dependent on your own foot; the Canaan mode of faith is the rain-waiting mode: resources in heaven, in the seasons, under His eyes. Terrain is theology: a land of hills and valleys forces a man to lift his head. (2) Note the wording of the care: not "that land yields abundantly," but "the eyes of the Lord thy God are always upon it" — Israel's security never rests on the land's stockpiles; it rests on the gaze of the One who cares for it. This is an inference, marked as such here. [New Testament reading layer] The echoes of this section in the New Testament, registered at the reading layer only: this section has no direct New Testament quotation; Matt 5:45, on the Father who "sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust" (registered as report), restates the rain motif from the angle of universal grace, differing from this chapter's covenantal reward-and-punishment structure of rain; only the agreement and difference are registered, without conflated reading. [Scholarly and historical discussion layer] Two matters set side by side. (1) What "wateredst it with thy foot" refers to, set side by side: (a) The foot-tread water-lifting reading: treading a water-raising device with the foot (such as the counterweight or treadwheel of a shaduf-type apparatus), drawing Nile water into channels. (b) The foot-opened watercourse reading: opening and closing with the foot the mud mouths of small field channels, leading water into the furrows, as a herb garden is watered furrow by furrow. Both readings share the common point of "irrigation by fine human labour"; the divergence concerns only the device; registered side by side. (2) The strength of rendering dāraš as "careth for," set side by side: (a) The caring reading: seeking and tending, in the direction of favour. (b) The requiring reading: dāraš also carries the sense of searching out and calling to account (registered as a view); the care carries sovereign inquiry within it. The two readings are set side by side; what can be fixed is this: this verse pairs the word with "eyes," and the direction of the care is primary. [Summary layer] One governing sentence for the section: That land is not like Egypt. Egypt relies on a river, on feet, on human hands; this land has hills and valleys, drinks the rain of heaven, and lives under His eyes from the beginning of the year to the end of the year. God moved you to a land where you must look up — the terrain is the first lesson.

[Summary layer] One governing sentence for the section: The Shema, spoken the second time, is no longer confession only; it is the daily round: hearken diligently, and the rain comes in season, and corn, wine, and oil are all gathered in; let the heart stray, and the heaven is shut. So store the words in your heart, bind them on your hand, teach them to your children, write them on your door posts — your days, and your children's days, are staked on these few sentences.

### V. A Blessing and a Curse Set Before You (11:26–32; 48:00–57:00)

[Factual layer] Verse 26, quoted in full — the chapter's hinge sentence: "Behold, I set before you this day a blessing and a curse;" Registered item by item: (1) "Behold" (Hebrew rəʾēh) is a singular imperative, addressing the assembly as one: the whole congregation is answered as one man — the choice here is corporate, and it is also each person's own. ## Devotional Extract

Scripture (KJV) — Deuteronomy 11:12: "A land which the Lord thy God careth for: the eyes of the Lord thy God are always upon it, from the beginning of the year even unto the end of the year."

Verification note: The English Scripture paragraph in the source manuscript's devotional extract was checked word by word against the KJV of Deut 11:12 fetched directly for this translation (BibleGateway KJV). It agrees verbatim; no correction was required, and this is registered here (discrepancies corrected on the authority of the KJV: 0 in this instance).

Reflection: Canaan is not a land watered by a river at your feet; it drinks the rain of heaven. Its supply is not in your hands — it is in the sky, in the seasons, under His eyes. From the beginning of the year to the end of the year, His eyes are always upon it: there is no off-season, no stretch of days outside His gaze. The "land" you live in today — your home, your work, your children — is secured the same way: not by stockpiles, but by being watched over. Yet the same chapter says the rest: the land He watches has two ways set before it, a blessing and a curse. The days when heaven is shut up and there is no rain usually do not begin with the weather; they begin with a heart deceived and feet turned aside. Today, take your eyes off the rain gauge. Look first at His eyes upon you, then at where you are standing: on the side of Gerizim, or on the side of Ebal?

Prayer: Lord my God, Thine eyes are upon the land where I dwell from the beginning of the year to the end of the year. Make me diligent to hearken and careful to keep Thy commandments; let my heart not be deceived, nor my feet turn aside from Thy way. Teach me, like that land, to drink only the rain Thou sendest, and to cleave unto Thee. In Jesus' name, Amen.

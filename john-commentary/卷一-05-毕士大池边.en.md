# Volume One · Article 5: The Man Who Had Lain There for Thirty-Eight Years Stood Up

> **Translation status:** Initial translation, pending review by a native-speaking Christian reviewer.
> **Note:** Translated from the Chinese manuscript «卷一 · 第 5 篇　躺了三十八年的人，站起来了».
> **Author:** Guangyi Zhao

- Author: Guangyi Zhao
- Core Scripture: John 5:1-18

---

In the previous article, by the well at Sychar, a woman set down her waterpot and went into the city calling people. In this article, the scene changes to the side of a pool in Jerusalem. It was full of sick people lying there, yet so quiet it weighed on the heart. No one shouted, and no one contended; everyone was waiting for the same thing: waiting for the water to move.

Among the crowd was one person who had already been lying there for thirty-eight years. What does thirty-eight years mean? A child lying there from birth into middle age, his hair should already be showing white. Did he ever get the pool water he waited for? In this chapter, Jesus walked up to him and asked only one question. That question sounds almost superfluous: would a man who had been ill for thirty-eight years not want to get well? Yet Jesus asked precisely this question.

## I. The Scripture

John first describes the place in fine detail:

> After this there was a feast of the Jews, and Jesus went up to Jerusalem. Now there is in Jerusalem by the Sheep Gate a pool, which is called in Hebrew, Bethesda, having five porches. In these lay a great multitude of sick people, blind, lame, paralyzed. (John 5:1-3)

"Paralyzed" — in the Chinese Union Version an older expression for those whose limbs were withered and wasted, paralyzed and unable to move. Under the porches lay people who were all at the end of their road, staking their last bit of hope on this pool water.

> Now a certain man was there who had an infirmity thirty-eight years. When Jesus saw him lying there, and knew that he already had been in that condition a long time, He said to him, "Do you want to be made well?" The sick man answered Him, "Sir, I have no man to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up; but while I am coming, another steps down before me." (John 5:5-7)

> Jesus said to him, "Rise, take up your bed and walk." And immediately the man was made well, took up his bed, and walked. (John 5:8-9)

Trouble followed immediately. That day happened to be the Sabbath. The Jewish religious leaders stopped this man who had just become able to walk, and said that carrying his bed on the Sabbath was against the rules. He could only answer one sentence: He who made me well said to me, Take up your bed and walk. But he did not even know yet who the One who made him well was.

Later, Jesus found him in the temple, and there was a sentence He said to him alone:

> Afterward Jesus found him in the temple, and said to him, "See, you have been made well. Sin no more, lest a worse thing come upon you." (John 5:14)

By the end, the conflict landed on a startling sentence. Jesus explained why He also worked on the Sabbath:

> But Jesus answered them, "My Father has been working until now, and I have been working." (John 5:17)

John immediately made clear why these words were such a great offense:

> Therefore the Jews sought all the more to kill Him, because He not only broke the Sabbath, but also said that God was His Father, making Himself equal with God. (John 5:18)

First, let us clarify two expressions.

One is Bethesda. This name is Aramaic, and its meaning is roughly "house of mercy." The name is very warm, but the place was very cold: beside a pool called the house of mercy, a man lay for thirty-eight years, and no one put him into the water. The words "mercy" hanging on the door, and mercy truly coming upon a person, are two different things. In the second half of this chapter you will see that the hands lacking in the human house of mercy, Jesus personally supplied.

The other is "made well." The Greek original of Jesus's question, "Do you want to be made well?" asks: Do you want to become a healthy, whole person? What He asked was not only whether his legs could walk, but whether this person was willing from now on to change his way of living. Ill for thirty-eight years, lying there had already become a whole way of life: where he lay, what he lived on to eat, how others saw him, how he saw himself — all had been set in form. To stand up meant this whole set would all have to change. So this sentence was not asking what was already known; it was pushing the choice before him.

There is one more matter to state clearly in the body text first. When the sick man said "when the water is stirred up," he was referring to a saying circulating at the time: when the pool water was stirred, the first person to go down could be healed. The Chinese Union Version, in the second half of verse 3 through verse 4, has the explanation of an angel going down into the pool and stirring the water; this passage is not in the earliest manuscripts — it was added by later copyists to explain this circulating belief. This article does not build its argument on that added text; it reads only according to the words the sick man himself spoke: he truly believed this method, and he truly never once got ahead of others.

## II. Exposition

First, look at why Jesus sought him out first.

Many sick people lay beside the pool, and John does not say that Jesus healed them one by one. In this chapter, Jesus walked toward only one person, and it was the one who had been lying there the longest. The number thirty-eight years will sound familiar to those who read the Old Testament: the Israelites wandered in the wilderness for about forty years in total, and of these, from leaving Kadesh until crossing the brook Zered, Scripture specifically records thirty-eight years (see Deuteronomy 2:14). John may not necessarily have set a code here, but in the hearts of Jewish readers this number naturally carries the weight of a generation stuck in place, unable to enter the promised land. This man's life was a small wilderness: he could see Jerusalem, he could see the temple, the Sheep Gate was right beside him, and the sheep for sacrifice went in and out through it, yet he could only lie there.

His answer is the saddest passage in the whole chapter. He did not say he wanted to, and he did not say he did not want to. Jesus asked whether he wanted it; all he answered was difficulties: No one helps me; while I am coming, another steps down before me. In one sentence, it was all others and water; the only thing missing was whether he himself wanted it. When a person waits too long, he slowly becomes like this: what remains in his mouth is all why it cannot be done, and that sentence in his heart — I want it — can no longer be spoken. This is not dishonesty; it is the shape of despair simmered for a long time. Jesus did not correct him, nor wait for him to first set his attitude right. He went straight past that pool and commanded him to rise, take up his bed, and walk.

Pay attention to this order. The method of healing he had waited thirty-eight years for had one word at its core: scramble. As soon as the moment came when the water was stirred, whoever went down first obtained it. This was a method fated to make the weakest people suffer most: the blind could not see the water move, the lame walked the slowest, the paralyzed could not move at all. The more seriously ill a person was, the less he could scramble for it. Jesus's way was exactly the reverse: He did not tell people to scramble; He personally walked up to the person least able to scramble, and gave him the healing freely. The quietest and yet most powerful stroke in the whole chapter is right here: that man did not even know who He was, and he first received the healing. When grace fell on him, his knowledge of Jesus was zero.

Next, that bed. The bed was all his possessions: lying, it was padded under his body; walking, it should have been left in place. Jesus insisted that he take it up and walk. The action of walking carrying his bed was equal to a public declaration: I am no longer the man who was lying there. Yet this day happened to be the Sabbath. Carrying things on the Sabbath counted as work under the rules laid down by the rabbis; even the prophet Jeremiah once warned against bearing burdens in and out on the Sabbath (see Jeremiah 17:21-22). What the religious leaders fixed their eyes on was precisely this bed: the rule had been broken. What Jesus valued was that person: the man had stood up. In their eyes, the whereabouts of a bed mattered more than a man's thirty-eight years of illness. In chapter 9 we will see the same contrast again; this is its first appearance, and John remembered it clearly.

That sentence, "Sin no more," must also be read carefully. This sentence is very easily read as a formula: you see, he was ill because he sinned. But just a few chapters later, Jesus personally denied this kind of one-size-fits-all reckoning: when the disciples asked whose sin it was that a man was born blind, He said, "Neither this man nor his parents sinned" (see John 9:3; Article 9 of this book). Put the two places together and the meaning is complete: the road of inferring a person's sin from his illness — Jesus blocked this road dead; but toward this specific person, Jesus knew his story. He found him alone in the temple and gave him a weighty reminder: You have received a new body; do not go back to the way of living that will drag you back onto the bed. Healing is not the end point; where you walk after standing up is what Jesus truly cared about.

Finally, that sentence that provoked murderous intent: "My Father has been working until now, and I have been working." The Jews kept the Sabbath, rooted in Genesis: God worked six days and rested on the seventh. But the rabbis themselves also admitted that God did not stop upholding this world on the Sabbath; the sun rose as usual, children were born as usual, life did not stop for a moment. Jesus took the words one level further: the Father still works until now, and He also works, and He called God His own Father. Those who heard did not mishear at all, which is why their reaction was so great: this was not merely a Sabbath dispute; this was Him placing Himself in a position of equality with God. By this point in the Gospel of John, the conflict formally escalated: in the previous chapters, Jesus was a teacher who amazed people; from this chapter on, He became a question on which one must take a stand: is He merely a good man, or is He the One He claimed to be?

For this man who was healed, Scripture does not write much about his ending. It only records that he went back and told the Jews that it was Jesus who had made him well (see John 5:15). Some say he informed on Him; some say he was only telling the truth as it was; the text passes no judgment, and we need not determine his character for him. What can be certain is that he walked out carrying his bed. A life that had been lived lying down for thirty-eight years was, on that day, lived standing for the first time.

## III. Application

The entry point of this article is that person who waited too long. Jesus's question, "Do you want to be made well?" read today, almost seems to be asking everyone who has stayed in a predicament for a long time.

First, those three words: "no man." All the sick man's despair was packed into these three words: I have no man to put me into the pool. You may have your own version of this sentence: No one pulls me up, no one understands me, no money, no connections, no one willing to give me a chance. These words are not necessarily false; very often they are the actual situation. The most grinding part is that, year after year living by the same set of explanations, the explanations slowly grow into an identity: I am just the person no one helps, I am just the person who cannot scramble for it, this is just his fate. Jesus did not refute his actual situation, nor hand him a bowl of inspirational soup. What He did was another thing: He Himself became that "man." He said he had no man, and standing before him was precisely that One. Very often, a turning point is not that the conditions suddenly all fall into place; it is that a Person truly walked up to you, and He has the power, not merely sympathy.

Next, the matter of not being able to scramble for it. The rules of this pool are not at all unfamiliar to people today. There are only so many good opportunities, and if your hands are slow they are gone; as soon as places are released, they vanish in seconds; it is a contest of speed, of connections, of who gets the news first. Those in good health, with many resources and quick movements, take all the advantage; the people who truly need it most always rank last. In this chapter, the table Jesus overturned was precisely this one. His healing does not rely on scrambling, needs no queuing, and does not look at who moves fastest. The man who in thirty-eight years never once won the scramble was personally sought out and visited. If you always feel you are the slow one, the late one, the one who missed it, this chapter was written specifically for people like you.

One must also honestly ask: Do you truly want to stand up? This question sounds piercing, yet it cannot be avoided. Some predicaments, stayed in for a long time, grow a few things one is reluctant to let go. Lying down is bitter, but lying down requires no responsibility: if you cannot change, you can blame the illness; if you cannot get up, you can blame having no one to help. Standing up is different: you have to walk on your own, face real matters like finding work, repaying debts, and repairing relationships, and take responsibility for your own days. It is the same with habits that cannot be changed — drink, the phone, temper, a relationship known to be harmful; the pain of withdrawal is real, but the price of continuing, you also know clearly in your heart. Jesus asked that question not to humiliate him; it was to leave the most important step for him to say out loud himself. Help was already standing before him, but that sentence — I want it — no one can say it for you.

There is another layer: after standing up. Jesus found him in the temple, and what He said did not sound very pleasant, yet it was truly for his good: Sin no more, lest a worse thing come upon you. The mistake people who have obtained a turning point most easily make is to turn around and go back onto the old road, as if those thirty-eight years had never happened. The body gets well, and they stay up back into the old ways; the debts are cleared, and they swipe back into the old ways; a relationship is repaired, and they speak again the old hurtful words. Jesus's reminder was stated very plainly: some "worse things" do not fall from the sky; one walks back to them step by step oneself. He first gave grace, then gave the reminder; the order cannot be read in reverse: it is not that you first change for the better and only then deserve healing; it is that you are first helped up, and then do not lie back down.

Finally, a word about rules. The performance of the religious leaders that day is worth a look in the mirror by people today who call themselves believers, and also worth seeing clearly by those disappointed with religion: the ones who barred a man from carrying his bed were not Jesus, but people who kept the rules until there was no human heart left. If you were once blocked outside the door of faith by some rules and some cold faces, please separate these two groups of people. The One who walked to the side of the pool, bent down, and asked you whether you want to be made well — that is Jesus Himself. To this day He has not become too busy to care for the one in the furthest corner.

## IV. Response

Having read this far, you might pause and think about a few questions.

- If Jesus stood before you today and asked you that question — Do you want to be made well? — what would your first reaction be? Would it be a blurted-out "I want to," or would you first recount a string of difficulties: no man, no time, cannot scramble for it?
- Is there a bed in your life on which you have been lying for a long time? A matter you have grown used to explaining with bad fate and no one helping — how many years has it been lying on you?
- The sick man said he had no man. Have you discovered that, when you say you have no man, is it possible that there is One who has in fact always been there, only you have never counted Him in that number of "man"?
- If you truly stand up, what is the first thing tomorrow you will have to do with your own hands? Find a job, repay a debt, make an apology, or first break that unchangeable habit for one day?

If you are willing, you can follow along with a very short prayer:

"Lord Jesus, I admit I have lain here a long time; I have recited a big pile of reasons, yet that sentence in my heart — I want it — I cannot say it out loud. Today I will say this sentence out: I want to get well, I want to stand up. Please, just as You walked up to that man beside the pool, walk over to me here, help me up, and also stop me from lying back down. Amen."

There are also entrances for continuing onward. In the previous article we read chapter 4, where Jesus waited by the well for a woman who was avoiding people; in this article, He sought beside the pool a man who had waited until he was numb. In the next article we will read John chapter 6: after five thousand people were fed, Jesus said He is the bread of life; those who are full yet still empty must listen to where that sentence comes from. You can also find a Christian friend you trust, and frankly tell him what your bed is. Speaking it out is itself the first step from lying down to sitting up.
